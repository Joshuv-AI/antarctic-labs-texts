Increased incidence of glaucoma and intraocular pressure fluctuation after vitrectomy for macular hole and macular pucker
Authors: Tsung-En Huang, Eugene Yu-Chuan Kang, Chu-Yen Huang, Henry Shen-Lih Chen, Yu-Ching Wang, Wei-Chi Wu, Yung-Sung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:To investigate the incidence of glaucoma and changes in intraocular pressure fluctuation (IOPF) after vitrectomy for macular pucker (MP) and macular hole (MH).MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, 778 patients who underwent only one vitrectomy for MP or MH were enrolled from the Chang Gung Research Database. Glaucoma development was defined as the continuous prescription of anti-glaucoma medication for more than 3 months, and the diagnosis was made later than 60 days postoperatively (index date). Postoperative IOPF was defined as the standard deviation (SD) of IOP measurements after the index date, whereas postoperative pretreatment IOPF was defined as the SD of IOP measured from the index date until the initiation of glaucoma treatment. The incidence of postoperative glaucoma and IOPF in vitrectomized eyes was compared with those in the nonvitrectomized fellow eyes.RESULTS:Forty vitrectomized and 17 fellow eyes developed glaucoma after the index date during the follow-up period (60 months). The vitrectomized eyes showed a higher glaucoma incidence (9.06, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.25–11.86) than fellow eyes (3.75, 95% CI: 1.97–5.53), along with higher postoperative IOPF (1.93 vs. 1.74 mmHg, P = 0.004) and higher postoperative pretreatment IOPF (1.88 vs. 1.72 mmHg, P = 0.001). Among vitrectomized eyes, those with glaucoma development had higher postoperative IOPF (2.79 vs. 1.85 mmHg, P < 0.0001) and postoperative pretreatment IOPF (2.32 vs. 1.85 mmHg, P = 0.04) than those without.CONCLUSION:Higher postoperative IOPF and higher glaucoma incidence were observed in eyes that underwent vitrectomy for MP or MH.

Introduction

Pars plana vitrectomy is a widely used and generally safe surgical procedure for various vitreoretinal diseases.[12] However, complications can still occur after vitrectomy, and glaucoma development is reportedly one of them.[123] Although multiple studies have investigated the incidence of glaucoma or intraocular pressure (IOP) changes after vitrectomy, the results remain controversial. The contradictory findings may be attributable to the vitrectomy criteria.[4567] Studies focusing on macular hole (MH) or macular pucker (MP) found no significant increase in the development of open-angle glaucoma (OAG).[45] In contrast, studies on more complex vitrectomies, such as those for retinal detachment, have reported a significant increase in OAG development after vitrectomy.[67] The Prospective Retinal and Optic Nerve Vitrectomy Evaluation study also showed a significant elevation in IOP after vitrectomy for MH, but not for MP.[8] The main hypothesis for glaucoma development and IOP elevation after vitrectomy is oxidative damage to the trabecular meshwork. The vitreous acts as a buffer protecting the crystalline lens and trabecular meshwork from oxidative stress in the retina. After vitrectomy, loss of the vitreous exposes the trabecular meshwork to oxidative damage, reducing aqueous outflow and causing IOP elevation or glaucoma development.[9] More extensive vitrectomies, especially those combined with other procedures such as retinal laser photocoagulation and silicone oil or gas tamponade, may lead to a reduction in the vitreous protective effect, stronger postoperative inflammation, and prolonged topical steroid use, thereby increasing the risk of ocular hypertension (OHT) and glaucoma.

Several studies have shown that IOP fluctuation (IOPF) is a significant factor in glaucoma progression.[1011] IOPF is often defined as the standard deviation (SD) of IOP measurements. Patients with OAG exhibiting IOPF >2 mmHg are at higher risk of glaucoma progression, compared with those with IOPF of 2 mmHg or less.[12] Although changes in IOP after vitrectomy have been widely discussed in the literature, to the best of our knowledge, changes in the IOPF after vitrectomy have not been reported.

This study aimed to clarify the long-term incidence of glaucoma and changes in the IOPF after vitrectomy. We used a multi-institutional electronic database spanning 20 years, comprising a large number of cases and long follow-up period. To avoid the influence of heterogeneous indications of vitrectomy, we focused on patients who underwent only one vitrectomy for MH or MP. The primary outcome was the risk of glaucoma development after vitrectomy, which was evaluated by comparing the glaucoma incidence rate in the postoperative 60 months between vitrectomized and nonvitrectomized fellow eyes. The secondary outcome was the change in IOPF after vitrectomy, which was assessed by comparing the postoperative IOPF between the vitrectomized and fellow eyes. Among the vitrectomized eyes, we further compared the postoperative IOPF between patients with and without glaucoma development. The findings of this study may enhance our understanding of the development of IOPF and glaucoma after vitrectomy.

Materials and Methods

Study population

In this retrospective cohort study, we enrolled patients from the Chang Gung Research Database (CGRD) who underwent vitrectomy for MP or MH between January 2000 and July 2021. CGRD is the second largest electronic medical record database of data from multiple institutions in Taiwan, and includes 6.1% of outpatients and 10.2% of hospitalized patients in Taiwan.[13] The CGRD provides detailed laboratory and ocular examination data, such as visual acuity (VA), best corrected VA (BCVA), and IOP, which are usually unavailable in other databases. This study was approved by the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Institutional Review Board (No. 202101299B0C501) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged >18 years who underwent vitrectomy for MP or MH once were included in this cohort study. The exclusion criteria included two or more vitrectomies, other vitreoretinal surgeries, diagnosis of any glaucoma before vitrectomy or before the index date, and a follow-up period of <1 year [Figure 1]. The index date was defined as 60 days after the vitrectomy to exclude cases of short-term OHT associated with gas tamponade or steroid use in the early postoperative period.[141516]

Flowchart detailing the inclusion and exclusion criteria for vitrectomized patients in this study. *Index date was defined as 60 days after vitrectomy, †Glaucoma diagnosis was defined as continuous prescriptions of antiglaucoma eye drops for more than 3 months

Covariates

Ocular examination data, including VA and IOP, and demographic data, comprising age, sex, indication for vitrectomy, comorbidities, and follow-up period, were collected from the CGRD. The International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes for vitrectomy indications and comorbidities were used. National Health Insurance reimbursement codes were used for vitrectomy surgery.

Definition of glaucoma diagnosis after vitrectomy and intraocular pressure fluctuation

The diagnosis of glaucoma was based on a continuous prescription of anti-glaucoma eye drops for three or more months,[17181920] which should have been made no sooner than the index date to avoid the influence of short-term OHT after vitrectomy.[1415] Prescriptions of glaucoma medications are under the regular supervision of the National Health Insurance Ophthalmic Review Committee and are limited to patients diagnosed with glaucoma based on the optic nerve structure, IOP, and visual field results. Only those who meet the diagnostic criteria for glaucoma can receive a continuous prescription of anti-glaucoma medications for 3 months. In this study, the majority of patients with glaucoma returned to the clinic every 3 months for the prescriptions, and IOPF was defined as the SD of all IOP measurements within the indicated time period.[21] Preoperative IOPF referred to the SD of all the IOP data before the vitrectomy, while postoperative IOPF indicated the SD of the IOP data measured on the index date, at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively, and every 6 months after the 1st year. Additionally, the postoperative pretreatment IOPF referred to the SD of the IOP data from the index date to the initiation date of the antiglaucoma eye drops.

Outcomes analysis

The primary outcome measure in this study was glaucoma incidence, and the secondary outcome measure was IOPF after vitrectomy. The incidence rate of glaucoma from the index date to 60 months postoperatively was calculated and compared between the vitrectomized eyes and nonvitrectomized fellow eyes of the same patient. In addition, the vitrectomized eyes were separated into the MH or MP subgroups to investigate the association between the vitrectomy indications and glaucoma incidence rate.

We compared the preoperative IOPF, postoperative IOPF, and postoperative pretreatment IOPF between vitrectomized and nonvitrectomized eyes. In addition, vitrectomized or nonvitrectomized eyes were further separated into two groups: eyes with glaucoma development and eyes without glaucoma over the entire follow-up period to investigate the difference in IOPF. Longitudinal average changes in the IOP within 60 months postoperatively were also determined and compared between the vitrectomized and nonvitrectomized eyes.

Univariate and multivariate risk analyses with Cox proportional hazards regression were used to investigate the clinical factors associated with glaucoma incidence, including spherical equivalence, central corneal thickness, uncorrected and BCVA, preoperative IOPF, postoperative IOPF, postoperative pretreatment IOPF, intraoperative triamcinolone use, and vitrectomy indications. For the multivariate model, we selected all the significant univariate predictors and used backward selection to eliminate the nonsignificant variables at 0.1 level.

Statistical analysis

Postvitrectomy glaucoma incidence was calculated per 10,000 person-months, and the crude relative risk of glaucoma in vitrectomized eyes was compared with that in nonvitrectomized eyes as a reference. Gray’s test was used to compare the cumulative incidence of glaucoma between the groups. Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare the IOPF among different groups, while the paired t-test was used to compare the longitudinal mean IOP change between vitrectomized and nonvitrectomized eyes. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for univariate and multivariate analyses of the clinical factors associated with glaucoma incidence.

Results

As shown in Figure 1, a total of 7391 patients who underwent vitrectomy were identified in the CGRD between January 2000 and July 2021, of whom 778 patients met the inclusion criteria for this study. Forty new glaucoma diagnoses were made in the period between the index date and 60 months postoperatively in the vitrectomized eyes, whereas only 17 new glaucoma diagnoses were made in the nonvitrectomized eyes. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the study participants. The primary indication for vitrectomy was MP (69%), followed by MH (31%). In addition, 53% of the patients underwent clinical follow-up for more than 60 months, and the average follow-up time was 74.85 months.

Incidence of glaucoma after vitrectomy

We investigated the glaucoma incidence rate from the index date to 60 months postoperatively [Table 2]. The glaucoma incidence rates for total vitrectomized eyes, MP subgroup, and MH subgroup were 9.06 (6.25–11.86), 7.47 (4.42–10.52), and 12.72 (6.68–18.76) per 10,000 person-months, respectively. In addition, an increase in the crude relative risk for glaucoma incidence was observed in all vitrectomized eyes (2.4), MP (1.99), and MH subgroups (3.34), in comparison to nonvitrectomized eyes. Figure 2 shows that the cumulative probability of glaucoma was higher in all vitrectomized eyes and MP and MH subgroups at 60 months postoperatively. Most cases of glaucoma in vitrectomized eyes developed within 10 months after surgery, and the slope of glaucoma cumulative incidence gradually flattened and became parallel to the curve of nonvitrectomized eyes. Although the difference between the MH and MP subgroups did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.10) in 60 months, the cumulative probability of glaucoma in the MH subgroup was significantly higher at 48 months than in the MP subgroup at any moment [P = 0.03 at 48 months, not shown in Figure 2].

Intraocular pressure fluctuation after vitrectomy

Table 3 shows the IOPFs in the vitrectomized and nonvitrectomized groups, both preoperatively and postoperatively. There was no significant difference in the preoperative IOPF between the two groups. However, the vitrectomized eyes showed higher postoperative IOPF (1.93 mmHg vs. 1.74 mmHg, P = 0.001) and higher postoperative pretreatment IOPF (1.88 mmHg vs. 1.72 mmHg, P = 0.004) than the nonvitrectomized eyes. In addition, among the vitrectomized eyes, those with glaucoma development showed no difference in the preoperative IOPF (1.67 mmHg vs. 1.65 mmHg, P = 0.87). However, after vitrectomy, they demonstrated a higher postoperative IOPF (2.79 mmHg vs. 1.85 mmHg, P < 0.0001) and postoperative pretreatment IOPF (2.32 mmHg vs. 1.85 mmHg, P = 0.04) than those without glaucoma. Among the eyes that developed glaucoma, we observed no change in IOPF over time following antiglaucoma treatment. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, there was no significant difference in postglaucoma treatment IOPF between the first 2 years and the 3rd–4th years in either vitrectomized eyes (2.8 mmHg vs. 2.5 mmHg, P = 0.57) or nonvitrectomized eyes (2.6 mmHg vs. 2.5 mmHg, P = 0.88).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we focused on patients who underwent vitrectomy for MP or MH and observed not only an increased incidence of glaucoma after vitrectomy but also higher postoperative IOPF and higher postoperative pretreatment IOPF in vitrectomized eyes that developed glaucoma. In a systemic review and meta-analysis, Miele et al. reported an increased odds ratio (OR) for glaucoma after vitrectomy (OR = 1.67, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08–2.57),[3] which aligns with our finding. Despite an increased glaucoma risk, no elevation in the IOP was observed in our study. The average IOP in vitrectomized eyes was lower than that in the fellow eyes in the sixth to the eighteenth postoperative months. Similarly, Ki et al. reported that the IOP decreased 3 months postoperatively and returned to baseline by 5 years.[22] However, as shown in Figure 2, the cumulative probability of glaucoma in vitrectomized eyes increased rapidly in the 1st year after vitrectomy, overlapping the period of lower IOP. Based on these observations, the absolute IOP may not be the real cause of glaucoma development after vitrectomy, and increased IOPF after vitrectomy was suspected to be one of the reasons.

IOPF is a significant factor in glaucoma progression and may play a particularly important role in patients with normal IOP.[101123] The Advanced Glaucoma Intervention Study found that long-term IOPF was associated with glaucoma progression only in the low IOP group but not in the high IOP group.[24] Similarly, Fukuchi et al. reported that higher IOPF was associated with rapid visual field progression in patients with NTG, but not in patients with POAG.[25]

Each 1 mmHg elevation in IOPF increased the risk of glaucoma progression by 20–30%.[2426] Hong et al. reviewed 408 glaucomatous eyes with IOP <18 mmHg and found that eyes with IOPF >2 mmHg had more visual field progression (30% vs. 9.7%) compared to those with IOPF ≤2 mmHg. The IOP between the two groups demonstrated no significant difference, indicating that IOPF, rather than the average IOP, is associated with glaucoma progression.[12] In our study, the IOPF in vitrectomized eyes with glaucoma development increased from 1.67 to 2.32 mmHg postsurgery, exceeding the IOPF >2 mmHg threshold in the study by Hong et al.[12] In nonvitrectomized eyes with glaucoma development, the preoperative IOPF was already 2.73 mmHg, reflecting the risk of glaucoma development. Antiglaucoma therapy is associated with a reduction in diurnal and long-term IOPF.[2728] However, as shown in Supplementary Table 1, vitrectomized eyes that developed glaucoma showed no change in IOPF between the first 2 years and the 3rd–4th years after antiglaucoma therapy, and the IOPF remained above the 2 mmHg threshold. Therefore, whether patients who develop glaucoma after vitrectomy are at a higher risk of glaucoma progression requires further investigation.

Several factors may explain the increase in the IOPF after vitrectomy. Oxidative damage to the trabecular meshwork or trabecular hyperplasia due to prolonged steroid use may destabilize the aqueous outflow.[9] A short-term decrease in the anterior chamber depth by intravitreal gas tamponade may cause appositional angle closure, and postoperative inflammation may further lead to synechial angle closure.[29] Nuclear sclerotic cataracts with increasing lens thickness can cause pupillary blocks and fluctuations in the IOP.

Despite the supportive evidence, interpretation of the IOPF results requires caution. Although the postoperative pretreatment IOPF was significantly higher in vitrectomized eyes with glaucoma, it was not significantly related to the long-term glaucoma incidence in our univariate analysis. In addition, studies have presented conflicting results on the role of IOPF in the development of glaucoma. Medeiros et al. found no association between the IOPF and glaucoma conversion in patients with OHT.[30] Similarly, the Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial showed no association between IOPF and glaucoma progression.[21]

The surgical indications for vitrectomy may be associated with the incidence of glaucoma. Studies including patients with more complex vitrectomy surgeries, such as for MH, rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, or vitreous hemorrhage, have often reported a higher incidence of glaucoma or IOP elevation.[1415313233] However, studies involving patients undergoing vitrectomy solely for MP found no significant IOP change.[832] In our study, the MH subgroup also had a higher hazard ratio for glaucoma incidence than the MP subgroup within 48 months after surgery. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain this observation. The vitreous protects the crystalline lens and trabecular meshwork from oxidative damage,[9] and it may be removed more completely during complex vitrectomy surgeries. In addition, intravitreal gas tamponade and prone positioning, which are more frequently needed for MH, can cause IOP elevation due to secondary angle closure and pupillary block.[34]

In addition to the vitreous, the crystalline lens acts as a buffer to protect the trabecular meshwork from oxidative damage after vitrectomy.[9] Unfortunately, we could not evaluate the association between the lens status and incidence of glaucoma. Since the CGRD is not a nationwide database, we could not obtain accurate information on the lens status if the patient had undergone cataract surgery at other medical services. Several studies have reported that pseudophakic patients are at increased risk of IOP elevation or glaucoma development after vitrectomy,[89143135] while others have shown the opposite results.[1633] In a study conducted in the Taiwanese population, phakic patients without cataract surgery after vitrectomy had a higher glaucoma incidence rate compared to those already pseudophakic at the time of vitrectomy (crude relative risk = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.80–3.67).[16] Conversely, phakic patients who underwent cataract surgery after vitrectomy had a lower glaucoma incidence rate (crude relative risk = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.32–0.72).[16] Though pseudophakia may expose the trabecular meshwork to more oxidative stress, patients may benefit from IOP reduction after cataract surgery due to the deepening of the anterior chamber. The average IOP reduction rates after cataract surgery in the general population, patients with POAG, and patients with chronic angle closure glaucoma were 6.6%, 13%, and 30%, respectively.[3637] Given the higher risk of angle closure glaucoma in Asian populations,[38] further investigation is needed to understand the association between pseudophakia and glaucoma development after vitrectomy in Asia.

This study has several strengths. First, by leveraging extensive CGRD data, our research encompasses a significantly large sample size and a prolonged follow-up period. We identified 7391 patients who underwent vitrectomy for MP and MH and focused on 778 patients with an average follow-up period of 74.85 months, all of whom underwent vitrectomy in only one eye, with the fellow eye serving as the control. Our sample size was 2–3 times larger than that of most previous studies, with over half of the patients being followed up for more than 5 years.[3] Second, unlike other databases, the CGRD provides IOP data, allowing us to investigate the postoperative IOP changes in a large sample of patients. Most importantly, our study is the first to report changes in IOPF after vitrectomy. We found that vitrectomized eyes had a higher IOPF than their fellow eyes. Among the vitrectomized eyes, those that developed glaucoma had a higher IOPF than those that did not.

This study has some limitations. First, owing to the retrospective design of the database research, missing data and incomplete records were inevitable. We could not investigate the distribution of different types of glaucoma after vitrectomy without bias because many patients used an imprecise ICD-10-CM code, for example, code for unspecified glaucoma or absence of any glaucoma-related code, even though they used anti-glaucoma medication regularly for years. Second, in the real-world clinical setting, we found that the physicians sometimes used imprecise ICD coding. Hence, we used continuous prescription of anti-glaucoma medication for at least 3 months as the surrogate marker to identify the glaucoma cases, which has been validated in several database studies.[181920] Fujita et al. also reported that glaucoma diagnostic algorithms, including anti-glaucoma prescriptions for 6 months, showed noninferior results for the positive predictive value (96.9% vs. 84.4%) and negative predictive value (86.1% vs. 85.4%) in comparison to the diagnostic algorithms with ICD-10-CM codes.[39]

Conclusion

Our study showed that patients undergoing vitrectomy for MP or MH were at a higher risk of glaucoma development, and that increased IOPF may play a key role in its development. Future studies on glaucoma incidence and changes in IOPF after vitrectomy for other surgical indications are warranted to clarify the association between glaucoma development and vitrectomy.
