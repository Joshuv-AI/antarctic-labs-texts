Assessing the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani teaching hospital, Ghana
Authors: Antwi Joseph Barimah, Paul Gyan, Solomon Owiredu Boateng, James Dumba, Adom-Brobbey David, Larry Agyemang, Bernard Opoku Amoah, Suraiya Abdullah, Aboraa Ernestina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproper hygienic disposal of patient stools in healthcare settings presents considerable dangers to infection management and the well-being of healthcare personnel, patients, and the local community. Although health workers play an essential role in ensuring proper sanitation and preventing infections, investigations have revealed shortcomings in adherence to stool disposal protocols in numerous healthcare environments, especially in low- and middle-income nations such as Ghana. The study therefore assessed the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani Teaching Hospital. A descriptive cross-sectional study design was employed which allowed for association assessment but not causal inference. 315 participants were selected using stratified random sampling. Study participants were health workers. Respondents had surprisingly lower knowledge level on health hazards associated with improper disposal of patient stool (53.7%) and Moderate adherence level (77.8%) to hygienically dispose patient stool. Factors such as knowledge level, Training, resource availability, workload, Personal Risk Perception, Motivation and Attitude were found to be associated with low adherence level with p-values less than 0.05. Respondents demonstrated limited understanding of infection hazards but showed moderate adherence, largely influenced by institutional enforcement and available resources. The study concludes that while adherence to hygienic stool disposal was largely moderate, significant knowledge gaps remain that require targeted interventions. Improving continuous training, supervision and supply of disposal materials could enhance sustained compliance among healthcare workers.Keywords: Stool, Hygienic Disposal, Adherence, Health Hazard, Waste ManagementSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Health occupations, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 85% of healthcare waste is non-hazardous, similar to domestic waste. About 10% is classified as infectious, which includes cultures and stocks of infectious agents, waste from infected patients, materials contaminated with blood and its derivatives, discarded diagnostic samples, infected laboratory animals, and contaminated equipment. The remaining 5% consists of hazardous healthcare waste, encompassing chemical and radioactive materials1.

Healthcare waste generation, collection, transportation, storage, treatment, and disposal have recently emerged as important concerns in public health due to their potential harmful impacts on the environment and human health2. The primary sources of healthcare waste include health facilities, laboratories, chemical businesses, pharmaceutical stores and units, mortuary and autopsy centers, animal research laboratories, blood banks and collection centers, and nursing homes3. Literature has shown that 5.9 tons of the total 1.47 billion tons of solid waste generated globally are estimated to originate from hospitals4.

In developing countries, the expansion of healthcare facilities and the concomitant increase in waste present a significant challenge, compounded by insufficient technological and financial resources for the effective management of hospital waste5. Inadequate waste segregation, storage, transportation, and disposal practices result in increased environmental and occupational risks6.

Improper hygienic disposal of patient stools in healthcare settings presents considerable dangers to infection management and the well-being of healthcare personnel, patients, and the local community. Although health workers are play essential role in ensuring proper sanitation and preventing infections. Investigations have revealed shortcomings in adherence to stool disposal protocols in numerous healthcare environments, especially in low- and middle-income nations such as Ghana. The shortcomings observed are frequently linked to a lack of proper training, limited resources, and excessive workloads, which undermine compliance with hygiene standards7.

Improper handling of healthcare waste has been associated with multiple epidemiological and environmental concerns, such as unpleasant odors, unattractive debris, pollution, fire hazards, and the release of toxic compounds8. Injuries and the transmission of infectious diseases affecting humans, animals, and plants, including typhoid, diarrhea, cholera, malaria, schistosomiasis, and dermatological conditions, represent major health-related epidemiological issues9.

Research indicates that the adoption of sustainable waste management practices can reduce the negative effects of improper healthcare waste disposal on environmental and public health10. Sustainable solutions that advocate for the renewal of healthcare waste are strongly encouraged globally for the purpose of public health safety and environmental hygiene11.

One of the most important aspects of infection control in hospital settings is the sanitary disposal of patient faeces. Numerous pathogens, such as bacteria, viruses, and parasites, can be found in patient faeces. If left untreated, these pathogens can significantly increase the chance of healthcare-associated illnesses (HAIs) spreading12. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, poor disposal procedures can cause cross-contamination in hospital settings, which raises the risk of nosocomial infections and aids in the spread of antibiotic resistance13.

The implementation of appropriate biohazard stool management procedures, such as the secure disposal of patient feces, is spearheaded by health professionals. Their use of personal protective equipment (PPE) and adherence to established guidelines are crucial in reducing threats to the health of both personnel and patients. Hygienic disposal practices are considerably more likely to be followed when employees receive frequent training and strictly follow standard operating procedures (SOPs), according to research14. Nonetheless, research shows that difficulties still exist, especially in low-resource environments, where inadequate supplies, manpower, and infrastructure make it difficult to apply stool management techniques effectively15.

No known study has been conducted on the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers in the study area. This study therefore sought to bridge the gap by assessing the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani teaching hospital. The present study aims to assess the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani Teaching Hospital, Ghana. The scope of the study encompasses knowledge, adherence levels, and factors influencing compliance with stool disposal protocols. This study contributes novel evidence from a tertiary healthcare facility in Ghana, where empirical data on stool disposal practices among health workers remain scarce, addressing key gap in infection prevention research within low-resource hospital settings.

Methodology

Study area and design

The Sunyani Teaching Hospital, formerly referred to as Sunyani Regional Hospital, is the ultimate institution in a series of ultra-modern hospitals established by the Ministry of Health (MOH) in the late 1990s. The Hospital functioned as a secondary level facility from 4th August 2003 under the auspices of the Ghana Health Service (GHS) until its transition to a Teaching Hospital. The hospital is located in the Sunyani Municipality of the Bono Region of Ghana, approximately at latitude 7.336°N and longitude − 2.327°W. The hospital was selected because it serves as a tertiary referral centre for multiple districts in the Bono region, handling high patient volumes and diverse clinical services. A descriptive cross-sectional study design and a quantitative research approach were used to assess the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani teaching hospital. As a descriptive cross-sectional design, the study captures associations rather than causal relationships between factors influencing adherence. The study, which is conducted in a privileged Hospital had healthcare workers as the targeted population. Hence, health workers were the primary focus of the investigation. The total enrolment included health workers actively involved in patient care or waste management within the healthcare facility.

Sample size and sample technique

The study utilized a sample size of 315 health workers which was determined using Yamane formula at 95% confidence interval. Stratified random sampling was the probability sampling technique used in selecting participants for the study. The participants were grouped into categories and members in each stratum was randomly selected based on a proportionate distribution as shown in Table 1 below. Proportional allocation of strata was based on staff distribution obtained from the hospital’s Human Resource database to ensure representativeness of all professional categories.

Instrument for data collection and procedure for assessing the hygienic disposal of patient stools among health workers at the Sunyani teaching hospital

The data collection instrument for this study was a structured questionnaire designed to gather quantitative data on the hygienic stool disposal practices among health workers at Sunyani Teaching Hospital. The questionnaire included closed-ended questions to assess knowledge, influencing factors, and adherence to stool disposal protocols, as well as open-ended sections to capture additional comments or suggestions. It was divided into sections covering demographic details, awareness of hospital policies, availability of resources, and self-reported practices. The structured questionnaire was developed based on WHO12 and CDC13 guidelines for infection prevention and waste management. Content validity was established by three infection control experts. A pretest involving 30 healthcare workers at a comparable facility was conducted to assess clarity and face validity. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for reliability, yielding α = 0.78 for the knowledge scale and α = 0.82 for the adherence scale, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency.

Data collection was conducted over a specified timeframe (from January to February, 2025), during which questionnaires were distributed to health workers within their individual departments. Participants were informed about the study’s aims and guaranteed the anonymity of their responses. The questionnaires were administered in person by trained research assistants who offered assistance as needed and ensured prompt completion. Completed questionnaires were gathered on the same day to mitigate loss or delays. Non-respondents were followed up to enhance the response rate, guaranteeing thorough data collection within the specified timeframe.

This study employed descriptive and inferential statistical techniques for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, summarized demographic data, health workers’ knowledge levels, and adherence to hygienic stool disposal practices. Knowledge levels and adherence levels were categorized into low, moderate and high. Categorization of knowledge levels and adherence levels followed classification approaches used in prior studies on biomedical waste management (Agyekum & Ansah7, Tolera et al.,16).

Adherence data were analysed using mean composite scores on the 5-point Likert scale to preserve variability and minimize overestimation. Cross-tabulations were conducted to examine the relationships between variables, including job roles and compliance levels. Inferential analysis utilized chi-square tests to evaluate associations among categorical variables and logistic regression to determine predictors of adherence, including training, resource availability, and knowledge levels. Assumptions for Chi-square and logistic regression were tested and satisfied, including independence of observations, expected cell frequencies > 5, and absence of multicollinearity (VIF < 2.0). This analytical framework offered a thorough understanding of the factors influencing hygienic stool disposal practices at individual and organizational levels.

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations that ensures high ethical standards.

Discussion

Knowledge Level of Health Workers on Health Hazards associated with patient stool disposal

The study relied on self-reported adherence data, which may be influenced by social desirability bias, potentially inflating compliance estimates.

This section discusses the findings of the study by comparing it with other studies. The study found that over half (53.7%) of the health workers demonstrated a low level of knowledge while about 44.1% had a moderate level of knowledge with only 2.2% exhibiting a high level of knowledge. This finding is in line with an earlier study which reported low biosafety knowledge and awareness among health workers in Nigeria where improper biomedical waste disposal practices led to the detection of parasites in patient stool samples17. 87.2% of waste disposal workers in Egyptian outpatient clinics were seen to exhibit low knowledge level on patient stool disposal18. This shows that low knowledge level on waste disposal is quite evident among health workers. To support this further, another revealed that over 85% of nurses and doctors had low knowledge level on waste disposal19. Sanitary staff, and other lower-cadre health workers, are often untrained and unaware of proper stool disposal protocols. Poor knowledge and awareness lead to lots of risky practices which could put the health worker and other workers at risk. This is backed by another study which revealed that poor awareness among sanitary staff led to risky practices like unsegregated faecal waste disposal, exposing the hospital and the population to faecal illnesses20.

Emmanuel et al.21 conducted a cross-sectional study among 400 healthcare workers at the Children’s Hospital in Lahore and found that 56.5% of respondents had insufficient knowledge about healthcare waste management, while only 43.5% demonstrated adequate understanding (p < 0.05; 95% CI: 51.2–61.8). Their study also established that 50% of respondents practiced improper waste handling, including stool and liquid waste, reinforcing that low knowledge translates directly into unsafe hygienic behaviour.

Similarly, Nooreen et al.22 reported that only 58.3% of doctors and 39.8% of nurses in private hospitals in Pakistan were aware of healthcare waste management protocols, and just 6.5% of laboratory technicians had good awareness levels. The authors found significant disparities in knowledge levels across job categories (p < 0.01), indicating that nursing and support staff often responsible for stool handling possessed the lowest knowledge levels. This mirrors the pattern at Sunyani Teaching Hospital, where similar cadres are primarily responsible for patient excreta disposal.

In a more recent study, Suram et al.23 assessed biomedical waste management knowledge among 163 healthcare workers in India and found high knowledge scores (≥ 80%) among doctors and nurses, yet only 66.6% of auxiliary staff achieved comparable scores (p < 0.05) Although these figures appear more favourable than those of the current study, the authors emphasized that knowledge did not necessarily translate to compliant stool disposal practices, due to inconsistent training and supervision.

Adherence level of health workers to the disposal of patient stools

The study found that only 1.6% of medical professionals reported poor adherence to hygienic standards in waste disposal, which can significantly impede the incidence of nosocomial infections. Even though small deviations in adherence to infection prevention strategies can lead to outbreaks of major healthcare-associated illnesses like Clostridium difficile24. In contrast, 20.6% of healthcare professionals show strong commitment to hygiene standards, while 78% show only modest adherence. This trend is consistent worldwide, with a consistent pattern of moderate adherence with low rates of high adherence25. However, the small minority of low adherence should not be discounted. Studies consistently reveal an unfavourable association between hand hygiene compliance and hospital-acquired infection (HAI) rates26. Even minor improvements in hand hygiene compliance can considerably lower HAI rates, particularly when compliance surpasses 60%. Factors contributing to inadequate adherence include high workload, understaffing, lack of access to alcohol-based hand rubs, skin irritation due to frequent washing, behavioural concerns, and lack of institutional reinforcement27. Technological breakthroughs offer intriguing options for progress, such as thermal imaging devices, feedback-based learning models, and embedded IoT devices monitoring hand hygiene habits in real-time. Multimodal techniques remain the gold standard for sustainable progress, emphasizing system transformation, training and education, evaluation and feedback, reminders, and institutional safety culture. The Geneva model emphasizes the importance of frequent audits, feedback loops, and leadership engagement. Behavioural reinforcement through peer modelling and non-punitive feedback methods is highly effective28.

Comparable findings have been reported across Africa and Asia, particularly in hospital-based sanitation and infection prevention studies. Tetteh et al.29, in a cross-sectional study at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, Ghana, observed that 72.4% of nurses displayed moderate adherence to hygienic excreta disposal protocols, while only 18.3% demonstrated consistent adherence (p = 0.037; 95% CI: 67.9–76.5). The authors attributed this to a lack of continuous training and inconsistent supervisory monitoring. These results closely mirror the moderate adherence (77.8%) seen at Sunyani Teaching Hospital highlighting a persistent gap between policy and practice within Ghana’s healthcare facilities.

Likewise, Kumar et al.30 investigated adherence to infection control and biomedical waste segregation, specifically stool and urine disposal, among 215 healthcare workers in India. They found that 68.9% adhered moderately, 21.4% highly, and 9.7% poorly (p < 0.05; 95% CI: 63.2–73.9). The study demonstrated a strong positive correlation between years of experience and adherence level (r = 0.49), suggesting that habitual reinforcement and professional maturity are major determinants of consistent stool disposal behaviour.

A study by Amponsah-Tawiah et al.31 assessing hygiene and waste disposal practices in three Ghanaian district hospitals reported that only 24% of respondents complied with all hygienic excreta disposal standards, while 70% practiced partial adherence (p = 0.021). In another study by Barimah et al.32 on solid waste disposal and environmental impacts they found inadequate collection services, poor supervisory oversight, and limited recycling infrastructure perpetuate unsafe waste disposal practices in the Ahafo Region. Importantly, this research identified insufficient logistics (such as disposal bins and disinfectants) as a statistically significant predictor of non-adherence (β = −0.37, p < 0.05). These logistical barriers are consistent with conditions observed in many tertiary hospitals across sub-Saharan Africa, including Sunyani Teaching Hospital.

Factors influencing proper stool disposal practices among health workers

Knowledge level was seen to be a significant predictor for adherence to patient waste disposal. Healthcare professionals with strong knowledge level are more likely to adhere to recommended practices since education enhances risk awareness and behavioural compliance16. Years of service was also found to be a significant predictor of adherence to patient stool disposal (p = 0.021). This is consistent with an earlier study which observed that more experienced staff were more compliant. Hence professional maturity influences protocol adherence among healthcare professionals33.

Other significant factors identified were Training (p = 0.033), resource availability (p = 0.011), workload (p = 0.047), Personal Risk Perception (p = 0.041), Motivation and Attitude (p = 0.03). Training reinforces risk perception and ethical responsibility which has a strong positive effect on healthcare workers behaviour34. Availability of gloves, bins, disinfectants and dedicated disposal tools is an infrastructural prerequisite for adherence by healthcare workers. Even knowledgeable staff will fail to comply in resource-constrained environments35.

The study further delineates personal risk perception as a significant predictor of adherence to disposal of patient stool. This finding is in line with conclusions drawn by a previous study which indicated that healthcare professionals who exhibit an elevated consciousness of infectious hazards engaged in more consistent usage of personal protective equipment16. In a similar study, it was also observed that an individual’s conviction regarding their susceptibility to infection enhances adherence to infection prevention and control protocols, through both emotional and cognitive investment in the potential health effects35. It is worth mentioning that susceptibility to infection can visit adverse health conditions on individuals subjecting them to poverty emanating from catastrophic health expenditure, hence preventing these hazards is paramount36. More so, it is vital to highlight that infections that are not diagnosed early and treated can reach aggravated levels thereby culminating in further catastrophic health expenditure37.

Conclusion

This study found that 53.7% of health workers exhibited low knowledge of health hazards associated with improper stool disposal, while 77.8% demonstrated moderate adherence and 20.6% high adherence to hygienic stool disposal protocols. Knowledge level, years of service, training, resource, availability, workload, risk perception, and motivation significantly predicted adherence (p < 0.05). These findings highlight the need for structural continuous training, improved supply of disposal resources, and institutional reinforcements to sustain safe stool disposal practices in hospital settings.
