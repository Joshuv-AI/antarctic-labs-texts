Leptin’s potential mechanisms in ICU-acquired weakness
Authors: Min Li, Luying Yang, Ting Li, Yanmei Miao, Jun Yang, Shaolin Chen, Xinglong Ma, Peng Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) represents a prevalent and debilitating complication among critically ill patients, distinguished by profound skeletal muscle atrophy and functional deterioration. Leptin, a hormone predominantly released by adipose tissue, has gained recognition for its regulatory roles in appetite control, energy homeostasis, and muscle metabolism. Recent evidence indicates that leptin May contribute to ICU-AW pathogenesis through dual protective and detrimental mechanisms. During physiological conditions, leptin supports muscle preservation by enhancing anabolic signaling while suppressing catabolic processes. The hormone modulates essential pathways, including the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, Forkhead Box O (FOXO) transcription factors, and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which collectively promote protein synthesis and energy utilization in skeletal muscle. Nevertheless, throughout critical illness, leptin signaling becomes frequently compromised. Increased leptin concentrations may inappropriately stimulate the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signalling pathways, initiating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These cytokines promote muscle wasting through insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) inhibition, enhanced insulin resistance, and compromised protein synthesis. Furthermore, they stimulate SPRY domain-containing SOCS box protein 1 (SPSB1) expression, which disrupts myocyte fusion and myogenic differentiation. Compounding these consequences, mitochondrial dysfunction, prevalent in critically ill patients, impairs AMPK activity, further hindering muscle regeneration and energy metabolism. In the later stages of critical illness, leptin levels typically decrease, leading to reduced IGF-1 production, disrupted FOXO regulation, and persistent suppression of AMPK signaling. These alterations worsen muscle atrophy and impede recovery. In summary, leptin demonstrates a dual function in ICU-AW, exhibiting both protective and pathological influences based on the illness phase and context. A deeper understanding of its intricate regulatory mechanisms could provide valuable insights into ICU-AW pathogenesis and potential therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Leptin, Cytokines, Insulin-Like growth factor i (IGF-I), Forkhead transcription factors (FOXO), AMP-Activated protein kinases (AMPK), Muscle weakness

Introduction

Intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) represents a prevalent neuromuscular complication in critically ill patients, distinguished by progressive skeletal muscle atrophy, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and delayed long-term recovery [1, 2]. Despite extensive investigation into its multifactorial pathogenesis—including systemic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disrupted protein homeostasis—effective therapeutic interventions remain scarce [3]. Leptin, a hormone primarily involved in metabolic regulation, also plays a role in muscle inflammation and homeostasis [4–6], but its involvement in ICU-AW has yet to be fully elucidated.

Preliminary evidence suggests that leptin may influence muscle outcomes in critical illness, but a comprehensive understanding of its phase-dependent roles and underlying mechanisms is still lacking, particularly in relation to key signaling pathways governing muscle protein turnover, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses [7–10]. This review aims to fill this knowledge gap by systematically analyzing the molecular and cellular mechanisms through which leptin may contribute to either muscle homeostasis or dysfunction in critically ill patients, laying the groundwork for future translational research.

ICU-AW: risk factors, diagnosis, pathophysiological mechanisms, and treatment challenges

ICU-AW represents the predominant neuromuscular dysfunction affecting critically ill patients, defined as “clinically detected weakness in critically ill patients with no reasonable cause other than critical illness” [1]. Clinically, it typically presents as muscle weakness, impaired mobility, and/or muscle atrophy [2]. The incidence of ICU-AW among patients in the ICU ranges from 25% to 31%, with higher rates of 56% to 74% observed in surgical ICUs [11]. Recognized risk factors include advanced age, female sex, corticosteroid use, prolonged mechanical ventilation, denervation, multiorgan failure (MOF), and systemic inflammatory responses (such as sepsis) [12, 13]. Although neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) have been linked to ICU-AW, the evidence to conclusively establish this relationship is currently insufficient [14]. The principal clinical risk factors include sepsis, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and multiple organ failure [2]. Over 70% of septic patients develop ICU-AW [15], and nearly 100% of patients with severe sepsis and SIRS are affected [16]. In the first week of ICU hospitalization, critically ill patients experience rapid muscle atrophy [17], with nearly 2% of their skeletal muscle mass lost per day [18], affecting both limb and respiratory muscles symmetrically [1, 2, 19, 20]. Muscle weakness and atrophy contribute to prolonged hospital stays and increased mortality, significantly impairing recovery and long-term quality of life. These conditions impose a considerable burden on patients and healthcare systems alike [19].

The diagnosis of ICU-AW relies on the assessment of limb weakness utilizing the Medical Research Council sum score (MRCss) and handgrip dynamometry (HGD), as well as the identification of difficulty in weaning from mechanical ventilation due to diaphragm weakness (DW) [21, 22]. ICU-AW encompasses a range of neuromuscular complications in critically ill patients, most notably critical illness polyneuropathy (CIP), critical illness myopathy (CIM), and their overlapping form, critical illness polyneuromyopathy (CIPNM) [23]. These subtypes can be differentiated through electrophysiological studies (EPS) and/or muscle biopsy [20]. CIM is primarily characterized by muscle fiber damage and atrophy, whereas CIP involves neuropathic injury that impairs neuromuscular transmission. CIPNM, in contrast, presents with features of both CIP and CIM, involving both nerve and muscle dysfunction [24, 25]. All three subtypes affect both limb and respiratory muscles, leading to difficulties in ventilator weaning, extended ICU stays, increased mortality, and long-term functional impairment [26].

Although EPS and pathological biopsies can assist in identifying potential mechanisms underlying ICU-AW, the pathological process remains highly complex and not fully elucidated. Known pathological mechanisms include disruptions in protein balance, inflammatory responses, bioenergetic dysfunction, axonal degeneration, muscle atrophy, impaired autophagy activation, microvascular dysfunction, acquired sodium channelopathies, failure of satellite cell (SC) recruitment, mitochondrial damage, and oxidative stress [23, 27, 28].

Due to the heterogeneous nature of ICU-AW, current preventive measures remain limited, and a definitive treatment strategy is yet to be established. Although early mobilization has shown positive effects on the short-term prognosis of ICU patients [29, 30], its impact on long-term outcomes remains inconclusive [31]. Intensive insulin therapy, which aims for stricter glucose control, has been associated with a reduced incidence of ICU-AW; however, it also increases the risk of life-threatening hypoglycemia, limiting its clinical applicability [32]. The effectiveness of neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) in treating ICU-AW remains uncertain [33]. Recent studies on ICU-AW treatment have not produced satisfactory results, underscoring the need for the development of targeted therapeutic approaches or the identification of specific therapeutic targets in critically ill ICU patients.

Leptin and leptin receptors

The human leptin gene is located on chromosome 7 and encodes a 16 kDa protein [34], characterized by a four-helix bundle structure typical of long-chain helical cytokines [35, 36]. Leptin is chiefly synthesized and released by adipocytes into the bloodstream and is commonly referred to as an adipokine or adipocytokine [37, 38]. Plasma leptin concentrations demonstrate positive correlation with adipose tissue volume and transmit information regarding energy reserves to the brain [6, 39]. Through action on the central nervous system, leptin diminishes food consumption, indicates the body’s energy stores, and serves a crucial function in controlling body weight and energy homeostasis [40]. Previous studies linked leptin to conditions like morbid obesity, insulin resistance (IR), infertility, and somnolence [41–43]. Further research revealed leptin’s dual function as both a hormone and a cytokine, acting as a pleiotropic cytokine that regulates numerous biological processes in peripheral tissues and the central nervous system [44].

The leptin receptor (Ob-R) gene is positioned on chromosome 4, with its encoded Ob-R protein functioning as a single-membrane transmembrane receptor [45]. Leptin receptors are classified within the class I cytokine receptor family, incorporating gp130, a shared signaling component found in the IL-6-related cytokine family [46, 47]. The extracellular domain of Ob-R comprises two cytokine receptor domains along with four fibronectin domains [48]. Leptin receptors establish homodimers and become activated through conformational modifications triggered by ligand binding [49]. The Ob-R gene undergoes alternative splicing from a single db gene, resulting in at least six identified isoforms: OB-Ra, OB-Rb, OB-Rc, OB-Rd, OB-Re, and Ob-Rf. These isoforms possess a shared extracellular (N-terminal) Ligand-binding domain, containing over 800 amino acids, while displaying variations in their intracellular (C-terminal) domains [50, 51]. Among these, OB-Rb represents the long isoform receptor, OB-Ra, OB-Rc, OB-Rd, and Ob-Rf constitute short isoform receptors, and OB-Re functions as a soluble isoform receptor. The physiological effects of leptin largely depend on the specific Ob-R isoform it binds to [51–53]. As detailed in Table 1, leptin receptors are widely distributed in the brain, skeletal muscle, and other tissues. By binding to these receptors, leptin exerts various biological effects, including the regulation of appetite, skeletal muscle metabolism, and immune responses.

The biphasic role of leptin in ICU-AW

Leptin, as a cytokine, exerts multifaceted regulatory effects on skeletal muscle, influencing metabolism, inflammation, and cellular stress responses [61–65]. Under physiological conditions, leptin maintains skeletal muscle homeostasis by regulating appetite, enhancing insulin sensitivity, promoting IGF-1 expression, activating AMPK, and modulating lipid and glucose metabolism [4, 66]. It also supports muscle integrity by regulating autophagy and promoting myogenic cell proliferation through leptin receptors expressed in muscle tissue [67–70]. In this context, proper leptin signaling is considered protective, preserving muscle mass and function. Numerous studies and animal models have demonstrated that a reduction in leptin levels leads to significant muscle loss [4, 71, 72].

Conversely, under pathophysiological conditions—such as critical illness—leptin may exert harmful effects. Several studies have shown that critical illnesses (including sepsis) and major surgeries lead to elevated leptin levels upon ICU admission or a gradual increase in leptin levels during ICU hospitalization (Table 2). This effect is closely associated with factors such as inflammatory responses, stress responses, and the release of inflammatory cytokines [73–79]. Leptin is also recognized as a pro-inflammatory cytokine [5], demonstrating inflammatory characteristics comparable to other acute-phase reactants through the enhancement of secretion for multiple inflammatory cytokines, encompassing TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1. Additionally, contact with inflammatory stimuli including TNF-α and IL-1 elevates leptin expression within adipose tissue and increases circulating leptin concentrations. This interaction forms a vicious cycle that perpetuates inflammation [80]. Therefore, in ICU patients, elevated leptin signaling may further intensify inflammation and promote the discharge of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1), which can exacerbate muscle protein breakdown and atrophy [81].

Leptin exhibits a disease stage-dependent, biphasic function in critical illness. Throughout the acute phase, inflammation-induced elevations in leptin may facilitate muscle atrophy through upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 [83–85]. Circulating leptin levels typically decline within 24–48 h after ICU admission (Table 1), potentially mitigating its catabolic effects. However, in conditions such as prolonged fasting, hypermetabolism, or metabolic failure, leptin levels may fall below the physiological threshold [86–89], leading to disruptions in skeletal muscle homeostasis and accelerating muscle loss. This biphasic pattern indicates that excessive leptin exacerbates muscle wasting, while insufficient leptin fails to sustain muscle mass and function. Under conditions like IR, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, these imbalances contribute to reduced protein synthesis and enhanced proteolysis [90–92], during which leptin’s regulatory capacity may be further compromised.

Although leptin plays a multifaceted role in regulating skeletal muscle homeostasis, its regulatory dynamics become increasingly complex in the context of critical illness, and the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. To explore potential mechanisms, this study investigates known factors and associated proteins involved in skeletal muscle atrophy, encompassing TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IGF-1, human F-box protein-1 (atrogin-1), muscle-specific ring finger protein 1 (MuRF1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), TNF-related weak inducer of apoptosis-fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (TWEAK–Fn14), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [93]. Leptin regulates many of these factors and proteins, suggesting that ICU-AW may be associated with leptin’s action. This review synthesizes existing evidence regarding the association between leptin and ICU-AW, emphasizing the molecular mechanisms underlying muscle protein synthesis and breakdown. Through consolidating results from available studies, it seeks to elucidate leptin’s potential involvement in ICU-AW pathogenesis.

Leptin elevation: a potential contributor to ICU-AW

Leptin levels are elevated in critically ill patients hospitalized in the ICU [82]. The leptin receptor (Ob-Rb) exhibits widespread expression throughout the immune system, with the long isoform present on monocytes, lymphocytes, and macrophages. When leptin binds to these receptors, immune cells become primed for proinflammatory cytokine production. Ob-Rb receptor activation on monocytes and macrophages initiates critical signaling pathways, encompassing JAK/STAT, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt [94–98]. These pathways then activate transcription factors like NF-κB [99], resulting in enhanced proinflammatory cytokine release, encompassing TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 [100–105]. Leptin’s role as a proinflammatory regulator is well-established [62].

In vivo studies utilizing a mouse model of sepsis have demonstrated that elevated leptin levels, along with exogenous leptin administration, not only increase inflammatory cytokines but also enhance adhesion molecules, macrophage infiltration, and endothelial barrier dysfunction levels. The lack of the long isoform of the leptin receptor blocks these outcomes [106]. This investigation underscores the comprehensive regulatory function of leptin in inflammatory processes. Proinflammatory cytokines encompassing TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 serve as pivotal components in both localized and systemic inflammatory cascades [81, 107]. Their release is a major trigger for muscle and neurological dysfunction in critically ill patients. Dysregulation of inflammatory signaling accelerates muscle atrophy and neuronal damage, thereby promoting the onset of ICU-AW (Fig. 1). As key mediators of critical illness, proinflammatory cytokines actively participate in inflammatory response magnification and muscular damage advancement, further exacerbating ICU-AW development [108–114].

Leptin facilitates the secretion of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1, and TNF-α, triggering an inflammatory cascade. These cytokines stimulate multiple signaling pathways, including the JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways, encompassing the JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways, which further contribute to muscle atrophy and protein degradation, ultimately exacerbating muscle damage (by Figdraw)

IL-6 promotes muscle degradation

IL-6 is synthesized by macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells [115, 116]. The IL-6 family comprises 10 ligands (e/g., IL-6) and 9 receptors (IL-6R), with gp130 serving as a shared component across the IL-6 cytokine family. IL-6 signaling is transduced through the membrane glycoprotein gp130, a 918-amino-acid protein containing a transmembrane domain. When cytokines from the IL-6 family bind to IL-6R, they interact with gp130, leading to signal transduction via gp130 homodimers or heterodimers containing gp130. Some cytokines exhibit functional redundancy with IL-6, utilizing gp130 as a signaling molecule in their receptor systems [115, 117–120].

The injection of low concentrations of IL-6 into mouse muscle tissues enhances protein catabolism [121]. Higher doses of IL-6 significantly inhibit the expression of late-stage myogenic differentiation markers in C2C12 myoblasts [122]. Additionally, IL-6 can initiate an acute phase response (APR), where it is released alongside TNF-α from peripheral blood lymphocytes, driving tissue catabolism [123].

IL-6 signal transduction through gp130 occurs predominantly via phosphorylation of JAK family kinases that bind to gp130’s cytoplasmic domain. Following IL-6 receptor binding, JAK triggers STAT3 phosphorylation, resulting in STAT3 homodimer formation. These homodimers migrate to the nucleus where they modulate transcriptional processes [115]. Examination of muscle biopsy specimens from ICU-AW patients demonstrated IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation, causing elevated suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) expression. Functioning as a cytokine signaling negative regulator, SOCS3 suppresses the insulin/PI3K/Akt pathway through facilitation of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) degradation. The decline in IRS-1 corresponds with diminished Akt activity, which typically enhances protein synthesis while suppressing atrogen expression and protein breakdown, thereby supporting muscle development. Therefore, IRS-1 degradation compromises protein synthesis and accelerates protein breakdown, ultimately promoting muscle atrophy [124, 125]. Moreover, soluble IL-6R (sIL-6R), a product of selective IL-6R splicing, serves as a circulating receptor. The binding of IL-6 to sIL-6R and gp130 activates “trans-signaling” in various tissues, amplifying the biological effects of IL-6 [122].

IL-1 promotes muscle degradation

IL-1 is produced by stimulated macrophages or T cells and exists in two isoforms, IL-1α and IL-1β, which are encoded by distinct genes located on chromosome 2. Both isoforms share a high degree of sequence homology and are synthesized as precursor proteins. These precursors are cleaved by the cytosolic cysteine protease caspase-1 into their active forms before being released into the extracellular environment [126].

IL-1α serves as an early signal of injury, present on platelets and released by neutrophils at sites of infection or trauma [127]. Cooney et al. [128] demonstrated that a 6-day infusion of IL-1α reduced muscle mass and protein content in the rat gastrocnemius muscle, which predominantly consists of fast-twitch fibers, while sparing the soleus muscle, which is mainly comprised of slow-twitch fibers. The underlying mechanisms involve increased lysosomal proteolysis and net protein degradation [129]. Administration of a specific interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra) can block the inhibition of peptide chain initiation and the downregulation of eIF-2B expression during sepsis, thus reducing sepsis-induced suppression of protein synthesis [130].

IL-1β, activated early in sepsis, is one of the most highly upregulated cytokines in this condition [131–135]. IL-1β signaling occurs through the IL-1 receptor, which associates with IL-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1), resulting in activation of the transcription factor NF-κB [136]. In muscle, IL-1β induces the expression of MuRF1 and atrogin-1 [137, 138] and decreases slow myosin heavy chain (MyHC) expression in C2C12 myotubes. Inhibition of IL-1β reduces sepsis-induced muscle atrophy in mice [139]. Elevated IL-1β in the central nervous system initiate α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone secretion from proopiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons, which suppresses appetite by acting on melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4-R) neurons [140].

Both IL-1α and IL-1β also function through oxidative and AKT/Foxo-independent mechanisms, activating p38 MAPK, stimulating NF-κB signaling, increasing atrogin-1 and MuRF1 expression, and reducing myofibrillar proteins in differentiated myotubes [141]. IL-1 triggers the discharge of corticotropin-releasing hormone, subsequently enhancing adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol production, resulting in elevated muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases [142]. In this way, IL-1 promotes skeletal muscle catabolism by activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [143]. Administration of IL-1ra can alleviate sepsis-induced muscle mass and protein loss and prevent the inhibition of protein synthesis in the gastrocnemius muscle [144–146].

TNF-α promotes muscle degradation

TNF-α functions as a principal mediator in inflammatory responses, being synthesized mainly by activated macrophages, T lymphocytes, and natural killer cells [147]. Skeletal muscle expresses both TNF-α receptor subtype 1 (TNFR1) and subtype 2 (TNFR2), with TNF-α promoting protein loss and catabolism primarily through TNFR1 rather than TNFR2 [148, 149]. Li et al. [150, 151] found that TNF-α stimulation resulted in reduced total protein content, diminished mature myosin heavy chain levels, and a decrease in myotube diameter. In vivo studies also demonstrated that systemic administration of TNF-α triggered muscle mass reduction and diminished muscle fiber cross-sectional measurements [152, 153].

In critically ill patients and animal models of critical illness, circulating TNF-α concentrations are increased, stimulating the transcription factor NF-κB, which serves a central function in muscle protein degradation [108]. The conventional pathway for NF-κB stimulation is facilitated by the inhibitor of κB (I-κB) kinase complex and demonstrates sensitivity to redox alterations during TNF-α exposure [154]. Muscle-derived ROS positively regulate NF-κB activation, acting as second messengers for TNF-α [151], while the glutathione cycle negatively regulates this pathway [155]. TNF-α also rapidly stimulates MAPKs, encompassing p38 MaPK, extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2, and c-Jun N-terminal kinases in differentiated myotubes, resulting in enhanced expression of the atrogin-1/muscle atrophy F-box protein (MAFbx) gene in skeletal muscle [156]. TNF-α disrupts protein synthesis in skeletal muscle by modifying translation initiation, contributing to muscle wasting [157]. Additionally, TNF-α can reduce muscle contractile strength even in the absence of muscle atrophy [158]. In vitro, muscle fiber bundles isolated from mouse diaphragms and exposed to TNF-α showed a significant reduction in isometric force relative to their cross-sectional area [159, 160]. In vivo studies of mice overexpressing TNF-α indicated a trend toward decreased isometric force in the cross-sectional area of fiber bundles [161]. This effect was abolished when TNFR1, but not TNFR2, was knocked down [148].

Pro-inflammatory cytokines upregulate spsb1 expression and inhibit igf-1 synthesis

Spsb1 belongs to the SOCS box family of E3 ubiquitin ligases, distinguished by an SPRY domain and a C-terminal SOCS box, where the SPRY domain specifies the substrate targeted for ubiquitination [162]. SPSB1 and transforming growth factor-β type II receptor (TβRII) are colocalized on the cell membrane, where the SPRY domain interacts with TβRII, negatively regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway. The SOCS box of SPSB1 enhances the ubiquitination and degradation of TβRII, keeping its levels low [163]. Conversely, low levels of SPSB1 allow TβRII to stabilize [164].

Li et al. [165] documented elevated SPSB1 expression within skeletal muscles of ICU-AW patients and septic mice, which contributes to muscle atrophy. The underlying mechanism encompasses Spsb1 expression upregulation, facilitated by NF-κB via TNF and IL-1β, whereas IL-6 amplifies Spsb1 expression through the gp130/JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Consequently, TNF, IL-1β, and IL-6 synergistically stimulate Spsb1 expression. SPSB1 binds to TβRII, resulting in its ubiquitination and destabilization, thus suppressing the TβRII-Akt-myogenin signaling pathway and compromising protein synthesis within muscle cells. SPSB1 additionally diminishes expression of both early (myogenin (Myog), myomaker (Mymk), and myomixer (Mymx)) and late (myosin heavy chain 1 (Myh1), 3 (Myh3), and 7 (Myh7)) differentiation markers, compromising myoblast fusion and myogenic differentiation. Furthermore, increased IL-1 levels, combined with IL-6 and TNF-α, suppress IGF-1 synthesis, facilitating muscle degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). This process is further exacerbated by the accumulation of myostatin, mitochondrial ROS, and other factors, collectively driving skeletal muscle atrophy (Fig. 2) [93].

Leptin resistance and its implications in ICU-AW

Leptin resistance, characterized by reduced sensitivity to leptin’s biological effects despite elevated circulating leptin levels, has been widely studied in relation to obesity and metabolic syndrome [166]. Primary mechanisms underlying this pathological state include compromised leptin transport across the blood-brain barrier and disruptions in leptin receptor signaling pathways. SOCS3, protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B), and T cell protein tyrosine phosphatase (TCPTP) serve as critical molecular mediators in leptin resistance development [167–171].

While the role of leptin resistance in ICU-AW has not been thoroughly explored, several features common to critical illness—such as systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and elevated leptin levels—may synergistically facilitate the emergence of a leptin-resistant state. For instance, inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, which are frequently elevated during critical illness, are known to induce SOCS3 expression [124]. SOCS3 activation inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby disrupting the primary leptin signaling pathway—JAK2–STAT3—and promoting leptin resistance [172]. Moreover, prolonged immobilization and altered neuroendocrine feedback in the ICU may further impair leptin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, encompassing skeletal muscle [173]. Guerra et al. [174] demonstrated that even in healthy individuals, one week of bed rest can elevate circulating leptin levels and induce leptin resistance in skeletal muscle. These observations suggest that leptin resistance could be an underrecognized mechanism contributing to muscle wasting and metabolic dysfunction in patients with ICU-AW, but direct evidence remains limited.

In addition to inhibiting leptin signaling, SOCS3 has been shown to suppress multiple other signaling pathways, including those mediated by leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), interleukin-11 (IL-11), growth hormone (GH), and insulin [175]. Krebs et al. demonstrated that pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α induce SOCS3 expression, which disrupts insulin and IGF-1 signaling by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of IRS-1 and IRS-2. This mechanism is key to how inflammation contributes to IR [176]. Al-Shanti and colleagues demonstrated that SOCS3 likewise functions as a mediator of IL-6’s inhibitory impact on IGF-1 activity within skeletal myoblasts [177]. This could help explain the paradoxical observation in critically ill patients, where elevated leptin levels during hospitalization fail to enhance IGF-1 activity or preserve insulin sensitivity—effects typically expected under normal physiological conditions. In the context of systemic inflammation, upregulation of SOCS3 may impair downstream signaling despite high leptin concentrations, thus blunting its anabolic and insulin-sensitizing actions.

Leptin reduction promotes ICU-AW

As previously discussed, elevated leptin levels in critically ill patients have been linked to proinflammatory activity mediated by immune cells, contributing to muscle wasting and weakness. However, serum leptin levels gradually decrease during hospitalization, which may be attributed to increased metabolic demands, hormonal changes, prolonged fasting, or energy deficiency [86–89]. Sepsis, a common risk factor for ICU-AW, negatively impacts the neuromuscular system through various mechanisms [178]. A prospective clinical study revealed that although leptin levels were elevated upon admission in patients with sepsis, they significantly declined within one week [79]. A separate clinical study reported an approximate 80% reduction in leptin levels after 72 h of fasting in eight healthy men [60].

Collins et al. [4] conducted experiments on fat-free (FF) mice, which lack leptin, and observed significantly reduced fast-twitch muscle mass, peak contractile tension, slower contraction kinetics, and a smaller cross-sectional area of fast-twitch muscle fibers. These observations indicate that leptin, produced by adipose tissue, contributes to muscle mass regulation and contractile function, thereby preserving muscle homeostasis. Even low levels of circulating leptin can normalize muscle physiology. Leptin administration significantly increased the mass and fiber size of the gastrocnemius, extensor digitorum longus, and soleus muscles in ob/ob (leptin homozygous mutant) mice [69]. In vitro experiments demonstrated that leptin treatment of C2C12-derived myotubes enhanced protein synthesis while decreasing protein degradation [179]. Leptin can directly activate PI3K/Akt signaling in both whole skeletal muscle and isolated C2C12 cells [180, 181]. Furthermore, leptin suppresses myostatin expression while elevating cyclin D1 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen expression, consequently facilitating muscle cell proliferation and inhibiting myofibrillar protein breakdown [69]. Leptin reduction decreases IGF-1 secretion

IGF-1, primarily produced in the liver, is synthesized and released into the bloodstream under the regulation of growth hormone [182]. During fasting or caloric restriction, circulating levels of leptin, IGF-1, and muscle mass are reduced [183, 184]. IGF-1, a potent anabolic factor, supports overall and muscle growth by facilitating various anabolic processes within skeletal muscle. A reduction in IGF-1 concentration leads to skeletal muscle atrophy [185]. IGF-1 enhances skeletal muscle protein biosynthesis through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and PI3K/Akt/GSK3β signaling cascades. Furthermore, PI3K/Akt blocks FoxO-regulated transcription of E3 ubiquitin ligases MAFbx/Atrogin-1, muscle ubiquitin ligase of the SCF complex in atrophy-1 (Musa1), and specific of muscle atrophy and regulated by transcription (Smart), thereby reducing UPS function. The IGF-1/Akt signaling cascade additionally prevents muscle wasting by blocking cytokine and myostatin signaling via the NF-κB and Smad pathways, respectively [186].

Leptin regulates IGF-1 primarily by stimulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-growth hormone axis, which triggers the release of growth hormones and subsequently increases IGF-1 secretion [187]. Hamrick et al. [188] reported that, compared to saline-injected mice, those treated with a leptin receptor antagonist showed a significant reduction in liver-derived IGF-1 (20%) and an even more pronounced decrease in muscle-derived IGF-1 (> 50%). Effective anabolic processes are required for the repair and regeneration of muscle cells after damage to restore function. A reduction in leptin levels impairs IGF-1-mediated muscle synthesis, exacerbates muscle damage, and delays recovery.

Leptin reduction increases FOXO expression

The FOXO proteins, comprising four isoforms—FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, and FOXO6 [189]—are involved in regulating cellular energy production, antioxidant responses, cell survival, and proliferation, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis. Their function is strictly regulated through various post-translational modifications, encompassing phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and methylation, which influence their subcellular distribution, structural integrity, and transcriptional activity [190, 191]. A major regulatory input to FOXO activity comes from the PI3K/Akt pathway and growth factor-dependent stress signaling [192]. FOXO transcription factors, particularly FOXO3, are pivotal in regulating cellular protein degradation, and when activated, FOXO3 contributes significantly to muscle mass loss [193].

Leptin is known to activate PI3K/Akt signaling, as evidenced in both central nervous system and peripheral tissues, including skeletal muscle where leptin receptors are expressed [194, 195]. Upon activation, Akt phosphorylates FOXO transcription factors, resulting in their cytoplasmic sequestration and subsequent suppression of atrophy-related gene expression, encompassing MuRF1 and Atrogin-1, as established by Brunet et al. in 1999 [196]. Consistent with this mechanism, Zhang et al. [197] performed Tandem Mass Tag (TMT)-based proteomic analysis of liver and serum samples from leptin receptor-deficient db/db mice. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the serum were significantly enriched in the FOXO signaling pathway.

In ob/ob (leptin-deficient) mice, leptin treatment leads to diminished FoxO3a expression, enhanced PGC-1α expression, and suppression of atrophy markers such as Atrogin-1 and MuRF1, thus preventing myofibrillar protein degradation. Leptin treatment additionally elevates the expression of both slow and fast isoforms of the contractile protein TnT [63]. Furthermore, inhibition of FOXO transcriptional activity reduces myostatin/Smad pathway activity, increases MyoD transcription, promotes SC proliferation, and facilitates myofiber fusion, leading to hypertrophy of skeletal muscle fibers through de novo protein synthesis [198]. These findings suggest that leptin may counteract muscle atrophy by modulating both expression levels and functional activity of FOXO transcription factors. A reduction in leptin levels may impair this regulatory effect.

Although elevated leptin levels are a well-documented feature of the early phase of critical illness, paradoxically, increased expression of FOXO1/3 and their downstream targets has been observed in both animal models and critically ill patients. For instance, in septic rats, mRNA levels of FoxO1 and FoxO3 in skeletal muscle remain elevated during the early stage, accompanied by sustained increases in MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 expression [198]. Similarly, Wollersheim et al. [114] reported that in critically ill patients, the expression of MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 increased by day 5 of ICU stay, whereas MyHC mRNA and protein levels rapidly declined, with this trend persisting through day 15. Notably, the upregulation of MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 promotes muscle protein degradation via the UPS [138]. Early elevations in MuRF1 protein levels have been closely associated with the severity of late-stage muscle wasting and weakness, accompanied by decreased synthesis and increased degradation of MyHC [114].

The concurrent elevation of leptin and FOXO observed during the early phase of critical illness may result from multiple contributing factors, including inflammatory cytokines, leptin resistance, and mitochondrial dysfunction. In this context, elevated leptin levels may fail to effectively activate downstream signaling pathways. However, the precise role of leptin in regulating FOXO activity under critical illness conditions remains to be elucidated.

Leptin regulation of AMPK activation inhibits ICU-AW

AMPK is a highly conserved energy sensor critical for maintaining cellular energy homeostasis. This enzyme represents a heterotrimeric complex composed of one catalytic α subunit along with regulatory β and γ subunits, where each subunit possesses multiple isoforms (α1/α2, β1/β2, γ1/γ2/γ3), and these isoforms assemble to generate a minimum of 12 distinct AMPK complexes in mammalian systems [199]. AMPK is primarily localized in the cytosol, where it regulates glucose uptake, lipid metabolism, and protein synthesis [200]. However, certain AMPK complexes—particularly those involving α1/α2, β2, and γ1 isoforms—can transiently localize to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), forming “mitoAMPK.” This localization is dynamic and context-dependent, with mitoAMPK playing a role in regulating mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, and energy metabolism during stress [201]. AMPKα1 also promotes anabolic processes in SCs (muscle stem cells) and regulates muscle cell regeneration [202]. Mice with AMPKα1/α2 knockout exhibit altered body weight, reduced muscle fiber size, and impaired muscle function [203].

Leptin stimulates AMPK phosphorylation in skeletal muscle, which then phosphorylates and inactivates acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC). This action relieves ACC-mediated inhibition of CPT1, promoting fatty acid oxidation. By activating AMPK, leptin increases fatty acid oxidation, preventing fatty acid accumulation in muscle tissue and reducing the risk of “lipotoxicity” [204]. Additionally, leptin directly promotes glucose uptake in skeletal muscle via AMPK activation, thereby enhancing energy utilization efficiency [205].

ICU-AW is marked by generalized weakness and skeletal muscle atrophy, with its pathogenesis closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction [90]. Key contributors to this dysfunction include imbalances in mitochondrial dynamics, disturbances in respiratory chain complexes, altered gene expression, and disrupted signaling pathways [206]. In critical illness, skeletal muscle mitochondria exhibit reduced respiration and severe morphological abnormalities, indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction [207]. This dysfunction impairs leptin-mediated glucose and fatty acid oxidation, leading to an insufficient energy supply to the muscles. Consequently, the accumulation of lactate and other intermediate metabolites occurs, further compromising muscle metabolic stability and function. Additionally, skeletal muscle regeneration depends on SCs, and the mitochondrial and metabolic alterations associated with sepsis induce long-term damage to these cells, ultimately impairing muscle regeneration [208]. In the later stages of ICU hospitalization, if mitochondrial dysfunction is coupled with reduced leptin levels, this combined effect may worsen the negative impact on skeletal muscle.

Leptin has a dual effect on insulin resistance and insulin sensitivity

Under physiological conditions, leptin receptors demonstrate abundant expression throughout peripheral musculoskeletal tissues, where the interaction between leptin and its receptors enhances insulin sensitivity, promotes glucose utilization, stimulates lipolysis, and facilitates skeletal muscle growth [194, 209, 210]. Clinical studies in patients with severe lipodystrophy have shown that long-term leptin replacement therapy reverses hepatic and muscular IR and alleviates fasting hyperglycemia. This effect is attributed, at least in part, to a significant reduction in ectopic lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle and the liver [211].

In contrast, central leptin signaling controls peripheral glucose metabolism via hypothalamic circuits. Direct leptin administration into the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) has been demonstrated to improve glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity in red skeletal muscle fibers [212, 213]. This centrally mediated response involves activation of AMPK, the sympathetic nervous system, and β-adrenergic receptors (β-ARs) within the VMH. The resulting signaling cascade enhances insulin signaling and glucose uptake in skeletal muscle through a β-AR-dependent mechanism. AMPK activation results in phosphorylation and inhibition of ACC, decreasing malonyl-CoA levels and promoting fatty acid oxidation. Furthermore, AMPK stimulates the translocation of fatty acid translocase/CD36 to the plasma membrane, additionally enhancing lipid oxidation and energy production [214].

Despite these insulin-sensitizing effects of leptin under normal physiological conditions, its role appears to shift under pathological states. Leptin levels are typically elevated in critically ill patients during hospitalization, yet this elevation is paradoxically not associated with improved insulin sensitivity. On the contrary, IR is commonly observed in these patients, even in the absence of preexisting diabetes or cardiovascular disease [92]. IR is characterized by impaired glucose metabolism in tissues such as skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver [215]. This pathological state is partially linked to increased concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines, notably tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [216, 217], which diminish insulin action in skeletal muscle, disrupting glucose metabolism and compromising muscle mass maintenance [218]. Additionally, IR promotes gluconeogenesis, suppresses β-oxidation, and impairs triglyceride transport, leading to triglyceride accumulation within skeletal muscle [219]. IR contributes to reductions in body weight and muscle strength, as it not only enhances lipolysis and the release of free fatty acids but also suppresses the GH/IGF-1 axis and increases myostatin levels in muscle, ultimately inhibiting skeletal muscle protein synthesis [220].

Thus, in the context of critical illness, elevated leptin may contribute to IR rather than ameliorating it. This paradox may be explained by leptin’s role in promoting systemic inflammation and disrupting insulin signaling pathways. These opposing effects highlight the context-dependent and compartment-specific actions of leptin, emphasizing that its impact on insulin sensitivity is regulated by the physiological state, target tissue, and hormonal environment.

Leptin regulation of miRNA expression

Microarray technology has been employed to analyze quadriceps biopsy samples from critically ill patients, obtained 7 days and 6 months after ICU discharge, and compared with those from healthy individuals. Combining functional transcriptomics, 36 miRNAs, including miR-424-3p/5p, miR-490-3p, and miR-744-5p, were identified as potential regulators of gene networks linked to muscle mass and strength recovery following critical illness [221]. In muscle atrophy-related diseases, elevated miR-424-5p expression may inhibit protein synthesis and reduce muscle mass [222]. Examination of miR-542-3p/5p concentrations in the quadriceps muscles of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and ICU-AW revealed that elevated miR-542-3p/5p may facilitate muscle atrophy through promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and stimulating SMAD2/3 phosphorylation [223]. In an ICU-AW rat model, enhanced expression of miR-181a triggered mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation through activation of the JAK/STAT pathway via IGFBP5 [224].

The role of miRNAs in muscle atrophy in critically ill patients is increasingly recognized. However, the precise mechanisms by which leptin interacts with the miRNA network remain unclear. Further exploration is needed to understand how leptin influences mitochondrial function, protein catabolism, and muscle atrophy through miRNA regulation in critical illness. Future research could focus on the “leptin–miRNA axis” to explore leptin’s regulatory effects on miRNAs under various conditions, potentially unveiling its mechanisms in muscle repair, regeneration, and atrophy. Discussion

This review summarizes current research that highlights how alterations in leptin levels exacerbate the pathological progression of ICU-AW by affecting skeletal muscle protein synthesis and degradation. Both elevated and reduced leptin levels exert detrimental effects on muscle, presenting a clear paradox. However, the pathophysiology of ICU-AW is multifactorial and continuous, with leptin’s influence spanning multiple pathways and disease phases.

Leptin levels typically rise during the early phase of ICU hospitalization [77]. As an adipokine, leptin engages with receptors on monocytes and macrophages, triggering signaling cascades including JAK/STAT and NF-κB [94–98]. These cascades facilitate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, encompassing TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, which lead to muscle atrophy and weakness [108–114]. Inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, may also exacerbate insulin and leptin resistance through SOCS3 [124, 227], while promoting mitochondrial dysfunction [228]—an issue commonly observed in critically ill patients. According to Liu J et al. [229], skeletal muscle from both humans and animals with leptin resistance exhibited reduced muscle mass, decreased fatty acid oxidation, and impaired glucose uptake. Consequently, even when leptin levels are elevated, its regulatory effects on skeletal muscle through the GH/IGF-1 axis [230] and AMPK signaling may be impaired. Early FOXO upregulation, along with mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic disturbances during critical illness, may collectively impair satellite cell (SC) proliferation [198, 208]. Additionally, inflammatory mediators can directly suppress IGF-1 expression, further exacerbating muscle wasting [93]. Early and sustained elevations in FoxO3, MuRF1, and atrogin-1—key genes involved in skeletal muscle atrophy—have been observed in septic rat muscle [198], suggesting that elevated leptin may fail to adequately inhibit the FoxO pathway. Under such conditions, the effects of leptin on muscle are predominantly catabolic.

In the later stages of critical illness, prolonged energy deficiency and excessive energy expenditure result in reduced leptin levels. Although this decrease may suppress pro-inflammatory responses, it concurrently impairs IGF-1-mediated anabolic signaling in skeletal muscle and attenuates AMPK-driven energy sensing, thereby delaying muscle regeneration and worsening atrophy. If IR or mitochondrial dysfunction persists during this phase, muscle wasting is further exacerbated.

Although this review emphasizes leptin’s biphasic role in skeletal muscle regulation, direct evidence linking circulating leptin levels to muscle mass loss in ICU patients remains limited. However, several studies have used various methods to assess skeletal muscle wasting in critically ill patients. For instance, Puthucheary et al. employed ultrasound to measure rectus femoris thickness, reporting that ICU patients May lose approximately 2% of muscle mass per day during the first week of hospitalization [17]. Jiang et al. proposed that changes in the urea-to-creatinine ratio (ΔUCR) could serve as a biochemical predictor of rapid muscle wasting (> 2% per day), showing good sensitivity and specificity in septic patients [231]. Additionally, Kolck et al. used abdominal CT imaging to compare changes in psoas muscle area under different disease conditions, finding that patients with COVID-19 experienced significantly faster muscle loss than other ICU populations [232]. These approaches provide valuable tools and potential avenues for future research examining the relationship between leptin dynamics and muscle atrophy in critically ill patients.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesizes current knowledge on the dual regulatory roles of leptin in the onset and progression of ICU-AW. By integrating evidence from molecular, cellular, organismal, and clinical levels, it explores leptin’s influence on inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial function, insulin sensitivity, and skeletal muscle regeneration. Emphasis is placed on the biphasic nature of leptin’s action in critically ill patients—its potential catabolic effects during the acute phase and inadequate anabolic signaling in individuals with prolonged critical illness. Additionally, leptin’s involvement in key signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt, FOXO, JAK/STAT, AMPK) is discussed, providing a mechanistic framework for understanding its role in the molecular regulation of ICU-AW.

However, this study has several limitations [1]. Most existing studies rely on cross-sectional data or animal models, lacking longitudinal investigations capable of establishing a causal relationship between leptin levels and the progression of ICU-AW [2]. The ICU population is heterogeneous in terms of underlying diseases, treatment strategies, and metabolic conditions, which limits the generalizability and external validity of current findings [3]. The dose-dependent threshold underlying leptin’s biphasic effects remains undefined, and the related mechanisms are yet to be fully elucidated [4]. The dynamic interactions among IR, leptin resistance, cytokine profiles, and mitochondrial dysfunction are complex and not yet fully understood, underscoring the need for rigorously designed translational and clinical studies [5]. Serum leptin levels exhibit circadian variation [233], and differences in sampling time may significantly impact study outcomes; however, only a subset of the reviewed studies accounted for this factor.

Conclusion

This review highlights the biphasic and context-dependent role of leptin in the development of ICU-AW, highlighting its contribution to early inflammation-induced catabolism and later impaired anabolic signaling during prolonged critical illness. By synthesizing evidence across molecular, cellular, and systemic levels, the review clarifies how leptin influences muscle wasting through key regulatory pathways such as JAK/STAT, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and AMPK, offering a mechanistic framework for future investigation.

From a clinical perspective, leptin and its downstream signaling components may serve as early biomarkers for identifying ICU patients at increased risk of muscle wasting [234], particularly those in prolonged catabolic states or with persistent metabolic dysregulation. Incorporating leptin-related indices into patient monitoring could enhance risk stratification and support individualized rehabilitation strategies, such as early mobilization or nutritional optimization. Future research should focus on validating these biomarkers, exploring dose-response relationships, and determining the timing of leptin dysregulation in relation to functional outcomes. These efforts may ultimately guide precision supportive care approaches and help mitigate long-term physical impairments in ICU survivors.
