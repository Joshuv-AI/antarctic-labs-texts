Unveiling bioactive components in Sargassum fusiforme via phytochemical and biological evaluation: A heterogeneous information ensemble approach
Authors: Yue Zhu, Yuting Ai, Jianfu Zhou, Xiaozhuan Jia, Shuying Liu, Bo Zhu, Yimin Qin, Jianping Jiang, Zhenzhong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSargassum fusiforme (SF), a brown algae, is a marine traditional Chinese medicine with nutritional value. However, studies on the bioactive components in SF are limited. The aim of this study is to investigate phytochemical and biological properties of SF from different geographical origins and botanical parts, as well as to explore bioactive components within SF.MethodsSF from different origins and botanical parts were collected for integrated phytochemical analyses using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS), mass spectral molecular networking and liquid chromatography-single quadrupole mass spectrometry (LC-MSD), etc., followed by the systematic chemical profile evaluation. Subsequently, the glycolipid metabolism regulation and anticoagulation of SF were examined. Furthermore, the overall information acquired was assembled using multivariate analysis and machine learning to discover bioactive components.ResultsA total of 123 compounds in SF were identified with the assistance of mass spectral molecular networking. The variations in the content of amino acids, fucoidan, alginate, phlorotannin, alkaloids, terpenoids, etc. were observed in SF from different origins and botanical parts. SF possessed the hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant effects, and SF from Dongtou, Wenzhou, Zhejiang, was outstanding among origins in both phytochemical and biological evaluations. A heterogeneous information ensemble approach was adopted to discover the bioactive components in SF, namely 3-indoleacrylic acid, phlorotannin and fucoidan.ConclusionThis study evaluated the phytochemical and biological properties of SF, revealed the bioactive components using heterogeneous information and provided a reference for the rational consumption and further development of SF.Keywords: anticoagulant, chemical characteristic fusion analysis, chemometrics, fucoidan, machine learning, Sargassum fusiforme (Harv.) Setch

Introduction

Sargassum fusiforme (Harv.) Setch. (SF), a brown algae thriving in warm-temperate oceans, is recognized for both nutritional and medicinal properties. The algae body of SF is yellowish-brown in color and is distributed along the coastline of China, the Korean Peninsula, and Japan (Zhang, Zhang, Tang, & Mao, 2020). As one of the few recorded marine traditional Chinese medicines, SF has been used in East Asia for more than 2000 years for treatment of oedema and cardiovascular disease (Chen et al., 2016).

Meanwhile, SF has long been used as a nutritional algae with health benefits in several Asian countries. Food’s significant influence on both mental and physical well-being highlights its role as a potential component in medical treatment (Chen, 2023, Li et al., 2023). In addition to being rich in proteins, vitamins, minerals and trace elements, SF also provides special components compared to terrestrial plants, such as fucoidan and phlorotannin (He, Gao, et al., 2023). Bioactive components derived from SF were found to possess hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic, anticoagulant, antioxidative, and renal protective activities (Zhang et al., 2020, Zhao et al., 2018).

The quality of herbal medicines is strongly linked to their bioactivity, which is influenced by multiple factors including habitat, germplasm, culturing and processing techniques, transport, etc. Among these, the habitat significantly impacts the metabolism process, traits and natural products of algae, which is attributed to ecology and genetics (He et al., 2020). The variations in the content of natural products then lead to differences in bioactivity. The geographical origin thus is one of the key factors affecting the therapeutic effect of herbal medicines. However, there is currently no consensus on the optimal geographical origin of SF.

Prior studies have focused on the isolation, purification, structural characterization of bioactive polysaccharides from SF and the determination of heavy metals in SF (Zhang, Zhang, Tang, & Mao, 2020). Further research is required to advance the medicinal value of SF, which includes the clarity of the distribution of chemical components in different botanical parts, systematic evaluations of SF from different geographical origins and the characterization of bioactive components within SF (He, Gao, et al., 2023).

In this study, a heterogeneous information ensemble approach was implemented to conduct the phytochemical and biological evaluation of SF from different geographical origins and botanical parts, unveiling the bioactive components (Fig. 1). In integrated phytochemical analyses, qualitative investigations were conducted with assistance of mass spectral molecular networking, and quantification of phytochemicals was performed. In addition to that, a chemical characteristic fusion analysis was proposed for the systematic chemical profile evaluation. Subsequently, the bioactivity assays encompassing glycolipid metabolism regulation and anticoagulation were performed. Using multivariate analyses and machine learning (ML) algorithms, the acquired phytochemical and biological information was assembled for exploration of bioactive components within SF.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

SF from four origins was collected, including Dongtou District (DT, Wenzhou, Zhejiang, China), Wenling City (WL, Taizhou, Zhejiang, China), Rongcheng City (RC, Weihai, Shandong, China) and Xiapu County (XP, Ningde, Fujian, China). The samples were randomly harvested at commercial maturity, and the relevant information was listed in Table S1. Subsequently, the collected samples were morphologically authenticated as the whole algae of S. fusiforme by Dr. Zhenzhong Yang (College of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Zhejiang University). SFV and SFB were vesicles and branches of SF from DT obtained by manually sorting, respectively. All samples were harvested fresh and ambient-air dried under sunlight.

Chemicals and reagents

Formic acid, 3-indoleacrylic acid, indole-3-carboxaldehyde and acarbose were bought from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). L-(−)-Fucose was obtained from Bio Basic Inc. (Markham, Canada). Acetonitrile was acquired from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Ninhydrin coloring solution kit for HITACHI, amino acids mixture standard solution type H and γ-aminobutyric acid were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Folin & Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent, sodium citrate and octyl gallate were bought from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 3-Phenylphenol, argatroban, coagulation factor IIa (FIIa) from bovine plasma, S-2238, sheep plasma, betaine and guanine were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gallic acid was acquired from Chengdu Desite Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Multienzyme complex (consisting of alginate lyase, pectinase and cellulase) was bought from SUNSON Industry Group Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Pancreatic cholesterol esterase (CEase), 4-nitrophenyl butyrate (p-NPB), pancreatic lipase (PL), 4-methylumbelliferyl oleate (4-MUO), α-glucosidase (AG) and 4-nitrophenyl α-D-glucopyranoside (p-NPG) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) kit, thrombin time (TT) kit and prothrombin time (PT) kit were obtained from Shanghai Sun Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). AB-8 macroporous resin was acquired from Shanghai Nankai resin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Orlistat was bought from Dalian Meilun Biotech Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade, and the purity of reference standards was all > 98%.

Analysis of amino acids

Detailed steps for the analysis of amino acids were described in the supplementary material. The FAO/WHO essential amino acid pattern for adults was taken to score the composition of essential amino acids (EAA) (FAO/WHO/UNU, 1985).

Analysis of fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin

Fucoidan was extracted via hot water reflux, and CaCl2 was added to remove alginate. Alginate oligosaccharide and phlorotannin were extracted simultaneously using a method of multienzyme complex, and the SF residue was treated with 40% ethanol for a secondary extraction of phlorotannin. Detailed steps for extraction and preparation of fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin were provided in the supplementary material. Fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin were measured using the PhOH-H2SO4 method, m-hydroxydiphenyl assay and folin–ciocalteu assay, respectively (Lim et al., 2014, Kumar and Kumar, 2017, Blainski et al., 2013).

Identification of compounds using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS)

The powder sample was weighed, and 75% ethanol was added at a mass to volume ratio of 1:20 for 1 h reflux extraction. After centrifugation at 3 235×g for 10 min and rotatory evaporation, the supernatant was analyzed. A Waters ACQUITY UPLC (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an AB SCIEX Triple TOF 5600 + mass spectrometer (Framingham, MA, USA) was used. Chromatographic separation was performed at 35 °C on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). Water containing 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile were used as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The flow rate was 0.25 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 μL. The optimized elution condition was as follows: 0–12 min, 1%–5% B; 12–18 min, 5%–10% B; 18–28 min, 10%–70% B; 28–30 min, 70%–100% B; 30–35 min, 100% B.

In positive mode, parameters were set as follows: curtain gas, 241.32 kPa; gas 1 (N2), 379.21 kPa; gas 2 (N2), 379.21 kPa; ion spray voltage, 5.5 kV; collision energy, 10 V; declustering potential, 80 V; ion source temperature, 600 °C; scan range, the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) 100–2000.

Compounds in SF were identified based on literature, Reaxys database, PubChem database and global natural product social molecular networking (GNPS). The raw data of UPLC-Q-TOF MS were submitted to GNPS (https://gnps.ucsd.edu) for mass spectral molecular networking construction (Aron et al., 2020). The parameters were optimized for generating reproducible, informative and reliable mass spectral molecular networking (Table S2). The visual representation of the networking was generated using Cytoscape 3.9.1 (Shannon et al., 2003).

Semi-quantification of compounds using liquid chromatography-single quadrupole mass spectrometry (LC-MSD)

The sample preparation and chromatographic separation of LC-MSD were performed in the same way as for UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, as described in 2.5. An Agilent 1260 Infinity LC (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent 6160 Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for the semi-quantification of the compounds.

Selected ion monitoring was performed in the positive mode. Parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 5.5 kV; dry gas temperature, 350 °C; dry gas flow, 10 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 35 psi; fragmentor voltage for all selected ions, 110 V.

Each compound was identified based on its m/z and retention time, before being assigned the parameters for peak detection and integration. The area under the curve was used as the semi-quantitative indicator of the compound.

Bioactivity assays

Powder sample (1 g) was weighed and 20 mL of water was added. The mixture was reflux extracted for 1 h. After centrifugation at 3 235×g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected, and the volume was adjusted with water to 25 mL, resulting in the high-concentration sample. Each sample was individually diluted at 1:4 and 1:24 ratios, achieving medium- and low-concentration samples.

Pancreatic cholesterol esterase

The CEase inhibitory activity assay was carried out as described with some modifications (Mostafa, Al-Ahbabi, Adiamo, Mudgil, & Maqsood, 2022). Briefly, the CEase solution was prepared in PBS buffer (containing 0.1 mol/L NaCl and 5.16 mmol/L sodium taurocholate hydrate). The sample (20 μL) and CEase (100 μL, 0.35 U/mL) were mixed and incubated for 15 min at 25 °C. The reaction was initiated by adding p-NPB (20 μL, 2 mmol/L). Pancreatic lipase

The PL inhibitory activity was determined according to the previous study with some modifications (Mostafa, Al-Ahbabi, Adiamo, Mudgil, & Maqsood, 2022). Briefly, the sample (25 μL) was incubated with PL solution (25 μL, 1 mg/mL) and 4-MUO solution (50 μL, 0.1 mmol/L) in tris buffer (13 mmol/L tris–HCl, 150 mmol/L NaCl and 1.3 mmol/L CaCl2) at 25 °C for 20 min. The reaction was stopped by adding citrate buffer (100 μL, 0.1 mol/L, pH 4.2). The released 4-MUO was detected by a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 320 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. The PL inhibitory activity was calculated using Eq. (1).

α-Glucosidase

The AG inhibitory activity assay was carried out as described with some modifications (Yang, Wang, Wang, & Zhang, 2012). Briefly, the sample (50 μL) was incubated with AG (50 μL, 0.5 U/mL) in PBS buffer and kept at 37 °C for 10 min. The reaction was initiated by adding p-NPG (100 μL, 5 mmol/L). After a 15 min incubation, the Na2CO3 (100 μL, 1 mol/L) was added to terminate the reaction, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. The AG inhibitory activity was calculated using Eq. (1).

Coagulation factor IIa

The FIIa inhibitory activity was determined according to the previous study with some modifications (He et al., 2021). Briefly, the sample (50 μL) was mixed with FIIa solution (50 μL, 0.25 mg/mL) in PBS buffer. After a 30 min incubation at 37 °C, the substrate S-2238 (100 μL, 1 mg/mL) was added and incubated for 10 min. The absorbance was analyzed at 405 nm, and the FⅡa inhibitory activity was calculated using Eq. (1).

APTT, TT and PT

The APTT, TT and PT assays were measured as described with some modifications (He et al., 2021). The values of APTT, TT and PT were expressed as multiples of the clot formation time relative to the control group.

For APTT assay, the sheep plasma (40 μL) was mixed with the sample (10 μL). After a 3 min incubation at 37 °C, the APTT reagent (50 μL) was added and incubated again for 3 min. The time of clot formation was recorded immediately after adding the pre-warmed CaCl2 solution (50 μL, 0.025 mol/L).

For TT or PT assay, the sheep plasma (40 μL) was mixed with the sample (10 μL) and incubated for 3 min at 37 °C. The time of clot formation was recorded immediately after adding the pre-warmed TT reagent (50 μL) or PT reagent (100 μL).

Systematic chemical profile evaluation

Chemometrics

Chemometrics was used to process and analyze the data from LC-MSD, as shown in detail in the supplementary material.

Chemical characteristic fusion analysis

The data from the integrated phytochemical analyses were standardized using Z-score transformation. Subsequently, the values were scaled to a unified range of 0–1. Afterwards, the data were classified, averaged and utilized to generate a chemical characteristic radar chart where the evaluation score was obtained by calculating the area of the polygon.

Unveiling bioactive components

Multivariate analysis

Pearson correlation analysis, grey relational analysis (GRA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were applied to explore the correlation between phytochemicals and their activities. The coefficient values of Pearson correlation, GRA and OPLS-DA were calculated, so that each phytochemical was assigned three coefficients for a bioactivity.

Machine learning

Support vector regression (SVR), random forest regression (RFR) and gradient boosting regression tree (GBRT) were performed to analyze the correlation between phytochemicals and their activities. Three ML algorithms were implemented via the scikit-learn library on python programming language (version 3.8) (Pedregosa et al., 2011). The bioactivity and phytochemicals dataset were pre-processed and divided into training and test sets at a ratio of 80:20. The hyperparameters of all models were tuned with grid search and 5-fold cross validation. The mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) were used to evaluate the models. For RFR and GBRT, each phytochemical was evaluated through “feature importance” method. For SVR model, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for each phytochemical.

The compounds that ranked within the top 3 in two or more models were further filtered using Lipinski’s rule of five, as detailed in Fig. S1. Subsequently, these components were identified as potential bioactive components.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical difference among groups was assessed using One-way analysis of variation followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results and discussion

S. fusiforme from all origins was a well-balanced source of protein for adults

As a nutritional algae, the content of amino acids is an important indicator for quality evaluation. The content and composition of EAA are equally crucial for the nutritional value of food.

The amino acid composition of SF was assessed (Fig. S2), showing that the EAA content of SF from different origins and botanical parts varied with a ranking pattern of XP > WL > RC > DT and SFV > SFB (Table 1). The EAA content of SF from XP reached (30.09 ± 0.58) mg/g dw, and the EAA content variation in SF from different geographical origins might exceed 15%. There was an even greater difference between the vesicle (31.89 ± 4.35) mg/g dw and the branch (20.22 ± 1.99) mg/g dw, which was reaching 30%. The EAA was scored as well to examine the EAA composition of SF (Table S3). The first limiting amino acids were all histidine, and the scores were all over 100, indicating SF was a well-balanced source of protein for adults (FAO/WHO/UNU, 1985).

Content of fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin varied across S. fusiforme from different origins and botanical parts

Fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin were selected for content determination due to their bioactivities and applications. SF from XP contained the highest amount of fucoidan with a mean of 5.45% (Fig. 2A). The main source of fucoidan is brown algae, and fucoidan is widely distributed in the cell wall of SF to act as a protection against desiccation stress (Holdt & Kraan, 2011). Recently, fucoidan has raised a great deal of interest for its numerous bioactivities and nutritional value as a food ingredient. For alginate, XP was the highest with a mean of 38.11% (Fig. 2B). Alginate is also a component mainly derived from brown algae, and properties such as biocompatibility, low toxicity and relatively low cost have enabled it to become a widely used biomaterial. For phlorotannin, DT was the greatest with a mean of 9.05‰, while WL, RC and XP were 8.00, 7.98 and 8.36‰, respectively (Fig. 2C). Phlorotannin, a tannin derivative composed of multiple phloroglucinol units linked together in various ways, is primarily found in brown algae. As a secondary metabolite, phlorotannin possesses bioactivities such as antioxidation, anti-inflammation and anti-diabetic effects (Shen et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2020).

SFV was higher in fucoidan and phlorotannin but lower in alginate compared to SFB. The midrib of Alaria fistulosa P. et R., another brown algae, is rich in alginate, while its sporophyll is rich in fucoidan (Usov, Smirnova, & Klochkova, 2005). In terms of morphology and function, the midrib of A. fistulosa plays an equivalent role as SFB and the sporophyll corresponds to SFV (Lin, Ma, Chen, & Wu, 2021). Our findings were consistent with those in A. fistulosa.

Although the accumulation of carbohydrates and phlorotannin have been found to vary significantly in different developmental phases of brown algae, few studies have compared the fucoidan, alginate and phlorotannin of SF from different origins. For each origin, SF was randomly harvested at commercial maturity to eliminate natural product differences due to variations in the developmental phases. Given the aquatic environmental conditions of DT and due to its high exposure to solar radiation, an overall increase in the accumulation of phlorotannin can be expected (Álvarez-Gómez et al., 2017). DT has a subtropical monsoon climate with an average annual temperature of 17.5 °C, 1885.2 h of sunshine and a salinity of 2.6%–2.8% in coastal waters, which is suitable for the growth of algae (Zhang and Zhang, 2009, Zhao et al., 2015). Despite the benefits of solar radiation for SF, sea surface temperatures are high for an extended duration of time in the warm summers of southern China. Under the prolonged heat stress which is common in southern China, the membrane system of SF is damaged, and membrane lipid peroxidation occurs, leading to the death of algae (Liu & Lin, 2021). As a result, large-scale SF cultivating is rare below the 20th parallel of the Northern Hemisphere.

A total of 123 compounds in S. fusiforme were identified

In the case of SF, the investigation into its chemical profile is limited. By utilizing UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, base peak chromatograms of SFV and SFB were obtained in positive ion mode (Fig. 2D and E). A total of 123 compounds were identified based on literature (Blunt et al., 2013, Cui et al., 2020, Shen et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2019), databases and molecular networking. Specifically, the identified compounds comprised 16 alkaloids, 27 amino acids, 18 polyphenols and phenolic acids, 24 nucleosides and nucleotides, 18 terpenoids, six lipids and 14 other compounds (Table 2).

Compounds identified by molecular networking.

The mass spectral molecular networking was constructed using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS data obtained from SFV and SFB. A total of 1454 nodes and 1230 edges were generated, which provided reliable and valuable information about chemical composition in SF. Notably, compounds with high spectrum similarities were grouped together, which in turn facilitated the identification of structurally similar compounds (Yang, Li, Chen, Zhao, & Wang, 2022). Ten molecular clusters in the mass spectral molecular networking of SF were categorized into distinct chemical families (Fig. 3A). Following this, a total of 21 compounds were identified in the cluster of polyphenols and phenolic acids, nucleosides and nucleotides, as well as terpenoids using library hit nodes and edges between nodes with details shown in Table 2.

Compound 29 was a library hit node which was identified as 5′-deoxy-5′-(methylsulfinyl) adenosine or isomer. The base of such compounds did not undergo fragmentation. The ion at m/z 178 was formed by a cleavage within the ribose and the loss of a methylsulfinyl group, resulting in a cyclic structure. Subsequently, the ion at m/z 178 produced the ion at m/z 164 by the loss of a methylene group and yielded the fragment ion of the base at m/z 136 ultimately. Through analysis of shared edges and ion signals, compounds 73, 71, 31 and 28 were structurally identified (Fig. 3C). For compound 73, the presence of the ion at m/z 136 provided evidence for the presence of an adenine base. Additionally, product ions observed at m/z 131, 97 and 89 indicated radical rearrangement cyclization resulting from molecular fragmentation (Fig. 3C, compound 73). Based on these observations, a structure for compound 73 as 5′-S-(2-carboxyethyl)-5′-thioadenosine was proposed (Fig. 3G). For compound 71, the node only connected to compound 73, and the ions at m/z 136 and 97 were detected. On this basis, the structure of compound 71 as a thioadenosine derivative was hypothesized. The ion at m/z 274 was determined as a base-deprived fragment ion. The ion at m/z 144, which carried an acetylated homocysteine skeleton, was formed by the loss of deoxyribose from the ion at m/z 274. Subsequently, the ion at m/z 144 underwent further fragmentation with the loss of a carboxyl group to produce the ion at m/z 98 (Fig. 3C, compound 71). These findings supported the structure of compound 71 as 5′-S-adenosyl-N-acetyl-homocysteine (Fig. 3H). For compound 31, the structure of adenosine analogue was confirmed by the detection of ions at m/z 136 and 97. Furthermore, the presence of the phosphate group was observed. The structure for compound 31 as 2′,3′-dideoxy-2′,3′-didehydroadenosine 5′-phosphate was determined (Fig. 3C, compound 31). Compound 28 was identified as an isomer of compound 29, which was an adduct in the [M + Na]+ form, according to the observation of the mass differences between the precursor ions, as well as ions between m/z 186 and m/z 164 (Fig. 3C, compound 28).

Using the same approach, compounds 50 and 51 with a purine skeleton were characterized from the library hit node of compound 25 and its characteristic fragment ion at m/z 152 (Fig. 3C). Structural identification of compounds 48, 42, 59 and 43, which are nucleotides, were likewise obtained. Of these, the base fragment ions at m/z 152, 136 and 112, as well as the nucleotide fragment ions resulting from the cleavage of phosphodiester bonds at m/z 348, 330, 324 and 307 were essential in providing critical structural insights (Fig. 3C).

The loss of hydroxyl, methoxy and carboxyl groups, along with radical rearrangement cyclization processes, were the main observed fragmentation pathways for polyphenols, phenolic acids and terpenoids (Fig. 3B and D–F). Compound 57 is a phenylalanine derivative. For phenylalanine, the ions at m/z 152, 135 and 107 were formed via sequential bond-cleavage reactions involving ion–molecule complexes and NH3 loss (Lam & O'Hair, 2010). Ions at m/z 152, 135 and 107 were also detected in compound 57, as well as two additional ions at m/z 176 and 162. The ion at m/z 176 was produced by the loss of carboxyl group through precursor ion at m/z 222 and formed ion at m/z 162 via the loss of a methyl group (Fig. 3B, compound 57). Thus, a structure of 3,4-dihydro-6,7-dihydroxy-1-methyl-3-isoquinolinecarboxylic acid was obtained (Fig. 3I). Compound 106 is a terpenoid, which produced ions at m/z 181, 139, 125, 107, 83 as same as the identified compound 111 (Fig. 3F, compound 106). These findings supported the structure of compound 106 as ethyl 1-hydroxy-2,6,6-trimethyl-β,4-dioxo-2-cyclohexene-1-butanoate (Fig. 3J).

Chemometrics and chemical characteristic fusion analysis were applied for systematic chemical profile evaluation of S. fusiforme

A total of 123 compounds, which had been previously identified by qualitative analysis, underwent a semi-quantitative analysis using LC-MSD. Of these, 55 compounds exceeded the limit of quantification and were subjected to chemometrics. The chemical fingerprint combined with chemometrics is a commonly used tool for phytochemical evaluation (He & Zhou, 2021). The chromatographic analysis together with chemometrics was employed to evaluate Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F. H. Chen and Chenopodium formosanum Koidz. from different geographical origins (Xie and Chen, 2021, Yang et al., 2018).

In this study, principal components analysis (PCA) and OPLS-DA were conducted to discriminate the difference in compounds among SF from different origins. PCA was an unsupervised dimension reduction method. The PCA model was constructed with two principal components contributing 56.3% of the total variance (R2X = 0.360 for PC1 and R2X = 0.203 for PC2) (Fig. 4A). The score plot showed that SFV and SFB differed from each other and from all SF whole algae samples. Furthermore, DT was located to the right of PC1, suggesting the capability of PC1 to separate DT from other origins. While on the left of PC1, XP was slightly separated from WL and RC.

OPLS-DA, a supervised method, was performed among three groups (DT, XP and WL&RC) (Fig. 4B). The OPLS-DA model (R2X = 0.534, R2Y = 0.870, Q2 = 0.511) was validated to be reliable by permutation test (the intercept of Q2 is less than zero) (Fig. 4C). The variable importance in projection (VIP) method was employed to analyze the contribution of each compound (Fig. 4D). A total of 30 compounds with VIP value >1 were found, indicating their potential as differential compounds for distinguishing SF from different origins.

For visualizing the content difference of compounds between various origins, all quantified compounds were plotted on a heatmap (Fig. 4E). Compounds were categorized by hierarchical clustering analysis into cluster 1 and cluster 2. The SF from DT exhibited a higher content of the 32 compounds in cluster 2 (Fig. 4E). In cluster 2, 10 compounds were revealed to be the most abundant in DT significantly (P < 0.05) relative to those in the remaining three regions, which were list in Table 2. Among these compounds, rhinomilisin E showed cytotoxicity on human cancer cell lines, and dehydrovomifoliol demonstrated anti-inflammatory activity (Cui et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2018).

The objective of chemical characteristic fusion analysis is to merge and refine chemical data obtained from integrated phytochemical analyses, thereby establishing a systematic and quantitative evaluation method. A correlation heatmap was created based on the content of amino acids, fucoidan, alginate, phlorotannin, alkaloids, terpenoids, etc. among SF samples to reveal the possible connections between natural products found in SF (Fig. S3). It was observed that compounds with similar structures and same classification demonstrated strong correlations and were approximately grouped. Thus, the content of a certain classification of natural products was integrated in the chemical characteristic fusion analysis (Fig. 4F). The content weightage of specific natural products in SF was also taken into account. Therefore, SF from different origins was systematically evaluated with respect to the composition of alkaloid, amino acid, nucleoside and nucleotide, phlorotannin, polysaccharide, terpenoid and others.

The chemical characteristic radar chart showed that SF from DT was more enriched in alkaloid, nucleoside and nucleotide, phlorotannin, and terpenoid (Fig. 4F), most of which were reported for their bioactivities (Cui et al., 2020, Shen et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2018). SF from XP was found to be rich in polysaccharide and amino acid (Fig. 4F), highlighting its potential as a functional food (Tanna & Mishra, 2019). By calculating the polygon area in radar chart, an evaluation score was obtained to quantitatively assess the systematic chemical profile of SF. DT exhibited the highest evaluation score (0.684), followed by XP (0.440), WL (0.397) and RC (0.361). SF from DT showed a greater biosynthesis capacity towards a broader range of bioactive components. DT provided an environment conducive to the growth of SF due to its favorable solar radiation and aquatic conditions. Meanwhile, DT has been cultivating SF since 1988, and the development of its culturing and processing techniques has resulted in wide recognition.

S. fusiforme demonstrated hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant effects

As a traditional Chinese medicine, SF has been recorded for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases since 2000 years ago (Zhang, Zhang, Tang, & Mao, 2020). Hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia and thrombosis are common factors in the development of cardiovascular diseases. Thus, the hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant effects of SF were investigated in this study.

Dietary lipids undergo digestion in the intestinal lumen by crucial metabolic enzymes, PL and CEase, to produce cholesterol and triglycerides (Ding et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2023). The uptake of these lipids is driven by the concentration gradient or facilitated by transport proteins (Ko, Qu, Black, & Tso, 2020). In lipid regulation, particular attention is given to the levels of total cholesterol and total triglycerides. Therefore, the hypolipidemic effect can be achieved by inhibiting key enzymes associated with lipid metabolism and digestion. Orlistat has been developed and clinically validated based on this principle.

The assays were performed on SF from different origins, revealing that SF exhibited an inhibitory effect on both PL and CEase (Fig. 5A and B). The most potent inhibitory activities were observed in samples from DT, with an inhibition rate of 68.07% against CEase and 60.62% against PL. Based on the comprehensive findings, it appeared that the geographical origin exerted an influence on the inhibition effect. The average CEase inhibition rate of SF from DT was 48.15%, while SF from WL was 7.90%. Notably, samples with obvious CEase inhibitory effect exhibited a dose–response relationship (Table S4). Similarly, the average PL inhibition rate of SF from DT was 35.07%, while SF from RC was 5.97%. This phenomenon might be attributed to the algae’s habitat, inducing variations in the content of bioactive components and leading to differences in bioactivities, which have been observed in brown algae (Balboa, Conde, Moure, Falqué, & Domínguez, 2013). Gallic acid and orlistat were employed as positive controls in the evaluation of CEase and PL inhibition, with respective IC50 values of 0.29 mg/mL and 24.90 ng/mL (Fig. S4A and B). Fucoidan isolated from SF has been proven to regulate blood lipid levels in type 2 diabetic mice (Jia et al., 2020). The inhibition of lipid metabolic enzymes by SF is just one of many pathways in regulating lipid levels, the hypolipidemic effect may be realized through an extensive set of mechanisms.

AG acts upon the glycosyl structure of carbohydrates, generating monosaccharides and oligosaccharides, thereby contributing to the rise in postprandial blood glucose. Acarbose, a commonly used antidiabetic drug, exerts a hypoglycemic effect by inhibiting AG, with an observed IC50 value of 1.33 ng/mL (Fig. S4C). The inhibitory effect of SF on AG varied across geographical origins, with DT sample demonstrating the most potent effect (Fig. 5C). An average inhibition rate of 65.04% was exhibited, with the highest observation at 84.83%, and there was also a dose–response relationship (Table S5). The hypoglycemic properties have been found in numerous bioactive components derived from algae (Zhao et al., 2018). Through this study, SF showcased significant nutritional benefits and promising signs as a potential source of anti-diabetic drugs.

FIIa is the central enzyme within the blood coagulation pathway. FIIa initiates the cleavage of fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, concurrently activating platelets and other coagulation factors (Krissanaprasit et al., 2021). All SF samples exhibited strong inhibition on FIIa accompanied by a significant dose–response relationship, and the inhibition rates for three samples from DT exceeded 90% (Fig. 5D). Even at low-concentration, the inhibition rates for all samples remained above 50% (Table S6). Although differences in inhibition rates among geographical origins were observed, the differences were minor, emphasizing the reliable and trustworthy anticoagulant effects of SF. Argatroban served as the positive control with an IC50 value of 3.34 μg/mL (Fig. S4D).

In clinical coagulation function tests, APTT, TT and PT are commonly employed to assess the blood status of patients. APTT reflects the intrinsic coagulation pathway, involving coagulation factors VIII, IX, XI and XII. TT reflects the transformation of fibrinogen to fibrin. PT reflects the extrinsic coagulation pathway, involving coagulation factors III and VII. The result indicated a significant dose–response prolongation (P < 0.05) of both APTT and TT across all samples (Table S7). The most effective sample demonstrated an 8-fold increase in APTT and a 5-fold increase in TT, while only two samples showed a slightly weaker effect in APTT compared to 2 mg/mL heparin sodium (Fig. 5E and F). PT was not significantly prolonged in any samples, suggesting that SF affected the intrinsic and common pathways in anticoagulant effect (Fig. 5G). In terms of geographical origin, samples from DT and XP performed comparably, with RC and WL following behind. The anticoagulant properties of SF were validated in both in vivo mouse models and in vitro human venous plasma models (He et al., 2023a, Zhang et al., 2020). Additionally, SF was observed to stimulate the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells (Zhang, Zhang, Tang, & Mao, 2020). All these studies pointed to polysaccharides as the anticoagulant components. Compared to heparin, which carries a risk of serious bleeding and thrombocytopenia, polysaccharides from non-animal sources exhibit less toxicity and a lower risk of bleeding (Li et al., 2022). Thus, SF showed promising potential for development as a functional food for thrombotic disease prevention and as an anti-thrombotic drug.

In bioactivity assays, different botanical parts, SFV and SFB, showed similar results (Fig. 5). However, variations in the integrated phytochemical analyses between SFV and SFB were noted. Our findings implied that the bioactivity of SF may not exhibit a simple one-to-one liner relationship with the bioactive component. The hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant bioactivities of SF were attributed to a variety of bioactive components in SF. Our study provided a reference for the rational utilization of resources in SFV and SVB.

Multivariate analysis and machine learning for unveiling potential bioactive components

While SF has shown hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant effects, the specific bioactive components responsible for these effects remained unclear. However, the conventional post-isolation screening procedure for tracking the bioactivity of natural products is time-consuming and labor-intensive (Yang, Li, & Li, 2018). In this study, multivariate analyses and ML algorithms were utilized to explore the correlation between bioactivities and phytochemicals, contributing to unveiling the bioactive components.

The relationship between bioactivities and phytochemicals could be linear or nonlinear, and it might even switch between the two under different concentrations. As such, it is not appropriate to utilize one ML algorithm to unveil bioactive components. In order to explore the relationship between this complex mixture system and bioactivity properly, linear and nonlinear aspects, high-dimensional data and robustness were taken into account. Accordingly, Pearson correlation, GRA, OPLS-DA, SVR, RFR and GBRT were selected for the prediction.

The predictivity of the generated model for each bioactivity was assessed using MAE and MSE. The optimization of the hyperparameters was conducted under the monitoring of MSE (Table S8). The well-generated models combined with multivariate analyses permitted the evaluation of each phytochemical. Following that, through a specific strategy (Fig. S1), 11 potential bioactive components were found (Fig. 6A). Among them, 3-indoleacrylic acid, octyl gallate, phlorotannin, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, betaine, fucoidan and guanine were validated for their corresponding activities. The validation indicated that 3-indoleacrylic acid was an inhibitor for CEase and FIIa with IC50 values of 0.53 and 2.11 mg/mL, respectively (Fig. 6B and D). 3-Indoleacrylic acid is an antialgal agent, which plays a potentially vital role in maintaining the ecological balance within algal communities (Hu, Effiong, Liu, & Xiao, 2023). It is noteworthy that few studies have reported the bioactivity of 3-indoleacrylic acid against CEase and FIIa. Furthermore, phlorotannin was confirmed for PL inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value reaching 18.28 μg/mL (Fig. 6C). Phlorotannins derived from various brown algae exhibited potent PL inhibition, and studies suggested that phlorotannins, characterized by a polyphenolic structure, bind to PL through non-covalent interactions (He, Chen, et al., 2023). The anticoagulant effects of fucoidan were evidenced by prolonged APTT and TT (Fig. 6E and F). Notably, the bioactivity of fucoidan is influenced by multiple variables, including source, molecular weight, monosaccharide composition and structural feature (Zhang, Zhang, Tang, & Mao, 2020). Fucoidan is currently the most prominent bioactive component in SF. SF holds great promise in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases. However, the complex macromolecular structure and the intricate structure–activity relationship of fucoidan remain major challenges for researchers in elucidating its mechanism and establishing precise quality control standards.

Conclusion

This study proposed a heterogeneous information ensemble approach for the phytochemical and biological evaluation as well as unveiling the bioactive components in SF. The integrated phytochemical analyses showed the chemical profile and the content variations in amino acids, fucoidan, alginate, phlorotannin, alkaloids, terpenoids, etc. of SF from different geographical origins and botanical parts. In addition to that, a chemical characteristic fusion analysis was introduced for the systematic chemical profile evaluation, discovering that DT excelled among origins. Subsequently, the bioactivity assays demonstrated that SF possessed the hypolipidemic, hypoglycemic and anticoagulant effects with SF from DT being prominent. Furthermore, the acquired information from phytochemical and biological evaluation was delved deeply to explore meaningful connections. With the assistance of multivariate analysis and ML, 3-indoleacrylic acid, phlorotannin and fucoidan were unveiled as the bioactive components in SF. Our study provided a reference for the rational consumption and further development of SF.
