Red cell antigen exposures in patients with sickle cell disease receiving transfusion from Black and Hispanic donors
Authors: Julia Mewha, Sunitha Vege, David F. Friedman, Shaimaa Makrm, Gorka Ochoa, Connie M. Westhoff, Stella T. Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with sickle cell disease (SCD) have an elevated risk for alloimmunization because of frequent transfusions and antigenic disparities with donors. Extended antigen matching beyond Rhesus (Rh; CcEe) and Kell (K) can reduce alloimmunization but is not broadly applied because of logistic and economic challenges. Red cell antigen exposures may be minimized when donor and patient populations are from similar racial and ethnic backgrounds. This study assessed alloantigen exposure across 35 antigens in patients with SCD who received transfusions from donors who self-identified as Black or Hispanic. Seven hundred units transfused to 28 chronically transfused patients were genotyped using a single nucleotide polymorphism–based assay. When combining Rh and K-matching with donor unit selection from racially similar populations, patients were exposed to an average of 1.6 antigens per transfusion, compared to the previously reported 3.5 exposures per transfusion across the same 35 antigens using a general donor inventory. Among 514 units transfused to Black recipients from Black donors, 22% had no foreign antigen exposure across 35 antigens. The most frequent alloantigen exposures were Jkb, S, and Doa. One clinically significant anti-Jkb formed during the study. These findings suggest combining donor unit selection with blood group genotyping may improve compatibility and transfusion safety for high-risk populations. These trials were registered at www.ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT04156893 and NCT04156906.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) predisposes patients to complications that often require intervention with red blood cell (RBC) transfusions, including chronic transfusion therapy.1,2 However, repeated RBC transfusions significantly increase the risk of alloimmunization, which reduces the number of compatible donor units, increases the likelihood of delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions, and raises the costs of all subsequent transfusion therapy.3,4

To mitigate these risks, the American Society of Hematology and the International Collaboration for Transfusion Medicine Guidelines recommend prophylactic Rhesus (Rh; C, E or C/c, E/e) and K matching to avoid exposure to the antigens that the patient lacks.1,5 Although this practice has been shown to decrease alloimmunization prevalence from 35% to 18% when compared with ABO and RhD matching alone, it does not eliminate the risk.1,6–8 Exposure to other antigens (eg, Jka/Jkb, Fya/Fyb, S/s), which is associated with clinically significant transfusion reactions, persists. Extended prophylactic matching across these antigens can further reduce alloimmunization, but identifying sufficient donors is challenging.9–12 In addition, undetected genetic RH variation remains a significant contributor to alloimmunization even with prophylactic serologic Rh antigen matching.3,8,12–14

Blood group genotyping identifies single nucleotide polymorphisms that encode RBC antigens and provides a more comprehensive approach to donor-recipient matching.15–18 A previous study found that with Rh- and K-matched transfusions, patients were exposed to an average of 3.5 foreign RBC antigens per transfusion, and ~3% of transfusions avoided recipient exposure to a foreign antigen among 35 tested antigens.12 Because antigen frequency is population-specific, selecting units from Black or Hispanic donors may reduce antigenic disparity and alloimmunization in patients with SCD.19

The objective of this study was to prospectively evaluate the frequency of foreign antigen exposures across 35 RBC antigens in a cohort of Black and/or Hispanic patients with SCD who received units selected from Black and Hispanic donor pools. By quantifying the RBC antigen exposure in this high-risk population, our findings confirm that selecting donor units based on self-identified race and ethnicity reduced foreign antigen exposure and highlighted the value of RBC genotyping to improve transfusion compatibility and safety.

Study design

A total of 28 patients with SCD who received chronic transfusion therapy were enrolled in 2 studies from 2020 to 2025, which were approved by the institutional review board at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (supplemental Table 1). All human participants research in this study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients and donor units were genotyped using the PreciseType Human Erythrocyte Antigen BeadChip (Immucor, Warren, NJ), which predicts 35 RBC antigens across 11 blood group systems (supplemental Table 2). Patients self-reported race and ethnicity. The donor questionnaire options regarding self-identified race and ethnicity included Asian, White, Black, American Indian, Hispanic/Latino, multiple race, and other and did not distinguish between White and Black Hispanic (supplemental Table 3). Patients were enrolled in 2 clinical trials that provided RH genotype-matched blood from donors who self-identified as Black and Hispanic (clinicaltrials.gov identifiers: NCT04156893 and NCT04156906) and who were ABO compatible and Rh and K matched. The exception was 5 RhD-positive patients, enrolled in (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04156906), with historic anti-D reactivity who received 59 RhD-negative units from donors who identified as White.20 For the RhD-negative units, the blood center provided units from White donors because <5% of the Black population is RhD negative. A statistical comparison of antigen exposure from White vs Black donors was not included because units from White donors were only provided to 5 patients. For patients with existing alloantibodies, antigen-negative units were provided except for the presence of anti-M.

Results and discussion

Patient characteristics

A total of 24 patients self-identified as Black and non-Hispanic, 1 identified as White/Black and non-Hispanic, 2 patients identified as other and Hispanic, and 1 patient identified as White and Hispanic (Table 1). The median age at enrollment was 9 years. Of the enrolled patients, 21 received simple transfusions, 5 underwent RBC exchange, and 2 transitioned from simple transfusions to RBC exchange. Before enrollment, patients had received a median of 25 (range, 3–1650) serologic Rh- and K-matched units from primarily Black donors with 5143 units transfused across the cohort. Eight patients were alloimmunized with 15 antibodies identified, including 5 anti-D, 2 each of anti-Kpa, -C, and -M, and 1 each of anti-V, -Goa, -K, and -Jsa The prestudy alloimmunization rate was 0.29 antibodies per 100 units transfused.

Blood group genotyping and antigen distribution

RBC antigen genotyping predicted that no patient lacked the high prevalence antigens k, Jsb, Lub, Dib, Coa, Hy, Joa, LWa, or Sc1 and all lacked expression of the low prevalence antigens Cob, LWb, and Sc2 (Figure 1A). Rh antigen expression matched the African and Hispanic population frequencies.19 Among 35 tested antigens, patients lacked a median of 14 antigens (range, 10–17). Only 1 patient was at risk for anti-Fyb because most had the GATA site mutation in the ACKR1 promoter that causes erythroid-specific loss of Fy(b) expression.

Donor selection and antigen exposure

A total of 700 genotyped units were transfused at 460 visits. These units were from Black (n = 565), Hispanic (n = 76), and White (n = 59) donors. Black, non-Hispanic recipients (n = 24) received 514 units from Black donors and 38 from Hispanic donors, including 37 double red cell units (Table 2). The Black/White, non-Hispanic recipient (n = 1) received 11 units from Black donors. The other, Hispanic recipients (n = 2) received 30 and 26 units from Black and Hispanic donors, respectively. The White, Hispanic recipient (n = 1) received 10 and 12 units from Black and Hispanic donors, respectively. Among the units transfused from Black donors to Black recipients, 22% (114/514) had no foreign antigen exposure across 35 antigens tested. Similarly, 39.5% (15/38) of units from Hispanic donors to Hispanic recipients (n = 3) led to no foreign antigen exposure.

When White donors were excluded, the mean foreign antigen exposure for all recipients was 1.59 per transfusion episode and 1.35 per unit (Table 2). Black, non-Hispanic recipients were transfused with an average of 1.43 units per visit and had a mean exposure of 1.35 antigens per unit when Black donors were selected as opposed to 2.68 and 2.32 antigens when the donors were Hispanic or White, respectively (P < .0001; Table 2).

For patients who received units from Black and Hispanic donors, the alloantigen exposure is shown in Figure 1B. Black, non-Hispanic recipients (n = 24) were frequently negative for the antigens Fya and Fyb (Duffy null), Jkb in the Kidd System, and S in the MNSs system, similar to Black donors (supplemental Table 4). Among all patients, the most frequent foreign antigen exposures were Jkb (46.7% of units [n = 486]), S (28.7% [n = 464]), and Doa (39.5% [n = 334]; Figure 1B). Among the units selected based on Rh and K matching, 46% of Black and 68% of Hispanic donors were Jkb positive, 21% of Black and 60% of Hispanic donors were S positive, and 39% of Black and 53% of Hispanic donors were Doa positive (supplemental Table 4). For patients who lacked M, N, and Dob, 76%, 69%, and 100% of units from Black and Hispanic donors were antigen positive, respectively (Figure 1B).

Alloimmunization events during study

During the study period, 2 new alloantibodies were identified, namely an anti-Jkb and an uncommon antibody specific to the MN CHO system. The anti-Jkb alloantibody was difficult to detect with a negative antibody screen and direct antiglobulin tests during hospital testing. Alloimmunization was suspected because of unexpected hemoglobin S vs A levels at the next transfusion visit. Reference laboratory–sensitive test methods identified anti-Jkb; 5 of the last 8 transfused units were Jkb positive. The clinically insignificant MN CHO system antibody was identified when an investigation was pursued for a positive antibody screen, positive direct antiglobulin test (1+ immunoglobulin G), and multiple incompatible unit crossmatches. Excluding the MN CHO antibody, the overall alloimmunization rate was 0.14 antibodies per 100 units.

This study demonstrated improved mitigation of foreign RBC alloantigen exposure in patients with SCD who receive Rh- and K-matched transfusions with units from donors, selected based on self-declared race and ethnicity. Patients demonstrated a low mean of 1.59 antigen exposures per transfusion episode when units were selected from Black and Hispanic donors. Because the units in this study were also selected based on RH genotype, patients were not exposed to the V and VS antigens, which serologic-based matching would not achieve. Exposure to antigens more frequently found in Black donors was expected; 10% (61/589) of units transfused to Jsa -negative patients were antigen positive.

Extended antigen matching has been shown to reduce the alloimmunization risk; however, it remains logistically and economically challenging. Implementing broad RBC antigen genotyping for both patients and donors could significantly improve transfusion safety and efficiency. Genotyping enables more precise identification of antigen exposure, aids in complex antibody identification, and reduces reliance on labor-intensive serologic methods.17

Costs and data management remain key limitations to the widespread adoption of extended genotyping. However, alloimmunization incurs its own set of costs, including subsequent antibody formation, reference laboratory evaluation, and transfusion delays. For patients with multiple alloantibodies, these risks and logistical burdens are amplified. Studies have shown that patients with existing alloantibodies are at increased risk of forming additional antibodies upon subsequent antigen exposures.7,21 Thus, comprehensive genotyping can serve as both preventive and resource-saving.

Our study contributes to the growing body of evidence that minority donor recruitment, coupled with Rh and K antigen matching, can enhance donor compatibility. Future efforts should focus on integrating RBC antigen genotyping with enhanced hospital and blood center laboratory information systems to facilitate more precise transfusion matching and management for patients with SCD.
