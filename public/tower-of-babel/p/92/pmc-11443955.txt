Successful left atrial catheter ablation in a patient with cor triatriatum sinister navigated by 3D volume rendering technique computed tomography
Authors: Pietro G Lacaita, Markus Stühlinger, Fabian Barbieri, Gudrun M Feuchtner, Andriana Anagnostopoulou, Christoph Sinning, Raheel Ahmed, Ivan Wong
Published: 2024

Case description

A 57-year-old male presented with refractory persistent atrial fibrillation and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45%, prompting an urgent need for rhythm control by left atrial (LA) catheter ablation.

Transesophageal echocardiogram revealed a cor triatriatum sinister (type 3). For planning of LA catheter ablation, cardiac computed tomography (CT) with multiplanar reformations (MPR) and 3D volume rendering technique (VRT) was performed (Figure 1, upper panel; Supplementary material online, Movie S1). Computed tomography located the membrane’s origin from the interatrial septum, extending laterally towards the left lateral atrial wall, primarily affecting the cranial sections while sparing the caudal parts. As there was not direct attachment of the membrane to the pulmonary ostia, LA radiofrequency catheter ablation was deemed possible. The membrane’s proximity to the right inferior pulmonary vein (RIPV) and the fossa ovalis necessitated a posterior transseptal access. Transseptal puncture site was posterior and cranial of the membrane (Figure 1, mid panel). After transthoracic echocardiogram–guided puncture, the sheath could be successfully advanced with a 270-degree bond around the membrane into the RIPV (see Supplementary material online, Movies S2 and S3). The ablation was started on the posterior wall of the right pulmonary veins. At the lower edge of the RIPV, the catheter repeatedly dislocated into the right atrium, necessitating the regaining of the transseptal access. The ablation was then continued by ablating the left pulmonary veins, which were easily accessible.

(Upper panel) Cor triatriatum by coronary computed tomography angiography: the membrane (yellow arrows) is dividing the left atrium into two chambers, with close proximity to left pulmonary vein by thin slice 3D volume rendering technique (left panel) and corresponding multiplanar reformations (right panel). RPV, right pulmonary vein; RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle; LV, left ventricle; LAD, left anterior descending coronary artery. (Middle panel) Transesophageal echocardiography: transseptal puncture site was cranial and posterior of the membrane (upper left panel, white line), in close proximity to the cor triatriatum membrane (arrow). Puncture was successful (right panel). The multipolar catheter could be successfully advanced with a 270-degree loop over the membrane to the right inferior pulmonary vein ostium. Access to right inferior pulmonary vein was more difficult than to the right superior pulmonary vein, but knowing the exact course of the membrane from computed tomography facilitated the procedure. Fluoroscopic image (lower panel, left) is showing the multipolar catheter located in the right inferior pulmonary vein. After transseptal puncture, the multipolar catheter could be successfully advanced over the sheath into the right inferior pulmonary vein ostium. (Lower panel, right: colour Doppler transesophageal echocardiogram showing the membrane in the left atrium (arrow).

Despite these challenges, successful LA catheter ablation was finished without complications and pulmonary vein isolation was achieved by ablating wide circular lesions.

Normal sinus rhythm persisted until a 5-month follow-up, with no symptoms. Medication (apixaban and beta-blocker) was continued. This case highlights the importance of advanced CT imaging, including 3D VRT visualization, for precise planning of LA ablation in the presence of a cor triatriatum membrane.
