Aging and liver health: liver chemistries and associated factors in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Farah Safdar Husain, Siew Ling Tey, Dieu Thi Thu Huynh, Jeffery Oliver, Geraldine Baggs, Choon How How, Wai Leng Chow, Magdalin Cheong, Tar Choon Aw, Samuel Teong Huang Chew, Ngiap Chuan Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver chemistries are used to measure liver function or damage, and it is vital to differentiate between the changes due to aging versus other causes. The literature has also reported a decline in serum alanine transaminase (ALT) in older age independent of other factors. In this study, we seek to (1) evaluate liver chemistries in healthy community-dwelling older adults, and (2) investigate the associations with age, sex, use of statins, and other factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 400 older adults aged ≥ 65 years with normal nutritional status and who took part as a non-randomized reference group in the Strengthening Health In ELDerly through nutrition (SHIELD) study. Blood samples were analyzed for ALT, aspartate transaminase (AST), albumin, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein, and globulin. Analysis of variance and multiple linear regression models were used to evaluate the desired associations.ResultsMean values of all the liver chemistries were within normal laboratory reference ranges for both sex and age categories. Males had higher ALT, albumin, and total bilirubin than females (all P ≤ 0.023), whereas females had higher ALP and globulin than males (both P ≤ 0.015). Participants aged 65 to < 75 years had higher ALT and lower total bilirubin than those aged ≥ 75 years (both P ≤ 0.020). There was a significant relationship between ALP and intensity of statin therapy (P = 0.023), in which high-intensity statin therapy had significantly higher ALP than low-intensity statin therapy (P = 0.045). However, these values were still within the normal range for ALP.ConclusionsLiver chemistries in community-dwelling older adults with normal nutritional status were within the normal reference range used for adults. There were small but statistically significant differences with age, sex, and intensity of statin therapy. ALT declined with age, and further prospective studies are required to determine cut-offs for increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes.Trial registrationNCT03240952.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06329-2.Keywords: Liver chemistries, Community-dwelling, Older adults, Statin

Background

Healthcare needs are changing as the world population grows, and the proportion of adults over 65 years is predicted to expand toward 1.5 billion older adults (16%) by 2050 [1], with more than 50% of those age 65 and above now residing in Asia [1]. Along with this exponential increase in the older population, the prevalence of many diseases will also increase, e.g., heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes [2]. Health screenings are often the first port to detect these prevalent medical conditions among the older adults. Routine laboratory chemistries are widely used in such health screening, including liver function testing with liver enzymes and other biochemistry [3], which is the focus of our present study.

In assessing liver health, it is vital to differentiate between the ordinary decline in liver function due to aging and specific changes that reflect liver disease or injury. With aging, the volume of liver may decline by 20–40% [4, 5]. Such loss is associated with up to 50% reduced liver blood flow [6] and with aging-related lessening of liver regeneration [7]. These and other physiological changes are marked by changes in liver function that can be detected by liver chemistries. Liver chemistry markers include total protein, globulin, total bilirubin, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) [3].

Liver chemistries can detect liver health or damage, as they are affected by physiological, biological, and medical factors [8]. For example, AST is released at elevated levels from injured liver cells; ALP is released excessively when cholestasis occurs [3]. Total bilirubin reflects hepatobiliary excretion, which can be elevated by infection, chemical toxicity, and some cancers [3]. Other tests evaluate the liver’s synthetic and metabolic capabilities (e.g., albumin) and are thus true assessments of liver function [3]. Biological factors also play a part, as variations occur by age and sex; some experts call for separate reference ranges for children [9], adults and older adults [10], and for men and women [10]. ALT, albumin, and total protein levels are reported as higher in men relative to women, and lower in older adult population compared to younger adults [10–12]. Albumin levels differ among older people, with lower levels associated with lower household incomes [13], poor nutritional status, and loss of muscle mass [14]. Certain liver diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and drug-induced liver injury tend to be more common in older adults due to the increased metabolic risk with aging [7]. Some long-term medications used by older people with medical conditions, e.g., for hyperlipidemia and gout, are recognized for their potential to cause liver toxicity [15, 16].

In recent years, statins, used in the treatment of hyperlipidemia and in patients with cardiovascular disease, have received significant social media and internet coverage, with messaging focused on the risks rather than the benefits [17]. Public awareness and knowledge of statin effects remains poor and possibly influenced by these media outlets [18]. In Singapore, statins are widely prescribed for treatment of hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular disorders, particularly in older people. A local study highlighted concerns that statins may have adverse effects, with up to 70% believing that statins will cause them liver or kidney damage, which naturally would compromise on compliance of these drugs [19]. The efficacy of statin use, particularly in the older population, has been called into question, but meta-analysis has shown statin benefits in reduction of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [20, 21]. However, statin dosing still needs to be individualized with consideration of a risk-benefit ratio.

In older adults, liver chemistry level is likely to vary from the population average due to the physiological changes with aging, multi-morbidities, and resultant increased use of medications. Hence, it is important to examine the liver chemistry profile and associated factors in this cohort. Thus, the objectives of this study were: (i) to determine liver chemistry levels in an Asian population of healthy adults above the age of 65, and (ii) to investigate factors associated with changes in liver chemistry values, including the use of statins.

Methods

Study design and population

The population for this study was part of the Strengthening Health In ELDerly through nutrition (SHIELD) study. A total of 1211 participants took part in the study, which included 400 participants with normal nutritional status (non-randomized reference group) and 811 participants at risk of malnutrition who were enrolled in a randomized controlled trial [22, 23]. The 400 participants in this cross-sectional study came from the non-randomized reference group with normal nutrition and were enrolled between August 2017 and November 2017.

The participant profile was that of community-dwelling males or females aged ≥ 65 years who were ambulant with or without aid. They all had a Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) score of 0, the ability to consume food and beverages orally, and the ability to communicate and follow instructions. Participants with chronic disease were recruited if their conditions were stable, asymptomatic, and well controlled.

The SHIELD study excluded persons with dementia, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, any active infectious disease (such as tuberculosis, Hepatitis B or C, HIV infection), severe gastrointestinal disorders (including but not limited to celiac disease, short bowel syndrome, pancreatic insufficiency, gallbladder disease), cystic fibrosis, end stage organ or pre-terminal diseases, acute myocardial infarction within the last 30 days from the screening, or active malignancy within the last five years. These conditions which are determined based on public healthcare records can affect liver chemistries, nutritional status, dietary requirements, and digestive absorptive abilities.

Study sites

The public healthcare landscape in Singapore is divided into three regional clusters that serve the nation. Each cluster has multiple primary care clinics and a few tertiary hospitals. For this study, the recruitment sites included three primary care clinics and outpatient clinics at a tertiary hospital within one healthcare cluster. Participants were also recruited from the general public, community centers, senior activity centers, and by referrals from healthcare professionals.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board in Singapore reference number 2017/2273. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT03240952.

Questionnaire administration

At the baseline visit, all study participants were asked to complete the following questionnaires and assessments: socio-demographic questionnaire, co-morbidities, malnutrition status, physical activity level, and activities of daily living. Socio-demographic data included age, sex, ethnicity, education, recent hospital admission within the last six months, number of prescribed drugs currently taking, smoking status, and alcohol consumption [24]. The intensity of statin therapy was defined by the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association Task Force practice guidelines on cholesterol treatment [25].

Participant’s comorbidity level was determined using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [26, 27]. Malnutrition status for study eligibility was determined using the MUST questionnaire; MUST consists of three components, i.e., body mass index (BMI), weight loss, and acute diseases that can affect risk of malnutrition due to non-oral intake [28]. Participants’ physical activity levels were determined using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) [29]. Modified Barthel Index (MBI) was used to measure functional independence for ten activities of daily living; dependency level scores ranged from 0 to 20 (totally dependent) up to 100 (totally independent) [30].

Clinical assessment

Blood samples and anthropometric measurements were collected. Blood samples were obtained via venipuncture for the analyses of the liver chemistries, i.e., ALT, AST, albumin, total bilirubin, ALP, total protein, and globulin using chemistry analyzer COBAS c702. Our laboratory is accredited by the College of American Pathologists (CAP) since 2007. We participate in the CAP external quality assurance program (EQAP) to monitor the performance of our tests, including liver chemistries. The clinical chemistry tests (including liver) are under the direction of a medical doctor with specialist certification in chemical pathology from the Royal Australasian College of Pathologists. The reference ranges in our laboratory (including liver) comply with the standards set by the US Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) – EP28A3C 3rd edition 2010 and is derived using the 95th percentile of the local healthy adults aged 20 years and above.

Height was measured without shoes by a stadiometer (Avamech B1000), and the measurement was recorded to the nearest millimeter. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a BIA (Tanita MC-780), which was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by weight in kg divided by height in meters squared.

Data analysis

The initial sample size was calculated for the SHIELD randomized controlled trial [22]. The sample size of 400 participants from the non-randomized reference group studied in this manuscript was adopted to be roughly the same size as each of the intervention and control groups in the randomized controlled trial [22]. This sample size was sufficiently large to allow for meaningful descriptive and inferential statistics.

Baseline characteristics of the study participants were reported as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Two participants were excluded from the statistical analysis of liver function tests due to subsequent discovery of liver and gallbladder pathology.

Analysis of variance was used to compare liver chemistries between sex, age categories, individuals on statin therapy and those without such medication. ANOVA was also used to compare liver chemistries in participants with different intensity of statin therapy, i.e., low, moderate or high. For this analysis, when the overall P value was significant between groups of different intensity of statin therapy, Stepdown Bonferroni test was performed. All correlations with liver chemistries were analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficient.

A multiple linear regression model using adaptive lasso with model averaging selection method choosing Schwarz Bayesian information criterion (SBC) for assessing model fit was used to examine the associations between liver chemistries and all identified potential variables or factors. Thirteen variables that are associated with liver function tests were included as candidate variables in the model, i.e., age, sex, PASE score, MBI score, CCI score, BMI, ethnicity, education, housing type, smoker status, frequency of drinking, hospital admission, and number of prescribed drugs currently taking. Due to their relevance, model selection was made with the forced inclusion of age and gender in the final gender-combined model and age in the by-gender models.

SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Factors associated with liver chemistries

As shown in Table 7, compared to females, males had significantly higher ALT (P = 0.001), albumin (P = 0.015), and total bilirubin (P < 0.001), while significantly lower ALP (P = 0.005). With every 1-year increase in age, ALT decreased by 0.18 U/L (P = 0.026) and total bilirubin increased by 0.09 µmol/L (P = 0.026). ALT was significantly related to MBI score in a positive direction (P = 0.039). Participants with no formal education had significantly higher AST than those studied University and above (P = 0.049). Albumin was inversely related to BMI (P = 0.005). ALP was significantly related to CCI score and BMI; both in a positive direction (both P ≤ 0.049). Participants without hospital admission had significantly lower total protein than those admitted to the hospital (P = 0.001). Of note, only 18 of 400 older adults in this study had hospital admission. Total protein was significantly related to no formal education (in a positive direction) (P = 0.027). Participants who did not smoke cigarettes had significantly higher total protein than those who smoked cigarettes (P = 0.049). Globulin was significantly related to BMI (in a positive direction), and no hospital admissions (in a negative direction) (both P ≤ 0.019).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind to determine the liver chemistries and associated factors in a relatively healthy community-dwelling older adults in Singapore. Our study results showed that liver chemistries for this population were within the normal reference intervals for all adults. We found small but statistically significant differences by age, between sex, and with intensity of statin therapy. We specifically found significant ALT changes with age, where every 1-year increase in age corresponded to an ALT decrease of 0.18 U/L. In older adults on statin therapy, mean values of liver chemistries were within the normal ranges, although we found significantly elevated ALP levels in a subgroup on high-intensity statin therapy compared to those on low-intensity therapy. In the present study, all participants were ambulant and 98% had CCI score of 0, and nearly half were on statins. 83% were of Chinese ethnicity, which is reasonably close to the ethnic distribution in Singapore. Our present findings show normal liver chemistries in community-dwelling older adults with normal nutritional status.

Although the liver is known to regenerate after injury, aspects of liver morphology and liver chemistries do change with aging. Reports are inconsistent regarding whether age-related volume losses are greater in men or women [7, 31]. Regardless of some volume loss, liver chemistries appear to be relatively well preserved in healthy older adults when using current laboratory cut-offs for adults [7]. Our overall findings in the present analysis showed that liver chemistries remained within current ranges considered normal for adults.

We observed age-related differences in ALT and total bilirubin for the population between the ages of 65–75 and above the age of 75. ALT levels were observed to decline with age at a rate of 0.18 U/L per year of age, while total bilirubin increased at a rate of 0.09 µmol/L per year of age. These changes in ALT with age, and this is consistent with reports in the literature [10, 32–35]. This phenomenon is known as the ALT-aging paradox, as it is not what would have been expected with aging. This is because ALT is closely related to and expected to rise with the increasing incidence of metabolic complications that happen with aging, such as obesity, rising blood glucose, elevated triglycerides, lower high-density lipoprotein levels, increasing blood pressure and NAFLD [36–38]. Local studies showed that almost 1 in 2 older adults above age 85 had metabolic syndrome [39], a condition that increases with age among older adults [40], carrying the expectation that ALT would increase with age. The literature suggests that this linear relationship holds true in younger population less than 60 years of age. However, in older population, studies have shown that low ALT is associated with increased mortality and may be an independent marker of increased frailty, sarcopenia, and pyridoxine deficiency, which may indicate the presence of malnutrition [35, 41]. In line with previous studies, we had also found a positive association between ALT and muscle mass (r = 0.193, P < 0.001). There was no association between ALT and fat mass (r = −0.052, P = 0.030).

In our study, age was positively correlated with the rise in total bilirubin levels. Recent research findings have demonstrated unexpected antioxidant and cytoprotective properties with mildly elevated bilirubin levels. The rise in bilirubin levels with age has been postulated to be a result of increased number of older erythrocytes with increased turnover of heme metabolism [42].

Sex-specific differences were observed in our study as well. ALT, albumin, and total bilirubin levels were higher in men, which is likely due to the larger liver volume in men [3, 31]. ALT levels being higher in men is a consistent finding in the literature and is true for across all age groups from adolescence to older adults [3, 9, 32, 43]. Despite the statistical significance of age and sex on ALT levels, the difference in absolute numbers is small, the mean values still lie well within the reference ranges, and the clinical significance is uncertain. Further research is required to understand these relationships.

Albumin is a protein produced exclusively in the liver, with its production stimulated by the action of hormones such as insulin and growth hormone but inhibited by pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and tumor necrosis factor [44]. Albumin levels can be low from states of critical illness, inflammatory states [45], liver disease, or increased renal, gastrointestinal, and extracellular losses, and therefore need not solely reflect problems with liver function. In the present study, all the participants had normal nutritional status, and albumin levels were shown to be higher in males and those with lower BMI in the adaptive lasso regression model. Previous research reported that males had higher albumin levels due to higher albumin fractional synthesis rate, independent of protein intake [46]. In a meta-analysis, Choi et al. found a positive relationship between BMI and C-reactive protein levels [47]. Given that C-reactive protein is a marker of inflammation, and inflammation reduces albumin levels, this may explain the inverse relationship between BMI and albumin levels.

The literature suggests that there is a dose-dependent relationship between statin and elevation in liver chemistries, with the most common biomarkers affected being ALT, AST, ALP, and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) [48, 49]. In the present study, the majority of participants were on low-intensity statin therapy (48%) and only 10% were on high-intensity statin. This is in keeping with the low cardiovascular risk profile of our cohort, whereby 98% of the study participants had a CCI score of 0, which indicated the absence of cardiovascular disease burden (myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular accident, transient ischemic attack) [50]. In addition, diabetes was one of the exclusion criteria for this study.

There was no evidence of abnormal liver chemistries with statin therapy in our participants, although there was a trend of higher mean ALP levels as the intensity of statin increased from low (60 U/L) or moderate (65 U/L) to high (71 U/L). It is important to highlight that these mean levels are within the normal reference range (32 to 103 U/L). This relationship is unexpected, as the more common liver chemistries to rise as a result of statin therapy are ALT and AST [3]. However, a study on adverse reactions due to statin therapy in Sweden reported that the use of atorvastatin can result in isolated rise in ALP, whereas the use of simvastatin is associated with a rise in ALT and AST in patients with idiosyncratic adverse reaction [51]. Despite its statistical significance, the changes in ALP are unlikely to have clinical significance. These findings can help clinicians address misperceptions of statin use in healthy older patients, thus supporting their adherence to statin therapy and improved rates of prescriptions by clinicians.

Although there were differences noted by age, sex, BMI, hospitalization, smoking, and alcohol consumption for the various liver biomarkers, all the values fell well within the normal reference ranges used in a local tertiary hospital laboratory. This suggests that reference ranges for adults used in clinical practice are sufficiently inclusive for a population of healthy older adults. However, given physiological changes with aging [4, 6], different propensity for disease in older adults [7] and different sex susceptibilities [52], different thresholds for disease detection may still be required in old and very old adults. The potential role of low ALT as an independent poor prognostic indicator will require further prospective study to determine the cut-off points, which may indicate adverse health outcomes in older adults.

Our study highlights the importance of contextualizing interpretation of biochemical data and adds to the existing literature on how liver chemistries may vary with age. In view of the multi-ethnic composition of the Singapore population, our study provides important baseline data for the aging Asian population, which makes up more than 50% of all global older adults aged 65 years and above, with this trend expected to continue to 2050 and beyond [1]. A limitation of this cross-sectional study is the small sample size. In addition, due to the nature of the study design, we are only able to establish correlations and not causality. Selection bias may exist as a proportion of participants were recruited from primary care clinics and outpatient clinics. Recall biases may also be present when obtaining information from participants, particularly with regards to subjective data such as exercise, smoking or drinking habits. Our results are representative of a healthy older population and cannot be extrapolated to older adults with advanced chronic diseases, multi-morbidity, or polypharmacy. However, this limitation is also the strength of our study, providing a comparator dataset of what can be expected in terms of liver chemistries in healthy community-dwelling older adults. Further research would be needed to confirm and determine the clinical implications of our findings with regards the ALT-aging paradox in this population group.

Conclusion

The main clinical findings in our study were (i) older community-dwelling Singaporeans who are ambulant with normal nutritional status have liver chemistries which are within the 95th percentile for healthy adults in Singapore, (ii) there is an age-related decline of serum ALT, (iii) older individuals who were taking statins did not show evidence of liver chemistries outside of normal ranges, and (iv) higher intensity statin use was associated with a trend towards higher ALP levels, which remains within the normal limits for adults.

Further longitudinal studies will be required to clarify the clinical implications of our findings as low ALT levels may potentially serve as an important independent marker for malnutrition, sarcopenia, frailty and mortality risk in older adults, and elevated levels of ALP in older adults initiated on high-intensity statin therapy may be a signal to the clinician to monitor the patient closely for any further changes in liver chemistries, particularly if dose escalation or high-intensity statin therapy is being considered.
