Hand gesture 3D pose estimation method based on swin transformer and CNN
Authors: Rong Dang, Gang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Existing gesture pose estimation methods commonly exhibit limitations such as singular feature extraction for hand characteristics and the neglect of long-range topological relationships between joints, thus restricting their prediction accuracy. To address these issues, this study proposes a gesture pose estimation method that takes depth images as input. First, rough gesture features are extracted using a convolutional network, while a Swin Transformer module captures the topological relationships between joints and global features through spatial information. A U-shaped network is used to process the features hierarchically, preserving local joint information at various resolutions, which is fused with global features. Two-dimensional Gaussian heatmaps are introduced to represent the distribution of keypoints, improving network supervision for target feature regression. The backend network outputs the final keypoint coordinates. We evaluated the method on a newly constructed dataset, achieving a reduced average squared error of 7.012–4.776 mm lower than that of the baseline model. The experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed method, when comparing it to state-of-the-art mainstream pose estimation networks.

Introduction

Natural and intuitive gesture-based interaction is a essential approach for enhancing human–computer interaction experiences, with broad applications in areas such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), smart cockpits, sign language recognition, and remote collaboration1–4. Hand pose estimation refers to the process of recovering the three-dimensional spatial positions of hand joints from input visual data, such as RGB images or depth maps. Specifically, the goal of pose estimation is to obtain accurate information about the position and orientation of the hand using computer vision methods, which is essential for natural human–computer interaction5. For example, by employing hand pose estimation, users can interact with objects in virtual environments through gestures or perform tasks such as sign language recognition6. The key to enabling these applications lies in robust and accurate hand pose estimation techniques, whose objective is to recover the 3D coordinates of predefined hand keypoints from input visual data (e.g., RGB images and depth maps). Such techniques allow users to interact with computers and devices in a natural and intuitive manner, thereby achieving a more intelligent, convenient, and user-friendly interaction experience7.

For example, Oikonomidis et al. proposed a multi-view method that employs a particle swarm optimization algorithm to minimize the discrepancy between projected 2D hand model features and those extracted from input sequences8. This approach effectively incorporates physical constraints as critical reference information, significantly improving estimation accuracy under occluded background conditions. Furthermore, with the remarkable success of deep learning in human–computer interaction, Tompson et al.applied CNNs to hand pose estimation9. By generating 2D heatmaps through convolutional neural networks and leveraging inverse kinematics, they predicted the coordinates of hand joints. Ye et al. further introduced a hand pose estimation method based on a kinematic hierarchical strategy10. Their approach integrates the proposed strategy into the input space of a spatial attention-based discriminative method and the optimization process of a hierarchical particle swarm optimization-based generative method, thereby improving the accuracy of pose calibration to some extent.

Although significant progress has been made in the field of Hand pose estimation, existing methods still face several common challenges: (1) the extracted features are often limited in diversity and lack effective fusion of multi-scale gesture information, ranging from global structures to local details; and (2) the intrinsic spatial topology of the hand, particularly the long-range dependencies between non-adjacent joints (e.g., collaborative motion constraints among fingers), is not fully exploited. These issues directly hinder further improvement in pose estimation accuracy.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes a hand pose estimation method based on Swin transformer and CNN, which aims to simultaneously overcome the limitations of single-feature extraction and the neglect of topological dependencies. Related work

At present, from the perspective of gesture modeling, hand pose estimation methods can be categorized into three types: generative model-based methods, discriminative model-based methods, and hybrid model-based methods12.

Generative model-based estimation methods typically use optimization algorithms to combine the motion trajectory of the hand with predictions from a dynamics perspective, followed by the extraction of inputs, integrating shape features of the model to minimize matching errors and obtain precise pose parameters13. Qian et al. improved this method by constructing a geometric model of the hand14. They adopted the iterative closest point (ICP) model to accelerate convergence through local optimization and utilized the particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm for stochastic optimization. By expanding the search in the parameter space, they effectively avoided becoming trapped in invalid local optima. Zhang et al. employed a multi-task learning framework to simultaneously estimate a three-dimensional (3D) hand pose, a hand mask, and a mesh-based anthropomorphic hand outline (MANO) mesh15. Chen et al. proposed I2UVHandNet, which unfolds the low-resolution MANO 3D hand mesh into a 2D UV plane, enhances its resolution through a super-resolution network layer, and then reconstructs it into a high-resolution MANO mesh16. However, such generative methods require the construction of intricate hand models and combination of deep network models to find the most similar pose parameters within a consistent time frame, making real-time performance a challenge.

On the other hand, the core idea of discriminative-based estimation methods is to directly infer the positions or poses of hand joints from single-frame images or depth data using machine learning or deep learning models.These methods do not rely on predefined hand models or templates, instead learning the mapping relationship from input to output directly via training on large amounts of annotated data. Ge et al.17 proposed an end-to-end trainable hand mesh generation method using graph convolutional networks. Zhang et al. proposed an end-to-end hand pose estimation method consisting of three parts: the first part estimates the 2D heatmaps of hand joints, the second part iteratively regresses the hand model parameters using these heatmaps as input, and the third part generates the corresponding hand model based on these parameters18. To address the challenge of balancing accuracy and efficiency in hand pose estimation, Fan et al. introduced the adaptive computationally efficient (ACE) network, which dynamically switches between lightweight and complex networks using a Gaussian kernel-based gating module19. Pavlakos et al. presented HaMeR (hand mesh recovery), a method that achieves 3D hand reconstruction from a monocular input. In particular, it employs a Transformer-based architecture that significantly enhances the accuracy and robustness of hand analysis compared to existing technologies20.

Rezaei21 introduced TriHorn-Net, a novel model that decouples the 3D hand pose estimation task into two sub-tasks—namely, estimating the 2D joint positions (UV coordinates) in the depth image space and estimating the corresponding depth values aided by two complementary attention maps—significantly improving the accuracy of hand pose estimation based on depth images. Wen et al.22 developed a Transformer-based temporal modeling framework, constructing a cascaded dual Transformer encoder hierarchical network that utilizes short-term temporal cues for effective hand pose estimation in the first encoder and integrates per-frame pose information with object interaction features for the recognition of actions over longer time spans.

However, as only 2D positions are predicted, the performance of such methods in scenarios with joint occlusion is sub-optimal. Hybrid-based estimation methods primarily treat the constructed hand model as a prerequisite for generating hand coordinates or as a discriminative condition for regression targets. For example, Keskin et al.23 conducted experiments on synthetic depth images, attempting to use a local mode search algorithm to predict hand joint coordinates from different results. Sridhar et al.24 introduced an innovative detection-guided optimization strategy that enhances the robustness and speed of pose estimation. Later, Sridhar et al.25 presented a 3D joint Gaussian mixture alignment strategy for hand pose estimation that is suitable for rapid pose optimization, employing a regularizer to address occlusion issues arising from hand–object contact. Panteleris et al.26 proposed a three-step approach for hand pose estimation: first, an existing and effective hand detector is used to localize and segment the hand; second, the 2D positions of hand joints are estimated using a pre-trained OpenPose discriminative network model; finally, the 2D pose is fitted to a 3D pose using nonlinear least squares.

Although existing methods have made progress, they generally suffer from limited feature representation and insufficient global topology modeling. The method proposed in this paper, which integrates Swin transformer with CNN, is designed to address these shortcomings.

Methods

The overall design of the proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1, and the model design involves multiple steps for gesture feature extraction. First, the depth image of the hand region is input into a convolutional feature extraction network, where rough gesture features are extracted through a 7 × 7 convolution layer and further optimized using a pooling unit and bottleneck residual modules. Next, the features are processed in parallel through two branches: one branch employs a U-Net to obtain local joint features, while the other branch uses a Swin Transformer encoder to capture global features. The output feature maps from both branches are fused, and a Gaussian heatmap containing joint distribution information is introduced to supervise and correct the extracted features. This process is repeated twice to further refine the extracted gesture features, ultimately resulting in accurate three-dimensional joint coordinates.Figure 1The overall gesture estimation network structure.

Global topological information extraction

To better understand the relationships between hand joints in the task of hand pose estimation (i.e., their relative positions and connections in space), this study introduces a Swin Transformer to extract the topological information of hand joints. Compared to the traditional Vision Transformer mechanism, the Swin Transformer incorporates a window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA), which divides the input features into smaller patches. Through the use of multi-level attention mechanisms and cross-layer fusion, the model can more efficiently process long-range dependency information, integrating local hand feature information captured at different levels into a global representation. This structure also reduces the computational complexity, to a certain extent. The specific architecture of the Swin Transformer encoder is illustrated in Fig. 2.Figure 2Design of the swin transformer encoder–decoder structure.

Design of the swin transformer encoder–decoder structure.

Figure 2 illustrates the specific structure of the Swin Transformer encoder. The first part is the W-MSA, while the second part is the shifted window-based multi-head self-attention (SW-MSA). Each component is composed of a layer normalization layer, an attention module, and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The overall structure includes an input section, a patch partition block, and four stages for extracting hand gesture features. The Swin Transformer effectively captures the long-range dependencies among hand joints through its windowed multi-head self-attention mechanism. Input features are divided into smaller blocks (windows), with self-attention calculated within each window, significantly reducing computational costs. The shifted window mechanism allows for the transfer of information across layers, facilitating the effective fusion of hand joint features at different scales. This multi-scale attention is crucial for accurate gesture pose estimation. The specific process is outlined as follows:

First, a feature map of size 48 × 48 is fed into the Swin Transformer Block, with the window size set to 4 × 4. At this point, the image is divided into 12 × 12 windows and self-attention computation is performed within each window, converting the regional pixels into one-dimensional vectors to be used as the effective input sequences. Then, residual connections are introduced through the MLP for linear embedding, aiming to project the features according to their dimensions. As shown in Fig. 2, after passing through the W-MSA in the block, the resulting hand feature map undergoes another SW-MSA structure, where the window positions are shifted by 2 pixels in both the x and y directions to enable the exchange of information between different windows.

The remaining three stages adjust the size and channel count of the feature map through patch merging operations, while the Swin Transformer block module performs a series of linear transformations and attention mechanism operations. Each stage modifies the tensor dimensions to capture hand features at different levels, aiding the model in globally understanding the feature information. Finally, the output is processed through a layer normalization layer to maintain consistency in the feature distribution and obtain the corresponding results. In standard Transformers, the computational complexity of the self-attention mechanism grows quadratically with increasing sequence length. For images, directly treating individual pixels as a sequence can lead to a significant computational burden. The Swin Transformer mitigates this issue by partitioning the image into multiple non-overlapping local windows and calculating the self-attention within these windows independently, which significantly reduces computational costs.

To facilitate information exchange between different windows and capture a broader context, the shifted window mechanism is introduced. Between successive layers, the positions of the windows shift, resulting in overlapping portions between adjacent layers. This design not only maintains a lower computational load but also allows for the effective integration of information across windows. Following these principles, the Swin Transformer captures subtle features and spatial dependencies in gesture pose estimation images through block-based processing, self-attention mechanisms, and multi-scale hierarchical modeling, providing powerful modeling capabilities for gesture pose estimation.

Local joint feature information aggregation

In some image processing tasks, neural networks are stacked to gradually extract feature information. In shallow networks, the model primarily learns low-level features. As the network deepens, the information learned by the network becomes more abstract as the resolution of the feature maps decreases. However, for tasks such as hand pose estimation, which require the extraction of fine-grained features, the key information for different joints may exist in feature maps of varying resolutions. Therefore, by aggregating these diverse features distributed across different resolutions, more accurate information can be captured to guide the network’s learning process. Inspired by the hourglass network proposed by Newell’s team27–29, this study designs a U-shaped encoder–decoder to capture multi-scale feature information from feature maps with different resolutions through progressive down-sampling and up-sampling operations. Figure 3 illustrates the structure of the U-shaped encoder–decoder.Figure 3U-shaped network structure design.

U-shaped network structure design.

Figure 3 illustrates the structural design of the U-Net, which extracts and aggregates multi-scale features from feature maps of varying resolutions through downsampling and upsampling operations. Among the components of this network, B-RE represents the bottleneck residual structure, POOL represents the max pooling structure, and UP represents the up-sampling structure. The introduction of the bottleneck residual module and pooling layer enables the network to learn detailed features of the gestures at different scales. The upsampling phase restores the resolution of the feature maps and combines low-level gesture features at the same resolution with high-level features resulting from residual processing via skip connections. This multi-resolution feature aggregation process is crucial for accurate gesture pose estimation.

In the top-down process, the bottleneck residual structure and the max pooling structure appear in groups. The network starts from a high-resolution feature map and reduces the resolution by a factor of 2. At each step, the detailed information of the features is enhanced and the number of feature channels doubles accordingly. In the bottom-up process, the bottleneck residual structure and the up-sampling structure appear in groups. In this part, the output result of each previous layer needs to be up-sampled by a factor of 2, with the aim of restoring the original resolution of the feature map. In this step, it is also necessary to add—through a skip connection—the low-level gesture features processed by the B-RES module at the same resolution.

At the same time, the network starts from a low-resolution feature map and increases exponentially. The number of feature channels also decreases by a factor of 2 until it finally returns to the original state. At this point, the obtained features are the gesture feature information aggregated from four different resolutions. The up-sampling method adopted in this study is the bilinear interpolation method. Through considering the weights of the four adjacent pixels, the value of the target pixel is estimated and each pixel in the input feature map is interpolated to generate a feature map with a larger size, thus achieving smooth up-sampling of the image and restoring the original information of the feature map to a large extent.

The bottleneck residual module (B-RES) is a type of module used for constructing deep neural networks in the field of deep learning, which is an improvement on the basis of the traditional residual network (ResNet) structure. By adding a bottleneck layer, it introduces more depth and nonlinear transformations into the network, enabling the network to better adapt for the extraction of multi-scale features and endowing it with greater flexibility. In the U-shaped network, the number of feature channels also exhibits a U-shaped trend, increasing first and then decreasing, and the obtained features also differ. Introducing the bottleneck residual structure helps the network to better transmit residual information: it not only preserves and transmits global and local information, but also captures and aggregates the hand feature information at different scales. This structure can also reduce the computational complexity to a certain extent, making the network lighter and more suitable for practical deployment.

Regarding multi-resolution feature maps, the bottleneck residual module can play a more effective role in gesture feature extraction when compared to the ordinary residual module. A comparison between the bottleneck residual and traditional structures is shown in Fig. 4, where Conv represents the convolution operation constructed through 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and 1 × 1 convolution kernels. This effectively reduces the number of parameters in the network, making the model lighter and more capable of handling multi-scale features. The bottleneck residual structure reduces the number of input feature channels in the first 1 × 1 convolutional layer, then conducts more in-depth feature extraction in the second 3 × 3 convolutional layer, and finally restores the depth of the features in the third 1 × 1 convolutional layer.

Therefore, it can extract richer and more complex feature information while maintaining the size of the feature map. In addition, when considering multi-resolution feature maps, the effect of gradient propagation is particularly important. When extracting features at different scales, the stable propagation of gradients can help the network to better learn the feature representations at different scales. The bottleneck residual structure directly transmits the input to the output end through the residual connection, which helps to alleviate the problem of gradient vanishing and makes the network easier to train.Figure 4Comparison of residual structures.

Gesture heatmap supervision

In the task of gesture pose estimation, the three-dimensional information representing the positions of the key points of the hand joints ultimately needs to be obtained through the established model. During this process, a fully differentiable model is often used for training. The error between the input data and the target value can be directly utilized to optimize the model parameters, which simplifies the training process and improves the training efficiency. Due to this end-to-end weight update mode, the forward and backward propagation speeds are relatively fast. However, especially for tasks such as gesture pose estimation, there may be significant differences in the gesture shapes and postures between different users and in different environments. Therefore, the lack of spatial generalization ability may cause the model to perform poorly in practical applications.

Inspired by reference30, in this study, a two-dimensional Gaussian distribution centered on the positions of the points representing the hand gesture joints is generated. The variance of the Gaussian distribution is usually controlled using pre-set parameters. The pixel with the highest intensity value indicates the position of the key point, and the intensity values of the surrounding pixels gradually decrease. By superimposing the Gaussian distributions generated for all key points, the heatmap value for each pixel can be obtained. Therefore, by finding the position with the highest confidence value in the heatmap, the key points in the hand gesture can be more accurately located. At the same time, the topological connections between the joint points are also retained according to the peaks in the distribution. In this way, the model can process multiple key points simultaneously, enabling the model to better understand the structure and dynamics of the hand gesture and make more accurate predictions.Figure 5Example of heatmap-based supervision module operation.

Example of heatmap-based supervision module operation.

The structural design of the heatmap supervision module is illustrated in Fig. 5. The input is a two-dimensional matrix containing the distribution information of keypoints, where the intensity value of each pixel represents the confidence of the keypoint. The role of the convolutional kernel is to slide over the keypoint heatmap, using a 1 × 1 convolution to increase the number of input feature channels to 256. The learned weights are then used to identify and capture the patterns and features of the gesture keypoints. Subsequently, a 3 × 3 convolution operation processes the features obtained through the 1 × 1 convolution, outputting a Gaussian heatmap of size 24 × 24 with a number of channels corresponding to the number of gesture keypoints.

This extracts the patterns and features of the gesture keypoints from the input features. Datasets

The experimental dataset used in this study comprises two parts, as illustrated in Fig. 6. On the right side is the MSRA dataset, which was constructed by Microsoft Research Asia. It includes 76,375 depth images generated from nine subjects presenting diverse gestures under varying viewpoints and lighting conditions, with each image accurately annotated for the 3D coordinates of 21 hand joints.Figure 6Examples from the LiDAR and MSRA datasets.

Examples from the LiDAR and MSRA datasets.

The scientific design of this dataset is evident in its rigorous collection protocol: all gesture actions encompass typical postures used in daily interactions, including gripping, pinching, and spreading, while accounting for variations in hand size and skin color to ensure the representativeness and diversity of the data. As shown in the table below, the MSRA dataset is the preferred benchmark for validating the performance of 3D hand pose estimation algorithms due to its real-world collection environment (non-synthetic data), high-precision annotations (sub-millimeter accuracy), and extensive number of samples.

Conclusions

This study addresses common challenges in hand 3D pose estimation, such as the limited feature extraction and insufficient modeling of joint topological relationships. We propose a novel method for hand gesture pose estimation based on Swin Transformer and CNN. The method uses depth images of the hand region as input and utilizes Swin Transformer and CNN to capture global features and local joint features, respectively. After feature fusion, a two-dimensional Gaussian heatmap supervision is applied to refine the feature extraction process, significantly improving its accuracy and robustness.

Experimental results show that our method outperforms existing mainstream depth-based hand gesture pose estimation algorithms on both our custom dataset and the MSRA dataset. Particularly in more challenging scenarios, where finger features are less distinct or the background is more complex, our method demonstrates strong robustness and accuracy. Compared to traditional hand pose estimation methods, our approach effectively addresses the issue of insufficient modeling of long-range topological relationships between hand joints, significantly enhancing the model’s adaptability across various application scenarios.

Although the method performs well in single-hand scenarios, it still faces limitations in complex environments involving hand-object interactions and severe occlusions. Future research will focus on improving algorithm performance in interactive gestures and hand-object occlusion scenarios, further strengthening the model’s robustness and real-time adaptability to address more complex interactive tasks.
