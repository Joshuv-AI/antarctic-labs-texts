Viral hepatitis co-infections with tuberculosis in India: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Arohi Chauhan, Abhinav Sinha, Yogesh Patel, Pankaj Nimavat, Sandeep Chauhan, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesTuberculosis (TB) and viral hepatitis (hepatitis B and C) co-infection is a significant threat in low-and middle-income countries like India. To optimise treatment protocols and prevent liver-related complications, it is essential to estimate the burden of these co-infections. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to determine the prevalence of hepatitis B and C co-infection with tuberculosis in India.MethodsIn line with PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL, and Web of Science was conducted for English-language observational studies published up to January 6, 2026, that reported hepatitis B or C co-infection among TB patients in India. Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using a random-effects model, heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and risk of bias was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool.ResultsA total of 11 studies with 4502 participants reporting hepatitis B or C co-infections in among TB patients in India were included. The pooled prevalence of hepatitis B was 10% [95% Confidence Interval (CI):5%–16%], while the pooled prevalence of hepatitis C was 6% (95% CI:5%–7%). Among TB patients co-infected with HIV, the pooled prevalence of hepatitis B was 17% and pooled prevalence of hepatitis B among drug-resistant TB patients was 11%. Higher rates were reported in Southern and Western India.Interpretation and conclusionsHigh prevalence of hepatitis B and C co-infections among TB patients in India suggests the need for integrated screening and management of these co-infections within the national programme strategies.

Tuberculosis (TB), and viral hepatitis are major infectious disease of public health importance in India.1 India alone contributes to more than one-fourth of the global TB burden.2 Whereas the prevalence of hepatitis B among general population ranges from 0.87%-21.4%, and hepatitis C prevalence varies from 0.57 to 53.7% in India.3 Together, these infections represent a dual burden with significant implications for healthcare system.

TB-hepatitis co-infection presents complexities in clinical as well as programmatic management.4 Chronic hepatitis B and C infections predisposes individuals to hepatic dysfunction, increasing the susceptibility to drug-induced liver injury (DILI) associated with first-line anti-TB treatment, notably isoniazid and rifampicin.5 Additionally, hepatotoxicity may lead to treatment interruption, modification, or discontinuation, jeopardising treatment adherence, completion, and outcomes.6 Beyond individual-level clinical challenges, TB-hepatitis co-infection also imposes additional burden on already strained healthcare system.7 Co-infection necessitates continuous monitoring of liver function, hepatitis-specific diagnostics and intensified clinical supervision throughout TB treatment.7 In the absence of standardised, integrated care pathways, these requirements can cause fragmented service delivery, delayed diagnosis and sub-optimal patient outcomes posing a serious threat to national TB elimination efforts.8,9

Despite the evident challenges of TB-hepatitis co-infection, country-specific data from India remain sparse. A recent meta-analysis by Olaru et al5 reported pooled prevalence of hepatitis B as 4.6% (2.2% - 7.7%) and hepatitis C as 7.6% (3.2%–13.6%) among TB patients in the South-East Asia Region. The review did not distinguish between pre-existing chronic viral hepatitis co-infection at TB diagnosis and DILI, two conditions with distinct etiologies, diagnostic approaches, and management pathways. This conflation limits the interpretability of pooled estimates and their application to national policy. Given India’s substantial share of the global TB and hepatitis burden, and the need for integrated, context-specific strategies, there is a critical need to generate accurate, India-specific estimates focused exclusively on chronic hepatitis B and C co-infection in TB patients5,6 To address this gap, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to provide the first comprehensive estimate of hepatitis B and C prevalence among TB patients in India, in order to inform national TB and hepatitis programmes on the need for integrated screening, treatment coordination, and co-management strategies.

Methods

Protocol and inclusion criteria

A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guideline for reporting. It was registered in the International Register of Systematic Reviews [(PROSPERO) CRD42024595951]. The PRISMA checklist is available as Supplementary Table I.

Supplementary Table I

Primary studies that evaluated TB patients and assessed the prevalence or proportion of hepatitis B or C infections (present at or prior to TB diagnosis or starting therapy), irrespective of the diagnostic tests employed, were included. Studies in which inclusion was conditioned on treatment-emergent liver outcomes (e.g., drug-induced liver injury) without reporting baseline hepatitis B and/or C prevalence for the entire TB cohort were excluded. Both observational cohort studies and cross-sectional studies were eligible. Reviews, commentaries, case reports, perspectives, opinion articles, study protocols, editorials, theses, conference abstracts and studies not reporting specific data on the co-infection rates were excluded. Studies involving paediatric populations were also excluded unless they provided separate data for adults. The PICO table can be found in Supplementary Table II.

Supplementary Table II

TB was defined as a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb).10 Hepatitis B and Hepatitis C were defined as viral infections caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV), respectively, which primarily impact the liver and can lead to both acute and chronic conditions.5

Databases, study selection, and data extraction

Five electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL and Embase, were searched to identify studies published from January 1-6th, 2026. Keywords and MeSH terms related to ‘tuberculosis,’ ‘hepatitis B,’ ‘hepatitis C,’ and ‘India’ were utilised in the searches. Peer-reviewed records published in English and conducted in India were included, with no restrictions on the settings. An expert in medical information retrieval reviewed the search strategy. The detailed search strategies as per the databases are available in the Supplementary Table III. Two authors (SC and AS) independently conducted the searches across the specified databases and screened the titles as well as abstracts of the retrieved studies to determine eligibility, with a third reviewer (AC) resolving any uncertainties or disagreements. The same two researchers also carried out the full-text screening, with conflicts resolved by a third reviewer (AC). Additional literature was identified through hand searching and reviewing citations and reference lists. All citations retrieved were initially imported into EndNote to eliminate duplicate records. The refined entries were then transferred to Covidence software for further processing.

Supplementary Table III

A standardised form was used to extract the data. Information such as authors, country, study design, publication year, sample size, study area, and the proportion or prevalence of hepatitis B or C, along with the diagnostic tests employed was retrieved. Where studies reported both hepatitis B and C prevalence in the same cohort, data were extracted separately for each outcome. When data were incomplete, missing, or the full text unavailable, corresponding authors were contacted via email to obtain the required information.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers (AS and SC) evaluated the methodological quality and risk of bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tools, with third reviewer (AC) resolving any discrepancy.11,12 JBI is a specialised questionnaire, design for evaluating systematic reviews with cohort, cross-sectional and randomised controlled trial study designs.11,12 Studies were classified as having ‘low’, ‘moderate’, or ‘high’ risk of bias as per the scores. Majority of the studies were found to have a low risk of bias, with a JBI score above 70%. Two studies were identified as having a moderate risk of bias, with a score between 50 and 69%. No studies were excluded based on the quality assessment outcomes (Supplementary Tables IV-VI).

Data synthesis and analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarised using either standard deviations or medians. The pooled prevalence rates of hepatitis B or C were calculated using STATA, with a 95% confidence interval (CI). For studies that reported both hepatitis B and C prevalence within the same TB cohort, the data were included as separate prevalence estimates in their respective meta-analyses. A random effects model was used using MetaXL (MS Excel) software, and effect sizes were reported as odds ratios, for dichotomous data. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q statistic and the I2 statistic. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of pooled prevalence estimates and to explore sources of heterogeneity. For hepatitis B, we repeated the meta-analysis after excluding studies with moderate risk of bias based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) assessment, a stricter threshold of ≥80% was applied to enhance methodological rigor and assess the robustness of the pooled estimate (Supplementary Tables IV-VI). The sensitivity analysis was restricted to studies reporting chronic hepatitis B infection resulted in a reduced set of studies with improved methodological homogeneity. Pooled prevalence estimates and heterogeneity statistics (Q and I2) were recalculated using the same random-effects model. Due to low heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was not performed for hepatitis C.

Results

A total of 1359 records were identified, after removing 252 duplicates, and screening titles and abstracts, 117 full texts were reviewed. Eleven studies met inclusion criteria, with three additional studies identified via cross-referencing. Of the 100 excluded full-texts, 59% were excluded due to non-relevant study population, 26% due to irrelevant population and 15% due to inappropriate study design. Two records were excluded as they were published before 2006 ( Fig. 1).

Amongst them, the majority (n=5) were conducted in northern India, followed by western India (n=1), central India (n=1), and southern India (n=2). Pooled prevalence of hepatitis B among TB patients

The pooled prevalence of hepatitis B among TB patients was 10% (95% CI:5%,16%;Q=142.8, P<0.001, I2=94%), with substantial heterogeneity ( Fig. 2). After excluding studies with moderate to high risk of bias, the sensitivity analysis ( Fig. 3) yielded slightly higher prevalence of 16% (95% CI:14%,18%) with zero percent heterogeneity (Q=3.84, I2=0%, P=0.43). Specifically, among pulmonary TB patients, the prevalence was 15% (95% CI:7%,24%; Q=212.1, P<0.001, I2=97%), while 10% (95% CI:3%,22%; Q=4.32, P=0.04, I2=77%) for extrapulmonary TB. Among HIV-TB co-infected patients, the prevalence was 17% (95% CI:10%,27%, Q=6.52, P=0.09, I2=54%) and 11% (95% CI:6%,16%; Q=0.08, P=0.78, I2=0%) among drug-resistant TB patients.

Regionally, the highest prevalence was observed in western India (24%, 95% CI:6%, 48%; Q=16.78, P<0.001, I2=94%), followed by southern India (15%,95% CI:10%, 21%; Q=1.77, P=0.18, I2=44%). And northern India (10%, 95% CI:4%, 17%; Q=10.11, P=0.02, I2=70%). For eastern and central India, only one study each reported data (Nayak et al,22 reported it as 11.11% and Hussain et al,19 as 2.96%, respectively), limiting the ability to derive a pooled prevalence for that region. When stratified by study design, the pooled prevalence of hepatitis B was 10% for cross-sectional studies (n=6), and 6% for case-control studies (n=2), though heterogeneity remained higher in the former ( Table II).

Pooled prevalence of hepatitis C amongst TB patients

Based on data from five studies, the pooled prevalence of hepatitis C among TB patients was 6% (95% CI:5%,7%; Q=3.13, P=0.5, I2=0%), indicating low heterogeneity and consistency across the included studies ( Fig. 4). Further, based on the availability of the data, only one study (Bansal et al14) reported a prevalence of 0.26% for triple infection of hepatitis-C, TB and HIV. For hepatitis C, the pooled prevalence among cross-sectional studies was 6% (95% CI:5%–7%) with low heterogeneity (I2=0%) ( Table II).

Publication bias

The Doi plot, with an LFK index of -0.69 (no asymmetry), suggests no significant publication bias, aligning with the findings from the sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated consistent and robust results with a pooled hepatitis B prevalence of 16% and zero heterogeneity (I2=0%) whereas for hepatitis C, I2=0% with zero heterogeneity.25

Discussion

Chronic infectious diseases like TB, hepatitis B and C, remain major public health concerns in India. This review reported a 10% and 6% prevalence of hepatitis B and C among TB patients, underscoring a substantial co-infection burden. Notably, hepatitis B prevalence was 17% among TB-HIV co-infected individuals, highlighting the compounded risk within ‘syndemic infectious complex’ (SIC).

Compared to the global estimates for South-East Asia region reported by Olaru et al5 (4.6% for TB and 5.8% for drug-resistant TB), our findings suggest a higher prevalence of hepatitis B, 14% among all TB cases and 11% among drug-resistant TB patients.5 This elevated burden may reflect a true epidemiological difference, including the influence of co-factors such as HIV, enhancing susceptibility to both co-infections and better case detection through targeted screening.5 Lower estimates may also be due to inclusion of studies that did not distinguish between baseline hepatitis B co-infection and hepatitis emerging as DILI.5 This prevalence of hepatitis B among TB patients exceeds that reported among general Indian population (3.7%), reinforcing the need for systematic screening among TB patients.18 Co-infection also increases the risk of drug interactions and hepatotoxicity.7 This concern is amplified in drug-resistant TB, where regimens include hepatotoxic drugs.26 Emerging evidence also suggests better liver outcomes in co-infected patients receiving hepatitis B treatment, supporting integration of hepatitis B screening and management within TB care to improve outcomes, and reduce co-infection related complications and support broader disease elimination efforts.5,7,27

Sensitivity analysis, which excluded lower-quality studies and those using non-HBsAg diagnostics, yielded a slightly higher pooled prevalence of 16% with zero heterogeneity (0%), affirming the robustness of findings. Higher prevalence was reported from western and southern India, aligning with regional differences in hepatitis B endemicity documented in national surveillance data.3 Heterogeneity in the primary analysis (I2 = 96%) was largely attributable to study design, TB subtype, and sample size variability, suggesting the need for standardised study designs.

This review found 6% prevalence of hepatitis C among TB patients, higher than the general Indian population (1%), suggesting the need of routine hepatitis C screening among those affected by TB, irrespective of the spectrum of disease.18,28 While our review did not provide the disaggregated data on hepatitis C prevalence specifically among the drug-resistant TB patients due to unavailability of data, treating hepatitis C among drug-resistant TB improves treatment success rates, reduces treatment failure and loss to follow up, and may slightly lower mortality.29 This insights support the case for integrated screening and management of hepatitis C within TB programme, particularly in high-risk or co-infected groups.5,7,28

A high prevalence (17%) of hepatitis B was observed among TB patients with HIV co-infection, reflecting the syndemic nature of these infections. This syndemic infectious complex (SIC), where TB, hepatitis B and HIV wherein these diseases synergistically worsen disease trajectories, increase vulnerability due to exacerbated social inequities, heightens treatment complexity, risks of hepatotoxicity and poor clinical outcomes.30,31 Further, higher risk of hepatic damage from anti-TB treatment and antiretroviral treatment (ART) challenge management within SIC.32 Disease burden of hepatitis B and C is further amplified by the injectable drug use and HIV increasing the vulnerability to poorer outcomes and higher mortality.33 While the impact of hepatitis B is clearer, data on triple co-infection with hepatitis C remain limited in India, indicating a need for further longitudinal research on disease interplay and outcomes.

Despite the limited number of eligible studies, the findings reflect diverse geographic regions, improving generalisability. Key limitations include lack of data on socio-demographics, liver disease severity, and treatment outcomes, as well as absence of cost-effectiveness evaluations. Second, the small number of studies in certain subgroups may have introduced heterogeneity in the findings.

The review highlights a higher prevalence of hepatitis B and C among TB patients compared to the general population, with a notably greater burden among those with HIV and drug-resistant TB indicating a syndemic overlap. These findings support integrating routine hepatitis B and C screening into baseline TB care, alongside systematic liver function monitoring, and tailored treatment approaches. Strengthening diagnostic platforms, surveillance systems, and programmatic planning will be crucial to address this syndemic and align with India’s TB elimination goals.
