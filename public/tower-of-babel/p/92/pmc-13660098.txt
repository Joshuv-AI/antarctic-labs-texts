A short-term randomized clinical trial testing feasibility of and physiological responses to a Ruminococcus torques strain in healthy overweight humans
Authors: Joachim Gæde, Yong Fan, Liwei Lyu, Evelina Stankevic, Lærke Smidt Gasbjerg, Peter Rossing, Matej Oresic, Tuulia Hyötyläinen, Susanne Brix, Asger B. Lund, Dorte Aalund Olsen, Eva Rabing Brix Petersen, Ivan Brandslund, Bolette Hartmann, Jens Juul Holst, Filip Krag Knop, Oluf Pedersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe human gut microbiota interacts with host biology as exemplified by the effects of the Ruminococcus torques (RT) ATCC 27756 strain—a commensal human intestinal bacterial strain that synthesizes the polypeptides RORDEP1 and RORDEP2 which in rodents improve metabolism. Motivated by the metabolic effects of the RT strain in the rodent host, we explored in a short-term double blind and placebo-controlled randomized cross-over trial the feasibility and potential physiological responses of the same bacterial strain in humans.MethodsThe trial undertaken at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital, Denmark included 32 healthy overweight adults. Decided by block randomization with blocks of six, we infused either 3.1 × 1011 colony forming units of the live RT ATCC 27756 strain or placebo into the duodenum during an observation period of six hours including a two-hour oral glucose tolerance test. Insulin sensitivity measured as Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index was the primary endpoint.ResultsThe infusion of the bacterium was safe and well-tolerated. We found no effects on the primary endpoint of the trial. Compared to placebo, short-term RT infusion induced a relative rise in plasma concentrations of glucagon-like-peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY) in parallel with a relative decline in gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP). These intestinal hormone responses mirrored those previously reported from studies in rats. The abundance of secondary bile acids in plasma as well as a plasma marker of bone remodeling increased after infusion of RT compared to placebo. Measures of glucose tolerance, energy expenditure, cutaneous thermography, and main markers of systemic low-grade inflammation remained unchanged.ConclusionsDuodenal infusion during six hours of the RT ATCC 27756 strain in healthy overweight humans shows that the bacterial strain is well tolerated, and the short-term effects on intestinal hormone release align with those previously reported in rodents.Trial registrationThe trial is registered prospectively in ClinicalTrials.gov on 2022–06-28 with ID NCT05448274 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05448274?intr=ruminococcus%20torques&viewType=Card&rank=1).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13073-026-01708-9.

Background

Development and clinical use of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) analogues alone or in combination with other pertinent pharma represents a considerable therapeutic advance within the field of obesity and type 2 diabetes [1–4]. Still, there is a non-negligible fraction of individuals treated with GLP-1 analogues who discontinue their treatment due to adverse effects [4]; observations pointing to an unmet need to develop additional efficacious drugs potentially including pharmabiotics with fewer side effects for prevention and treatment of chronic metabolic disorders.

Within the space of probiotics that are cultured from environmental sources, most randomized clinical trials (RCTs) with probiotics are rather small and the results are often nonconclusive [5]. One of the relatively few exceptions is a human trial with Lactobacillus reuteri showing an increase in insulin secretion, possibly due to augmented GLP-1 release [6]. An alternative source for discovery of novel probiotics and postbiotics to regulate metabolism is the myriads of microorganisms living within the human gut, and the multitude of bioactive compounds that they synthesize.

Currently, several commensal bacteria isolated from human gut microbiota including strains of Akkermansia muciniphila, Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Bifidobacterium infantis, Clostridium beijerinckii and Clostridium butyricum are investigated as candidates of what is termed next-generation probiotics with the recent outcome of studies indicating beneficial effects on markers of host metabolism and inflammation [7–11]. In addition, the bacterial proteins, Amuc_1100 and P9, synthesized by Akkermansia muciniphila are studied for their potential as postbiotics for prevention or treatment of metabolic dysfunction [12].

Some strains of the commensal Ruminococcus torques (RT) species inhabiting the human gut may similarly be considered as a candidate for a next-generation probiotic [13].

Studies in mice show that the abundance of RT is enriched following time-restricted feeding, and a RT-synthesized metabolite, 2-hydroxy-4-methylpentanoic acid, suppresses the hypoxia-inducible factor-2α-ceramide pathway and thereby ameliorates inflammation and fibrosis in steatotic liver disease [14]. Recently, we identified strains of RT with a sequence in their genomes encoding the 1271 amino acids RUMTOR_00181 protein [13]. This large bacterial protein is proteolytically cleaved into two bioactive fibronectin 3-containing polypeptides named RumtOR DErived Protein1 and 2 (RORDEP1 and RORDEP2) with lengths of 87 and 88 amino acid residues, respectively, that are subsequently released into the gut [13]. In studies in rodents and humans, RT and RORDEPs show beneficial metabolic effects mediated via the enteric nervous system [13, 15]. These effects include increased insulin sensitivity, adipose tissue thermogenesis, GLP-1 and PYY release, and improved glucose tolerance [13, 15].

Thus, in an eight-week intervention in obese mice, a live RT strain (ATCC 27756) given by oral gavage twice weekly induces an improved glucose tolerance, a reduced body weight gain, a decrease of adiposity and an increase of tube bone density compared with placebo [13]. In addition, experiments in mice and rats with recombinant RORDEP1 (r-RORDEP1) administered intraperitoneally show an improved glucose tolerance due to an increase in plasma GLP-1 and peptide YY (PYY) in parallel with a relative decline in plasma gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) [13]. When r-RORDEP1 is given intraduodenally, it induces an enhanced suppression of insulin-mediated hepatic gluconeogenesis [13].

Since interventions with RT until now have been done exclusively in rodents, we designed a short-term RCT for healthy overweight adults to assess the feasibility and short-term physiological responses to intraduodenal RT infusion. The present study approach is inspired by a previous trial investigating the short-term effects of duodenal infusion of A. soehngenii [7]. The live RT ATCC 27756 strain or placebo was infused into the duodenum twice during six hours. The primary endpoint of the trial was a change of insulin sensitivity while secondary endpoints included tolerability and safety, changes in plasma hormones, metabolites, cytokines, measures of energy expenditure, cutaneous thermography of supraclavicular and neck area, and gut microbiota composition.

Methods

Study outlines

The study was designed as a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind (for both investigators and study participants), crossover trial, and was conducted between May and September 2023 at Center for Clinical Metabolic Research, Department of Medicine, Herlev-Gentofte University Hospital, Copenhagen. A study overview is provided in Fig. 1. The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.org and was initiated on May 3rd, 2023. Data collection was finalized on September 14th, 2023. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct and reporting of the trial.Fig. 1Overview of the trial design and the timeline for each intervention day. All study participants received two interventions: one with RT and one with placebo. The order of the interventions was randomized 1:1 using block randomization with block size six through https://www.sealedenvelope.com/. Blocks were divided into male and female blocks, but due to a slight sex unevenness, one of the blocks was gender mixed. The randomization was done on the first intervention day by an investigator who was not associated with the project. Four weeks were interspaced between the two interventions to minimize the risk of any carry-over effects.

Cohort

Volunteers were recruited from the capital region of Denmark through advertisements. Inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 40 years, BMI above 27 kg/m2, and absence of acute or chronic diseases. Female participants were required to show a negative pregnancy test and to have a regular menstrual cycle to be eligible to participate. The latter to ensure that each study visit was conducted during the same period of the menstrual cycle (pre- or post-ovulatory). Exclusion criteria were current use of any medication (contraceptives excepted) including treatment with antibiotics during the recent three months; acute or chronic gastrointestinal symptoms or diagnosed disease; lactose intolerance; smoking; abuse of alcohol or drugs; intake of creatine supplements; or abnormal biochemical markers apart from alanine aminotransferase (ALT) where values up to three times the normal value were accepted.

During the inclusion period, between January and July 2023, 60 individuals gave written informed consent and underwent anthropometrics and biochemical profiling. Of these, 43 (17 males and 26 females) were included in the study. Five of these individuals withdrew their informed consent before start of interventions due to unwillingness to have a naso-duodenal tube for infusion installed. Two individuals started treatment with antibiotics before study start and one individual developed constipation that required treatment before study start and were excluded, leaving 35 individuals (15 males and 20 females) for inclusion in the trial. One individual withdrew the informed consent after the first intervention due to unwillingness to have a naso-duodenal tube installed for the second intervention. Further two individuals were excluded from the study: one due to vomiting during the first seven minutes of the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at the first intervention visit; and for another individual, it was not technically possible to install a naso-duodenal tube. In total, 32 individuals (14 males and 18 females) completed both interventions and follow-up visits (see Additional file 1: Fig S1). Study baseline characteristics of study participants are shown in Additional file 1: Table S1. All individuals met recruitment criteria for being overweight or obese, and most of them were insulin resistant with an average value of homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) of 2.6 (interquartile range: 2.1 to 4.1). None had diagnosed diabetes, and all had values of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and biochemical markers of hematology, liver, and kidney functions within the accepted range. Participants used no medication during the study period except for eight participants who used contraceptives (one vaginal ring, three intrauterine devices, and four oral contraceptives).

Culture and production of the food grade Ruminococcus torques ATCC 27756 strain

The RT ATCC 27756 strain used in our trial, was on request cultured by the Nizo Company, The Netherlands. After testing multiple anaerobe culture conditions, the bacterial strain was finally cultured in a plant-based, food-grade medium containing 2% glucose/dextrose, 1% yeast extract, and 1% Kerry soy hydrolysate (Hy-soy) developed by Nizo. The final culture medium also included disodium phosphate (0.14 g/kg); calcium chloride (0.05 g/kg); magnesium chloride hexahydrate (0.2 g/kg); manganese sulphate (0.04 g/kg); polysorbate (tween 80, 1 g/kg); ammonium chloride (0.79 g/kg); dipotassium phosphate (5.57 g/kg); butyric, acetic, valeric, and propionic acid (all 0.625 g/kg); cysteine (1.76 g/kg); plant heme (6.5 μg/kg); iron (II) sulphate (5 mg/kg); ZnSO4 * 7H2O (5 mg/kg); and CuSO4 * 5 H2O (2.5 mg/kg). Additionally, a vitamin mix was included in the medium consisting of vitamin B2 (0.1 mg/kg); vitamin B6 (0.2 mg/kg); calcium pantothenate (0.1 mg/kg); vitamin B12 (0.02 mg/kg); biotin (0.05 mg/kg); 4-amino benzoic acid (0.1 mg/kg); folic acid (0.05 mg/kg); cholin (0.1 mg/kg); nicotinic acid (0.1 mg/kg); and thiamin (0.1 mg/kg). Ultra-high temperature of 141 °C for 12 s or filter sterilization (0.22 μm of mesh) were used to sterilize all components of the culture medium.

In a 600 L fermenter, 410 L of medium was inoculated with 1% volume/volume of a RT ATCC 27756 starter preculture. The atmosphere in the fermenter consisted of 95% N2 and 5% CO2; pH was kept at 6.5 ± 0.1 using sodium hydroxide, and temperature was kept at 37 °C ± 0.1 °C during the fermentation that had a duration of 22 h. The fermentation was ended by lowering the temperature, whereafter the fermented product was concentrated using cross flow filtration. Finally, 10 kg of concentrated biomass was mixed with 2 kg of sterile and anaerobic cryoprotectant consisting of 10% glycerol in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer.

Vials were filled with 10 ml of the product while being flushed with nitrogen to maintain an anaerobic environment. The vials were frozen by submerging them in liquid nitrogen and were then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for storage.

The final concentration of RT ATCC 27756 in the product was 3.1 × 1010 colony forming units (CFU) per ml corresponding to 3.1 × 1011 CFU per 10 ml vial.

The placebo consisted solely of 10% glycerol in PBS and was of similar volume (10 ml).

Primary and secondary trial endpoints

Our studies in mice and rats showed that intraduodenal infusion of r-RORDEP1 for three hours enhances insulin-mediated glucose metabolism [13]. Therefore, an improvement of insulin sensitivity as measured by Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index [16] following duodenal infusion of RT compared with placebo was chosen as the predefined primary physiological endpoint of the trial.

Furthermore, the studies in rats have shown that intraperitoneal injection of RORDEP1 during hyperglycemia compared to placebo induces a rise in the plasma concentration of insulin, GLP-1 and PYY but a decline of GIP and glucose [13]. Therefore, secondary endpoints included, besides measures of safety and tolerability, differences over six hours in plasma concentrations of insulin, C-peptide, GLP-1, PYY, glucagon, GIP, and glucose during an OGTT. Two prespecified exploratory endpoints, heart rate and blood pressure, were reevaluated during the trial to only serve as clinical safety markers on the intervention days and not as endpoints.

Feeding mice by oral gavage with RT ATCC 27756 for eight weeks induces an increase of gene and protein markers of adipose tissue thermogenesis and an increase of femoral bone density [13]. Consequently, additional secondary endpoints included resting energy expenditure; changes in brown adipose tissue activity assessed by thermal imaging of supraclavicular and neck temperature; plasma concentration of leptin, adiponectin, metabolites and cytokines, procollagen type 1 N-propeptide (P1NP), a marker of bone formation, and C-terminal telopeptide (CTX), a marker of bone resorption. Finally, any changes of gut microbiota composition were examined.

Intervention and physiological measures

Each intervention day had a duration of around eight hours and started in the morning after 10 h overnight fast. Study participants were requested not to consume alcohol or perform vigorous exercise for 48 h prior to each of the interventions.

First, venous blood was sampled for biochemical analyses and resting energy expenditure (indirect calorimetry) and supraclavicular cutaneous temperature (thermography) were measured as a proxy for brown adipose tissue activity. Next, a naso-duodenal tube was installed using the electromagnetic-guided CORTRAK 2 Enteral Access System (Avanos) to verify the duodenal placement of the tube. After finalizing tube installment, the investigators blindfolded the study participant and left the room. A nurse, who was not affiliated with the project team, infused a bolus of live bacteria or placebo into the duodenal tube. At the end of these initial infusions, the tube was flushed with 10 ml of water, and the study participant continued the fast for another four hours. Four hours after the first bolus, another bolus of the bacterium identical to the initial one was given, and the tube was removed. Immediately hereafter, an OGTT was performed: the study participant drank 75 g of glucose dissolved in 250 ml of water and was observed with frequent blood samplings during the following two hours.

To assess whole body insulin sensitivity, the Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index was calculated using the Excel form downloaded from the Matsuda Index website (https://mmatsuda.diabetes-smc.jp/english.html, downloaded 7th of November 2023). Intervention responses of circulating glucose, insulin, C-peptide, GLP-1, GIP and PYY were estimated both as area under the curve (AUC) during the full study day and as AUC during the two hours following the OGTT. AUC-values were calculated using the trapezoid method.

Analysis of standard biochemical variables

Blood glucose was measured bedside using Cobas Pulse. Blood leukocytes, eosinophils, lymphocytes, neutrophils, platelets, and hemoglobin were analyzed using ADVIA 2120i 3M. Plasma ALT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase, albumin, creatinine, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, and creatine kinase were measured using Siemens Atellica CH 930. Plasma triglycerides and total cholesterol were measured using Cobas c702.

Analysis of plasma hormones

Venous blood for analysis of insulin, C-peptide, GLP-1, PYY, GIP, glucagon, leptin, and adiponectin was collected in chilled EDTA tubes containing 10 μL valine pyrrolidine (1 mmol/L) per ml of blood and centrifuged. After centrifuging, plasma was collected and stored at −20 °C (for GLP-1, PYY, GIP, and glucagon) or −80 °C (other analyses). Plasma insulin and C-peptide were analyzed using Siemens Atellica IM analyzer. GLP-1 [17], PYY [18], GIP [19] and glucagon [20], were analyzed as previously described. Leptin and adiponectin were analyzed using Quantikine™ ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of plasma markers of bone remodeling

For this purpose, venous blood was collected in lithium-heparin tubes. After centrifuging, plasma was collected and stored at −80 °C. CTX and P1NP were measured as reported [21].

Semi-quantification of plasma metabolome

Semi-quantification was done as previously described in another article [22]. In short, an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system coupled with 6545 Quadrupole Time-Of-Flight Mass Spectrometer (QTOFMS) interfaced with a dual jet stream electrospray (dual electrospray ionization (dual ESI)) ion source (Agilent Technologies; Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for analyses. MassHunter B.06.01 software (Agilent Technologies; Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for data acquisition. Identification was based on an in-house library based on liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) data on retention time and mass spectra. The identification was done with a custom database based on authentic standard compounds or on MS/MS identification. For the differential analysis of the secondary bile acid pool, the abundances of individual bile acids were log10-transformed and standardized (z-scored) before being summed to obtain the total secondary bile acid concentration.

Analysis of plasma serotonin

To analyze plasma serotonin the following method was used: The extracted plasma samples were analyzed by Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) in positive mode using an ACQUITY PREMIER UHPLC coupled to a XEVO TQ-XS (Waters, MA, USA). The chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY BEH Amide, 1.7 µm, 150 mm × 2.1 mm I.D. column equipped with an ACQUITY BEH Amide VanGuard Pre-column (Waters, MA, USA). Mobile phase was composed of A) 1 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in LC–MS grade water and B) 1 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in 5% LC–MS grade water and 95% acetonitrile solution. The linear gradient elution from 95% B (0–1.5 min) down to 35% B was applied (from 1.5 to 7 min) and these conditions were held for 0.1 min. Subsequently, initial chromatographic condition was maintained as a post-run during 3 min for column re-equilibration resulting in a total run-time of 10.1 min. The flow rate was 400 µL/min, column temperature 30 °C, and sample injection volume 2 µL Quantification was performed using a 9-point calibration (0 to 1000ng/mL) and isotope dilution using serotonin-d4 (TMAO-d9, choline-d9, creatinine-d4, betaine-d11, taurine-d4, l-carnitine-d3, 5-hydroxyindoleacetate-d6, caffeine-d3, tryptophan-d5).

Analysis of plasma cytokines

Plasma cytokines were measured with high-sensitivity immunoassays as reported [23]. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C, but otherwise analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 8-point standard curves to calculate concentrations [23]. There were no undetectable values in the high end of the data set. Undetectable samples in the low end were set to half of the minimum detection limit of the given cytokine.

Resting energy expenditure

Resting energy expenditure was measured applying indirect calorimetry (Vyntus CPX Canopy. Vyaire Medical, Hochberg, Germany). Participants were placed in a relaxed, supine position in bed. A hood was fitted on their head, and they were instructed to breathe normally and relax during the measurement. Eyes should be closed, but participants should not sleep. Each measurement lasted 15 min aiming to capture a steady state. For each measurement, we found a period of five minutes using an Excel macro where the variation was acceptable. If there was no period of five minutes, the macro would be run again to find a period of four minutes. If a period of four minutes could not be found the value was listed as missing. The period with the lowest average energy expenditure during a measurement was chosen for further analysis.

Cutaneous thermal imaging

To indirectly estimate brown adipose tissue activity, we measured the bilateral cutaneous temperatures of the neck and clavicular areas capturing any thermogenesis from the brown adipose tissue depots in these areas.

Since cutaneous temperature can be altered by numerous factors, such as sun exposure or fluctuations in room temperature, we measured the cutaneous temperature of the sternum for reference. The temperature difference was then calculated as the average between the two sides of the active areas minus the reference point.

Participants were placed in a lying position with their upper bodies elevated 30 degrees. They were instructed not to move their upper bodies from the position they were placed in during the measurements to improve the quality of the pictures. A photo was taken at 30 s intervals for a total of 15 min per session. Data analysis was done in FLIR tools +.

Stool sample collection

Study participants were instructed with a standardized stool sampling kit to collect a stool sample at home and earliest 48 h before each of the intervention days and similarly on the post-intervention follow-up days. The stool sample was immediately stored in a home freezer (−18 °C) until it, in an ice-cooled box, was transferred to a −80 °C freezer at the experimental facility.

Microbial DNA extraction and 16S rDNA sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from approximately 250 mg of human stool samples using the NucleoSpin Soil Mini Kit (MACHEREY NAGEL) according to the manufacturer's instructions as was the case in a previous study [13]. In short, the stool samples were first lysed through a combination of chemical treatment and mechanical disruption. The resulting crude lysate was then treated to remove inhibitors, ensuring sample cleanup. Next, the purified lysate was mixed with DNA binding solution and passed through a silica-based spin filter. The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured using a Qubit Flex Fluorometer and the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific).

DNA libraries were prepared using the Quick-16S Plus NGS Library Prep Kit (Zymo Research) as described previously [13]. The V3 and V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Illumina® 8-nt dual indices were then added via a second PCR step. After quantification using the Qubit fluorometer, the DNA libraries were circularized using the Element Adept Library Compatibility Workflow and sequenced on the Element AVITI platform with the AVITI 2 × 150 Cloudbreak Sequencing Kit.

Bioinformatics analysis of gut microbiota

Analysis of gut microbiota was carried out as previously described [13]. In short, primer sequences were removed from the raw data using Cutadapt [24]. Quality trimming was performed with DADA2 [25]. To compare gut microbial composition between baseline and follow-up samples, taxonomic abundances at the family level were visualized in a stacking bar plot. Alpha diversity was calculated using the Shannon index, and the results were plotted for comparison by using linear mixed models. All the data analysis was performed in R (version 4.3.2).

Gut bacterial cell counting and estimation of absolute abundance of R. torques

Bacterial cell counting was done as previously described [26]. The relative abundance of RT ATCC 27756 strain was quantified using qPCR [13]. Total RT ATCC 27756 bacterial cell counts were multiplied with the relative abundance of RT ATCC 27756 to calculate the absolute abundance.

Post-intervention examinations to evaluate tolerability

One week after each intervention, participants paid a short follow-up visit. Blood was sampled for standard biochemical analysis focused on markers of hematology, inflammation, and liver and renal functions. Further, participants were interviewed by the investigator to explore if any side effects had occurred. During each of the seven days following the intervention, participants were asked to note in a diary before bedtime if they experienced onset/worsening of the following symptoms: nausea, vomiting, flatulence, bloating, borborygmi, reflux, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pains or cramps, headache, dizziness, sleep disturbances, fatigue, pruritus, and skin rash – a procedure also used in a previous study [15]. In addition, participants were asked to mark on a visual analogue scale their general well-being on each of the follow-up days.

Statistics

Using the statistical software R, we calculated a required sample size of 28 participants to detect a change in the Matsuda Index (our primary endpoint) of 1.75, which was considered clinically relevant. We aimed to include 30 participants to avoid falling below the required number due to drop-outs but ended with a total of 32 participants completing the study. We used a significance level of 5% and a power of 90% in the sample size calculations.

The statistical software R version 4.3.2 was similarly used for all statistical analyses except for counts data where we used version 4.4.0. We used linear mixed effects models with an unstructured covariance pattern to analyze continuous outcome variables (package: LMMstar). For counts data we used generalized mixed models (package: lme4) with a Poisson distribution. To view the metabolome profile, we created a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot (package: MixOmics). AUC was calculated in Microsoft Excel using the trapezoid method. To assess correlations, we calculated the partial correlation using the function pcor.test (package: ppcor) to account for the paired nature of our data.

All mixed models were adjusted for sex, age, and treatment period, except for in the generalized models for rumbling in the stomach and reflux where the model was unable to converge with adjustment for age and so it was left out. Further, baseline values of the variable in question were adjusted for whenever possible. The linear model for the difference in skin temperature was adjusted for room temperature at the start of the measurement.

If a value of the frequently measured variables, (GLP-1, GIP, PYY, glucagon, glucose, insulin, and C-peptide) was missing during the OGTT, the corresponding value on the other treatment day was excluded to maintain symmetry between the study days.

Two individuals vomited during the OGTT and the time points following were excluded from analyses. The same time points were also removed on the study day without vomiting to make the data comparable between the two study days. Due to the missing values, it was not possible to calculate a Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index for the two participants, and they were left out of the analysis for the primary endpoint.

Results

Trial tolerability and safety

Both infusions were well tolerated. Compared with the placebo intervention, study participants reported significantly lower occurrences of bloating, flatulence and diarrhea after the RT infusion (Additional file 1: Table S2). Within the timeframe of the study, individual values of clinical biochemistry remained stable after duodenal infusion of either RT or placebo (Additional file 1: Tables S3 and S4) confirming safety of intervention. We noted a slightly lower blood lymphocytes count (0.18 × 109/L (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.058 × 109/L; 0.30 × 109/L), p = 0.01) and a higher hemoglobin (0.1 mmol/L (95% CI 0.02 mmol/L; 0.2 mmol/L), p = 0.02), and higher plasma potassium (0.08 mmol/L (95% CI 0.02 mmol/L; 0.1 mmol/L), p = 0.01) six hours after infusion of RT (Additional file 1: Table S3). These biochemical changes were considered co-incidental and without clinical relevance.

Trial effects on metabolism and inflammation

Our studies in rodents have shown that RT and its synthesized polypeptides, RORDEP1 and RORDEP2, enhance glucose tolerance and glucose disappearance rate [13]. Therefore, an improvement of insulin sensitivity as measured by Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index following duodenal infusion of RT compared with placebo was chosen as the predefined primary physiological endpoint of the trial. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint (Additional file 1: Fig S2 and Table S5).

Among the prespecified secondary outcomes, we found that duodenal infusion of RT was well tolerated and safe. During the six-hour trial, the plasma GLP-1 response (area under the full-period curve) increased by 7.8% (95% CI 2.3%; 14%, p = 0.006) while the plasma PYY response during the OGTT increased by 21% (95% CI 0.4%; 45%, p = 0.045). Conversely, the plasma GIP response declined by 13% (95% CI 8%; 17%, p < 0.0001, area under the full-period curve, Fig. 2 and Additional file 1: Table S6).

Despite differences in the plasma incretins, GLP-1 and GIP, in response to the two interventions, plasma concentrations of glucose, insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Fig S3 and Fig S4 and Table S6). Similarly, the interventions did not impact responses in plasma lipids or the adipose tissue-related plasma leptin and adiponectin hormones (Additional file 1: Fig S2 and Table S5).

We observed no differences between responses to interventions in physiological readouts of indirect calorimetry or cutaneous neck and supraclavicular region thermography (Additional file 1: Fig S2 and Table S5).

We tested if markers of bone dynamics responded to the short-term intervention. To know if RT infusion altered the intestinal microbial communities in the short term, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was performed using DNA extracted from fecal samples collected at baseline of the intervention day with RT and the follow-up visit one week later. Duodenal infusion of the bacterial strain twice during six hours did not impact gut microbiota composition or alpha-diversity after one week (Shannon index, p = 0.11, Additional file 1: Fig S5).

Furthermore, overall gut microbiota composition or alpha-diversity was unchanged a month after infusion with RT, again suggesting that no carry-over effect occurred at the second infusion day (p = 0.67, Fig. 5).

Moreover, as a post hoc analysis we applied a statistical approach to explore if the absolute abundance of RT on a given study day might influence trial outcomes. We included the log 10 transformed absolute abundance of RT as a covariate in the statistical models searching for potential relationships between type of intervention and responses of insulin sensitivity (the primary endpoint) and plasma concentrations of incretins that were among the secondary endpoints. The adjustments in the statistical analyses did, however, not change any trial outcomes (Additional file 1: Table S9).

Discussion

In the present short-term feasibility trial of 32 healthy overweight adults we examined tolerability, safety and potential physiological responses following duodenal infusion of a bioactive RT ATCC 27756 strain that has previously been reported to have beneficial metabolic effects in mice [13]. The short-term study design was similar to the one applied to show biological effects of the human gut commensal A. soehngenii following a six-hour duodenal infusion in healthy adults [7]. Our intervention with the gut commensal RT was safe and well-tolerated as evaluated from standard hematological variables and biochemical markers of organ function as well as from diary recordings of adverse effects. The trial did, however, not meet its primary endpoint, which was changes of whole-body insulin sensitivity. Compared with placebo, we observed short-term changes in plasma concentrations of the incretins, GLP-1 and GIP, as well as PYY, secondary bile acids and markers of bone dynamics following six hours of observation after infusion of the live bacterium.

To optimize statistical power, the trial was run in a crossover design. Importantly, the order of interventions did not influence the fecal microbiota composition, or the absolute abundance of RT; findings that exclude intervention-caused carry-over effects mediated by potential alterations of gut colonization of the infused bacterial species or secondary adaptations of the overall gut microbial community.

In our intervention, we applied the Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index as a surrogate estimate of whole-body insulin action in an attempt to substantiate a previous finding of increased insulin sensitivity in rats after three hours of intraduodenal infusion of the RT derived peptide RORDEP1 [13]. We did not manage to find any significant increase in insulin sensitivity when comparing infusion with live RT and placebo. The reason for this could be the acute nature of the trial. Koopen et al. failed to show an increase in insulin sensitivity following six hours of duodenal infusion of A. soehngenii [7]. However, in the same short-term trial, they found an increase in the plasma GLP-1 concentration that correlated temporally with an increase of plasma concentrations of secondary bile acids [7]. The latter have in experiments in rodents been shown to promote a release of GLP-1 via an activation of the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor on enteroendocrine L cells [27].

In our six-hour trial of healthy overweight adults, we observed a consistent increase of the plasma concentration of GLP-1 following live RT infusion compared with placebo. The finding aligns with results from our short-term studies in rats where plasma GLP-1 was measured 45 min after intraperitoneal injection of r-RORDEP1 showing a rise in the plasma concentrations of GLP-1 and insulin and a decline in the plasma glucose concentration when compared with placebo [13]. However, the clinical significance of this observation is unknown since plasma insulin and glucose were unaffected by the intervention.

In addition, we found increased levels of several secondary bile acids in plasma after infusion with RT, changes that we interpret as signs of RT activity. Though seeing parallel increases in GLP-1 and secondary bile acids, we did not, however, find any statistically significant relationship between the variables.

Since it has been suggested that serotonin may modulate GLP-1 secretion [28], we measured serotonin concentrations in plasma, but we found no difference between infusions of either placebo or RT.

Apart from a rise in the plasma GLP-1 concentration following infusion of RT, plasma PYY was also increased while the plasma GIP concentration decreased. These findings align with our studies in rats showing a rise in plasma PYY and a lowering of plasma GIP after intraperitoneal injection of r-RORDEP1 [13]. Importantly, in a recently conducted randomized clinical trial of healthy men, a duodenal bolus of recombinant RORDEP1 caused a significant rise in plasma GLP-1, insulin and C-peptide and a decline in plasma GIP and glucose, while also significantly increasing whole-body insulin sensitivity as measured with the Matsuda Index of Insulin Sensitivity [15].

In our eight weeks intervention in obese mice where RT was given twice weekly, we observed a reduced weight gain and in parallel an enhanced expression of marker genes of adipose tissue thermogenesis and browning [13]. In the present human trial, we therefore tested the idea of a potential acute effect of RT on measures of thermogenesis and energy expenditure by monitoring cutaneous thermography and by performing indirect calorimetry. However, none of these measures suggested any acute effects on energy expenditure.

In the same intervention of eight weeks duration in mice, RT induced an increase in the cortical thickness of the femoral bone [13]. The dynamics of bone formation are complex and encompass phases in time and space of alternating increases in osteoclast or osteoblast activities [29]. Six hours after the first duodenal infusion of RT was given, we found a higher plasma concentration of CTX (a systemic marker of osteoclast activity) compared with placebo, while plasma P1NP (a systemic marker of osteoblast activity) was unchanged. The finding of an increase in plasma CTX may be representative for a time window where CTX, as a part of the bone remodeling dynamics, is released from bone matrix after cleavage of type I collagen by osteoclasts. Though, it is also possible that the increase in plasma CTX could, at least partially, be explained by the decrease in plasma GIP, since GIP may diminish bone resorption [30]. We tested for a possible temporal relationship between the decline in AUC for plasma GIP and the increase in plasma concentration of CTX and found an inverse correlation between plasma CTX and AUC for GIP.

While the eight weeks intervention with RT in mice was associated with a reduced adipose tissue gene expression of TNF-α [13], we observed, in our human trial, an increase in plasma TNF-α at the end of the study day (360 min after the first infusion of RT). Though these findings may seem contradicting, they are not directly comparable since most of TNF-α in circulation is produced by the immune system rather than by adipose tissue [31]. However, a potential difference in adipose tissue and plasma TNF-α concentrations could also be explained by the timeframe of the two studies; a long-term exposure to a higher gut abundance of RT might allow the immune system to adapt in a way to cause a decrease the circulating levels of TNF-α. Apart from changes in TNF-α we found modest changes of several additional circulating cytokines, some of them predominantly with a known anti-inflammatory effect, and others with pro-inflammatory properties. The reason for this is unknown, but the RT bacterium is not native to the duodenum where the infusion is taking place. The duodenal immune system may therefore react to the change in upper intestinal microbiota and cause a release of a variety of cytokines. Despite the variegated responses of plasma cytokines to the RT intervention, conventional clinical markers of systemic low-grade inflammation including plasma CRP, Il-6 and IL-1β, remained unaffected.

We acknowledge that our study has limitations. Due to the short-term nature of our explorative intervention, we assume that some effects of the RT infusion may not have manifested fully due to lack of RT engraftment. A future trial with a duration of several months where RT is given daily in acid resistant entero-coated capsules with extended release potentially also allowing for engraftment of RT is warranted while also allowing for the clinical relevance of RT as a probiotic to be tested. Another limitation is that we cannot determine which bacterial compounds synthesized by RT caused the observed hormonal changes. Supplementation of RT strains to patients with colitis is reported to ameliorate the bowel inflammation and gut barrier dysfunction by modulating gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism, but the authors of the paper did not explore any effects on intestinal hormone release [32]. For our study outcome, we hypothesize that the rapid changes in intestinal hormone release after infusion of the RT ATCC 27756 strain is triggered by a localized response to the presence of the bacterium, which we, in turn, suggest may be mediated by the release of RORDEP1/2 from the bacterium to the intestinal lumen. This interpretation of human data aligns with our findings in rats where duodenal infusion or intraperitoneal injection of r-RORDEP1 causes significant and rapid changes in blood glucose and incretins within 30 to 180 min [13]. Furthermore, we have previously reported how both RORDEPs are released from the RT ATCC 27756 strain to the culture medium overnight [13] suggesting that sufficient amounts of RORDEP1 may have been produced during the course of the intervention, but since RORDEPs do not penetrate the epithelial wall of the gut within the six-hour duration of the study, we are unable to use plasma measurements to detect a potential increased production of RORDEPs [13].

Conclusions

The outcome of the present RCT in healthy overweight humans suggests that short-term duodenal infusion of the RT ATCC 27756 strain is without adverse effects and the effects of the bacterial strain on intestinal hormone release are in accordance with those previously reported in rodents, though a potential clinical significance could not be established. Our findings call for longer-term RCTs to explore the potential clinical relevance of oral administration of the live RT ATCC 27756 strain as a probiotic in acid-resistant and slow-release capsules in ascending doses to assess the metabolic efficacy of the bacterial strain.
