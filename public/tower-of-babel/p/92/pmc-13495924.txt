Maternal‐Fetal Administration of Risdiplam Partially Rescues the SMNΔ7 Mouse Model of Spinal Muscular Atrophy
Authors: Emma R Sutton, Ariane Beauvais, Rebecca Yaworski, Hugh J McMillan, Rashmi Kothary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is caused by deletions or mutations in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene and subsequent reduction in the expression of survival motor neuron (SMN) protein. The disease is characterized by degeneration of α motor neurons and subsequent muscle atrophy. Postnatal disease modifying therapies improve outcomes yet fall short of a cure. We have investigated a prenatal therapeutic strategy using the SMNΔ7 mouse model of SMA.MethodsA risdiplam‐like compound was administrated by oral gavage to pregnant mice with treatment initiated mid‐gestation and halted on day of birth. Reproductive metrics, liver toxicology, and immune function were assessed in the pregnant dams during gestation and post‐birth. Progeny were monitored phenotypically, and pathological hallmarks of SMA were investigated including, but not limited to motor neuron, neuromuscular junction, and muscle health.ResultsMaternal‐fetal drug transfer was safe for the pregnant dam and substantially increased weight, motor function, and survival of severe SMA mice. It also rescued and maintained myofiber area. Presynaptic components of the neuromuscular junction were marginally improved in SMNΔ7 pups, whereas postsynaptic endplate size was increased following prenatal risdiplam treatment. Motor neuron pathology was not fully rescued by prenatal exposure to SMN protein, likely because of the lack of continued postnatal intervention. By restricting risdiplam treatment to the prenatal period, we have isolated and assessed therapeutic effects attributable solely to embryo exposure of the therapeutic.InterpretationOur efforts demonstrate a combination of prenatal and postnatal therapy must be used for optimum therapeutic outcomes in the treatment of patients with SMA. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:584–599

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a devastating neuromuscular disorder caused by deletions or mutations in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene and subsequent reduction in the expression of SMN protein. 1 During fetal development SMN protein is robustly regulated, high at early stages of development while steadily declining post‐birth. 2 Biallelic variants in the SMN1 gene lead to reliance on the paralogous SMN2 gene. SMN2 shows copy number variability, with each copy producing only 10 to 15% of full length SMN protein. 2 There is mounting evidence that disease onset begins during gestation, particularly for newborns with fewer SMN2 copies. 3 , 4 Although low levels of SMN protein is compatible with survival to term, it may not prevent disease progression during gestation. Babies born with infantile‐onset SMA do not typically survive beyond the first 2 years of life if left untreated. Understanding this critical threshold during development may determine an optimal therapeutic window before any irreversible neuronal damage in SMA. 2

Specifically, unlike healthy age‐matched controls, the SMN protein does not increase in SMA spinal cords during the second and third trimester of gestation and the first 3 months post‐birth. 5 The spatial and temporal requirements of SMN protein differ throughout development as the neonatal motor unit develops. 6 It is likely that this neonatal period of reduced SMN protein is when motor neurons are most vulnerable in SMA. Genetic or pharmacological intervention can dramatically reverse the SMA phenotype when delivered early postnatally, an effect that is lost when intervention occurs later. 7 Currently the 3 disease modifying therapies (DMTs) for SMA are United States (US) Food and Drug Administration approved for postnatal administration only. Although groundbreaking in their beneficial impact on patients with SMA, they still fall short of a cure for the disease. 8 , 9

Prenatal and newborn screening (NBS) strategies can identify pre‐symptomatic SMA. With the recent increase in the implementation since 2021, 33 countries now offer NBS for SMA as of 2024. 10 , 11 Carrier testing is offered in some countries as an alternative to NBS. 12 , 13 , 14 Additionally, pregnancies with a family history of SMA can be screened by chorionic villus sampling (10–14 weeks) or amniocentesis (16–20 weeks). Taken together, with 3 effective DMTs, this early diagnosis makes prenatal therapeutic intervention more appealing in cases where an antenatal diagnosis is available. 15

Several pivotal trials (NURTURE, SPR1NT, and RAINBOWFISH) for each of the 3 DMTs, nusinersen, onasemnogene abeparvovec, and risdiplam, respectively, have demonstrated early treatment to be associated with the most favorable clinical outcomes. 8 , 16 Patients that receive pre‐symptomatic treatment reach milestones not attained where treatment is delayed until after clinical symptoms have emerged. 8 , 16 Even in these pivotal trials, some newborns with 2 copies of SMN2, who appeared clinically silent, still showed delay in the age at which they walked independently and/or had signs of proximal muscle weakness indicating that disease progression was occurring without overt symptoms being clinically apparent. This is supported by recent neurofilament (NfL) data that shows elevated levels in newborns with SMA and outcome measures such as CHOP‐INTEND and compound motor action potential (CMAP) amplitude being more favorable in newborns with 2 copies of SMN2 who were born at an earlier gestational age. 17 , 18 This emphasizes a need for early restoration of the SMN protein and ideally, the possibility of administering DMTs prenatally. 19

Current postnatal therapies also do not address the needs of patients with very severe SMA (type 0) who present with prenatal disease onset. 20 Recent efforts have investigated the possibility of in utero therapy for SMA in both small and large animal models. 21 Intra‐amniotic delivery of an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) in an SMA mouse model successfully increased SMN protein levels in the central nervous system (CNS), improved motor axon development, motor function and survival compared to postnatally treated mice. 21 Gene therapy has also been investigated as a potential avenue for in utero therapy via either intrauterine (IU) or intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration of AAV9‐SMN1 in a severe mouse model of SMA. 22 IU therapy improved muscle pathology, survival and motor neuron counts. 22

Specifically, in the context of risdiplam, proof‐of‐concept studies have demonstrated the requirement of in utero therapy to restore axonal growth and maturation as well as prevent detrimental neonatal axonal degeneration. 23 Furthermore, the first clinical attempt to treat SMA prenatally was recently reported. 24 A pregnant woman carrying an SMA affected fetus was treated with risdiplam by oral administration starting at 32 weeks of gestation. The baby had no clinical signs of SMA at 30 months of age, but did present with congenital abnormalities and global developmental delay. 24 Of note, the child also received postnatal risdiplam therapy from 8 days of age, preventing any favorable outcomes from being solely attributable to the prenatal exposure. Given this was a single case study, potential treatment‐related congenital effects cannot be completely excluded, more systematic efforts are needed to gauge the efficacy of prenatal therapy for SMA.

With growing evidence that SMA is a developmental disorder, it is likely there is a narrow‐therapeutic window for optimal treatment. 23 , 25 Therefore, establishing prenatal therapy for SMA has the potential to bridge this therapeutic gap and facilitate progression toward a cure for SMA. In the present manuscript, we have investigated this novel therapeutic strategy by administering a risdiplam‐like compound to pregnant dams carrying fetuses from the SMNΔ7 mouse model of SMA.

Methods

Animals

The SMNΔ7 mouse line (Smn −/−; SMN +/+; SMNΔ7 +/+ [aka SMNΔ7]) was used in this study. Smn +/−; SMN +/+; SMNΔ7 +/+ (RRID:IMSR_JAX:005025, friend leukemia virus B congenic background) male and female heterozygous mice were crossed to generate SMNΔ7 SMA mice. SMNΔ7 mice are a severe mouse model of SMA, developing progressive weakness at approximately 4 days, with an average lifespan of approximately 13 days when left without treatment.

All mice were maintained on a 12 hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The Jackson Laboratory methods on timed matings were followed. In short, all dams were mated with a stud male to generate pregnancies, male bedding was introduced into the female cage 23 days before pairing of the mice. Dams with vaginal plugs (considered embryonic day [E] 0.5) indicative of mating were monitored for weight gain to confirm pregnancy before use in the study.

Sex as a Biological Variable

Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups regardless of sex. Males and females are affected at similar rates clinically, for this reason, no strong biological sex‐specific effects were expected.

Genotyping

An ear notch was collected from each pup at point of collection. The Phire Tissue Direct polymerase chain reaction (PCR) master mix was used as per manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Scientific cat F170L, Massachusetts, USA). Briefly, genomic deoxyribonucleic acid was added to a multiplex PCR reaction for the mouse Smn WT forward 5′‐TCT GTG TTC GTG CGT GGT GAC TTT‐3′, reverse: 5′‐CCC ACC ACC TAA GAA AGC CTC AAT‐3′. Lac Z forward: 5′‐CCA ACT TAA TCG CCT TGC AGC ACA‐3′, reverse: 5′‐AAG CGA GTG GCA ACA TGG AAA TCG‐3′.

Drug Administration

Following a positive vaginal plug (E0.5), pregnant dams were monitored for 9 days before treatment commenced (E9.5). Dams were randomly assigned to 1 of 2 treatment groups RG7916 (risdiplam‐like molecule) or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC, vehicle). Pregnant dams were dosed daily for a 10‐day period (E9.5–E19.5) by oral gavage at 10 mg/kg/day RG7916 in 0.5% HPMC and 0.1% Tween‐80 as previously described (Fig 1). 26 , 27 Weight was measured at the time of drug administration and during the remaining gestation period and used for dosage calculations.

Schematic representation of the maternal‐fetal treatment of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) mice. Timed matings were performed and positive plug checks confirmed embryonic day (E)0.5. Pregnant dams were administered drug (RG7916) or vehicle (hydroxypropyl methylcellulose) daily for 10 days from E9.5–E19.5 via oral gavage. No postnatal treatment was provided. Dam and pups were monitored post‐birth, pathological analysis in pups was performed postnatal day (P) 13. [Color figure can be viewed at www.annalsofneurology.org]

Phenotypic Analysis

Dams were weighed daily throughout the 10‐day treatment period during gestation. Pups were weighed every 2 days from day of birth: postnatal day (P) 1 until humane endpoint. Survival cohorts that made it to P21 were weaned and housed by sex.

The development of tail necrosis was noted and considered endpoint when severe.

Litters were monitored for weight, motor function (righting reflex) and survival, and sacrificed at P13 for tissue analysis. Phenotypic analysis was performed until humane endpoint, defined as 4 days consecutive weight loss, 20% overall weight loss, or severe tail necrosis.

Motor Function

Righting reflex (P5–P20) was performed on vehicle or RG7916‐transplacentally treated pups during the first 3 weeks of life, post‐birth. The righting reflex was performed as previously described (NMD4C_SOP0003). 28 Briefly, a pup is placed on its back and the time it takes to right is recorded.

Immunoblot

Dam tissues (liver) were flash frozen in cryovials in liquid nitrogen after euthanasia 2 weeks post‐birth. Pup tissues (brain, spinal cord, and muscle) were similarly flash frozen at P13. Tissues were homogenized in radio‐immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Cell Signalling, Massachusetts, USA) using the PowerGen 125 homogenizer, 7 × 95mm generator (Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). A Bradford assay (Thermo‐Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) was used to determine protein concentrations. Protein (20μg) was loaded onto a polyacrylamide (10%) gel and examined by immunoblot following gel electrophoresis. A polyvinylidene fluoride membrane was used for the protein transfer and blocking was performed for 1 hour in Odyssey blocking buffer (LI‐Cor, Nebraska, USA). An Odyssey (LI‐Cor) imager was used to detect protein signal, α‐tubulin was used as a housekeeping loading control.

Immunohistochemistry

Hematoxylin and Eosin Stain

On collection, dam liver and pup tibialis anterior muscle were washed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 hours at 4°C. Tissues were then transferred to 70% ethanol before processing and embedding. Tissues were embedded in wax using a LOGOS microwave hybrid tissue processor (Milestone medical, Kalamazoo, Michigan, USA) by the University of Ottawa Pathology and Laboratory Medicine department. A microtome was used to section the paraffin blocks at 3 to 4μm thickness, sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using an XL CV5030 autostainer (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and scanned with a MIRAX MIDI digital slide scanner (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Samples were visualized for hepatotoxicity (liver) using ImageJ or myofiber pathology (tibialis anterior) using MIRA vision, an artificial intelligence (AI)‐based system. 29 , 30 , 31

Motor Neuron Staining

Lumbar spinal cord was collected and fixed in 4% PFA for 24 hours at 4°C. Spinal cords were then transferred to 30% sucrose. Lumbar sections were mounted in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound cryomold blocks and frozen using vapor phase liquid nitrogen freezing. Spinal cords were sectioned and stained for motor neuron markers choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) (1:100) and anti‐neurofilament heavy (NF‐H) (SMI‐32) (1:500) as previously described. 32 The number of ChAT and SMI‐32 positive cell bodies were counted per ventral horn.

Neuromuscular Junction Staining

For neuromuscular junction (NMJ) analysis, the transversus abdominis muscle was dissected, fixed in 4% PFA for 15 minutes then washed 3 times for 10 minutes in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). A secondary dissection was later performed to separate the transverse abdominus muscle from the rib cage as previously described. 33 NMJs were stained for NfL (1:100), synaptic vesicle protein (SV2, 1:250) and α‐bungarotoxin (BTX, 1:1000). In short, the TVA is incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in tetramethyl rhodamine (TRITC) conjugated bungarotoxin. Sections were then incubated in NF and SV2 primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. DAKO medium (Agilent) was used to mount the sections ahead of taking Z‐stack images on a fluorescent Axio imager M2 microscope (Zeiss). NMJ occupancy, NfL accumulation, and post‐synaptic endplate size was analyzed using ImageJ.

Blood Collection and Plasma Analysis

Dams were euthanized by decapitation after asphyxiation in a CO2 chamber. Blood (160μL) was collected on decapitation using Microcuvette CB 300 K2E tubes (Sarstedt, North Carolina, USA) coated with K2 EDTA. Blood was centrifuged for 5 minutes at 2000g using centrifuge 5242R (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Supernatant was stored at −80°C ahead of analysis. Dam plasma (80μl) was sent to the Centre for Phenogenomics (Toronto, Canada) for liver health paneling. With the remaining plasma we performed a mouse cytokine/chemokine focused 14‐plex assay to determine immune function of the dam post‐gestational treatment with RG7916.

Pups were similarly euthanized by decapitation after asphyxiation in a CO2 chamber at P13, and plasma was collected as described above. Pup plasma was used to determine NfL protein concentration (Roche Elecsys NfL kit on the Roche Cobas Pro e801 instrument, Basal, Switzerland). As per manufacturer's instructions this was run as a batched assay by Eastern Ontario Regional Laboratory Association at the Ottawa Hospital General Campus.

Liver enzyme clinical chemistry analysis of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST) and total bilirubin (TBIL) was performed by the pathology core at the Centre for Phenogenomics. Inflammatory cytokine multiplex analysis was performed by Eve Technologies Corp (Calgary, Canada) using the Luminex 200 platform. The mouse cytokine/chemokine focused 14‐plex discovery assay array (MDF14) was conducted. Fourteen markers were measured simultaneously including but not limited to: granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor, interferon γ (INF‐γ), interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α).

Statistical Analyses

GraphPad Prism 11 was used to perform statistical analyses. Data are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Parametric unpaired t‐tests, 1‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test or 2‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's post hoc tests were performed. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test. α‐Level 0.05 was used to determine significance. Statistical significance is indicated by number of asterisks; *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001. Outliers were determined using Grubbs' test, GraphPad calculator. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, regardless of sex, and images were blinded before quantification. Sample size was determined by the resource equation approach (E = total number of animals‐total number of groups). In this study, E falls between 11 and 14 (Smn −/− treated [n = 8] vs vehicle [n = 8] E = 14, Smn +/− treated [n = 6] vs vehicle [n = 7] E = 11), where E10–E20 can be considered as an adequate sample size. Where sample size was lower than n = 5, distribution was considered normal.

Study Approval

The University of Ottawa Animal Care Committee approved all animal experiments in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (protocol: OHRIe‐4588). Animal care was performed in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care.

Results

RG7916 (Risdiplam‐like) Is Well Tolerated in Pregnant Dams

Previous work has demonstrated the potential of in utero risdiplam administration in both murine models of SMA and human patients with SMA. 23 , 24 Specifically, Sumner and colleagues 23 provide thorough evidence that prenatal therapy can prevent neonatal axon degeneration and enhance motor axon function, beyond the therapeutic ability of early postnatal treatment increasing radial outgrowth of myelinated axons only. 23 The feasibility of prenatal therapy for SMA has been published in a single human case study by Finkel et al 24 in the New England Journal of Medicine. Although no signs of SMA have appeared to date in the offspring, congenital abnormalities, optic nerve hypoplasia, and global developmental delay were present. An inability to generalize findings because of the low sample size warrants further investigation into maternal‐fetal risdiplam therapy for SMA.

We set out to investigate the safety and therapeutic potential of maternal‐fetal risdiplam administration using a preclinical mouse model of SMA. We have restricted drug exposure to the embryo enabling investigation of prenatal risdiplam (RG7916). RG7916 was administered via daily oral gavage to pregnant dams during gestation, starting on E9.5, for a 10‐day period. Both dam and pups were monitored to assess the safety and efficacy of maternal‐fetal treatment in the SMNΔ7 mouse model of severe SMA (Fig 1).

During the 10‐day treatment period, pregnant dams were closely monitored, and comprehensive non‐invasive health checks were performed daily by observation. Of 10 pregnancies, 3 did not carry to term likely because of the stress of therapeutic intervention, although we cannot rule out a drug‐related contribution (Fig 2A). Dam weight was recorded, and in all successful pregnancies there was a steady increase in weight (Fig 2B). To determine reproductive performance after RG7916 treatment during gestation we monitored key metrics (gestation length, abortion rate, stillbirths, litter size, and underdeveloped embryos). Gestation length was significantly reduced, and fetal stillbirths were significantly increased (Fig 2C–G, Tables 1 and 2). Violin plots indicate the variation in gestation length and abortion rate is largely attributable to litter‐level differences. Specifically, 2 dams formed a distinct cluster characterized by shortened gestation length and increased still births, whereas 1 dam carried her litter to term despite a high incidence of stillbirths. This pattern suggests that maternal or litter‐specific factors may be contributing to the observed outcomes.

To understand whether RG7916 (10mg/kg) affects systemic immune and metabolic balance in pregnant dams, we performed histological analysis and functional liver tests as well as plasma cytokine profiling to identify any patterns of drug induced toxicity. Post‐weaning, whole‐tissue (liver), and plasma were collected from the parous dams. Histological analysis of the liver revealed no drug‐induced pathological findings, and no biochemical evidence of liver injury post‐drug exposure (Fig 2H–L). Immune system and inflammatory recovery were consistent with base‐line levels of the control at 5 weeks postpartum. Immune transition from pregnancy to non‐pregnancy remained within a healthy range (Fig 2M).

Prenatal RG7916 Embryonic Exposure Substantially Improves Phenotypic Outcomes in SMNΔ7 Mice

By terminating treatment at birth, we have assessed prenatal RG7916 embryo exposure and the impact this has on both phenotypic outcome and pathological hallmarks of SMA. Phenotypic appearance is improved, with mice looking significantly healthier (P13) following prenatal treatment compared to untreated mice (P13) (Fig 3). We monitored weight, motor function, and survival until humane endpoint in SMNΔ7 mice. Weight and righting reflex were recorded every 2 days. Daily administration to the pregnant dam from E9.5–E19.5 increased the weight of SMA mice compared to vehicle treated SMA mice, with a significant shift in weight gain toward an earlier developmental stage resembling that of healthy control littermates until approximately P13. Ability to right was significantly improved from day of birth following prenatal treatment, motor function was comparable to that of a healthy littermate control. Prenatal treatment with RG7916 alone significantly improved weight and motor function compared to postnatal treatment with either gene therapy (AAV9) or ASO (Fig S1A, B). Survival was also significantly improved by prenatal RG7916 treatment compared to vehicle treated SMA mice. Prenatal embryo‐exposure to RG7916 significantly improves survival compared to postnatal ASO (14μg) and is not significantly different from survival rate of SMA mice treated postnatally with AAV9‐SMN1 (Fig S1C).

SMN Protein Levels Remain Elevated at Postnatal Day 13 in the Spinal Cord and Skeletal Muscle Following Prenatal‐Only Administration of RG7916

Following maternal‐fetal transfer of RG7916, we investigated whether SMN protein would remain elevated at P13, despite the lack of postnatal‐therapy. We extracted protein from brain, spinal cord, and skeletal muscle at P13 and performed immunoblot analysis. At P13 SMN levels were not maintained in the brain (Fig 4A, B). There was a significant increase in SMN protein levels in the spinal cord and skeletal muscle between prenatal RG7916 treated mice and vehicle treated mice (Fig 4C–F). A lack of maintained SMN levels in the brain is likely because of the short half‐life (t 1/2) of RG7916, which is approximately 50 hours. 34 With termination of treatment at birth, and a half‐life of ~2 days for the SMN protein, the drug will have been substantially eliminated from the body by the time of immunoblot analysis. 35 Despite this, the early increase in SMN protein expression during gestation contributed to the increased survival of the SMA mice.

Prenatal Embryonic Exposure to RG7916 Is Not Sufficient to Fully Rescue and Maintain Motor Neurons in SMNΔ7 Mice

Following maternal‐fetal transfer of RG7916, we investigated both gross brain structural defects and neurodegeneration of lower motor neurons in the spinal cord. Reports of reduced visual acuity caused by optic nerve hypoplasia and global developmental delay, in the only case study to date of an infant treated with risdiplam via transplacental passage, led us to examine gross brain pathology in our mice. On macroscopic investigation of the brains from prenatally treated mice, there is a significant increase in brain volume (Fig 5). To determine whether prenatal therapy has an impact on motor neuron integrity, defined by a count‐based metric of viable neurons in the ventral horn of the lumbar spinal cord, we assessed the extent of neuronal loss and soma size, as well as plasma NfL levels. Although there appears to be a trend to protection of motor neurons, we demonstrate prenatal therapy alone is not sufficient to fully maintain motor neuron counts. Nonetheless, soma size is rescued in SMNΔ7 mice at P13. In addition to neuronal integrity, we assessed NfL levels in plasma from prenatally treated mice. NfL levels are significantly elevated at P13 following prenatal treatment.

Prenatal Embryonic Exposure to RG7916 Restores Postsynaptic Motor Endplate Size in SMNΔ7 Mice

One of the earliest and most prominent hallmarks of SMA pathology seen in both patients and murine models is NMJ dysfunction. 36 In murine models, the NMJ begins to form around approximately E12.5 and continues to mature throughout the perinatal period. In SMA, there is a significant disruption to normal development of the NMJ during the first 2 weeks post‐birth. For this reason and to fully address neuromuscular defects in SMA pathophysiology, it is likely that therapy needs to be delivered during or before NMJ maturation. We investigated the effects of prenatal RG7916 treatment on pre‐ and post‐synaptic pathology toward the end of the perinatal period when NMJs should be fully matured (Fig 6). We demonstrate a partial restoration of presynaptic health, although pathology remained at P13 despite prenatal therapy, a modest reduction in NfL accumulation was seen. Endplate occupancy remained similar between RG7916 prenatally treated SMNΔ7 mice compared to vehicle treated mice. Moreover, we did see encouraging postsynaptic modifications. Embryonic‐exposure to RG7916 via maternal‐fetal transfer during development rescued the size of the postsynaptic motor endplate. This could represent either a motor‐neuron cell autonomous phenotype whereby activity of the remaining motor neurons is enhanced, or additional sprouting of the endplate occurs to keep up with hypertrophic muscle rescue as a result of prenatal SMN protein exposure.

Prenatal Embryonic Exposure to RG7916 Restores Skeletal Muscle to a Healthy Physiological State in SMNΔ7 Mice

Some studies demonstrate that the SMN protein is critical in the initial maturation of myofibers, with intrinsic muscle defects seen before significant motor neuron death. 37 We investigated whether exposure to RG7916 during gestation could rescue myofiber area and maturity (Fig 7). Myofiber area in tibialis anterior skeletal muscle was significantly increased following prenatal treatment with RG7916 compared to vehicle treatment. The number of centrally located nuclei was also significantly decreased in prenatally RG7916 treated mice compared to vehicle treated mice demonstrating muscle maturation and repair continues post‐birth despite termination of therapy at P0. Percentage area of connective tissue remained unchanged across all groups. Induction of SMN protein expression during early development may prevent intrinsic muscle defects, enough to maintain overall muscle health later in life.

Discussion

Although the therapeutic landscape of SMA has drastically changed over the last decade, attributable to 3 effective DMTs, individuals are still not cured from this devastating neuromuscular disorder. Although the trajectory of the disease is fundamentally altered, patients with SMA who have a lower SMN2 copy number and/or are more symptomatic at the time of treatment may show varying degrees of muscle, respiratory, and bulbar weakness that still require ongoing care, monitoring, and supportive therapies. 6 , 19 Timing of treatment initiation has a significant impact on patient outcome. 23 , 24 , 38 We now know starting therapy before symptom onset, using newborn screening efforts, has the best therapeutic outcome. 8 With a focus on bridging the gap between existing treatments and achieving full functional motor recovery we have sought out to investigate the effects of prenatal‐embryo targeted exposure to risdiplam (RG7916), without postnatal therapeutic intervention.

In this study, we have begun to uncover the safety and efficacy profile of prenatal maternal‐fetal transplacental delivery of RG7916 by administering the drug during pregnancy to dams with SMA‐affected fetuses in the SMNΔ7 mouse model. Our initial investigations provide insight into dam safety and tolerability. We demonstrate RG7916 (10mg/kg) remains safe for both dam and pup post‐birth with significant phenotypic improvement seen in SMA‐affected pups that received prenatal therapy. Although most pregnancies continued through to full term, we did see multiple cases of miscarriage and reduced litter size compared to controls undergoing the same procedure. Although oral gavage procedures are highly stressful for the pregnant dam, loss of pregnancy did only occur in the RG7916 treatment group, therefore, a drug‐related contribution cannot be ruled out. Nonetheless, group size effects may also contribute to these results, and therefore, we caution such conclusions without further confirmation of true drug impact. As risdiplam is predominantly metabolized by the liver we set out to identify any patterns of drug induced toxicity or immune system activation in the pregnant dam. 39 , 40 We assessed liver enzyme clinical chemistry and immune system function. No biochemical evidence of liver injury post‐drug exposure was evident in treated dams. Immune system and inflammatory recovery were also consistent with baseline levels of control dams. This supports previous clinical trial data whereby no significant changes in liver function tests were observed, with no cases of drug induced liver toxicity. 41

RG7916 treated litters survived postnatally allowing phenotypic assessment to be conducted post‐birth. We performed weight, motor function, and survival analysis on maternal‐fetal treated pups that received either RG7916 or vehicle. Our embryonic administration of an SMN2 splicing modifier was sufficient to substantially increase the weight and improve motor function of SMA mice, back to that of the healthy littermates in the neonatal period (P0–P15). This improvement in phenotypic outcome, beyond what is reported by postnatal therapeutics, demonstrates the significance of early intervention and a possible influence on overt metabolic homeostasis. 25 , 42 Ultimately, survival was significantly improved with lifespan increasing by over 2.5‐fold in SMA mice that received RG7916 via transplacental transfer compared to vehicle. Of note, we saw a plateau in weight of SMA mice after P15 as well as a sharp decline in survival after P36. This is likely attributable to the recurrence of pathology in the absence of ongoing therapeutic intervention postnatally. This highlights a critical therapeutic window for treatment of SMA to improve on rescue observed by postnatal treatment.

Having improved on survival by embryonic SMN protein exposure, we collected tissue for histopathological analysis at P13, an otherwise late‐pathological stage in untreated SMNΔ7 mice. First, we performed immunoblot analysis of brain, spinal cord, and skeletal muscle to determine whether SMN protein levels were elevated postnatally. We observed a significant increase in SMN protein levels in the spinal cord and skeletal muscle between prenatally RG7916 treated mice and vehicle treated mice. This result demonstrates prenatal exposure to a risdiplam‐like compound can produce a cumulative increase in SMN protein lasting approximately 2 weeks postnatally. Therefore, SMN protein restoration during prenatal development is sufficient to rescue the mouse and prevent the SMA phenotype for an extended period. However, our data also shows that continued treatment post‐birth is necessary to maintain and add on to the benefits observed from prenatal treatment.

Next, we began pathological examination on the spinal cord, specifically through quantification of motor neuron number and size. We found prenatal treatment alone did not completely maintain postnatal motor neuron counts, but did rescue soma size quantified at P13. Considering a significant increase in SMA survival, and a trend toward increased motor neuron number, we speculate that motor neurons may have been rescued prenatally yet degenerate post‐birth because of the lack of continued postnatal treatment. This aligns with the known requirement of SMN protein to be highest in the third trimester of fetal development and the first 3 months of life. 5 , 24 Furthermore, the added impact of an “oxygen shift” day of birth will likely have contributed to the vulnerability of motor neurons in the disease state. 43 Combined with our termination of SMN protein therapy on the day of birth, oxidative stress, and a drop in SMN protein levels was likely detrimental to a complete recovery. This strongly supports the notion that prenatal‐postnatal therapeutics will be required for SMA. Of note, we do not rule out the possibility of greater benefit had treatment been administered even earlier than E9.5 in the mouse model. 3

Furthermore, we investigated multiple pathological hallmarks of the NMJ including occupancy, accumulation, and endplate size. 44 , 45 , 46 We found motor neuron occupancy at the NMJ was not improved on following prenatal therapy, likely because of a lack of change in motor neuron numbers. Although not significant, we do see a trend in reduction of presynaptic NfL accumulation at endplates suggesting a possible reduction in active degeneration, this may signify postponing of disease pathology as a result of prenatal treatment. In support of this hypothesis, we also see a significant increase in endplate size. This suggests prenatal SMN protein restoration may drive improved signaling mechanisms from the remaining motor neurons, thereby directly affecting postsynaptic maturity. 47 This potential recovery from structural disruptions at the NMJ may have resulted in better communication between the motor neurons and the muscle fibers, which may in turn promote muscle fiber growth. Alternatively, additional synaptic sprouting may occur to keep up with hypertrophic muscle.

We did see a significant increase in myofiber area and reduction in centralized nuclei demonstrating prenatal therapy alone can substantially improve muscle health and maturity in severe SMA mice. Although we consider the possibility of improved motor neuron function despite low counts, this phenomenon could be independent of the neuron and a result of improved muscle intrinsic health. We demonstrate early SMN protein restoration is enough to rescue and maintain muscle health postnatally. As skeletal muscle satellite cells play a crucial role in maintenance and repair of muscle, we speculate that embryonic SMN protein was sufficient to correct dysregulated myogenic processes in muscle stem cells during myogenesis (E10.5+). 48 This supports the notion of a prenatal etiology for muscle pathophysiology.

As the “dying back” hypothesis would suggest, withdrawal of presynaptic nerve terminals may be because of protein disruption in the muscle, this theory can be used to explain our findings such that the rescued muscle impacts only postsynaptic pathology at the NMJ. 46 Changes in postsynaptic endplate size may be a consequence of improved myogenesis during development, postnatal maturation, and ultimately muscle growth as a direct result of embryonic‐exposure to SMN protein. Increased sprouting encourages contact with the growing muscle, but it does not mean synaptic efficiency was improved on. Further work is required to determine functional impacts of this therapy including, but not limited, to compound muscle action potential and motor unit number estimation.

Fundamental toxicology studies that informed the only case study to date on maternal transfer of risdiplam in humans by Finkel et al, 49 identified a high‐dose of risdiplam (12 mg/kg) to have toxic effects in multiple rodent models. We acknowledge the use of a similarly high‐dose of RG7916 (10mg/kg) in the present study, and the possibility that such a dose may also be toxic and contribute to our data displaying a few unsuccessful pregnancies and a lack of complete rescue in the motor neurons. For this reason, a drug‐related contribution to pregnancy loss cannot be ruled out. Additional work is required to understand toxicity of this dose and possible off‐target splicing of genes such as FOXM1 and MADD, involved in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. 49 With so little known about the effect of risdiplam in utero, this work will help inform timing and dose of treatment for both dam and pup.

These findings support the concept that intervention during early development may improve disease outcomes in SMA. Translation to the clinic will require consideration of the feasibility of carrier testing and prenatal diagnosis, optimization of therapeutic delivery during fetal development, and careful evaluation of maternal and fetal safety. In addition, developmental differences between mice and humans necessitate further studies in larger animal models and long‐term assessment of efficacy before clinical application can be considered.

Overall, we have explored embryo‐only drug exposure through maternal‐fetal transfer of the risdiplam‐like compound RG7916. This has enabled us to isolate the effects of prenatal RG7916 without the influence of postnatal therapies. RG7916 delivered via maternal‐fetal transplacental transfer rescues and maintains muscle health and postsynaptic components of the NMJ. As we know, motor neurons have the earliest and largest requirement for SMN protein perinatally, we conclude a combination of prenatal and postnatal therapy must be used for optimum therapeutic outcomes. Although we continue efforts to identify the critical window for the treatment of SMA, in utero therapeutic intervention will require a fine balance between dose and timing of treatment with careful consideration on which DMTs to combine as we develop peri‐neonatal therapeutic strategies.
