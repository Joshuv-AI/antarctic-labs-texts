Optimization Analysis of In-Situ Conversion and Displacement in Continental Shale Reservoirs
Authors: Zhaobin Zhang, Maryelin Josefina
Briceño Montilla, Shouding Li, Xiao Li, Yanzhi Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of growing global energy demands and the need for efficient extraction techniques, this research, based on numerical analysis, addresses the high-energy demands of in situ conversion by introducing a two-stage development strategy. The strategy begins with an initial continuous heating stage, followed by a thermal stabilization stage. It culminates in a hydrocarbon production stage, which is divided into primary recovery and water injection-enhanced recovery. The findings demonstrate that the reservoir temperature continues to increase even after the stop of heating. Consequently, the reactions within the reservoir persist, leading to increased hydrocarbon generation. The heating stage also helps restore reservoir pressure, enabling high production rates of hydrocarbons during the first year of primary recovery. However, natural depletion subsequently occurs, requiring an enhanced oil recovery (EOR) method. While water injection is a viable EOR method, it proves less effective due to high water breakthroughs in the producer well. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis reveals that hydrocarbon generation and production are closely related to the calibration of energy input and the duration of injection. These results underscore the critical importance of precise energy management and injection timing in optimizing hydrocarbon recovery. By enhancing our understanding of the thermal dynamics and reaction kinetics within the reservoir, this research contributes to the development of more efficient and sustainable extraction technologies, ultimately improving the feasibility of commercial shale oil production.

Introduction

Continental shale oil reservoirs are lacustrine, organic-rich shales containing oil and high kerogen content.1 These organic-rich shales are usually in the immature to early mature stages, including converted but not expelled oil.2 Common enhanced recovery methods include chemical flooding3,4 and in situ heating techniques. Due to thermal maturation, the in situ heating methods has emerged as a promising technique for extracting these resources.5 This process involves heating the shale formations to high temperatures, which enables the thermal decomposition of kerogen into producible hydrocarbons,6 subsequently reducing the heavy oil viscosity, and improving its flow characteristics.7 In-situ heat technologies are categorized based on different heating modes, which include the wellbore electrical heating, steam injection, low-frequency electrical heating method, hot gas injection, etc.(6,8,9) These methods provide opportunities to extract oil from continental shale oil formations that are otherwise inaccessible with conventional methods.10

However, these formations exhibit complex geology with high heterogeneity in lithological and structural characteristics,11 which presents a significant challenge in optimizing in situ conversion techniques. Therefore, the process requires precise control of heating rates and uniform temperature distribution to be effective. Ensuring that the shale formation reaches the necessary temperature for kerogen decomposition without excessive energy loss is crucial for maximizing efficiency and production yields. Recent studies have shown advancements in in situ thermal methods for continental shale oil recovery.2,12 These include analyses of the effects of temperature on porosity and permeability, revealing how heat improves these reservoir properties.13,14 However, as noted by Brandt.,15 a significant amount of energy is required to perform in situ conversion of shale. This underscores the high-energy demand required for organic matter decomposition and highlights the importance of understanding the process in detail to ensure its efficiency. By comprehending the effects of temperature distribution and reservoir properties on kerogen decomposition and hydrocarbon production, strategies can be implemented to optimize the process and maximize production, ultimately enhancing the recovery of shale oil.

The in situ conversion process for shale oil extraction has shown promise through numerical and experimental analyses.16,17 For example, Fan et al.18 and Huang et al.19 have established the relationship between well spacing and patterns with energy and production efficiency. Egboga et al.20 identified that increased reservoir pressure is a key factor for improved recovery. Song et al.21 proposed using multilateral wells to maintain stable and continuous heat injection. Gao et al.22 demonstrated the spacing between hydraulic fractures might influence the recovery efficiency greatly. Using neural networks, Liu et al.23 proposed a computational framework to redefine the performance of the ICP method, identifying seven main factors affecting reservoir temperature and hydrocarbon production, including the heat injection rate and temperature. Lee et al.24 found that natural fracture spacing impacts hydrocarbon and water production. These analyses provide reliable insights into property behaviors and allow for the evaluation of various operational variables to enhance oil recovery. However, an optimization process that combines primary production and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) methods has seldom been studied.

Generally, numerical models of in situ conversion are designed with continued injection and simultaneous production.18,19,24 This is due to the fluids generated by decomposition saturating the pore space left by kerogen, helping restore the pressure of shale reservoirs. However, this production strategy leads to a rapid decline in reservoir energy as the temperature increases reaches the production well, and fluid production starts.25 In this context, to promote reservoir energy restoration and more efficient energy utilization, a considerable period of heat injection before production is initiated is the most suitable technique.

For the production strategy of shale reservoirs, the combination of horizontal wells with multiple transverse fractures makes primary production by pressure depletion the most viable recent technique for hydrocarbon extraction.26 However, field analysis shows that the production period of shale oil wells declines rapidly and then remains low for an extended period, barely reaching a recovery factor of approximately 7%.27,28 This rapid decline is due to the low permeability and complex pore structure of shale formations, which hinder the flow of hydrocarbons once the initial pressure is depleted. Therefore, EOR methods are necessary to improve recovery rates. Injection of fluids is an effective method to increase oil recovery,29,30 with water injection appearing as a viable option to achieve the sweeping process and improve reservoir pressure and connectivity.31

Under this approach, our research focuses on developing an optimization strategy for shale oil production using a self-developed simulator. The strategy integrates the in situ conversion and production processes to enhance heating efficiency and maximize hydrocarbon output. To achieve optimal heat distribution, the injection process is divided into a continuous heating phase and a thermal stabilization stage. This is followed by a hydrocarbon production phase consisting of five years of primary recovery and five years of enhanced recovery. By optimizing these processes, our research contributes to more efficient and cost-effective methods for extracting valuable resources from shale reservoirs, ultimately enhancing the overall viability and sustainability of shale oil production.

Numerical Method

In this research, a self-developed Thermal-Flow-Chemical (TFC) coupling simulator is employed (accessible at IGG-Hydrate: https://gitee.com/geomech/hydrate) which has demonstrated successful application in previous studies.25,32,33 The model involves several complex physical processes, including thermal energy transformations, chemical reactions, and multiphase and multicomponent flow. Therefore, some assumptions to simplify the processes are required:

1.The model assumes five separated phases: gaseous, aqueous, light oil, heavy oil, and solid. In the gaseous phase, two components are identified: water steam and methane. The aqueous phase consists solely of liquid water. In the solid phase, two components, kerogen and char, are recognized. Importantly, because of their substantial differences in characteristics, heavy oil and light oil are regarded as different oily phases.

Chemical Model

For the chemical model, an equilibrium reaction model is employed to capture the decomposition and cracking process centered on reservoir-scale and multiyear development. Given that, the heating occurs for prolonged times, the chemical reactions primarily depend on the energy supply at the reservoir scale. Consequently, the detailed capture of kinetic reactions becomes less important. In this context, it is assumed that chemical reactions can be computed independently from thermal-flow coupled processes. A threshold temperature for chemical reactions is set; when exceeding these temperatures, the decomposition reaction occurs; resulting in the absorption of heat and a subsequent decrease in temperature; when the temperature drops below the threshold value, the reaction stops.

To simulate the thermal-flow-chemical coupling processes, an explicit iteration schemes is developed: Initially, the essential fluid properties and chemical reactions are defined to establish a comprehensive computational framework. Then, the computation unfolds as follows: first, the multiphase multicomponent flow process is calculated, ignoring heat transfer and chemical reactions. Next, thermal conduction within the rock matrix is computed to update temperatures in each grid cell. Finally, potential reactions within each grid cell are computed, updating component masses and cell temperatures while disregarding thermal conduction effects on the flow.

Model Settings

The central and western regions of China possess abundant shale oil resources, with the Chang 7 reservoir being a typical example of a medium to low-maturity continental shale oil reservoir.38,39 The Chang 7 reservoir is characterized by highly developed horizontal beddings, thus resulting in typical transversely isotropic properties. Within its matrix, the reservoir contains significant amounts of low-maturity kerogen, along with considerable quantities of heavy oil, light oil, and natural gas. Additionally, the effective thickness of the reservoir is approximately several tens of meters.2,40

In this context, the computational model was developed by considering the characteristics of the Chang 7 shale oil reservoir, as shown in Figure 2. The computational model is divided into three layers in the vertical direction, from top to bottom: a 15-m-thick overlying layer, a 30-m-thick reservoir layer, and a 15-m-thick underlying layer. The overlying and the underlying layers are considered to simulate heat dissipation resulting from adjacent layers. Given that rocks are poor conductors of heat, two-layer horizontal heating wells are used to heat the reservoir. Considering the symmetry of the model, the actual computational area is represented by the vertical two-dimensional area indicated by the purple box in the figure. The total height of this computational area is 60 m, with a width of 15 m, divided into 200 × 50 computational grids. Constant temperature boundaries are established at the top and bottom of the computational area, while adiabatic boundaries are configured on the left and right sides. For the overlying and underlying layers, a permeability of zero is assumed. Other specific parameters are shown in Table 1.

Based on this computational setup, the simulation covers 25 years in total, consisting of a 15-year heating stage and a subsequent 10-year production stage. The heating stage is further subdivided into two substages, comprising a 10-year constant power heating stage and a 5-year thermal equilibrium stage. Similarly, the production stage can be subdivided into two substages: a 5-year primary recovery stage and a 5-year stage of Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) by water injection. The reason behind setting a 5-year equilibrium stage is to promote the transfer of heat from the high-temperature zone to the low-temperature zone, thereby maximizing energy utilization efficiency. Similarly, the purpose of setting a 5-year water injection stage is to optimize the recovery of oil and gas.

The kerogen’s decomposition and heavy oil cracking reactions are a simplified adaptation of the Braun and Burnham.47 and Lee et al.24 models. Consequently, the model consists of the decomposition of kerogen into two liquid hydrocarbons and gas, followed by heavy oil cracking into light hydrocarbon, gas, and char. Additionally, the generation of water and a single type of char are considered. The simulator is set to compute the reaction base on mass factor decomposition with stoichiometry factors derived from kerogen decomposition studies, and laboratory experiments.48−50 To enable the kerogen decomposition in our model, it is necessary to provide enough heat injection to exceed the temperature threshold required for the reaction. These values are adopted based on Lee et al.51 modifications, with corresponding reaction temperatures of 565 and 603 K, respectively.

As previously mentioned, water is considered in the generation of decomposition products, in addition to establishing a certain water saturation in place in the reservoir. Therefore, the phase transitions of water vaporization and condensation are considered in the model. Which are governed by their respective enthalpy changes and temperature thresholds. Thus, to delineate precisely the timing and mechanism of these transitions, the Antoine equation52 is used to characterize vapor pressure as a function of local temperature.

Results

Key Features of the Heating Stage

In the preceding section, the in situ conversion model is established, and the entire process is delineated into heating and production stages. During the heating stage, production wells remain closed, and a constant power heat injection is maintained over the initial 10-year period, after which a 5-year equilibrium stage is initialized, through which heating is stopped. In this stage, the in situ conversion process takes place, and kerogen and heavy components decompose as temperatures rise. Recognizing temperature evolution as the fundamental driving force behind the entire in situ conversion process, comprehending its dynamics becomes imperative. Therefore, the evolution of the temperature field and the decomposition of kerogen will be analyzed first, including the distribution and evolution of the substance generated over the 15 years simulation period.

Temperature Evolution

In the in situ conversion process with heaters, heat conduction serves as the primary mechanism for heating the rock formation. The heaters generate heat, which is then transferred to the surrounding rock through heat conduction. This process involves the movement of heat energy from hotter regions (near the heaters) to cooler regions (deeper within the reservoir) through the solid material of the rock. As heat is conducted through the rock, it causes the temperature within the reservoir to increase. This heat conduction process can be observed in the temperature field contour depicted in Figure 3. The injection sources serve as the primary heat sources, distributing heat to the most distant areas of the reservoir. These sources enable the temperature to increase from 400K to 600K within 5 years. During the equilibrium stage, the entire reservoir achieves a uniform temperature, with heat energy evenly distributed throughout.

Reservoir temperature fields at different time points are presented as follows: (a) Reservoir temperature field at 5 years. (b) Reservoir temperature field at 10 years. (c) Reservoir temperature field at 15 years.

By analyzing the evolution of average temperature in reservoir, apparent variation trend can be observed. Figure 4a illustrates these patterns over the 10-years constant power heating stage and the subsequent 5-years thermal equilibrium stage. During the heating stage, the average temperature steadily rises from 350K to approximately 600K, with the rate of temperature change gradually diminishing from 30K per year to about 20K per year by the 10th year. Following the closure of the heating well, the reservoir temperature declines continuously over the equilibrium stage, approximately dropping from 600K to 570K over the 5 years. Additionally, the rate of temperature change slows down, decreasing from approximately 10K per year immediately after the heating well closure to about 4K per year by the end of the 15th year. The shifting characteristics of the average reservoir temperature highlight the inevitability of heat loss through the boundary layers. Throughout the constant power heating stage, the gradual decline in the rate of temperature change is linked to the phenomenon that higher reservoir temperatures lead to accelerated heat loss rates. Similarly, during the equilibrium stage, the sustained decrease in the average reservoir temperature predominantly results from heat loss through the boundaries.

In the in situ conversion, the effectiveness of heating conduction lies in the ability to reach the decomposition temperatures of kerogen and heavy oil. Once the temperature surpasses the decomposition temperatures, excessive heat becomes wasteful. Hence, in this research, the heated ratio is introduced. Illustrated in Figure 4b, this ratio is defined as the proportion of the reservoir volume reaching the decomposition or cracking temperature to the total volume. By measuring this ratio, this metric offers insight into how effectively the heat is being used. Since, by monitoring changes in the heated ratio over time, it is possible to assess the effectiveness of the heating conduction process and optimize heat utilization for in situ conversion. The heated ratio analysis is close related with the evolution of the heat in the reservoir, demonstrated the following characteristic:

During the initial 10 years, the reservoir undergoes active heating through constant power injection. The heating ratio gradually increases with a progressively accelerating rate. After the closure of the heating well, despite the gradual decline in the average reservoir temperature, the heating ratio continues to rise. Therefore, the evolution of the heated ratio follows a distinct pattern as the heat propagates from the injection points to distant areas within the reservoir, which can be described as follows:

1.Initial Absorption (0 years-P1): At the beginning of the heating stage, the reservoir starts to absorb heat from the injection points. The heated ration rise is relatively slow as the reservoir initially begins to warm up.

Therefore, the temperature evolution plays a critical role in the occurrences of the reactions of the reservoir. Water and Steam Behavior

In the process of in situ conversion, water and steam play crucial roles. Initially, the reservoir contains water, and as kerogen and heavy oil decompose, water is produced. Figure 7 illustrates the temporal and spatial evolution of water and steam. In regions close to the heating well, where temperatures are high, steam emerges, while the water content decreases. After the heating is closed, steam near the well may condense back into liquid water. During the 10-year constant power heating stage, as shown in Figure 8, steam generation remains relatively stable. However, after shutting down the heating well, steam content decreases rapidly, with all steam condensing into liquid water by approximately the 11th year, one year postwell closure. Despite high reservoir temperatures during in situ conversion, elevated fluid pressure results in a high boiling point for water, causing steam to exist transiently only in regions near the heating well with the highest temperatures.

The findings indicate a significant reliance on kerogen decomposition on temperature, leading to increased decomposition rates and subsequent substance generation during constant heat injection. However, the equilibrium phase demonstrates promising outcomes, suggesting that despite its short duration, heat propagation persists during this stage, enabling temperature increases to propagate and facilitating the generation of additional products. Additionally, the spatial distribution of the generated substances reveals the complex interplay between rock properties and fluid distribution, primarily influenced by the low permeability of the rock.

Key Features of the Production Stage

Following the heating phase, wherein the decomposition process establishes the hydrocarbon generation, the production stage plays a pivotal role in reaching the maximum fluid extraction in the reservoir. Within the production stage, the delineation of two distinct substages is presented: a 5-year primary recovery process followed by a calibrated 5-year enhanced oil recovery by water injection.

In the in situ conversion process, the full filling of the hydrocarbons released by decomposition in the porous volume leads to pressure increases in the reservoir.25 This pressure restauration in the reservoir results in sufficient energy to force hydrocarbons toward production wells driven by the pressure differential. Therefore, immediately starting the production stage, the optimal recovery of hydrocarbons ensues concurrently with the most substantial depletion of hydrocarbon reserves within the reservoir (Figure 9a). Here, the properties of the hydrocarbons control the rate of production rates. Where a notable trend is observed for the decrease in rates to be more pronounced for, less viscous and less dense products (Figure 9b). Specifically, the gas exhibits more accelerated production compared to its heavier, more viscous counterparts. This implies that the total gases generated in the in situ process will be extracted through primary recovery.

Evolution of (a) the mass of in situ hydrocarbons and (b) the mass and production rate of substances produced from the production well.

Primary production occurs without any pressure maintenance; therefore, the reservoir pressure decays rapidly, approximately two years after production begins. Consequently, the production decreases. This indicates a decrease in reservoir energy driven by fluid drainage, which allows the equilibrium pressure between the reservoir and the producing well to be reached. This energy decrease is a typical behavior in naturally drained reservoirs. During this process, recovery generally yields only a fraction of the total hydrocarbons within a reservoir, typically ranging between 5% and 20% of the original oil in place. Thus, a considerable quantity of hydrocarbons remains in the reservoir (Figure 9a). This demonstrates that the primary recovery method becomes ineffective over time, requiring the use of secondary recovery techniques to extract the additional hydrocarbons. In this context, for the next 5 years, the heat injection wells will be repurposed as water injection wells to displace the remaining fluid in the reservoir through a water-driven mechanism.

The results indicate that the water injection method initially boosts hydrocarbon production rates (Figure 9b). However, these production rates remain significantly lower than the initial rates observed during primary recovery. This discrepancy can be attributed to the relatively low increase in pressure compared to the initial reservoir pressure during primary recovery. This modest increase is likely due to several factors, including the uneven distribution of remaining hydrocarbons, which may not be uniformly displaced by the injected water, and the significant depletion the reservoir has already undergone during primary recovery, resulting in a substantial decline in reservoir energy and pressure, which water injection can only partially restore.

However, half a year after the start of steam injection, the production rates of all fluids decrease rapidly, and approximately five years after injection, the production rates of all fluids approach zero. In this case, the pressure of the reservoir decreases, but as water continues to permeate through the reservoir, displacing hydrocarbons, the effectiveness of water flooding diminishes. This decline can be attributed to the onset of water breakthroughs reaching the production well. Accordingly, as water saturation near the production wells rises, it leads to a simultaneous decrease in the saturation of other fluids. Based on Figure 10, it is evident that by the end of the water injection stage, particularly in proximity to the production wells, water saturation within the reservoir reaches notably high levels. This elevated water saturation substantially hampers the production of other fluids.

Overall, the production stages outlined in the provided analysis depict a comprehensive approach to hydrocarbon extraction following the heating phase in the in situ conversion process. The analysis highlights challenge inherent in the production stages, such as the rapid decline in production rates following water flooding due to water breakthroughs and high-water saturation near production wells. Addressing these challenges requires careful consideration and potentially the development of mitigation strategies to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the production process. By leveraging insights into fluid dynamics, recovery techniques, and reservoir behavior, operators can make informed decisions to maximize hydrocarbon recovery and optimize overall process performance. Consequently, the effects of key variables on fluid production are analyzed in the next sections.

Heating Strategy and Fluid Production

The main objective of in situ heat injection methods in oil shale reservoirs is to maximize the efficiency and recovery rate of liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons. Raising temperatures within the reservoir accelerate the kerogen decomposition process and facilitate the migration of hydrocarbons to producing wells, resulting in increased oil and gas production. Previous analyses of the production stage underscored the imperative to refine the heating process. Consequently, diverse heating strategies offer valuable insights into potential optimization approaches.

Effects of Heating Energy

Based on the analysis conducted in the preceding section, it is evident that the process of decomposition and fluid production during in situ conversion is driven by temperature evolution. To assess the impact of energy input on fluid production, several simulations were conducted wherein the heating power was adjusted to regulate the total energy input over the 10-year heating period. Subsequently, variations in fluid production corresponding to different levels of energy input were observed, as depicted in Figure 11. The analysis reveals that the development of in situ conversion unfolds in four distinct modes depending on the levels of energy input:

1.Insufficient energy: The figure illustrates that when the total energy is less than 2 × 104 kWh, production becomes negligible. This can be attributed to two main factors. First, owing to relatively low injection power regulated by heat conduction, energy is distributed into the surrounding area, hindering the attainment of sufficiently high temperatures. Second, the use of separate wells for heating and production results in spatial distance between them. Kerogen and heavy oil decomposition initiates from the heating well, but due to limited total energy input, decomposition primarily occurs in its vicinity. This is evident from the component distribution depicted in Figure 12, where even after 15 years, compositional changes are localized near the heating well, while the majority reservoir remains unaltered. Considering these factors, fluid production does not exhibit a linear correlation with energy input. Instead, energy input surpasses a certain threshold is significant fluid production.

In summary, as total injected energy increases, four different modes of in situ conversion development emerge. In practical development processes, it is imperative to steer clear of both insufficient and excessive energy states. Depending on the desired composition of the produced fluid, adjusting energy input accordingly is crucial. To prioritize the production of light oil and gas, higher energy input may be warranted, while for heavy oil production and enhanced energy utilization efficiency, lower power heating should be considered to enter the heavy oil priority mode.

High power and short-duration conditions favor the production of light oil and gas. This is because higher heating power results in elevated reservoir temperatures. Since the critical temperature for heavy oil decomposition is higher than that for kerogen, these elevated temperatures enhance the decomposition of heavy oil into light oil and gas. Therefore, higher power and shorter duration heating schemes are more conducive to light oil and gas production.

Conversely, under conditions of low power and long duration, heavy oil production is favored. Lower power and longer heating durations allow for more extensive heat redistribution within the reservoir, leading to a larger heated reservoir volume and lower reservoir temperature. Since the temperature required for kerogen decomposition is relatively low, and the temperature required for further heavy oil decomposition is higher, lower reservoir temperatures promote increased heavy oil generation and hinder heavy oil decomposition into light oil and gas. Therefore, low power and long duration heating schemes are optimal for maximizing heavy oil production.

Discussion

Limitations of the Model

For continental shale oil reservoirs, in situ conversion is a promising development scheme. This study applied numerical simulation to analyze the temperature field and evolution of various components, and further investigated the influence of total energy and heating power on the in situ conversion process. It must be acknowledged that the numerical model is a simplification of the actual problem, including:

1.Reservoir heterogeneity: For simplicity, the analysis did not consider the inherent heterogeneity of real reservoirs when illustrating temperature and fluid evolution characteristics. Including this heterogeneity would significantly increase the complexity of modeling temperature and component evolution.

Reservoir heterogeneity: For simplicity, the analysis did not consider the inherent heterogeneity of real reservoirs when illustrating temperature and fluid evolution characteristics. 2.Activation of fractures: During the heating process, changes in the temperature field could induce significant thermal stresses, and material decomposition could alter pore pressure. Consequently, alterations in stress and pore pressure might activate and propagate fractures, thereby modifying permeability. Considering the reservoir stress model suggests a computational demand. Therefore, the activation of fractures was not taken into account during the heating process.

Consequently, when applying the computational results, especially quantitative results, to different formations, the differences between different formations must be fully considered, as well as the computational settings of this study.

Energy Sources

The implementation of in situ conversion methods faces significant challenges due to high-energy consumption required. This process involves heating the reservoir to high temperatures, sufficient to heat both the organic matter and the surrounding rock. Consequently, there is considerable heat consumption.

On the other hand, alternative energy sources like wind and solar energy have limitations due to the presence of unusable energy. This is partly because these sources exhibit periodicity and are heavily influenced by factors such as weather and seasons, leading to fluctuations in energy output. Those fluctuations may not perfectly align with the electric power demand. Therefore, unused electrical energy during peak production periods is wasted.

In-situ conversion simplifies the process by converting electrical energy into thermal energy without requiring stability in power over short periods.53 This allows surplus energy from peak production periods of wind and solar energy to be used for heating the formation. During periods of high grid demand for electricity, priority is given to supplying electrical energy generated from wind and solar energy to the grid. Conversely, during low grid demand periods, surplus energy is injected into the formation. This not only alleviates pressure on grid demand but also significantly reduces energy costs for the in situ process by utilizing surplus energy that would otherwise be wasted for heating purposes.

Fortunately, in China, continental shale reservoirs are mainly located in the central and western regions, which also happen to be rich in wind and solar energy resources.54 This makes it feasible to utilize surplus energy from wind and solar sources for heating purposes.

Conclusions

For medium- to low-maturity shale oil reservoirs, in situ conversion offers a promising development method. Given its involvement in complex physicochemical processes, it is crucial to address questions regarding the efficiency, feasibility, patterns, and characteristics of heat transfer and substance decomposition during in situ conversion. To this end, this study established a computational model for in situ conversion based on the geological characteristics of the Chang 7 reservoir. The entire development process was divided into two stages: a constant injection power stage and a production stage. The injection stage includes continuous heat injection and a thermal equilibrium stage, while the production stage involves primary recovery followed by enhanced oil recovery techniques. The study analyzed the temperature field and the evolution of various substance components in the reservoir and during production, leading to the following conclusions:

1.Research on the temperature field indicates that after heating is stopped, the equilibrium of heat within the reservoir will continue to increase the heating proportion of the reservoir at a given threshold. Therefore, the existence of an equilibrium stage after heating can enhance energy utilization efficiency.
