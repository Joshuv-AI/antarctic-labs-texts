Effects of dietary alginate oligosaccharides on meat quality, oxidative stability and metabolome of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers
Authors: Qing-Hua Yao, Yao-Yao Wang, Xue-Qing Ye, Zhong-Xiang Zhao, Hui-Mei Liu, Wen-Chao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress (HS) is one of the major challenges in the current poultry industry. HS leads to the deterioration of meat quality by inducing oxidative stress and metabolic disorders, which have a significant adverse impact on broiler production. Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), recognized for their natural antioxidant properties, may have the potential to improve the meat quality of broilers under HS. The current study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary AOS on meat quality, oxidative stability and metabolome of thigh muscle in broilers challenged to HS. A total of 108 29-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers were randomly divided into three treatment groups: (i) thermoneutral (TN) group (fed with basal diet); (ii) HS group (fed with basal diet); (iii) HS + AOS group (HS group supplemented with 600 mg/kg AOS). The feeding trial lasted for 14 d. The results showed that dietary supplementation with AOS increased pH45min, yellowness (b*) values, the total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) level, and the enzyme activities of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and glutathione S-transferase (GST), while decreasing drip loss, shear force and the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) of thigh muscle in broilers under HS (P < 0.05). Compared to HS group, untargeted metabolomics results suggested that the concentrations of six differential metabolites were increased, including eicosanoic acid, PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0), capric acid, suberic acid, gingerol, and 4-methyl-2-pentyl-1,3-dioxolane, and the differential metabolites were enriched in nine metabolic pathways, including fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, etc. in the HS + AOS group (P < 0.05). Moreover, dietary supplementation with AOS upregulated the mRNA expression level of Nrf2 and GSTT1, GPX4, NQO1, SOD1, SOD2, MafF, MafG, MafK, MEF2C, SREBP-1, FAS, and downregulated the mRNA expression level of Keap1 and GSTO1 in broilers under HS (P < 0.05). In conclusion, dietary supplementation with 600 mg/kg AOS could improve the oxidative stability and alleviate lipid metabolism disorders, ultimately helping prevent HS-induced meat deterioration in broilers. These findings suggest that dietary AOS is a promising strategy to enhance meat quality in heat-stressed broilers.Keywords: Alginate oligosaccharides, Antioxidant capacity, Heat stress, Lipid metabolism, Meat quality

Introduction

In the context of global climate change, the elevated ambient temperature-induced heat stress (HS) has become a prominent factor that affects modern intensive broiler production (Goel et al., 2021). HS poses significant challenges to broilers' health, growth performance, and overall product quality (Al Sulaiman et al., 2024). It has been confirmed that HS negatively impacts broiler growth by reducing feed intake and hindering growth and development (Pontalti et al., 2025). In addition to decreased growth performance, HS exposure enhances oxidative reactions in broilers (Shakeri et al., 2019), thereby compromising meat quality through lipid peroxidation (Love and Pearson, 1971), protein oxidation (Song and King, 2015), and alterations in muscle fiber structure (Kikusato and Toyomizu, 2019). The excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) caused by HS trigger oxidative muscle damage (Aryal et al., 2025). The Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway, which mediates the transcription of antioxidant genes, is recognized as the most critical pathway in cellular antioxidant defense (Dodson et al., 2019). Meanwhile, HS modulates fiber-type switching in skeletal muscle (Yamaguchi et al., 2010), and types of muscle fibers directly affect meat quality (Mo et al., 2023). Also, metabolic disturbances induced by HS lead to deterioration in meat quality (Nawaz et al., 2021). Overall, HS shows unfavorable effects on meat color, pH, water-holding capacity, and tenderness due to the appearance of oxidative damage, metabolic disturbances, and muscle fiber transformation disorder (Bošković Cabrol et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2022).

Functional feed additives are thought to regulate the meat quality of broilers during HS (Bogolyubova et al., 2022). Natural plant extracts are gaining attention as functional feed additives for broilers (Mahasneh et al., 2024; Oni et al., 2024; Saracila et al., 2021). Among these, particularly marine brown algae have drawn significant interest due to their rich bioactive compounds (Costa et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). Studies have demonstrated that bioactive substances from brown algae have beneficial roles in poultry, such as promote intestinal health (Oretomiloye and Adewole, 2024) and improve meat quality (Li et al., 2025). Alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) are derived from brown algae, characterized by a low molecular weight, strong water solubility, and high stability (Wang et al., 2021). AOS also exhibits various biological activities, including antioxidant (Wang et al., 2024), anti-inflammatory (Wang et al., 2020), anti-tumor (Yao et al., 2023), anti-apoptotic (Guo et al., 2017), immune regulatory (Hu et al., 2021), and growth-promoting (Le et al., 2022) effects. As a bioactive agent, AOS can regulate lipid metabolism and prevent lipid peroxidation in mice (Wang et al., 2020). Also, the derivatives of AOS (AOS-nano selenium composite) have been reported to improve muscle quality in broilers via suppressing HS-induced oxidative stress (Yang et al., 2023). However, the protective effects of AOS on meat quality of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers have not been revealed yet.

Accordingly, the objective of this study is to investigate the effects of AOS on the meat quality, oxidative status, and metabolome of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers. The findings can promote the development and application of functional feed additives derived from seaweeds in reducing the harm of HS to broilers.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval statement

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the College of Coastal Agricultural Sciences of Guangdong Ocean University (Approval No. 20230910) and conducted following the guidelines for the ethical treatment of animals.

Animals and experimental design

Charoen Pokphand Group (Zhanjiang, China) provided 150 one-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers. All broilers were fed the same initial diet and given unrestricted feed and water. At 29 d of age, a total of 108 broilers with similar body weights were selected and divided into three treatment groups according to a single-factor completely randomized experimental design. There were six replicate cages per group and six broilers per replicate. The broilers were allocated into: (i) thermoneutral (TN) group (fed with basal diet); (ii) HS group (fed with basal diet); (iii) HS + AOS group (HS group supplemented with 600 mg/kg AOS). The basal diet was prepared based on the AA broiler feeding standards, and its composition and nutrient levels are detailed in Table 1. All broilers were given unrestricted feed and water. The environmental temperature of the TN group was maintained at 24 ± 1 °C. For the HS group and the HS + AOS group, the temperature was maintained at 33 ± 1 °C from 8:00 to 18:00 (10 h per day), and during the remaining time, it was the same as that of the TN group. The relative humidity of each group was controlled at around 65 %. The feeding trial lasted for 14 d. The AOS was provided by Qingdao HEHAI Biotech Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China), and has a purity of 91.8 %. The composition primarily consists of d-mannose and l-gulose; its number-average molecular weight (Mn) is 1387 Da. Due to limited research on the effect of AOS on meat quality in broilers, this study selected 600 mg/kg as the AOS supplemental dose by referring to previous studies on natural oligosaccharides (Lan et al., 2024, 2023; Zhu La et al., 2023).

Sample collection

Sampling was performed at the end of the feeding trial. 12 h before sampling, the experimental broilers were subjected to a fasting and water deprivation protocol. One broiler with the average weight was selected from each replicate cage. Following the euthanasia of the broilers, they were immediately slaughtered for sample collection. Thigh muscles were entirely excised. The left side measured the meat color and pH, while the right assessed the drip loss and shear force. Uniform right thigh muscle samples without blood were collected and transferred into 2 mL enzyme-free tubes. They were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Meat quality assessment

Meat Color. After slaughtering, the left thigh muscles of the broilers were collected. A Chroma Meter (CR-410, Konica Minolta Sensing) was placed in direct contact with the surface of the muscle to measure the lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) values. Three random positions were chosen for measurement for each sample, and the average value was calculated.

Meat pH. The pH values of the left thigh muscles were measured using a Spear Tip pH Meter (AZ8694, AZ Instrument Corp, Taiwan, China) at 45 min and 24 h post-slaughter (stored at 4 ℃). The probe was inserted at a 45° angle for the measurements. Three random positions were selected for measurement for each sample, and the average value was calculated.

Drip Loss. After slaughtering, the right thigh muscles were excised. A muscle sample measuring 4 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm was cut parallel to the direction of the muscle fibers. The initial weight of the sample was measured and recorded. After hanging at 4 °C for 24 h, surface moisture was removed using absorbent paper. Shear Force. After slaughter, the right thigh muscles were excised. A muscle strip measuring 3 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm was sectioned along the muscle fiber orientation, with all tendons, ligaments, and adipose tissue carefully removed. It was then heated in a water bath preheated to 80 ℃ for approximately 30 min, and then removed and left to cool to room temperature. Shear force was measured by the strip performing three shears using a meat tenderness analyzer (RH—N50, Guangzhou Runhu Instruments Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The results were expressed in Newtons (N).

Antioxidant capacity assessment

The right thigh muscles were homogenized to prepare 10 % tissue homogenates at a ratio of 1:9 (tissue weight: 0.9 % physiological saline). The protein concentration of the homogenate samples was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Kit number: T9300A) provided by Takara Bio Inc. The antioxidant capacity was detected using the following kits: Total Antioxidant Capacity (T-AOC) Assay Kit (Kit number: A015-2-1), Total Superoxide Dismutase (T-SOD) Assay Kit (Kit number: A001-3-2), Catalase (CAT) Assay Kit (Kit number: A007-1-1), Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH-Px) Assay Kit (Kit number: A005-1-2), and Glutathione S-Transferase (GSH-ST) Assay Kit (Kit number: A004-1-1), all provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Additionally, the Lipid Peroxidation Malondialdehyde (MDA) Assay Kit (Kit number: S0131S) is supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Untargeted metabolomics measurement

Sample Preparation. Weigh 50 mg (± 5 mg) of sample and add 500 μL of 80 % icy methanol solution. Samples were homogenized using a frozen tissue grinder (JXFSTPRP-CLN, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Extracts were incubated at 20°C for 30 minutes to precipitate proteins, then centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was centrifuged for 5 min again. The supernatant was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Metabolites Extraction. Chromatographic separation was conducted on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters, USA). The mobile phase consists of phase A (5 mmol/L ammonium acetate + 5 mmol/L acetic acid + water) and phase B (acetonitrile). The injection volume was 4 μL, with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min at a column temperature 40 °C. Mass spectrometry was analyzed using a Quadrupole Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (Thermo Q Exactive PLUS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. ESI temperature is 350 °C. The sweep gas pressure of the ion source is 0 Arb. Gas 1 (Auxiliary gas) pressures are set to 15 Arb, and Gas 2 (Sheath gas) pressures are set to 50 Arb. The mass spectrometric data were obtained with full scan and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) modes. In one acquisition cycle, the full scan acquisition range is 70-1050 Da.

LC-MS/MS Data Analysis. The LC-MS raw data were processed using XCMS software, followed by metabolite annotation using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Additionally, one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) were carried out. Significantly different metabolites were identified based on the Variable Importance in Projection score from the OPLS-DA model and the P-value from the ANOVA, with criteria of VIP > 1 and P < 0.05.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

The total RNA was extracted from the right thigh muscle using TRIzol reagent. The quality of the extracted total RNA was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration and A (260/280) ratio were determined using an Ultra-micro spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Shanghai, China). Immediately reverse-transcribed the extracted RNA into cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR kit (Kit number: R223-01, Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Real-time PCR amplification was conducted using a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The reaction volume is 20 μL, including 8.2 μL of ddH2O, 10 μL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Kit number: Q711-02, Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), 0.4 μL of Forward, 0.4 μL of Reverse, and 1 μL of cDNA. Real-time PCR was performed using the PCR program described in the kit's instructions. Relative mRNA expression, normalized to β-actin, was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) synthesized the primers used in this experiment. Detailed information regarding the primers is presented in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The results were presented as mean values ± standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM). The General Linear Model (GLM) procedure performed one-way ANOVA in SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Tukey’s multiple range test was used to compare the significance of differences among groups. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05, and tendencies were noted at 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10. Figures were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Effect of dietary supplementation with alginate oligosaccharides on meat quality of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers

As shown in Fig. 1, compared with the TN group, the drip losses and shear force of the thigh muscle in the HS group were increased (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001, respectively). Conversely, pH values in the HS group were decreased at 45 min postmortem (P < 0.001). Supplementation of AOS to the diet increased the pH45min and b* values (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) while decreasing the drip losses and shear force in the thigh muscle of heat-stressed broilers (P < 0.001). Concerning the pH24h, L* and a* values, no statistical differences were observed between the HS and TN groups, as well as between the HS + AOS and HS groups (P > 0.05).

Effect of dietary supplementation with alginate oligosaccharides on antioxidant capacity of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers

The antioxidant enzyme activities and MDA concentration of thigh muscle were presented in Fig. 2. The level of T-AOC and the activities of CAT and GSH-Px of broilers in the HS group were lower than those in the TN group (P < 0.001, P < 0.001, P < 0.05, respectively). In contrast, the activities of T-SOD and GST, and the concentration of MDA showed no significant alterations between the TN and HS groups (P > 0.05). Compared to the HS group, the level of T-AOC, the activities of T-SOD, GSH-Px, and GST were increased in heat-stressed broilers fed with AOS diets (P < 0.01), while the concentration of MDA showed a decreasing trend (P = 0.083). There was no significant intergroup difference in CAT activity between HS and HS+AOS groups (P > 0.05).

Effect of dietary supplementation with alginate oligosaccharides on untargeted metabolomics of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers

Quality Control and Multivariate Statistical Analysis. Total ion chromatograms (TIC) were obtained for the QC, TN, HS, and HS + AOS groups using untargeted metabolomics LC–MS/MS technology. A total of 3332 and 4177 metabolites were identified in the thigh muscle, respectively, under both negative and positive ion modes. The retention time and peak intensity overlap results of QC indicate that MS maintains a stable signal when detecting the same sample at different times (Fig. 3A, B). Within the 2.5-7.5-minute time range, there were differences in the peak heights and positions of TN, HS, and HS+AOS group, suggesting variations in metabolite composition and content among different groups. Furthermore, PLS-DA (Fig. 3C) highlighted the differences in metabolic profiles among groups. The model parameters for PLS-DA in the positive and negative ion modes were R2X(cum) = 0.552, R2Y(cum) = 0.797, and Q2 (cum) = 0.667, respectively. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 36.1 % and 16 %, respectively, indicating a distinct distinction among the three groups. The 200-permutation test confirmed the robustness of the PLS-DA model, demonstrating a low risk of overfitting (Fig. 3D).

Differential Metabolites Analysis. Applying ANOVA with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and PLS-DA with a VIP > 1 as screening criteria for differential metabolites across multiple groups yielded 6,647 metabolites. Among these, 361 metabolites exhibited significant differences across the three groups, with 18 successfully identified at the secondary level. As shown in Fig. 4, decreases were detected in the concentrations of eicosanoic acid and PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0) in the HS group compared to the TN group (P < 0.05). In comparison to the HS group, dietary 600 mg/kg of AOS increased the concentrations of capric acid (P < 0.01), eicosanoic acid (P < 0.01), suberic acid (P < 0.001), PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0) (P < 0.01), gingerol (P < 0.01) and 4-methyl-2-pentyl-1,3-dioxolane (P < 0.001).

Metabolic Pathways Enrichment Analysis. The functional exploration of differential metabolites primarily involves analyzing their chemical properties (HMDB compound classification) and biological functions (KEGG Pathway). As shown in Fig. 5A, a summary of the entries related to the secondary metabolites involved in the Pathway revealed that the differential metabolites were enriched in a total of 3 first-level KEGG metabolic pathways including human diseases, metabolism, organismal systems, and six second-level metabolic pathways including lipid metabolism, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, cancer overview, digestive, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, and nervous system. The findings indicated that the lipid metabolism pathway exhibited the most extraordinary richness of differential metabolites. As depicted in Fig. 5B, the differential metabolites were primarily benzenoids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic oxygen compounds, and organoheterocyclic compounds. As shown in Fig. 5C, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that there were nine enriched metabolic pathways (P < 0.05), including fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol biosynthesis, folate transport and metabolism, retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, and choline metabolism in cancer.

Effect of dietary supplementation with alginate oligosaccharides on relative MRNA expression of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the HS group showed an upregulation of the mRNA expression levels of kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) (P < 0.001), glutathione S-transferase omega 1 (GSTO1) (P < 0.001), glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1) (P < 0.05), GPX3 (P < 0.01), GPX4 (P < 0.001), NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) (P < 0.05), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) (P < 0.05), SOD2 (P < 0.05), MAF bZIP transcription factor F (MafF) (P < 0.01), MafG (P < 0.05) and MafK (P < 0.01), and a downregulation of the mRNA expression level of glutathione S-transferase theta 1 (GSTT1) (P < 0.01) in the thigh muscle compared with the TN group. In comparison with the HS group, the mRNA expression levels of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) (P < 0.001), GSTT1 (P < 0.01), GPX4 (P < 0.001), NQO1 (P < 0.01), SOD1 (P < 0.05), SOD2 (P < 0.001), MafF (P < 0.001), MafG (P < 0.001) and MafK (P < 0.001) were upregulated in the HS +AOS group. Conversely, the mRNA expression level of Keap1 and GSTO1 was downregulated by supplementing AOS (P < 0.01). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression level of glutathione S-transferase alpha 3 (GSTA3) and catalase 1 (CAT1) was detected with no statistically significant differences among all groups (P > 0.05).

No significant differences were detected for the mRNA expression level of MyH9 and MyH10 among groups (P > 0.05). For the mRNA expression level of genes involved in lipid metabolism, while adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) was identified as upregulated, fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) were identified as downregulated in the HS group compared to the TN group (P < 0.01). Compared to the HS group, broilers in the HS + AOS group showed a higher mRNA expression level of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1) and fatty acid synthase (FAS) (P < 0.05). Differential mRNA expression level of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and FABP3 did not reach statistical significance among groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

HS represents a critical challenge encountered in contemporary broiler production. During HS, broilers are prone to various physiological changes, which result in not only a decline in growth performance but also a deterioration of meat quality (Humam et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). The assessment of meat quality relies on several critical parameters, including pH levels, meat color, water-holding capacity, and tenderness. Generally, pH directly reflects muscle acid content, affecting meat shear force, drip loss, and color (Baghban Kanani et al., 2017). Previous research demonstrated that chronic HS increased shear force and drip loss while decreasing the pH of the muscle in broilers (Jing et al., 2024). Similarly, we also made the same discovery in the present study. HS impairs the meat quality, specifically manifested as pale, soft, and exudative (PSE-like) syndrome in poultry meat (Zhang et al., 2020). This condition is associated with a decline in the water-holding capacity and pH and an increase in drip loss in the muscle (Nawaz et al., 2021). This may be due to the high temperatures accelerating the conversion of muscle glycogen to lactic acid during and after slaughter, causing a rapid pH decline, while the synergistic action of elevated temperature and low pH leads to sarcoplasmic protein denaturation, ultimately reducing water-holding capacity (Zaboli et al., 2019). Interestingly, dietary supplementation of 600 mg/kg AOS could mitigate the detrimental effects of HS on meat quality. Similarly, previous studies have shown that dietary biogenic selenium nanoparticles synthesized using AOS decreased the freezing loss and shear force of muscle in broilers under HS (Yang et al., 2023). Reports also indicated that incorporating algae into the diets of monogastric animals enhances meat quality through nutritional improvements, sensory attributes, and shelf-life extension, providing significant benefits (Prates, 2025). Thus, supplementing 600 mg/kg AOS effectively improved meat quality of the thigh muscle in heat-stressed broilers.

Oxidative stress is the main consequence of HS (Afzal et al., 2023). Researches shown that HS causes the production and accumulation of reactive oxygen species and free radicals in cells (Humam et al., 2020), leading to the autoxidation of heme proteins from the ferrous (Fe2+) to the ferric (Fe3+) state results in the formation of the met forms of the heme proteins, which induces lipid peroxidation, causing the meat to turn brown and deteriorate in quality (Wu et al., 2024). T-AOC, T-SOD, GSH-Px, GST, CAT, and MDA are important indicators for measuring antioxidant status. The present study found that HS inhibited the antioxidant enzyme activities of the thigh muscle in broilers, as evidenced by decreased activities of CAT and GSH-Px, as well as the level of T-AOC. This result is consistent with previous studies (Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2018). Numerous studies suggested that natural antioxidants can maintain the redox balance in broilers, thereby preventing oxidative damage caused by HS (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). Similarly, the current study showed that AOS enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes in the thigh muscle and reduced the concentration of MDA in broilers suffering from HS. Thus, HS caused oxidative damage and decreased antioxidant enzyme activity in the thigh muscle, whereas dietary supplementation with AOS restored antioxidant defenses, reduced oxidative stress, and improved meat quality. Furthermore, it is commonly recognized that Nrf2/Keap1 is a key system that regulates the expression of antioxidant-related genes. In this study, AOS upregulated the mRNA expression level of Nrf2 and multiple antioxidant genes, and downregulated the mRNA expression level of Keap1 under HS. Analogously, fucoidan extracted from brown algae has been observed to enhance the mRNA expression level of antioxidant genes such as heme oxygenase‑1 (HO-1) and SOD1 by upregulating Nrf2 and downregulating Keap1 (Ryu and Chung, 2016). Chitosan oligosaccharides have also been reported to activate the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway and increase the HO-1 and GSH-Px gene expression in the muscle of heat-stressed broilers (Chang et al., 2022). Therefore, AOS can act as an activator of the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway, which upregulates the expression of antioxidant-related genes, strengthening the antioxidant defense system and reducing oxidative damage to muscle of broilers under HS.

Untargeted metabolomics LC-MS/MS technology was employed in this study to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with AOS on the metabolite composition of the thigh muscle. It was found that most of the screened differential metabolites belonged to lipids and lipid-like molecules. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differential metabolites were mainly enriched in lipid metabolism pathways. These results suggested that HS and AOS primarily alter meat quality in the thigh muscle via regulating lipid metabolism pathways. Specifically, HS reduced the concentrations of eicosanoic acid and PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0). Dietary AOS increased the concentrations of capric acid, eicosanoic acid, Suberic acid, PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0), gingerol and 4-Methyl-2-pentyl-1,3-dioxolane. eicosanoic acid, also known as arachidic acid, is a saturated fatty acid. Saturated fatty acids generally have greater oxidative stability than unsaturated fatty acids due to the absence of double bonds, which are more susceptible to oxidation (Kerrihard et al., 2015). HS decreased eicosenoic acid concentration, altering the saturated fatty acid composition in the thigh muscle, which increased susceptibility to lipid oxidation during HS. Dietary AOS increased eicosenoic acid concentration in this study, suggesting its potential to mitigate oxidative stress in the thigh muscle by modulating fatty acid composition. PC (22:2(13Z,16Z)/14:0), a phosphatidylcholine (PC) molecule classified as a glycerophospholipid, is an integral component of cellular membranes. Hydrolysis of PC molecules compromises the integrity of mitochondrial and cellular membranes. This speeds up the release of proteolytic enzymes and subsequent myofibrillar degradation, thus making meat more tender. It has been reported that PC species with long-chain unsaturated fatty acids, like those with 22:2 and 14:0 chains, are involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism, which is crucial for membrane structure, signaling, and lipid transport (Wu et al., 2018a, 2018b). As a result, AOS potentially exercises its effects by involvement in glycerophospholipid metabolism in the current investigation. Capric acid is a medium-chain saturated fatty acid. A study confirmed that capric acid-containing structured lipids produced from grape seed oil can mitigate obesity complications caused by oxidative stress and inflammation (Martínez-Galán et al., 2021). Research has demonstrated that capric acid improves fatty acid oxidation without affecting glycolytic flow, confirming its unique impact on skeletal muscle lipid metabolism (Abe et al., 2016). Suberic acid is a naturally occurring dicarboxylic acid. Impaired β-oxidation drives fatty acids toward metabolism via the alternative ω-oxidation pathway, consequently accumulating dicarboxylic acids such as suberic acid (Clomburg et al., 2015). In this study, AOS potentially activated the ω-oxidation pathway, reflecting an adaptive response to impaired energy metabolism in the organism. Gingerol is a key bioactive compound found in ginger (Zingiber officinale), widely recognized for its medicinal and nutritional properties. As a natural antioxidant, gingerol mitigates oxidative deterioration in muscle by scavenging free radicals and suppressing lipid peroxidation, thereby delaying myoglobin oxidation and lipid rancidity. In particular, 6-gingerol has been shown to have significant effects on metabolism. 6-Gingerol improves lipid metabolism by activating the AMPK pathway, which enhances mitochondrial function, reduces lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle and liver, and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis (Peng et al., 2024b, 2024a; Wang et al., 2019). 6-Gingerol enhances mitochondrial respiratory capacity, restores mitochondrial morphology, and reduces oxidative stress in metabolic tissues. This contributes to improved energy metabolism and protection against metabolic disorders induced by high-fat or high-fructose diets (Alipour et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2024). Additionally, gingerol has been shown to improve meat tenderness. A study reported that adding 5 % ginger extract lowered the shear force of chicken meat by 37.7 % (Cruz et al., 2020). Collectively, AOS modulates lipid metabolism pathways (encompassing fatty acid and glycerophospholipid metabolism) by altering key metabolite concentrations, thereby counteracting HS-induced metabolic derangements and enhancing meat quality.

To explore the regulatory mechanism of AOS on lipid metabolism, this study further detected the mRNA expression level of genes related to lipid metabolism. Lipid metabolism affects meat quality in various ways. Intramuscular fat (IMF) content and fatty acid composition correlate positively with meat flavor and tenderness (Pannier et al., 2010; Ramayo-Caldas et al., 2012). Research indicated that HS resulted in a reduction of IMF content (Gao et al., 2020). This occurs because HS induces energy deficiency in the body, activating AMPK, which decreases the expression of genes associated with fat synthesis; HS also activates ATGL, increasing fat breakdown (Xing et al., 2019). This process creates an imbalance between fat synthesis and breakdown, leading to disorders in lipid metabolism. This is precisely why the mRNA expression level of ATGL was downregulated in the thigh muscle of the HS group in this study. Our results are consistent with a prior study on constant-temperature (35 °C) HS. (Teyssier et al., 2023). However, contrary to the result in the liver (Yin et al., 2021), it is speculated that this is a manifestation of tissue specificity. At the same time, the downregulation of PPARγ inhibits adipocyte differentiation, leading to abnormal deposition of lipid droplets. Our study also found that dietary supplementation with AOS upregulated the mRNA expression level of SREBP-1 and FAS, maintaining basal lipid synthesis. Accordingly, AOS can alleviate HS-induced lipid metabolism disorder by balancing fat synthesis and decomposition.

We also evaluated the mRNA level of genes related to muscle fiber type transformation due to the muscle fiber type influencing the meat tenderness (Wright et al., 2018). The AMPK/PGC-1α pathway regulates muscle fiber metabolism (Liu et al., 2017). When cellular energy supply is insufficient, AMPK is activated and upregulates PGC-1α, promoting the formation of slow-twitch muscle fibers (Garcia and Shaw, 2017; Herzig and Shaw, 2018). In addition, muscle fiber conversion is associated with various molecules, including MEF2C (Feng et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2000). MyHC plays an important role in muscle contraction, muscle fiber type determination, and muscle development. A study showed that high temperature promoted the conversion of slow muscle fibers MYH7 to fast muscle fibers MYH1 in mice (Coria et al., 2023). The current study showed that HS downregulated the mRNA expression level of MyH1D, suggesting that HS caused a decrease in the proportion of type I muscle fibers in the thigh muscle. Also, the present study observed an upregulation of the upstream regulatory factor AMPKα1, PGC-1α, and MEF2C expression in the HS group. This phenomenon can be attributed to HS reducing the feed intake of broiler chickens, leading to a shortage of energy supply, and activating the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway. Unfortunately, the supplementation of AOS did not reverse this change, suggesting that AOS does not alter the meat quality of the thigh muscle through muscle fiber transformation but primarily influences oxidative stability and lipid metabolism in broilers under HS conditions. However, further verification is necessary.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 600 mg/kg AOS alleviates HS-induced impairment in meat quality of the thigh muscle by improving oxidative stability and modulating lipid metabolism disorder in broilers. The findings provide a nutritional strategy to improve the meat quality of broilers under HS, and can promote the use of seaweed active components as anti-HS feed additives in broiler production.
