A multifaceted investigation into the impact of m6A methylation-related genes on pancreatic cancer, integrating insights from various databases and foundational experimental research
Authors: Yanghui Wen, Zhilin Liu, Yunjie Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Despite advances in surgical techniques, immunotherapy, the mortality rate associated with pancreatic cancer (PC) has been on the rise in recent years. Understanding the importance of RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) in PC is critical for prognosis, tumor microenvironment, and immunotherapy efficacy. The study aims to identify m6A methylation regulators that play an important role in the development and progression of PC by mining databases. The effect of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3) on pancreatic tumors was explored, and the related mechanisms were explored.Methods:We analyzed the expression of m6A regulators in PC by digging deeper into the datasets of The Cancer Genome Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases, and analyzed its relationship with the prognosis of patients with PC, looking for m6A methylation regulators that play an important role in the development and progression of PC. Reuse the ConsensusClusterPlus package, Cox analysis, and unsupervised clustering to delineate three distinct m6A clusters – designated as m6A cluster A, m6A cluster B, and m6A cluster C single-sample gene set enrichment analysis, gene set variation analysis, Gene Ontology, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses evaluated the different pathway roles of these clusters in the development and progression of PC. Finally, the cell lines with IGFBP3 overexpression and knockdown were constructed by lentivirus transfection, the transfection effect was identified by WB, and the effects of IGFBP3 overexpression/knockdown on the survival and growth of PC cell lines were verified by cell cloning experiments and cell counting kit-8 experiments, and the possible related pathways were explored by KEGG.Results:Most m6A regulatory factors are highly expressed in PC, and their high expression is negatively correlated with the prognosis of patients with PC. Furthermore, m6A regulatory factors may influence the occurrence and development of PC through metabolic pathways, stroma activation pathways, immune regulatory processes, and the immune microenvironment. Finally, the overexpression of IGFBP3 promoted the growth of PC cells, and vice versa.Conclusions:Most m6A regulatory factors are differentially expressed in PC and are associated with the prognosis of patients with PC, potentially influencing the occurrence and development of PC through pathways such as the immune microenvironment. The overexpression of IGFBP3 can promote the growth of PC cells and vice versa.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC), a highly malignant tumor with a 5-year survival rate of only 10% and a 3- to 6-month median survival time, has become one of the common causes of death worldwide because of its high mortality, occult incidence, and low surgical resection rate[1,2]. Owing to early infiltration and metastasis, only 15–20% of patients have the opportunity to undergo surgery[3]. The short-term effect of surgical treatment for PC has significantly improved in the past 20 years, but the long-term survival performance is still unsatisfactory (85% of patients suffer recurrence after surgery)[4-6]. RNA modifications play a key role in the initiation and development of a variety of malignant tumors. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, the most common posttranscriptional modification, accounts for more than 80% of RNA methylation[7]. Research has shown that m6A is a dynamic and reversible regulatory process that regulates alternative splicing, nuclear export of mRNAs, selective translation, the translation rate of mRNAs, and RNA stability[8,9]. m6A methylation refers to the substitution of a hydrogen in the amino group connected by the no. 6 carbon atom of adenine by a methyl group, which involves three core enzymes, including m6A methyltransferases (METTL3, METTL14, METTL16, WTAP, VIRMA, ZC3H13, RBM15, also known as “writers”), m6A demethylases (YTHDC1, YTHDC2, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, HNRNPC, FMR1, LRPPRC, HNRNPA2B1, IGFBP1, IGFBP2, IGFBP3, RBMX, also known as “readers”), and m6A-binding proteins (FTO, ALKBH5, also known as “erasers”)[10–12]. In recent years, scholars have conducted extensive and in-depth research on the connection between m6A regulators and PC. For example, FTO was found to suppress the metastasis, proliferation and invasion of patients with PC by inhibiting the Wnt signaling pathway[13]. ALKBH5 also regulates the posttranscriptional activation of PER1 through demethylation, thereby serving as a tumor suppressor gene in PC[14]. HNRNPC mediates radiation resistance in PC in a RhoA/ROCK2-YAP/TAZ pathway-dependent manner[15]. A recent study demonstrated that LRPPRC acts as an oncogene in PC by maintaining mitochondrial stability and may serve as a prognostic predictor[16]

Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP-3), a member of the m6A family, has been identified as a conserved and multifunctional protein that can bind to 80% of IGF-I and IGF-II with high affinity and regulate IGF signaling[17]. IGFBP-3R was identified as a novel cell death receptor by Ingerman et al, who reported that the central domain of IGFBP-3 is essential for binding to IGFBP-3 R and that the activation of caspase-8 can induce apoptosis in an unconventional manner[18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that IGFBP3 negatively modulates insulin secretion and the insulin signaling pathway[19], exerting inhibitory effects on tumor cell proliferation and progression[20,21].Given the numerous m6A regulatory factors currently present and their unclear roles and mechanisms in PC, we believe that these regulatory factors may play a complex role in the occurrence and development of PC, warranting in-depth exploration.

Our research aims to identify m6A methylation regulators that play important roles in the development and progression of PC by digging deeper into datasets from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. This study aims to identify and validate the effects of the overexpression/knockdown of IGFBP3 on the survival and growth of PC cell lines and to explore the related mechanisms. We declare that this article complies with the TITAN2025 guidelines[22].

Materials and methods

PC data, sources, and pretreatment

First, we searched and screened RNA transcriptional information from normal samples and clinicopathological data from patients with PC in the TCGA and the GEO databases. Patients without survival information were excluded from further evaluation. We then analyzed three study-eligible PC cohorts (GSE28735, GSE57495, and TCGA-PC) for further analysis, aiming to identify different m6A methylation patterns. The collected data were analyzed.

To align the raw RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads) values for the PC gene expression data downloaded from TCGA with the GEO microarray values, we converted them into TPM (Transcripts Per Kilobase of exonmodel per Million mapped reads) values closest to the GEO microarray values via the limma package in R[23]. However, when multiple GEO and TCGA datasets are combined, batch effects are inevitable. To address this, we utilized the Combat package from the SVA package. This function is imported from the sva package, and it can be used to adjust for known batch effects via the empirical Bayesian framework described in Johnson et al[24]. It can use parametric or nonparametric methods to adjust the mean and variance of the data to eliminate or reduce batch effects. Second, it returns a corrected expression matrix that can be used for subsequent analysis. Finally, we analyzed the data via R (version 4.0.2), reshape2, and R Bioconductor. The experimental results were visualized via ggalluvial (0.12.0), ggpubr (0.4.0), maftools (2.10.1), pheatmap (1.0.12), and ggplot2 (3.3.5).

Gene variant analysis and functional annotation

To examine the disparities in m6A modification patterns within biological processes, we conducted a GSVA enrichment analysis utilizing the “GSVA” and “GSEABase” R package[25]. GSVA is a widely recognized nonparametric and unsupervised technique for assessing alterations in pathway and biological process activity within expression datasets[26]. To perform GSVA, we obtained the gene set “c2.cp.kegg.v6.2” from the MSigDB database. The functional annotation of m6A-related genes was accomplished via the clusterProfiler R package, which employs a significance threshold of a false discovery rate of 0.05.

Unsupervised cluster analysis of 23 m6A gene regulators

To investigate the role of 23 m6A RNA methylation regulators in the cutaneous melanoma cohort, we extracted these regulators from four comprehensive GEO datasets after conducting further screening to eliminate less detectable factors. We subsequently utilized these regulators to identify nonstop regulator-mediated m6A modification patterns. Among the 23 m6A regulators, there are 8 writers (METTTL3, METTTL14, METTTL16, WTAP, VIRMA, ZC3H13, RBM15, and RBM15B), 2 erasers (ALKBH5 and FTO), and 13 readers (YTHDC1, YTHDC2, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, HNRNPC, FMR1, LRPPRC, HNRNPA2B1, IGF2BP1, IGFBP2, IGFBP3, and RBMX). We employed unsupervised cluster analysis, which is a type of K-means clustering (K-means) performed via the Consensus ClusterPlus function, to identify distinct m6A modification patterns and classify patients into different clusters on the basis of the expression of these 23 m6A regulators. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we employed a consensus clustering algorithm[27] with 1000 iterations to determine the number and stability of clusters, thereby mitigating the impact of uncorrelated variables on the experimental outcomes[28]. To identify prognosis-related genes among the 23 m6A regulators and to derive their Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves, we employed the “limma” and “survminer” packages for a cyclic analysis of the expression profiles and clinical data. Concurrently, the “org.Hs.eg.db,” “DOSE,” “clusterProfiler,” and “enrichplot” packages were utilized to amalgamate the co-expression relationships of genes with prognostic significance and the previously acquired prognostic analysis results.

Identification of differentially expressed genes among different m6A phenotypes

Patients were categorized into three m6A clusters on the basis of the expression levels of m6A-associated genes associated with the three distinct PC m6A modification patterns to identify m6A-related genes. The “limma” package in the “R” programming language was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that overlapped among the three m6A modification patterns. An empirical Bayesian approach was utilized for this identification. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a comprehensive database that integrates genomic, chemical, and phylogenetic information to comprehend the intricate functionalities and utility of biological systems at the genomic and molecular levels. Its objectives encompass unraveling the genetic and chemical blueprint of biological phenomena to gain insights into the higher functions and utilities of living systems[29]. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses were employed to ascertain the functions of the identified DEGs using the human database of org.Hs.eg.db. These findings further substantiate that the co-occurrence of differential gene expression with m6A modifications and immune features can be considered distinctive attributes of m6A-related genes. On the basis of m6A expression, we stratified all patients with PC in this investigation into three clusters and assigned them numbers. The three groups A, B, and C were then analyzed for differential expression clustering via the ConsensusClusterPlus software package[27]. Annotations were utilized to visualize and integrate the discovery database with all options as defaults.

Estimation of TME cell infiltration

We utilized the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm to quantify the relative abundance of each cell infiltrate within the PC TME. In this particular scenario, the enrichment fraction calculated by ssGSEA was used to express the relative abundance of each TME-infiltrated cell in every sample. The ssGSEA method is an extension of the GSEA method and is designed primarily for single samples where GSEA is not feasible[30].

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA for IGFBP3 was performed on the top five genes ranked by risk coefficient using the datasets c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols and c5.go.v7.4.symbols and the test was conducted collaboratively using the packages “org.Hs.eg.db,” “DOSE,” “clusterProfiler,” and “enrichplot.”

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human PC cell line PANC-1 was supplied by the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were grown in 1640 medium (Gibco, Life Technologies, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Life Technologies, Australia), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Life Technologies, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The cell line was routinely authenticated for purity and being infection-free.

Lentivirus production and infection

The target gene and packaging helper genes were cotransfected into HEK-293T cells via the transfection reagent Lifofectamin (Invitrogen, USA). After filtration and collection of the virus particles, the PC cells were infected with the recombinant lentivirus containing the transducing units, along with 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Following infection, the cells were analyzed via FACS for GFP expression to identify PC cells with stable expression. An empty lentiviral vector served as the control.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted via lysis buffer (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) and quantified via the bicinchoninic acid method. Protein samples (30 μg) were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE. After being transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies against IFGBP3 (1:1000, E6C2E) and β-actin (1:1000, 8H10D10). The above antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The membranes were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G or goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology) at 37°C for 2 h, and the protein bands were visualized with ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) and a Biospectrum Imaging System (UVP, Upland, CA, USA). The relative protein expression levels were calculated via normalization to the expression levels of the β-actin loading control.

Cell proliferation assays

A cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was performed with a CCK-8 kit (New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co., Ltd.; C6005), and three replicates were used for each group. The cell concentration was approximately 2000 cells/well. After injecting 10 µL of CCK-8 solution, the cells were stored at 37°C for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm after 0, 24, 48, and 72 h of lentivirus transfection. The 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime; C0075S). After incubation with 10 μM EdU for 2 h, the transfected cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime; P0099) for 15 min, 0.2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime; ST677) for 30 min, and click reaction solution for 30 min (in the dark) at room temperature. Then, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst solution for 10 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope. For the colony formation assay, logarithmically grown PANC-1 cells (including overexpressing, sh, vector, and shCon cells) were counted, trypsinized, and seeded in 6-well plates at 500 cells/well. The clones were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 saturated humidity for 14 days to observe the formation of clones. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, crystal violet was added, and photos were taken for preservation. Colony numbers were analyzed with ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

In this investigation, we applied the R package and Strawberry Perl to analyze the data (all data processing was performed in R 4.0.2 software and Strawberry Perl 5.30.1), and all RNA-seq data were normalized via the ComBat function in the SVA package to remove known batch effects. One-way analysis of variance and the Kruskal‒ruskalska v were used to compare differences between three or more groups[31]. The KM method was used to plot survival curves, which were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. The Survminer and survival packages were used for m6A typing survival analysis, the “Consensus Cluster Plus” package was used for clustering classification, the ssGSEA algorithm was used for differential analysis to assess infiltrating immune cell content, and the “VennDiagram” package was used for the analysis of intersecting genes.

Results

The genetic variation landscape of m6A regulators in pancreatic tumors

We downloaded two GEO datasets (GSE28735 and GSE57495) with available clinical information to analyze the expression of 23 m6A regulators in patients with PC and their interrelationships and identified 23 regulators, including 8 writers, 2 erasers, and 13 readers; in particular, RNA methylation regulators were found to be critical for cancer development. In addition, we systematically examined the transcription levels of 23 RNA regulators in the TCGA database. First, we found that the expression of m6A genes, such as METTL16, RBM15B, YTHDF1, IGFBP3, and ALKBH5, differed between the tumor tissues and normal tissues of patients with PC (Fig. 1A). Our analysis of copy-number variations (CNVs) across 23 regulatory factors revealed amplification in CNVs for nine m6A-related genes (VIRMA, IGFBP2, ALKBH5, FMR1, HNRNPA2B1, YTHDF1, RBMX, IGFBP1, and IGFBP3), along with CNV deletions in 14 m6A-associated genes (YTHDC2, YTHDF3, HNRNPC, LRPPRC, WTAP, ZC3H13, METTL3, FTO, YTHDF2, RBM15, RBM15B, YTHDC1, METTL14, and METTL16) (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we summarized the frequency of somatic mutations in 23 pancreatic tumor regulators: 104 m6A regulators were mutated in 431 samples, with a frequency of 24.13% (Fig. 1C). Predominantly, missense mutations were observed in patients with PC. ZC3H13, VIRMA, YTHDF1, RBM15B, YTHDC1, YTHDF2, and RBM15 presented the highest mutation frequencies, each at 3%. Conversely, METTL3, RBMX, and METTL16 displayed no mutations. We subsequently analyzed the location alterations of these 23 m6A regulators in human chromosomal CNVs via a circular plot (Fig. 1D). Relationships between m6A regulators and survival in patients with PC

We analyzed the survival of 205 patients with PC in three PC cohorts (GSE28735, GSE57495, and TCGA-PC), and the results revealed that the expression of many m6A regulators was associated with the survival rate of patients with PC and that the expression levels of FTO, HNRNPC, IGFBP1, IGFBP3, LRPPRC, METTL16, RBM15, VIRMA, and YTHDF1 were inversely proportional to the survival rate of patients with PC, but the opposite was true for ALKBH5 (Fig. 2A–J). Biological distinctions among patients with PC across three m6A populations

To identify the biomolecular alterations associated with the three distinct m6A modification patterns, we conducted GSVA, ssGSEA, and GO analysis on the gene set of PC. As illustrated in Fig. 3D–F, the significantly dysregulated pathways between patients with PC from different m6A clusters are listed. Among them, certain pathways associated with stromal activation, such as the p53 signaling pathway, basal transcription factors, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, were significantly enriched in m6A cluster A. Additionally, pathways associated with metabolic pathways, including linoleic acid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, glycine serine, and threonine metabolism, were markedly enriched in m6A cluster B. Moreover, m6A cluster C was enriched in pathways associated with immunomodulatory processes, including primary immunodeficiency, glycosohingolipid biosynthesis globo series, and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis keratan sulfate. These data suggest the potential involvement of m6A regulators in PC metabolism, matrix activation pathways, and immunomodulatory processes, thereby influencing PC progression (Fig. 3D-F).

To our astonishment, the evaluation of tumor microenvironment (TME) infiltration through ssGSEA revealed increased immune cell infiltration in m6A cluster B, which included activated B cells, activated CD8+ T cells and monocytes (Fig. 4A). Our investigation of the TME suggested that m6A may contribute to carcinogenesis by modulating essential components of cellular signaling, either through upregulation or downregulation. IGFBP3 promotes PC cell survival and growth

To investigate the effect of IGFBP3 on the viability of PC cells in the m6A family, we constructed an IGFBP3 overexpression and knockdown virus, transfected PANC-1 PC cells, selected cells with a transfection rate higher than 98% after puromycin screening for follow-up experiments (Fig. 5A), and categorized them into the following groups: (a) vector, (b) overexpression, (c) shCon, (d) sh1, (e) sh2, and (f) sh3. Then, we tested the effect of lentivirus transfection via WB, and the results revealed that the expression level of IGFBP3 in the overexpression group was significantly greater than that in the vector group and that the expression level of IGFBP3 in the sh group was significantly lower than that in the shCon group (Fig. 5B–D). Furthermore, we evaluated the survival and growth capabilities of each group of cells (Fig. 5E–H). Compared with empty vector control, upregulated IGFBP3 expression significantly increased the survival and growth capabilities of PC cells (P < 0.05). Moreover, decreasing the expression of IGFBP3 inhibited the survival and growth of PC cells (P < 0.05). Finally, we found that IGFBP3 may play a role in functional pathways such as antigen processing and presentation, the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, and the regulation of autophagy (Fig. 5I). Discussion

PC is a fatal disease with a high mortality rate, and the current number of PC cases is increasing annually[32], thus it has become the focus of relevant research. Owing to the lack of early and effective screening, most patients have progressed to locally advanced or metastatic stages at the time of diagnosis. Thus, patients with PC have a high propensity for recurrence and death despite having been surgically treated with adjuvant chemotherapy or other standardized management methods[33,34]. Thus, the identification of suitable biomarkers is urgently needed to increase the number of therapeutic targets for PC and improve the survival rate of patients with PC.

Accumulating evidence shows that m6A RNA modification plays a dual role in cancer, which is reflected in its impact on the proliferation and development of tumors, metastasis, and drug resistance through regulating the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes or changing the expression and activity of m6A-related enzymes[35,36]. For example, METTL14 affects the proliferation and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma by regulating the expression of miR-126, whereas it accelerates the metastasis of breast cancer by regulating m6A modification and miR146a5p expression[37,38]. Another study demonstrated that METTL3 suppresses colorectal cancer proliferation and migration in a p38/ERK-dependent manner[39]. Interestingly, a recent study revealed that increased METTL3 expression is capable of maintaining the tumor activity of colon cancer by inhibiting SOCS2, which seems to contradict the findings of previous studies[40]. The m6A RNA methyltransferase RBM15 was revealed to regulate the expression and function of RRN3 and DIDO1 via m6A RNA methylation, thus promoting the progression and metastasis of osteosarcoma[41]. These findings prove that m6A modification is complex and diverse in its role in malignant tumors and may serve as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for tumors.

In our study, we initially observed distinctions in the expression of m6A regulatory factors and their correlation with patient outcomes, suggesting a potential pathogenic mechanism in PC. We subsequently examined 23 m6A regulatory factors for somatic mutations and CNVs. Notably, most m6A regulators were upregulated in tumor tissues in the context of somatic mutations, indicating an oncogenic role in PC. In CNV analysis, 14 m6A regulators presented deletions, whereas 9 presented a high frequency of amplification. The strong correlation observed between CNV and m6A in PC suggests that CNV may influence the distinct expression patterns of m6A regulators in this context. Furthermore, we depicted the chromosomal locations of these 23 m6A regulators via circular diagrams and constructed a network diagram illustrating the multidirectional correlations among them. Our results suggest that m6A regulators play an established causative role in pancreatic tumors.

Second, for a more in-depth exploration of m6A modulators, we utilized the ConsensusClusterPlus package, Cox analysis, and unsupervised clustering to delineate three distinct m6A clusters – m6A cluster A, m6A cluster B, and m6A cluster C. We subsequently assessed the immunomodulatory role of these clusters in the TME via ssGSEA, GSVA, GO, and KEGG analyses. The evaluation revealed that pathways significantly associated with m6A were associated primarily with metabolic, cell proliferation, and immune signal transduction pathways. Notably, recent research has underscored the pivotal role of m6A regulators in shaping the TME[42,43].

Furthermore, we constructed IGFBP3-overexpressing and IGFBP3-knockdown PC cells and compared them with control cells to observe their survival and growth. Our experimental results revealed that the overexpression of IGFBP3 promoted the growth of PC cells, whereas the inhibition of IGFBP3 inhibited their survival. Finally, we used a KEGG enrichment assay to detect possible functional pathways by which IGFBP3 functions.

In summary, we found that most m6A regulators are highly expressed in PC and that their high expression is negatively correlated with the prognosis of patients with PC. We further revealed that m6A regulators may affect the occurrence and development of PC through metabolic pathways, matrix activation pathways, immunomodulatory processes, and the immune microenvironment. Finally, we experimentally demonstrated that the overexpression of IGFBP3 can promote PC cell growth and vice versa.
