Crystal Patterning from Aqueous Solutions via Solutal Instabilities
Authors: Samantha A. McBride, Severine Atis, Amir A. Pahlavan, Kripa K. Varanasi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Fluid instabilities can be harnessed for facile self-assembly of patterned structures on the nano- and microscale. Evaporative self-assembly from drops is one simple technique that enables a range of patterning behaviors due to the multitude of fluid instabilities that arise due to the simultaneous existence of temperature and solutal gradients. However, the method suffers from limited controllability over patterns that can arise and their morphology. Here, we demonstrate that a range of distinct crystalline patterns including hexagonal arrays, branches, and sawtooth structures emerge from evaporation of water drops containing calcium sulfate on hydrophilic and superhydrophilic substrates. Different pattern regimes emerge as a function of contact line dynamics and evaporation rates, which dictate which fluid instabilities are most likely to emerge. The underlying physical mechanisms behind instability for controlled self-assembly involve Marangoni flows and forced wetting/dewetting. We also demonstrate that these patterns composed of water-soluble inorganic crystals can serve as sustainable and easily removable masks for applications in microscale fabrication.

Introduction

The exploration of patterns formed via drop evaporation on surfaces began as a quest to understand the simple ring-shapes formed by coffee stains,1 and is now a field onto itself with investigations utilizing the phenomena for advanced inkjet printing,2−4 sensors in biomedical applications,5−9 photonics,10−12 microelectronics,13,14 and self-assembled scaffolds for advanced materials design.15−18 Initial investigations demonstrated that ring patterns emerge when drops containing colloidal particles evaporate on a surface due to evaporative flux toward a pinned contact line.1,19 Colloidal particles transport to the drop contact line via evaporative flows and further enhance contact line pinning in a self-propagating effect.1,19 The disruption of the coffee-ring effect to create uniform, condensed deposits has been of great interest in printing applications and can be accomplished by alteration of contact line dynamics and pinning forces;20 for example, by using hydrophobic or superhydrophobic surfaces,21,22 addition of a surfactant or macromolecules to solution,2,23 changing the drop evaporation rate,11,12,24,25 alteration of the solvent properties,26 using multicomponent liquid mixtures, or by changing the particle shape or size.27,28

Beyond simple rings and uniform circular deposits, drying drops of colloidal solutions can yield a number of interesting morphologies such as spoke-like patterns,29 lines,30 and stripes.31 Hexagonal arrays of colloidal particles can assemble due to Marangoni flows induced by condensation of ethanol onto drops of water on hydrophobic substrates, while stripe-like patterns emerge from the same system on hydrophilic substrates.30 The physics that dictate patterning from colloidal deposition depend on particle–particle interactions, particle-substrate interactions, particle size/shape, evaporative flux, and interior drop flow caused by gradients in temperature or concentration.18

Switching the material deposited by evaporating drops from colloidal particles to dissolved solutes further enhances the complexity of patterns that can emerge due to the additional phase change (crystallization) and solutal gradients within drops.32−36 Crystallization is driven by solvent loss due to evaporation, triggering supersaturated conditions within the drop. Solutal gradients will emerge due to high rates of evaporative flux at triple-phase contact lines,37 leading to preferential crystallization near the contact line. Some of the patterns that have been reported from evaporative crystallization include dendritic patterns,33−35,38−40 flowers,35 and spirals.41 Emergence of dendritic patterns during rapid drying of solvents containing dilute concentrations of solutes is especially common due to diffusion limited aggregation.33−35,38−40 Crystalline patterns generally follow contact line motion,39 leading to the formation of spirals or concentric rings on high-contact angle hysteresis surfaces with stick–slip motion41 and rupture patterns for low contact angle hysteresis surfaces where a thin-film ruptures upon desiccation.36 However, unlike colloidal deposits, the internal crystal lattice and preferred morphologies for crystallites will also play a role in the final deposit morphology when comparing different solutes evaporated under otherwise identical conditions.32−34

Crystallization or colloidal deposition during drop evaporation leads to contact line pinning due to accumulation at the triple-phase contact line.1,19,33,42 The longer the contact line remains pinned, the more material is deposited at the perimeter such that longer pinning time scales lead to depletion of solutes/particles from the fluid.1,19 The interplay between contact line dynamics and solute concentration can be used to tune pattern formation, as pattern morphology depends on the velocity of the receding contact line, the height of the film, and the concentration of remaining solute.33 Variation of temperature and surface wettability leads to different pinning and evaporation times, which in turn control the amounts of remaining fluid and solute.37 In this work, we vary only these two parameters–temperature and substrate wettability–as knobs to control resulting pattern morphology.

Evaporative self-assembly is attractive as a method for masking applications due to its simplicity and extreme low-cost.16 Although a wide range of patterns can form via evaporating drops, it is often challenging to control which patterns arise.15 Here, we demonstrate that a single system composed of calcium sulfate salt dissolved in deionized water can generate distinct, extraordinarily ordered crystalline patterns including hexagonal arrays, branches, and sawtooth structures via the interplay between fluid instabilities and crystallization at the drop dewetting front. Because these patterns are composed of water-soluble inorganic crystals, they can be directly used as sustainable and easily removable masks for microscale fabrication. We demonstrate two potential applications for such masks: creating three-dimensional (3D) micropillar substrates such as those used for superhydrophobic surfaces, and two-dimensional (2D) patterned regions of sputtered gold and native silica.

Results and Discussion

Crystalline patterning is accomplished by evaporation of a single drop on a smooth substrate. A 5 μL drop of saturated calcium sulfate in water is deposited on a heated, oxidized silica substrate and evaporated. The drop spreads into an equilibrium shape with a pinned contact line at the initial drop contact radius Ro, as shown in Figure 1a and by the schematics of Figure 1b,1c. Crystallization at the contact line enhances contact line pinning, and volume reduces until a thin film pinned by the outer ring forms. When the film becomes thin enough, surface tension forces exceed pinning forces such that the contact line depins and retracts at velocity VCL (fourth panel of Figure 1a). As the front recedes, microscale crystallites continue to precipitate and deposit at the moving contact line. These evaporative deposits leave a record of contact line motion and form organized patterns including sawtooth structures, aligned branches, and hexagonal arrays as shown in Figure 1d.

Pattern formation is determined by the retraction dynamics, which in our system is tuned by varying substrate temperature, T, and equilibrium contact angle, θE. This leads to five distinct regimes within a single system, as shown in Figure 1e (See SI, Figures S1–S3 for additional images). Higher contact angles (θE > 20°) and lower temperatures (T < 60 °C) result in the formation of a single crystalline coffee ring with no interior patterning (open circles of Figure 1e), while lower contact angles (θE < 5°) and higher temperatures (T > 60 °C) lead to the formation of concentric rings (filled gray circles). At intermediate regimes, three general categories of patterns emerge: sawtooth structures superimposed onto dendritic crystallites (Figure 1d(i), yellow triangles of Figure 1e), branched structures composed of aligned crystal clusters (Figure 1d(ii), green squares of Figure 1e), and periodic arrays composed of larger crystal clusters (Figure 1d(iii), blue circles of Figure 1e).

Patterns for the phase diagram of Figure 1e were selected approximately halfway between the outer crystal ring and the drop center, as indicated in Figure 1b. However, the patterning phenomena is stochastic, with some experiments forming patterns throughout the entirety of the drop interior and others only forming a small region of patterns. This was especially true in the sawtooth regime, where a single drop could yield hundreds of triangles arranged in periodic rows while a second drop under identical conditions may only form a handful of triangles at a single location. In contrast, the array regime generally led to patterning over most of the internal drop region. Some experiments also saw a transition between patterns (i.e., arrays to branches, or branches to triangles) as evaporation proceeded. Additional images in the SI (Figures S1–S4) show some of these complexities and transitions.

The mechanisms resulting in the nonpatterning limits of the phase diagram are well understood. Single rings with no interior deposits commonly emerge in evaporative deposition and are called coffee-ring patterns.1 These form via radial evaporative flow transporting solutes or particles toward the contact line where they accumulate. Pinning of the contact line is self-promoting as the ring grows thicker while it remains pinned, and thicker rings delay depinning.1,19 When the pinned drop eventually depins from the outer ring, little solute remains to precipitate at the interior. In contrast, at lower contact angles and higher temperatures, the drop depins relatively early in the evaporation process while more solute remains.33 This, combined with spreading dynamics due to low contact angles, enables periodic pinning and depinning cycles that lead to concentric ring deposits.37

In between the nonpatterning limits, evaporation is fast enough such that there is sufficient solute remaining for patterning, but slow enough that ring formation delays contact line depinning. These dynamics give rise to the three pattern regimes shown in Figure 1d. High-speed videos allow for closer observation of patterning/crystallization dynamics at the retracting fluid front that result in array patterning (Figure 2a), branch patterning (Figure 2b), and sawtooth patterning (Figure 2c). The observations from the high-speed videos for both the array regime and sawtooth regime are shown in the cartoon schematics of Figure 2d,e, respectively. Experimental data shows a relationship between contact line velocity and patterning regime where faster dewetting is generally associated with array formation and slower dewetting with sawtooth formation (Figure 2f). However, there is significant overlap between the regimes. At VCL ∼ 1 mm/s, all three regimes coexist. Thus, differences in contact line velocity is insufficient to explain why the different regimes emerge at various temperatures and wettability.

Array Patterns

On substrates with moderate hydrophobicity and lower temperatures, periodic crystalline arrays emerge (Figures 2a and 1d(iii)). These arrays form via deposition and subsequent evaporation of ∼1–5 μm diameter droplets regularly spaced across the moving contact line (Figure 2d) to form small crystalline clusters with roughly spherical shapes (Figure 1d(iii)). Array patterns emerge for a wide range of contact line velocities (Figure 2f), and the transversal wavelength λ for these patterns ranges from 12 to 72 μm with most falling between 16 and 35 μm (Figure 2g).

Array patterns such as those observed here can be formed via a multitude of hydrodynamic instabilities arising from competing effects of evaporative and Marangoni flows,30,43,44 Rayleigh-Plateau breakup of a fluid ridge or thread,45 or forced wetting/dewetting of a viscoelastic fluid.46,47 Capillary forces lead to a transversal instability (Figure 2a), but we do not observe a longitudinal breakup of a fluid filament such as those seen during forced dewetting.46 Instead, individual drops are deposited in a row, with the previous drop having dried into crystals before the next drop breaks off from the retracting front.

Based on these observations, Marangoni flows are a likely mechanism for array patterning. There are two possible contributions to Marangoni flows in the present experiments. The first is evaporative cooling. Festoon-like instabilities have been observed for very slowly evaporating drops of water on oxidized silica, suggesting that evaporative heat transfer can sustain a pattern-forming instability.48 The majority of evaporation occurs at the contact line, and thus, a temperature gradient between the mobile contact line and bulk film can arise.48 The second potential contribution to Marangoni flows is the influence of the salt, which will increase surface tension in regions of higher concentration. This effect is expected to be small for calcium sulfate, which has limited solubility in water.49,50 However, on small enough length scales, even relatively small solutal gradients can cause Marangoni flow. Protruding segments of the contact line with more salt can induce Marangoni flow (Figure 2d), leading to formation and pinch-off of the microdroplets that lead to cluster formation upon complete evaporation.

While fingering instabilities can arise from Marangoni flows caused by either temperature or solutal gradients,30,43,44 experimental evidence suggests that solutal effects are important in the present experiments. Control experiments utilizing either DI water or colloidal-laden solutions in lieu of solutal drops under the same conditions do not exhibit this contact line instability, suggesting that the presence of salt is important in the array regime. These patterns are highly reminiscent of Marangoni-flow induced instabilities formed via differential evaporation rates of water and ethanol.30,51

Sawtooth Patterns

Sawtooth structures superimposed onto a layer of branched, dendritic crystals repeatedly emerge on the more hydrophilic substrates and at higher temperatures (Figures 2c and 1d(i)). Under these conditions, drop contact line velocity is relatively slow (Figure 2f). The appearance of these sawtooth patterns can be characterized by two parameters: the length L between adjacent triangle peaks defined by Figure 2d and shown in Figure 2g, and the sharpness of the angle of the triangle peaks ϕ shown in Figure 2h as a function of contact line velocity. The length between peaks encompass a huge range compared to the pattern wavelength in the array and branching regimes (Figure 2g). Peak angles are also observed over a huge range from extremely acute (ϕ ∼ 20°) to almost 90° (ϕ ∼ 85°). Unlike other pattern features (see SI Section S2), this angle exhibits a relationship with contact line velocity (Figure 2h).

Dendritic patterns commonly result from desiccation of dilute complex fluids,33−35,38−40 and can be attributed to diffusion-limited growth.52,53 Less obvious is the reason for the sawtooth structures observed within the dendrites. The sawtooth structures exhibit sharp boundaries between two or more phases of distinct crystalline structures where a thicker crystalline phase nucleates from a thinner phase, as shown in Figure 3. The tip of the thicker, secondary phase (which appears darker in images) always points toward the outer ring. Additional details on these patterns are included in the SI, where Figure S4 highlights some general observations, Figures S5 and S6 demonstrate that the two phases are composed of crystals of different thicknesses, Figure S7 shows how the different thicknesses result in different optical properties that make the thicker phase appear darker and the thinner phase appear lighter, and Figure S8 shows how dendritic growth aligns with the direction of flow.

The precise morphologies of the “thick” and “thin” phases of the sawtooth structures vary across different samples (Figure 3). The second, nucleated phase in Figure 3a has thicker, feather-like dendritic features with wider spacing compared to the thinner branches in the first phase. In contrast, the second phase of Figure 3b exhibits a closer packing (higher fractal dimension) than the thinner phase. The morphology of the secondary, thicker phase in Figure 3c is similar to the morphology of the thinner phase in this example, but still exhibits a sharp contrast in optical properties due to the increased thickness of the crystals in the thicker phase. Regardless of the exact morphology of the underlying dendritic features, the observation of sawtooth structures composed of a thinner phase and a nucleated thicker phase is consistent for the temperature and wettability conditions within the sawtooth regime of Figure 1e, even for substrates with different surface chemistry (Figure S9). We also observe that the two crystal phases are not continuous, with some separation between the thinner and thicker phases (Figure 3d–f). This is shown in the high-resolution SEM images in Figure 3e where the first phase terminates and in Figure 3f where the second phase nucleates.

Close observation of high-speed videos reveal that the contact line retracts faster over the thicker region compared to the surrounding thinner phase, as shown in the schematic of Figure 2e. This contact line retraction can be observed in Figure 2c, but is especially pronounced in the snapshot shown in Figure 4. The thicker phase spontaneously nucleates from the initially uniform, thinner phase and is associated with a change in the position and thickness of the contact line. The thinner phase region terminates once adjacent thick phases connect to form the sawtooth patterns.

The simultaneous coexistence of thicker and thinner fluid regions can be explained by the nonmonotonic form of the disjoining pressure for water on silica systems.48 However, it is unclear why the transition from a thinner region to a thicker one occurs in a sawtooth manner. A similar pattern has been observed for colloidal deposits, in which sawtooth patterns composed of mono- and bilayers of 0.1 μm nanoparticles were deposited at room temperature on hydrophilic mica (See SI Figure S12).31 Based on this observation, it is reasonable to hypothesize that similar phenomena drives both colloidal and crystalline sawtooth formation, and if so, that the presence of salt is less important in sawtooth patterning compared to array formation. However, unlike colloidal particles, crystals are constrained by their desired crystalline morphology, which often reflects the internal lattice structure of the crystals. Thus, the solute chemistry will influence the appearance of the triangular patterns. This is shown in Figure S10, where two additional sparingly soluble salts (silver sulfate and calcium iodate) also form sawtooth patterns upon precipitating from evaporating drops under the same conditions. Unlike calcium sulfate, these crystals do not form dendritic morphologies under these conditions and the resulting underlying structure of the crystals is different. Nevertheless, sawtooth patterns still emerge. We did not observe array or branched patterns for these salts. Further evidence that this instability is hydrodynamic rather than solutal is shown in Figure S11, where addition of surfactant alters the appearance of the calcium sulfate sawtooth morphologies to form patterns similar to Sierpinski triangles but does not prevent their formation.

A mechanism that allows for generation of triangular morphologies and is consistent with these observations is the nucleation of microscopic kink-like waves propagating azimuthally along the contact line.54 Nonlinear effects in very thin films can spontaneously amplify small local fluctuations into instabilities, even from films that are initially uniform in height.55 Thermo-capillary and thermosolutal instabilities can generate small waves seperating regions of locally thick and thinner regions,56−58 whose collision during evaporation would lead to sawtooth-like patterns. The presence of kink-like waves is suggested by high-speed videos where the contact line is more retracted over the thicker, darker triangular regions and exhibits a sharp transition to a less retracted position over the thinner, lighter region (Figure 4, see also SI Figure S14).

Numerical efforts support the idea that coherent wave structures can nucleate from initially uniform thin films containing solutal and temperature gradients (Figure S14).54 The propagation of the narrow, mobile kinks delineating regions of locally uniform but disparate film heights would lead to sawtooth structures seen here.54 Such a mechanism would explain sawtooth patterning of colloidal deposits and predict that the length scale between adjacent triangle features L is entirely stochastic. This prediction agrees with experimental data (Figure 2g), as L exhibits a much larger range (between 30 and 200 μm) than does λ for the branching or array patterns. Experimental data is in good agreement with eq1 (dashed line) and suggests a fixed VA of approximately 0.155 mm/s that is independent of VCL.

Although numerical efforts predict that kink-like instabilities can arise spontaneously from an initially uniform film containing thermosolutal gradients, it is possible that Marangoni forces also contribute to sawtooth formation. Stochastic nucleation of the thicker phase could locally deplete salt concentration and induces solutal Marangoni flow. In this case, VA would be proportional to a Marangoni velocity (VA ∼ Δσ/μ, where VA = 0.155 mm/s and μ = 10–3 kg/m-s). Based on typical values for VA, we can estimate Δσ as ∼0.15 × 10–6 N/m, which for calcium sulfate corresponds to a concentration difference of ∼0.1 mM (0.5% of the initial concentration of 20 mM).50 Thus, even minimal concentration gradients could be capable of driving azimuthal flows that lead to sawtooth patterns. However, such a mechanism does not account for similar patterns in colloidal systems where solutal flows are not expected to occur.31 In either case, the observed sawtooth-shaped patterns suggests that, once nucleated, the thicker phase grows faster than the thinner one. Our measurements indicate that the difference in phase growth velocity VA is independent of the contact line velocity, and that the formation of these structures follow the motion of the receding contact line.

Branching Regime

Between the dendritic sawtooth and crystalline array regimes lies an intermediate regime, in which individual crystal clusters still appear, but align into branched structures rather than into arrays (Figures 2b and Figure 1d(ii)). Clusters formed within this regime tend to be smaller than those formed in the array regime, with an associated decrease in spacing between “lines” of crystals. These branched patterns exhibit features of both the triangular and the array regimes, suggesting that this intermediary regime is influenced by the mechanisms responsible for the other regimes.

Contact Line Dynamics

To identify the process by which patterns organize into the three patterning regimes, we examine drop contact line dynamics. Contact line velocities in experiments containing calcium sulfate are significantly faster than retraction velocities for pure water due to contact line pinning delaying dewetting (see SI Section S2 and Figure S15). We observe that contact line velocities are generally faster in the array regime than they are for either the branching or triangular regimes (Figure 2e). However, there is overlap between the regimes near VCL ∼ 1 mm/s, suggesting that contact line velocity alone is insufficient to predict patterning regime. Contact line velocity also does not correlate to azimuthal pattern length scale (λ for arrays, L for triangles), as shown in Figure S16.

The nonpatterning regimes of the phase diagram depend strongly on the relative time scales of depinning and evaporation. Thus, we examine the influence of total evaporation time tE, the depinning time tD (the time at which the contact line depins from the outer ring of crystals to trigger pattern formation), and the retraction time tR which is the time spent between depinning and complete evaporation (i.e., tR = tE – tD). We plot the normalized contact line position R(t)/Ro as a function of normalized time t/tE for representative experiments in each of the three patterning regimes for a substrate with θE of 12° (Figure 5a).

(b) Magnitude of the ratio between the contact line retraction time to overall evaporation time as a function of substrate temperature and contact angle for 173 different experiments, demonstrating that this time scale ratio follows the same pattern as the regimes in Figure 1e.

In the absence of salt-induced pinning, contact line retraction for DI water on the hydrophilic surfaces (60 °C) proceeds according to the square-root dependence predicted for thin-film retraction in the absence of deposition and pinning (SI eq S7 and Figure S15).48 Examining the normalized contact line position for the triangular regime (80 °C), we find that it depins earlier in the process than it does for either the branching (60 °C) or array (40 °C) regimes. Thus, retraction time tR relative to the overall evaporation time tE is larger for the triangular regime than for the branching or array regimes; suggesting that more solute will be left for pattern formation in the triangular regime. Additional experiments at a large range of temperatures and contact angles were conducted to determine if the ratio of tR/tE is predictive for patterning regimes (Figure 5b). Comparing Figure 5b to Figure 1e, we see that tR/tE exhibits a similar trend with θE and T, suggesting that this ratio is sufficient to capture the change in dynamics leading to the distinct patterning regimes.

Following depinning, contact line velocity will be set by the underlying substrate contact angle and the evaporation rate. In this configuration, the thin-film dynamics are captured by the ratio between the retraction time and overall evaporation time. For example, on the more hydrophilic surfaces that give rise to triangle patterns, the drop depins from the outer contact line earlier in the process such that the depinning time scale tD/tE is smaller and the retraction time scale tR/tE is larger compared to the other two patterning regimes. This influences the contact line velocity, which retracts slower due to the larger retraction time scale (VCL = Ro/tR). This ratio also dictates the volume of water remaining during retraction. When the drop depins earlier, more water is left in the drop during retraction. When it depins later, less water remains during the retraction stage. The height of the thin film h at the moment of depinning will be approximately ∼ V(tD)/πRo2, where V(tD) is the volume of water remaining at the depinning time. The height of the liquid film during contact line retraction will dictate patterning regime. The wave instability proposed to generate sawtooth patterns occurs only for extremely thin films.54 Thus, this instability dominates at higher temperatures and lower contact angles because the thickness of the film is significantly reduced due to contact line pinning in the early stages of drop evaporation. The ratio between the dewetting time scale and overall evaporation time effectively captures the thickness of the film at the time of patterning because it reflects how long the drop evaporates while the contact line is pinned. The more time spent pinned, the thinner the film will be during dewetting. As with the patterning regimes, we find that contact line velocity does not correlate to pattern wavelength for any of the three regimes (Figure S16).

Applications

The types of patterns formed here have applications in sensors, diagnostics, optics, fog-harvesting, and more. To demonstrate their functionality, three-dimensional micropillar structures such as those used for superhydrophobic materials were fabricated using crystal patterns as a mask. Micropillar textures are typically fabricated by (1) coating of photoresist, (2) patterning the photoresist using light, (3) developing the photoresist in a chemical bath, (4) etching the regions not protected by photoresist using reactive ion etch (RIE), and (5) removing the photoresist. Steps 1,3 and 5 involve use of expensive and toxic chemicals. By using evaporation for patterned deposition, it is possible to skip steps 1–3 completely (see Figure S17). A substrate covered with patterned crystal arrays was placed directly into a reactive ion etch and etched to a depth of ∼10 μm, as shown in Figure 6a. The crystalline mask was then removed by soaking in a bath of saline water (the solubility of gypsum in water is increased in the presence of NaCl)60 for 10 min and rinsing with DI water. Thus, a three-dimensional microtextured surface was fabricated without the use of photoresist, developer, or organic solvents (Figure 6b). Additional images of three-dimensional etched patterns are shown in the SI (Figures S18 and S19).

Patterned metallic surfaces, which are notoriously difficult to fabricate, can also be generated by this technique. We sputter coated a patterned substrate with a 20 nm layer of gold followed by mask dissolution by soaking in a saline bath for 6 h, shown in Figure 6c,d. The resulting surface has patterned regions of gold and silica. Because this method is invariable to substrate chemistry, it is possible to deposit crystalline patterns on a variety of substrates. Thus, this procedure can be applied to generate any number of patterned chemical combinations while simultaneously improving process simplicity and sustainability compared to traditional methods.

The patterns formed via the current technique of evaporating drops of calcium sulfate in water lead to remarkably ordered structures. However, these patterns do have defects and imperfect periodicity, as can be observed in Figure 6 and other pattern images. For some applications in micro/nanoengineering, this imperfection may not matter. For example, the 3D micropillar array shown in Figure 6b has some variation in the spacing and size of the micropillars; but for applications in creating superhydrophobic surfaces, these small deviations will not matter significantly. However, small deviations will make a huge difference for other applications, such as microchip fabrication. In the future, it may be possible to achieve better control over pattern morphology and consistency by precisely controlling the evaporation conditions or by using techniques such as blade coating46 that allow better control over the thickness and retraction rate of evaporating interfaces.

Conclusions

Fluid instabilities can be harnessed for the self-assembly of patterned structures on the nano- and microscales by tuning of only two variables: the substrate temperature and the substrate contact angle. Alteration of these two variables alone allows for several patterning regimes to emerge due to the presence of one or more fluid instabilities. Crystallization at the contact line during evaporation leaves behind a record of fluid motion to create the patterns. The hexagonal array patterns are consistent with a Marangoni-driven instability, while the sawtooth patterns may emerge due to counter-propagating kink-like instability on the contact line, creating sharp boundaries between locally uniform thicker and thinner fluid regions. The branching regime exhibits features from the other two regimes including the regularly spaced crystal clusters observed in the array regime and the spontaneous formation of angular features similar to those observed in the sawtooth regime, leading us to conclude that both mechanisms are present in this intermediary regime. The regimes are dictated by contact line dynamics that are captured by the ratio between the retraction time scale and overall evaporation time, as this ratio reflects the height of the thin film during dewetting.

The ability to control and manipulate crystalline pattern morphologies through evaporative self-assembly enables new possibilities for various microscale fabrication applications including sensor arrays, photonics, bioengineering scaffolds, and superhydrophobic materials. Here we demonstrated two potential microfabrication applications for water-soluble crystalline patterns generated by drop evaporation. However, the current scalability of these patterns is limited to the area of drop. Translating this self-assembly process to larger length scales for widescale practical application could be accomplished by designing dip-coating systems with controlled evaporation rates and withdrawal rates. As demonstrated here, controlling both evaporation rate and contact line dynamics are key to predicting patterning morphology. In addition, fine-tuned control over solutal dynamics, which can be tuned by environmental parameters, should enable selection of wavelengths for the array regime for applications requiring specific wavelengths. In contrast, we predict that no such controllability over the wavelength between sawtooth patterns is possible due to the stochastic nature of the fluid instability responsible for those features.

Experimental Section

Salt Solution

Calcium sulfate solution was prepared by adding solid calcium sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich) salt to DI water at concentrations well above the solubility level. The solution was mixed for a period of at least 24 h before excess solid was filtered out, leaving a saturated solution free from solid crystals that could serve as nucleation seeds and thus influence crystal structures formed during evaporation. Other salt solutions, used in a limited number of experiments, were prepared in the same way.

Calcium sulfate is an ideal model salt for these experiments as its solubility is not significantly altered by temperature,49 with a solubility of 2g/L at 20 °C, a maximum solubility of 2.15 g/L at 40 °C, and a minimum solubility of 1.6 g/L at 100 °C. The relatively uniform solubility with temperature enables the use of heating to control patterning regimes without adding confounding factors related to altered solubility with changing temperatures. Calcium sulfate is also ideal for these experiments as it crystallizes readily and is sparingly soluble at room temperature. This is in contrast to soluble salts such as sodium chloride (solubility of 360 g/L at 20 °C) which forms 3-dimensional structures such as “igloos” and globes upon evaporation due to the large amounts of crystal mass,61,62 and insoluble salts such as calcium carbonate (solubility of 0.013 g/L at 20 °C) which will form only limited deposits.

Two other sparingly soluble salts with solubility concentrations similar to that of calcium sulfate, silver sulfate (solubility 8.2 g/L at 20 °C) and calcium iodate (2.4 g/L at 20 °C), were tested in a limited number of experiments to probe the universality of the patterning phenomena. We find that sawtooth patterns are able to emerge from these salts as well; though the appearance of the patterns is altered compared to those formed from calcium sulfate (SI Figure S10). Array patterns were not observed in these experiments.

Crystal Structure

Calcium sulfate is able to form a number of different crystal structures including anhydrite (CaSO4), bassanite (CaSO4-nH2O,where n ranges from 0.5 to 0.8), hemihydrate (CaSO4–H2O), and dihydrate (CaSO4-2H2O). The dihydrate form is also known as gypsum and is the most common form of calcium sulfate crystal under normal atmospheric conditions.63 With sufficient heating (temperatures >90 °C), gypsum can dehydrate into hemihydrate or bassanite. For the macroscopic crystals formed at the contact line in the present experiments, gypsum is most likely the dominant structure. If either bassanite or anhydrite were to form, they would readily rehydrate to gypsum at room temperature.

Substrates

Silica substrates from University Wafer were used as substrates in these experiments. Wafers were cleaned by successive rinses of DI water, acetone, and IPA; as previously described.32,38 Wafers were then plasma cleaned (Harrick Plasma PLASMAFLO PDC-FMG) using air plasma at a pressure of 100 mTorr and 45W RF power for at least 5 min to remove residual organics and generate hydroxyl radicals to create clean, wettable surfaces.64 The amount of time between cleaning and drop evaporation experiments was used for contact angle variation. Superhydrophilic substrates were used immediately or within hours after plasma treatment. Silica substrates return to their preplasma cleaning contact angle after approximately 2 weeks due to loss of hydroxyl groups with time (see SI Figure S20). Similar techniques have been previously used for achieving temporary hydrophilic and superhydrophilic substrate contact angles.65 Water contact angles were characterized using a Rame-hart goniometer just prior to experimentation. The macroscopic contact angles for DI water and for calcium sulfate solution are essentially identical, as the limited solubility of calcium sulfate is insufficient to cause strong deviation from deionized (DI) water contact angles. For comparison, contact angles of saturated sodium chloride solution (with a solubility 100× greater than that of calcium sulfate) only alters contact angles by a few degrees compared to DI water.66 Receding contact angles, which will be of particular importance for dewetting films, were approximately 0 degrees for all substrates.

Experiments

The experimental setup has been described previously.32,38 Briefly, substrates were heated on a hot plate with a layer of thermal paste for uniform heating. The ambient conditions were temperature controlled at 22 °C with relative humidity between 30 and 45%. Five μL drops of saturated calcium sulfate dissolved in water were placed on the heated substrates, and the process of evaporation was recorded from above using a Zeiss AxioZoom microscope. Two different types of movies were recorded: videos of global behavior (i.e., with the entire drop in view) recorded using a Nikon D300, and high-speed, high-resolution videos targeting specific locations were recorded using a high-speed camera which captured video at either 1000 or 2000 fps. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, SI Figure S6) images were taken of selected deposits to extract geometrical parameters of patterns.

Images and Processing

Data was extracted from the global videos using imageJ and included: initial contact radius, radius with time, overall evaporation time, time until depinning from the outer contact ring, and general classification of deposit formed (reported in Figure 1e). From high-speed videos, contact line velocities at specific locations were extracted. These same locations were imaged using Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Gemini 360) for a higher resolution measurement of the pattern geometrical parameters (λ, L, and ϕ) in order to correlate the contact line velocity to the pattern geometry (see SI Figure S16). SEM images were captured using two different detectors, an In-Lens scattered electron detector and an Everhart-Thornley detector for secondary electrons (SE2 detector). The SE2 detector is generally better for capturing morphologies, while the In-Lens detector is better for contrasting regions with differing optical properties. False color was added to the SEM images shown in Figure 6c,d. The other images were not color adjusted.
