Warm aortic arch repair: Redefining proximal aortic arch surgery
Authors: Shinichi Fukuhara, Robert B. Hawkins, Gorav Ailawadi, Barbara Hamilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe recently introduced warm aortic arch repair, a new novel approach that obviates the need for hypothermic circulatory arrest. However, there have been no comparative analyses with conventional repair.MethodsBetween 2018 and 2024, 919 patients underwent hemiarch or partial arch repair. Clinical outcomes were compared between the warm aortic arch repair (n = 210) and conventional arch repair (n = 709) groups.ResultsBaseline clinical characteristics were similar in the 2 groups, except type A aortic dissection was more common in the conventional repair group (49.2% vs 31.9%; P < .001). The warm repair group had significantly shorter cardiopulmonary bypass, cross-clamp, cerebral perfusion, and operative times, as well as a lower blood transfusion rate, compared to the conventional repair group. In-hospital mortality was similar (2.4% vs 3.9%; P = .28), the warm repair group had lower rates of disabling stroke (2.4% vs 6.5%; P = .022) and a composite outcome of mortality plus stroke (4.3% vs 9.9%; P = .011), along with lower rates of prolonged ventilation, renal failure, new dialysis, and shorter intensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay. After 1:1 propensity score matching among patients with hemiarch with or without aortic valve replacement, with a similar number of type A dissections in the 2 groups, the warm repair group’s advantages in operative efficiency and postoperative metrics persisted. However, there were no between-group differences in in-hospital mortality (2.1% vs 3.1%; P = 1.00), disabling stroke (1.0% vs 3.1%; P = .62), or composite outcomes (3.1% vs 6.2%; P = .31).ConclusionsAlthough the conventional aortic arch repair approach with hypothermic circulatory arrest is safe, warm aortic arch repair offers advantages, including better operative efficiency, lower transfusion rates, and improved postoperative metrics. This technique represents an opportunity to reevaluate the role of hypothermic circulatory arrest in aortic arch surgery.

Central Message

Warm aortic arch repair is safe and reproducible, offering advantages over conventional repair, including operative efficiency, lower transfusion rates, and improved postoperative metrics.

Perspective

While conventional aortic arch repair with hypothermic circulatory arrest is safe, our recently introduced warm arch repair offers advantages, including operative efficiency, lower transfusion rates, and improved postoperative metrics. The establishment of this technique represents an opportunity to reevaluate the role of hypothermic circulatory arrest and redefine the standard of care in arch surgery.

The evolution of aortic arch surgery is a narrative of extraordinary innovation, driven by the need to address complex aortic pathologies while preserving critical organ function. The development of hypothermic circulatory arrest by Griepp and colleagues1 in the 1970s represented a groundbreaking advancement. Their work demonstrated that profound hypothermia could protect the brain and vital organs during periods of circulatory arrest. Further refinements in the 1990s by Ueda and colleagues, who pioneered retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP),2 and Kazui and colleagues, who developed selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP),3 mitigated the risk of neurologic complications. The 1980s and 1990s heralded the emergence of hybrid techniques, combining open techniques with endovascular interventions, along with the development of the classic4 and frozen elephant trunk.5

Compared with those transformative milestones, the past 2 decades have witnessed relatively few paradigm-shifting innovations. Most progress has centered on incremental enhancements of existing techniques, including refined body temperature and perfusion strategies. Although these refinements undeniably have enhanced clinical outcomes, they have not fundamentally reshaped the surgical landscape. Moreover, reliance on hypothermic circulatory arrest, although effective, remains associated with such challenges as prolonged operative times and complications linked to hypothermia and circulatory arrest.6, 7, 8

We recently introduced a novel warm aortic arch repair technique9 that obviates the need for hypothermic circulatory arrest or peripheral cannulation. This approach may represent a significant departure from traditional methods, aiming to improve operative efficiency and reduce the complications related to existing techniques. In the present study, we compared the clinical outcomes associated with warm aortic arch repair and conventional arch repair with hypothermic circulatory arrest.

Methods

The University of Michigan Institutional Review Board approved all aspects of the study (HUM00133791; approved December 1, 2017). The approval included a waiver of informed consent.

Patients and Study Design

Between January 2018 and September 2024, a consecutive cohort of 919 patients underwent hemiarch or partial arch repair. Among them, 416 patients (45.3%) had acute type A aortic dissection. The extent of arch repair was hemiarch repair in 775 patients (84.3%), zone 1 repair in 42 (4.6%), and zone 2 repair in 102 (11.1%). Clinical outcomes were compared between patients who underwent warm arch repair (n = 210) and those who underwent conventional aortic arch repair (n = 709) (Figure 1). Prospectively maintained clinical data on aortic pathologies and operative details were used, supplemented by perioperative variables retrieved from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons data elements within the University of Michigan Cardiac Surgery Data Warehouse.Figure 1Study cohort. Patient Selection for Warm Aortic Arch Repair

The warm aortic arch repair approach was implemented in our practice beginning in 2019.9 Initially used for straightforward aneurysms during the early adoption phase, the clinical indications and the extent of arch repair have since expanded. Accumulated experience and a progressive learning curve have established warm aortic arch repair as a viable approach for broader clinical indications, encompassing both hemiarch and partial arch repairs. The presence of arch pathology associated with significant embolic risk at the intended clamp site should be thoroughly assessed. While only mild intimal thickening and calcifications are considered acceptable, warm arch repair should be avoided in patients with severe calcifications and/or atheromatous disease of the aortic arch.

Surgical Technique

Warm aortic arch repair

The standard setup included upper body and lower body arterial monitoring (right radial and femoral arteries), near-infrared spectroscopy, median sternotomy, and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with central arch cannulation and standard venous drainage (Video 1). Pharmacologic neuroprotective agents were not used. Aortic cannulation was performed at the anterosuperior arch between the left carotid and left subclavian arteries using double purse-string sutures (Figure 2, A and B). The left recurrent laryngeal nerve was carefully visualized during cannulation. In DeBakey type I dissections, the arterial cannula was inserted into the true lumen using a Seldinger technique with 100% technical success. Posteriorly located true lumens or intramural hematomas were not considered contraindications in our practice.9 The needle trajectory was planned using preoperative imaging studies to estimate the spatial relationship between the aortic entry site and the true lumen. For a posteriorly located true lumen, the needle was advanced from the false lumen across the septum into the true lumen, guided by tactile feedback. Guidewire position within the true lumen at the proximal descending aorta was confirmed by transesophageal echocardiography before serial dilation and cannula placement. After distal ascending aortic cross-clamping and standard myocardial protection, the arch repair was performed first regardless of any concomitant procedures. A Crawford clamp was applied to the zone 1 or zone 2 arch, depending on the pathology (Figure 2, C and D). ACP was delivered through 13- or 15-Fr balloon-tipped catheters (Gundry silicone retrograde cannula; Medtronic) inserted into the innominate and/or left carotid arteries after arch clamping. Zone 1 clamping used unilateral ACP with left carotid perfusion from the aortic cannula, whereas zone 2 clamping used bilateral ACP. The intervessel spacing between supra-aortic branches did not limit clamp placement; the cannulation location and thin jaws of the Crawford clamp allowed secure clamping independent of branch distance.

ACP flow was titrated to maintain a right radial mean arterial pressure of 50 to 80 mmHg. The median ACP flow was 8.6 mL/kg/minute (interquartile range [IQR], 7.5-10.0 mL/kg/minute) for unilateral ACP and 11.3 mL/kg/minute (IQR, 9.5-14.0 mL/kg/minute) for bilateral ACP. Straight Dacron grafts without branches were used for most hemiarch repairs. In DeBakey type I dissections, a single-branch graft was used, and the CPB arterial line was transferred to the graft side arm after distal anastomosis. The distal aortic anastomosis, with or without head vessel reconstruction, was then completed (Figure 2, E and F). Sequential arterial back-bleeding from the contralateral cerebral circulation was used to facilitate deairing and reduce embolic risk.

Conventional aortic arch repair

The conventional aortic arch technique has been described in detail elsewhere.10 Arterial cannulation included central, right subclavian, intrathoracic innominate, or femoral artery access. Systemic cooling was performed to the target temperature, monitored via bladder probes. Hypothermic circulatory arrest was then instituted, followed by adjunctive ACP and/or RCP and distal aortic anastomosis. Systemic rewarming was initiated after completion of the distal anastomosis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (IQR), and categorical variables are presented as count and percentage. Group comparisons used the χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. To adjust for measured confounders, propensity score matching was performed among patients undergoing hemiarch repair with or without surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) (n = 409). Propensity scores were generated using multivariable logistic regression including clinically relevant variables (Figure E1). Nearest-neighbor matching identified 97 pairs using a caliper of 0.2 standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD <0.1 indicating acceptable balance. All tests were 2-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and SPSS version 27.0 (IBM).

Compared with the conventional repair group, the warm repair group demonstrated significantly shorter CPB time (122 minutes vs 201 minutes; P < .001), aortic cross-clamp time (98 minutes vs 143 minutes; P < .001), cerebral perfusion time (14 minutes vs 20 minutes; P < .001), and operative time (269 minutes vs 411 minutes; P < .001). Blood transfusion rates were significantly lower in the warm repair group (36.7% vs 64.0%; P < .001), with an approximate 2-fold reduction in individual blood product utilization. The shortest operative time in the warm repair group was 76 minutes, and 20 cases (9.5%) were completed in under 120 minutes. The rate of concomitant procedures was similar, approximately 60% in both groups. Aortic root repair was more common in the conventional repair group, whereas aortic root enlargement, aortic valve repair, and Ross procedure were more frequent in the warm repair group.

Propensity Score Matching Among Patients With Hemiarch Repair ± SAVR

Propensity score matching included 409 patients undergoing hemiarch ± SAVR. After matching, baseline characteristics were well-balanced, with no significant differences in age, sex, or comorbidities (Table E1). Before matching, the rate of type A aortic dissection was higher in the conventional repair group (38.1% vs 53.5%; P = .008; SMD = 0.310). After matching, the rates were comparable (38.1% vs 35.1%; P = .66; SMD = 0.064).

The warm repair group had significantly shorter CPB, aortic cross-clamp, and operative times (Table E2), as well as a lower transfusion rates. Median ICU length of stay (56 hours vs 73 hours; P = .033) and hospital length of stay (6 days vs 8 days; P = .048) were also shorter in the warm repair group. In addition, trends toward lower rates of renal failure (3.2% vs 9.8%; P = .079) and dialysis (0% vs 4.3%; P = .059) were observed in the warm repair group. Mortality (2.1% vs 3.1%; P = 1.00) and rates of disabling stroke (1.0% vs 3.1%; P = .62) and the composite adverse outcome (3.1% vs 6.2%; P = .31) were similar between matched groups (Table E3). All mortalities occurred in patients with type A aortic dissection, with no deaths observed in patients with nondissection pathologies.

Among the subgroup undergoing isolated hemiarch repair, the median operative time was 154 minutes (IQR, 119-226 minutes) in the warm repair group and 322 minutes (IQR, 263-450 minutes) in the conventional repair group (P < .001). Other perioperative variables are compared in Table E4.

Discussion

Despite the well-established safety of hypothermic circulatory arrest and adjunctive cerebral protection, there remains a considerable opportunity for refining surgical strategies. The warm aortic arch repair concept embodies uninterrupted systemic blood flow and simplicity under normothermia. The primary findings of interest in this study are (1) among proximal arch repair cases, adoption of warm arch repair has increased steadily, with its case volume surpassing that of conventional approach recently; (2) the warm arch repair group demonstrated shorter CPB, aortic cross-clamp, and operative times, along with lower rates of blood transfusion; (3) both the warm repair and conventional repair groups had excellent clinical outcomes, despite the complex patient population; (4) the warm repair group had lower rates of disabling stroke, composite adverse outcomes, and renal complications and a shorter hospital length of stay; (5) on propensity score–matched analysis, the warm repair group maintained 1.5-fold shorter CPB, aortic cross-clamp, and operative times, with reduced transfusion requirements and shorter hospital stays, without a significant difference in mortality or neurologic outcomes; and (6) warm arch repair was associated with a more than 2-fold shorter operative time for isolated hemiarch repair among matched patients.

Although overall rates of adverse events were low regardless of the repair technique, warm aortic arch repair demonstrated some advantages, including enhanced operative efficiency, reduced transfusion rates, and improved postoperative metrics. The difference in cerebral perfusion time between the warm and conventional repair approaches primarily reflects differences in operative workflow. Differences in the efficiency and timing of initiation and termination of cerebral perfusion likely account for the longer perfusion times observed in the conventional repair group. Beyond clinical outcomes, these advantages are assumed to have broader implications for healthcare systems, particularly in terms of operating room efficiency, resource utilization, and potential resultant cost containment. However, despite improved operative efficiency and several favorable perioperative metrics, these differences did not translate into statistically significant reductions in mortality or neurologic complications after propensity score matching. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, most deaths occurred in patients with type A dissection presenting with shock and/or severe end-organ malperfusion, in whom outcomes are determined largely by the patient’s preoperative condition rather than by the operative strategy. Second, conventional arch repair is already a well-established and safe approach with excellent outcomes at experienced centers. In the context of relatively low adverse event rates and limited sample size, statistical differences in mortality or stroke may not be detectable despite numerically lower rates of these complications. In our experience, the warm repair approach is particularly advantageous for specific patient populations, such as Jehovah's Witnesses, those on direct oral anticoagulants, patients with preexisting kidney disease, and those presenting with time-sensitive complications, such as type A aortic dissection with malperfusion. Moreover, it may play a pivotal role in unplanned arch repairs, including iatrogenic dissection or unexpected arch enlargement during non-arch cardiac surgery.

Hypothermic circulatory arrest has been the gold standard for modern arch surgery; however, optimal temperature management remains uncertain.11 There has been a recent worldwide shift toward less intense hypothermia because of concerns regarding coagulopathy, systemic inflammatory response, and neuronal apoptosis associated with deep hypothermia. Retrospective nonrandomized studies have suggested increased morbidity and mortality with deep hypothermia.12 Concerns also remain regarding circulatory arrest itself, as it requires cessation of blood flow to the brain and other organs, exposing patients to ischemic and embolic injury. Leshnower and colleagues13 reported neurologist-adjudicated examinations with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Diffusion-weighted MRI demonstrated lesions in 100% (n = 9 of 9) of patients treated with moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest and ACP versus 45% (n = 5 of 11) of those treated with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest and RCP, despite no difference in clinical neurologic examinations. The authors suggested that MRI-detected injury may reflect the embolic burden from head vessel manipulation and clamping. We believe that the period of circulatory arrest and cessation of systemic blood flow—regardless of temperature—poses an inherent risk of brain and other end-organ injury. Furthermore, neurologic injury can result from factors related to cooling/rewarming alone, particularly excessive gradients and rapid rewarming.7,8 In the warm repair technique without any temperature fluctuation, sequential ACP initiation/discontinuation and robust back-bleeding at the conclusion of anastomosis may contribute to the reduction of embolic risk by effectively flushing debris and air from head vessels, leading to a lower rate of disabling stroke.

Warm aortic arch repair is widely applicable to various aortic pathologies but is not mutually exclusive with conventional approaches. One of the most critical lessons from this experience has been identifying when not to perform warm aortic arch repair. The sole neurologic mortality in our series was seen in a patient with severe atherosclerotic burden in the aortic arch—a clear contraindication for arch clamping in retrospect. A meticulous preoperative image review is essential, focusing on calcification, atherosclerosis, and mural thrombus in the aortic arch. Nonetheless, we estimate that more than 90% of proximal arch pathologies can be safely addressed using the warm repair technique. Additional limitations include the learning curve and the absence of a dedicated arch clamp optimized for this procedure.

Study Limitations

Significant controversy surrounds warm aortic arch repair as a recently adopted technique. Warm arch repair was performed predominantly in the latter part of the study period, which might have introduced era-related bias. Additional inherent limitations include the study’s retrospective nature and relatively small sample size. While propensity score matching was performed to mitigate these biases, residual confounding factors may persist. Patient selection was mostly affected by our learning curve and evolving surgical indications. This temporal evolution reflects not an intentional selection of “easier” cases, but rather the natural progression of our practice and growing experience with the technique. Instead, warm aortic arch repair has become the preferred approach for sicker patients to minimize surgical insults.

In summary, warm aortic arch repair has emerged as a valid technique for proximal arch repair. Although conventional approaches remain highly effective, warm repair offers significant advantages, including operative efficiency, lower blood transfusion rates, and improved postoperative metrics (Figure 4). The warm repair technique represents an opportunity to reevaluate the role of hypothermic circulatory arrest in the modern era. Future investigations involving larger cohorts and diverse institutions, as well as prospective randomized trials, are essential to validate these results and refine the indications for warm repair.
