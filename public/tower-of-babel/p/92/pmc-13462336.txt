Weight status and pathways to eating disorder referral from primary care in England and Northern Ireland: a cross-sectional analysis of routine care data
Authors: Chloe Gao, Jessica Wilkins, Alex Dregan, Karina L Allen, Jamie Scuffell, Ulrike Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThere is a need to understand how weight status influences access to eating disorder services. We aimed to examine characteristics of adults with eating disorder diagnoses in primary care settings in England and Northern Ireland and to assess the association between weight status and referral to specialist eating disorder services.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of electronic health records from Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum, which includes general practices in England and Northern Ireland, with an observation period of Jan 1, 2010, to Dec 31, 2023. We included people aged 18–80 years with at least one eating disorder diagnosis with or without an eating disorder referral, and excluded people with only an eating disorder referral without an eating disorder diagnosis. All data were extracted from CPRD Aurum except Index of Multiple Deprivation, which was extracted from the UK Office of National Statistics. To examine the association between the primary exposure of weight status (ie, with underweight vs without underweight) and the main outcome of eating disorder referrals from primary care, we conducted six sets of generalised estimating equation models accounting for practice-level clustering. All primary models were done with a complete-case analysis, with participants excluded if data were missing for any included variables. To explore potential multiplicative effects of the two exposure variables, weight status and eating disorder diagnostic category, on our outcome variable of eating disorder referral, we included an interaction term between weight and eating disorder diagnosis.Findings35 404 people with at least one eating disorder diagnosis aged 18–80 years were included in our analysis, of whom 26 822 (75·8%) did not receive an eating disorder referral. Most people identified as White (29 545 [83·5%] of 35 404), followed by Asian (2154 [6·1%]), unknown (1325 [3·7%]), Black (1028 [2·9%]), mixed/multiple (682 [1·9%]), and other (110 [0·3%]). There were 28 944 (81·8%) female individuals and 6460 (18·2%) male individuals. The interaction term between anorexia nervosa diagnosis and underweight was positively associated with receiving an eating disorder referral (odds ratio 4·05 [95% CI 3·54–4·63]; p<0·0001). The interaction term between having an avoidant restrictive food intake disorder diagnosis and underweight was not statistically significant (0·84 [0·53–1·32]; p=0·54). Similarly, the interaction term between having a binge eating disorder diagnosis and underweight was not statistically significant (0·62 [0·10–3·88]; p=0·61). The interaction terms between having an eating disorder diagnosis and underweight for bulimia nervosa (0·76 [0·64–0·90]; p=0·0018), other specified feeding or eating disorder (0·76 [0·64–0·90]; p=0·0018), and generic eating disorder (0·72 [0·65–0·80]; p<0·0001) were negatively associated with receiving an eating disorder referral.InterpretationFindings from this analysis indicate that people in England and Northern Ireland might encounter barriers to accessing eating disorder treatment within the National Health Service, where specialist care often requires General Practitioner referral. Disparities in eating disorder referral by diagnosis indicate a need to improve recognition of eating disorders beyond anorexia nervosa.FundingMedical Research Council, Arts and Humanities Research Council, Economic and Social Research Council, and the UK National Institute of Health Research.

Introduction

Eating disorders are serious mental illnesses with a lifetime pre-COVID pandemic prevalence of 8·4% in women and 2·2% in men.1 Despite substantial morbidity and mortality, many affected individuals do not access specialist care.2 Those who do access specialist care often have protracted periods of illness before accessing care, ranging from 2 years for anorexia nervosa to up to 8 years for binge eating disorder.3 Delayed treatment is associated with poorer long-term prognosis, including increased need for more intensive treatment.4 In the UK, primary care serves as the main point of entry into specialist eating disorder services.5 Individuals typically must obtain a referral via their general practitioner (GP) for specialist treatment4 and often remain engaged in primary care beyond referral, with 36% of National Health Service (NHS) eating disorder teams having what is known as shared care provisions for psychiatric comorbidities and 29% for physical health problems.6 However, existing evidence indicates that, although people frequently present to their GP with concerns related to eating disorders, referral rates to specialist services in England remain low.2

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for papers examining the effect of weight status on referral to treatment for any eating disorders published from database inception to Jan 10, 2026, with the search terms “eating disorder∗” OR “disordered eat∗” OR “anorexi∗” OR “bulimi∗” OR “binge eat∗” OR “ARFID” OR “OSFED” OR “UFED” OR “EDNOS” OR “feeding or eating disorder∗” [Title/Abstract] AND “weight status” OR “body weight” OR “BMI” OR “body mass index” OR “higher weight” OR “low weight” OR “weight stigma” OR “weight bias” [Title/Abstract] AND “referral” OR “referred” OR “pathway” OR “access to treatment” OR “service access” OR “primary care” [Title/Abstract] without any additional restrictions, including no language restrictions. Existing evidence suggests that eating disorders occur across the weight spectrum, but that people with underweight might be less likely to be diagnosed, referred, or admitted to specialist eating disorder treatment. There was more available research for individuals diagnosed with anorexia nervosa than individuals with other eating disorder diagnoses. Evidence examining patterns of diagnosis and referral in primary care in the UK was scarce. None of the identified studies provided direct evidence that primary care referral decisions are systematically affected by weight status across eating disorder diagnosis in the UK. No studies with similar aims or methods to our analysis were found.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to examine eating disorder referral patterns in primary care in England and Northern Ireland via routinely collected electronic health records for adults. Previous UK-based electronic health record studies have primarily described eating disorder incidence, prevalence, or diagnostic patterns, leaving referral to specialist care comparatively underexplored. By focusing on referral outcomes, this analysis addresses an important gap in understanding whether people with eating disorders can access specialist services after presenting to primary care.

Implications of all the available evidence

Findings from evidence to date, including our analysis, indicate that people might encounter barriers to accessing evidence-based eating disorder treatment. Findings from our analysis also indicate that barriers to accessing specialist eating disorder treatment might persist within the UK National Health Service, particularly for diagnoses other than anorexia nervosa. Allowing self-referral to eating disorder services could enable people with concerns about eating disorder symptoms to access care directly, reducing delays stemming from provider-level barriers and navigational challenges. These results add to calls to improve screening tools and education in primary care to improve eating disorder detection and facilitate timely access to specialist eating disorder treatment.

One in seven people accessing the NHS referred from primary to secondary care services for any condition end up in a suspended referral pathway, whereby referrals are delayed, lost, rejected, or not sent, suggesting gaps even after referral.7 Furthermore, epidemiological data indicate that only 17–18% of first recorded presentations of eating disorders in primary care in England are referred to specialist services, with substantial regional and demographic differences in referral rates.2 Barriers to referral include insufficient GP knowledge of eating disorders, little access to screening tools, stigma, and an absence of clear treatment pathways.8 Importantly, positive experiences with health professionals (eg, GPs) can improve treatment acceptance.9

There is growing recognition of the need for timely eating disorder treatment; however, gaps remain in understanding referral patterns to specialist services in people with eating disorders in the UK. Existing studies typically focus on anorexia nervosa, with comparatively little evidence on referral pathways for other diagnoses such as bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, or other specified feeding or eating disorder,2,10 despite these being more common in the general population.10 For example, in an internet survey of 22 397 US adults, 344 (1·5%) met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-5 criteria for binge eating disorder in the past 12 months but, of these, only 11 (3·2%) had ever been diagnosed by a health-care provider.11 However, no UK studies have examined eating disorder referral pathways by diagnostic category in a primary care context.

Although eating disorders occur across the weight spectrum, individuals with higher BMIs experience greater barriers to eating disorder referral than their lower-weight counterparts.12 Although obesity and eating disorders (including bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, and other specified feeding or eating disorder) frequently co-occur, people with both eating disorders and obesity are more likely to access obesity treatment than eating disorder treatment.13 However, weight-loss treatments are different from eating disorder treatments, and are less effective in reducing eating disorder symptoms than evidence-based psychological treatments.14 Furthermore, although the majority of individuals with eating disorders are without underweight, those with typical or higher weight frequently encounter delayed recognition and access to treatment.12 Cumulative evidence suggests a need to better understand how people with typical or higher weight and eating disorders access eating disorder services from primary care settings across diagnoses.

Electronic health records provide information from clinical encounters, offering a comprehensive picture of how individuals enter and move through the health-care system.15 Examining referral patterns through primary care data offers a proxy for access to specialist eating disorder services from primary care settings. However, previous work with electronic health records in England was largely concentrated on eating disorder incidence or prevalence,2 leaving referral patterns across the adult population largely unexamined. To our knowledge, this analysis is the first to investigate referrals with routinely collected primary care data for adults in England and Northern Ireland, thereby addressing a crucial gap in understanding how people with eating disorders access specialist care.

We aimed to examine characteristics of people with eating disorder diagnoses in England and Northern Ireland primary care settings and to assess the association between weight status and referral to specialist eating disorder services by eating disorder diagnosis. Given that delayed eating disorder treatment access is associated with poorer long-term prognosis, including increased need for more intensive treatment, it is crucial to identify which people with eating disorders might be under-referred to address primary care-level barriers to referral.4 We aim to inform strategies to reduce barriers to help-seeking and improve access to eating disorder treatment in England and Northern Ireland. Based on existing evidence, we hypothesised that people with eating disorders with normal or high BMI would be less likely to be referred to eating disorder services across all eating disorder diagnostic categories than their counterparts with underweight.12

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of electronic health records from Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum, which includes general practices in England and Northern Ireland. The observation period was from Jan 1, 2010, to Dec 31, 2023. CPRD contains anonymised data for 60 million historical patients, including 18 million who are currently registered.16 Data in CPRD are broadly representative of the UK population and currently an estimated 2000 (approximately 28%) of GP practices in England provide data, representing about 27% of the population. Ethics approval for this analysis was granted by CPRD’s Research Data Governance Process (reference number 24_004014).

Eligible individuals were adults aged 18–80 years and were identified with Read and/or Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) medical codes corresponding to at least one eating disorder diagnosis with or without an eating disorder referral. We excluded people with only an eating disorder referral without an eating disorder diagnosis. The index date was defined as the first date of eating disorder diagnosis. GP records from the preceding 24 months (fixed for all participants) were used to identify eating disorder referrals, BMI measurements, and other demographic information.

All data in CPRD are anonymised. Individual patient consent was therefore not required under the CPRD Research Data Governance Framework (Protocol Number 24_004014). People can opt out of sharing their records with CPRD at any time, even if their practice provides information to the database.

We worked with an advisory group of young people aged 16–25 years with lived experience of eating disorders who offered feedback on the protocol. A person with lived experience of an eating disorder was involved in the design, implementation, and analysis.

Procedures

Code lists (Read and/or SNOMED CT) for variables in this analysis were developed following the guidance and checklist provided by Matthewman and colleagues17 and reviewed by a primary care specialist on our research team (JS). Full details of each checklist and the full code list are available in the appendix (pp 18–36). We used these codes to classify people into diagnostic categories of anorexia nervosa, avoidant restrictive food intake disorder, binge eating disorder, bulimia nervosa, other specified feeding or eating disorder, or generic eating disorder. People could be in more than one category for this study. Using BMI data recorded in CPRD Aurum, we identified people with underweight and without underweight.

Our main outcome variable was eating disorder referral. Referral was defined as any recorded referral to eating disorder services during the 24-month observation period preceding the eating disorder diagnosis, or on the same date as the diagnosis. We identified referrals to specialist eating-disorder services using a prespecified list of referral codes that explicitly indicated a request for assessment or management by secondary care mental health or eating disorder teams (table 1).

Our confounders were Index of Multiple Deprivation 2019 (IMD) quintile, which is a commonly used measure in public health research relating to health inequalities in the UK and was extracted from the UK Office of National Statistics;2 sex, which was derived from CPRD Aurum registration records with the options male, female, or unknown, as recorded by the general practice; age, which was defined by age at index date; and region. Further details on each measure are included in table 1. Ethnicity data were collected from CPRD Aurum, which classifies these data using UK Office of National Statistics categories (ie, White, Asian, Black, mixed/multiple, and other ethnic group). Ethnicity was obtained from CPRD Aurum Ethnicity Records, which is an algorithm-derived variable that assigns a single ethnicity category for each patient via information recorded in primary care records, and links to Hospital Episode Statistics data to improve completeness.

To account for potential confounding, we compared estimates across unadjusted, minimally adjusted, and fully adjusted models, with adjustment for demographic characteristics, including age, sex, region, and area-level deprivation. The study used routinely collected CPRD data that have previously been shown to be broadly representative of the UK population.16

Statistical analysis

Data linkage and preprocessing were done in Stata version 18 by the author AD, before datasets were transferred to R version 4.2.2 for statistical analysis. CPRD Aurum records were cleaned and transformed into analytical variables, including diagnosis, ethnicity, BMI, referral, and deprivation categories, by author CG. To examine the association between weight status (ie, with underweight vs without underweight) and eating disorder referral status in people with eating disorders, we used generalised estimating equations (GEEs) with an exchangeable correlation structure accounting for potential clustering within practices. Underweight was defined according to WHO guidelines as a BMI of less than 18·5 kg/m2 at any time during the 24-month observation period. We assessed the distribution of continuous variables through visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. BMI displayed right-skewness, consistent with population-based distributions.19

To explore potential multiplicative effects of weight status and eating disorder diagnosis on eating disorder referral status, we included an interaction term between weight status and eating disorder diagnosis type as the primary exposure variable and eating disorder referral status as our main outcome. Unadjusted, minimally adjusted (with age), and fully adjusted GEE models with potential confounders (ie, age, sex, region, and IMD) were fit to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. This approach reflects the view that crude effect estimates are particularly important in quantitative analyses of health disparities using an intersectional lens. Adjusting for sociodemographic factors can mask these differences by netting out characteristics that are not equal across the groups being compared.20,21

Tests of interaction and comparisons between exposure groups were prespecified. For continuous variables, we conducted independent two-sample Student's t tests to examine statistically significant differences by eating disorder referral status. For categorical variables, we conducted χ2 tests of independence to examine statistically significant differences by eating disorder referral status.

All primary GEE models were conducted using complete-case analysis, with participants excluded if data were missing for any included variables. We used primary care records within a cohort-based design; GEEs were applied to account for clustering within practices, rather than to model within-individual repeated measures over time. Overall, we used six sets of models examining the multiplicative effects of weight status and eating disorder diagnosis (ie, anorexia nervosa for model 1, avoidant restrictive food intake disorder for model 2, binge eating disorder for model 3, bulimia nervosa for model 4, other specified feeding or eating disorder for model 5, and generic eating disorder for model 6) on eating disorder referral status. We included generic eating disorder as an exposure category, as almost half of the sample had no specific eating disorder diagnosis recorded.

To improve the interpretability of the interaction term models, we generated predicted probabilities of the outcomes (ie, eating disorder referral status) using a marginal effects approach in which categorical covariates were set to their modal values and age was set to its mean. All tests were two-sided and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons through the Benjamini–Hochberg correction.22

We conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative BMI measure, underweight at first eating disorder diagnosis, defined by the BMI measurement closest to the first eating disorder diagnosis date, to ensure that BMI measurement preceded eating disorder referral. To examine the influence of sex, we used a series of stratified models.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

There were 54 890 people with an eating disorder diagnosis or referral in CPRD Aurum and 35 404 people with at least one eating disorder diagnosis aged 18–80 years were included in our analysis, of whom 26 822 (75·8%) did not receive a referral (figure). Most people identified as White (29 545 [83·5%] of 35 404), followed by Asian (2154 [6·1%]), unknown (1325 [3·7%]), Black (1028 [2·9%]), mixed/multiple (682 [1·9%]), and other (110 [0·3%]; table 2). There were 28 944 (81·8%) female individuals, 6460 (18·2%) male individuals, and no participants with unknown sex. The fully adjusted model 3 showed that, in people without underweight during the observation period, those with a binge eating disorder diagnosis had an OR of 1·13 (95% CI 0·75–1·71; p=0·58), although this was not statistically significant. In those who did not receive a binge eating disorder diagnosis, people with underweight had an OR of 1·51 (1·43–1·60; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without underweight. The interaction term between having a binge eating disorder diagnosis and underweight was not statistically significant (OR 0·62 [95% CI 0·10–3·88]; p=0·61).

The fully adjusted model 4 showed that, in people without underweight during the observation period, those with a bulimia nervosa diagnosis had an OR of 1·88 (95% CI 1·73–2·04; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without a bulimia nervosa diagnosis. In those who did not receive a bulimia nervosa diagnosis, having underweight had an OR of 1·74 (1·64–1·86; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without underweight. The interaction term between having a bulimia nervosa diagnosis and underweight was negatively associated with receiving an eating disorder referral (OR 0·76 [95% CI 0·64–0·90]; p=0·0018).

In those who did not receive another specified feeding or eating disorder diagnosis, underweight had an OR of 1·56 (1·47–1·66; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without underweight. The interaction term between having another specified feeding or eating disorder diagnosis and underweight was negatively associated with receiving an eating disorder referral (OR 0·76 [95% CI 0·64–0·90]; p=0·0018).

The fully adjusted model 6 showed that, in people without underweight during the observation period, those with a generic eating disorder diagnosis had an OR of 2·34 (95% CI 2·16–2·53; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without a generic eating disorder diagnosis. In those who did not receive a generic eating disorder diagnosis, underweight had an OR of 1·79 (1·65–1·95; p<0·0001) regarding receiving an eating disorder referral compared with those without underweight. The interaction term between having a generic eating disorder diagnosis and underweight was negatively associated with receiving an eating disorder referral (OR 0·72 [95% CI 0·65–0·80]; p<0·0001).

Being in IMD quintile 5 was associated with lower odds of eating disorder referral in models 2–5 (table 3). Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of primary care electronic health records, weight status affected the likelihood of referral to eating disorder specialist services in adults aged 18–80 years in England and Northern Ireland.

Underweight (ie, BMI <18·5 kg/m2) increased the likelihood of referral for adults with a diagnosis of anorexia nervosa compared with those without underweight, suggesting that clinicians might be especially sensitive to low weight when individuals present with anorexia nervosa symptoms. This is important to consider in the context of ICD-10 and DSM-5 atypical anorexia nervosa, in which people can present with substantial weight loss and anorexia nervosa psychopathology but be at or above normal BMI. Previous evidence has indicated no meaningful differences in symptom severity between anorexia nervosa and atypical anorexia nervosa,23 and in ICD-11 the diagnosis of anorexia nervosa can be used across weight presentations. Additionally, weight stigma, which refers to how people with higher bodyweight experience weight-related discrimination in societies valuing a thin ideal,12 has been shown to increase the risk of developing eating disorders and persistence of symptoms over time, as clinicians might be less likely to recognise eating disorders in people with higher weight.12,24 Thus, our findings align with existing evidence illustrating that people with eating disorders without underweight may be less likely to receive evidence-based, timely eating disorder treatment.

People with a diagnosis of bulimia nervosa and with underweight had lower odds of eating disorder referral. However, underweight presentations would generally meet diagnostic criteria for anorexia nervosa (binge–purge) subtype, rather than bulimia nervosa.25

People with a diagnosis of avoidant restrictive food intake disorder or binge eating disorder had comparatively lower odds of receiving a referral to eating disorder services than people with a diagnosis of anorexia nervosa. There was no evidence that referral likelihood differed according to underweight status among people with a diagnosis of binge eating disorder, consistent with typical presentation of binge eating disorder, as affected individuals are more likely to be of typical or higher weight.26 However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the sample sizes for these subgroups were comparatively small.

Consistent with previous research, although all individuals had a clinician-recorded eating disorder diagnosis, most were not referred for treatment, highlighting potential barriers to accessing specialist eating disorder care.4 For people with avoidant restrictive food intake disorder, low referral rates might reflect little commissioning of services for this condition within the NHS. Consequently, GPs might be unlikely to refer when they know that specialist services cannot offer treatment.6 Similarly, people presenting with binge eating disorder might be more likely to be referred to dieticians or obesity programmes instead of eating disorder treatment, although analysis of such referrals was outside the scope of this analysis.18

Accessibility is often described as a key barrier to eating disorder treatment.27 Findings from this analysis indicate that people might encounter barriers within the NHS, where specialist care often requires GP referral. Furthermore, disparities in eating disorder referral by diagnosis indicate a need to improve recognition of eating disorders beyond anorexia nervosa. Allowing self-referral to eating disorder services could enable people with concerns about eating disorder symptoms to access care directly, reducing delays stemming from provider-level barriers.27 Our findings add to calls to improve screening tools and education in primary care to improve adherence to the UK National Institute of Health and Care Excellence guidelines, which recommend immediate referral for suspected eating disorders.28 International evidence from Australia suggests that introducing eating disorder screening tools and enhancing GP education might increase referrals to eating disorder services,8 highlighting the capacity of these interventions to target the disparities identified in our analysis.

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis examining eating disorder referral patterns for adults in primary care settings in England and Northern Ireland. The use of this large dataset provided rich demographic information and enabled exploration of potential inequalities in referral patterns, aligning with current priorities in NHS health equity research.29 Results of this analysis include referral events for people presenting to primary care with a range of eating disorder diagnoses, extending previous work focused on anorexia nervosa. Additionally, our findings revealed patterns in routinely collected electronic health record data rather than controlled research settings or self-reported data, supporting generalisability to primary care populations beyond England and Northern Ireland.

However, the current analysis has several limitations. Potential coding inconsistencies at the practice level might have affected the accuracy of eating disorder diagnoses, BMI recording, and referral events. Furthermore, we did not have access to clinical notes, which could have provided information on symptom severity and its influence on referral decisions. Likewise, we cannot determine why practitioners did not refer, and it would be inappropriate to assume this was always due to weight bias. Some individuals might have accessed treatment outside the NHS, and such care would not appear in CPRD Aurum records. We also cannot establish whether referrals were offered and declined, or whether individuals attended primary care for eating disorder-related concerns or for other reasons. These factors could all have influenced referral patterns beyond the effect of eating disorder diagnosis and BMI. As secondary care data (eg, Hospital Episode Statistics) were not available for this analysis, we were unable to independently confirm whether recorded referrals were received in secondary care. Additionally, analyses were limited to data from England and Northern Ireland and thus do not capture experiences of people in Wales and Scotland. Changes in diagnostic criteria over the observational period might also have influenced diagnosis and referral patterns. DSM-5 (2013) introduced substantial revisions to eating disorder criteria, and subsequent updates to ICD-10 (2016) and the transition to ICD-11 (2022) have altered eating disorder classifications and increased the number of people eligible for diagnoses.30 Furthermore, 14 881 (42·0%) of 35 404 of individuals in our sample did not have a specific eating disorder diagnosis, precluding our ability to delineate the association between specific eating disorder diagnoses and referrals for this group. Deprivation data were assigned through Lower Layer Super Output Areas of each GP practice (table 1). As practices do not map neatly onto single Lower Layer Super Output Areas, many serve individuals from multiple areas, potentially reducing the precision of the deprivation measure. The use of practice-level deprivation also meant that individual-level factors, such as employment or educational attainment, could not be included as potential confounders. Furthermore, we did not examine ethnicity data beyond reporting descriptive characteristics of the sample. However, another CPRD Aurum analysis from our author group has explored patterns of primary care use by race and ethnicity in adults with eating disorders before diagnosis.31 Finally, as with all retrospective analyses, our findings might be influenced by unmeasured confounding and systematic differences introduced by missing IMD data. Future research could further examine the effect of weight status on access to treatment by investigating whether higher weight status influences likelihood of referral to eating disorder services.

In conclusion, despite limitations inherent to retrospective epidemiological research, this cross-sectional analysis of primary care electronic health records found that eating disorder referral rates in primary care in England and Northern Ireland varied by weight status and diagnosis, with people with underweight more likely to be referred than those without underweight.
