Associations between neighborhood disorder and mental health service utilization in the All of Us study
Authors: Fahmida Rashid, Lisa Connor, S. Scott Jones, Mostafizur Rahman, Khalid M. Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeighborhood environment is critically important in shaping mental health outcomes. Among those with a history of diagnoses of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) the likelihood of ongoing visits and current medication/treatment can be influenced by social and physical neighborhood-related risk factors. Despite growing evidence, only a few large-scale studies have examined these associations. Using data from the All of Us Research Program funded by the National Institute of Health (NIH), we examined the association between neighborhood disorders on mental health service utilization among adults in the United States.MethodsThe present study analyzed the All of Us Controlled Tier Dataset V7, which included self-reported survey data collected between May 31, 2017, and June 30, 2022. The 39,438 participants responded to the "Basics" (demographics), "Social Determinants of Health" (SDOH), and "Personal and Family Health History" (PFHH) surveys, all completed within 365 days of enrollment. The Neighborhood Disorder Scale was used to assess social and physical disorder in neighborhoods. Self-reported history of anxiety, depression, or PTSD were collected via questionnaire on additional measures of ongoing provider care and current treatment or medication use. Data were analyzed via chi-square tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests, and t-tests, while univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models estimated the association between neighborhood disorder and mental health care engagement.ResultsGreater neighborhood disorder was linked to higher odds of ongoing mental health care (OR 1.18; 95% CI 1.12–1.24) and current medication or treatment (OR 1.13; 95% CI 1.07–1.18). Mental health care engagement declined with increasing age, with participants above 75 years being significantly less likely to receive care (OR 0.14; 95% CI 0.12–0.16) and treatment (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.16–0.21) when compared to those 18–44 years of age. Cisgender men had lower odds of care (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.54–0.61) and treatment (OR 0.54; 95% CI 0.51–0.57) compared to cisgender women, while non-cisgender participants showed significantly higher odds of both. Non-Hispanic White participants were more likely to receive care (OR 2.08; 95% CI 1.91–2.27) and treatment (OR 2.21; 95% CI 2.03–2.42) when compared with Hispanic/Latino participants.ConclusionFindings underscore the importance of neighborhood conditions in receiving mental health care. These results emphasize the need for targeted public health interventions addressing structural determinants of mental health care utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-026-02103-w.

Introduction

Mental health conditions such as anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder are a major public health issue in the United States [31]. Nearly one in five U.S. adults suffers from a mental illness in any given year (57.8 million in 2021) [14], leading to reduced quality of life, decreased productivity, and high healthcare costs [26]. Although biological and individual risk factors are important, growing evidence points to the influence of social and environmental determinants on mental health [17]. Among the environmental risk factors of mental health service engagement, neighborhood disorder has been recognized as a pressing public health issue in recent years [9].

Neighborhood disorder is one of the core domains of social determinants of health (SDOH) [30]. The concept of neighborhood disorder encompasses both the physical and social dimensions of a community that reflect neglect, risk, or instability [4]. Physical neighborhood-related risk factors are visible environmental indications such as graffiti, vandalism, boarded houses, noise, and litter. Violence between neighbors and pervasive social mistrust are the components of social neighborhood-related risk factors [28]. The connection among neighbors, the degree of trust, willingness to help, and shared values among one another are elements of neighborhood social cohesion, which is a key predictor of various dimensions of health and well-being [5]. Neighborhood social cohesion promotes healthy behavior, increases physical activity, improves sleep, and reduces the risk of age-related conditions (e.g., stroke), and is protective against mental health issues [16].

Research has consistently found significant correlations between neighborhood disorders and negative psychological outcomes. Exposure to harmful or disordered settings may produce increased depressive symptoms, increased risk of anxiety disorders, and enhanced stress responses [30]. Physical disorder, on the other hand, is predictive of perceived stress, sleep disorder, and emotional distress, like anxiety and depression [8]. Social risk factors, such as crime and drug use, are connected to PTSD as well as other trauma disorders [21]. These linkages are assumed to affect directly and indirectly that chronic exposure to disordered environments provokes stress physiology, limits access to repair activity (exercise or social connections), and lowers a sense of stability and belonging. Furthermore, adverse neighborhood conditions, such as having fewer resources available (neighborhood parks, medical facilities, community organizations), can serve as buffers against such threats [9]. Neighborhood risk factors can potentially not only induce mental health disorders but can also affect treatment and care-seeking behaviors [24, 34]. People living in an unsafe or unsupportive neighborhood may be less likely to visit a mental healthcare provider. This can happen for psychological reasons like mistrust, safety concerns, or fear of stigma [22]. On the other hand, living in a disadvantaged neighborhood environment can make people more aware of mental support, which can increase the chances of seeking treatment [11]. These complex and sometimes opposing effects emphasize the need to examine not only whether neighborhood disorder is linked to mental health issues, but also how it influences patterns of regular provider visits and treatment engagement.

Demographic factors also modify how neighborhood disorder influences mental health care-seeking behavior [2]. Young adults, already at higher risk of depression and anxiety, are particularly susceptible to the stresses of social and physical disorder [3]. Older people, conversely, have lower contact with mental health services, perhaps due to competing physical health problems, generational stigma, or reduced mobility, which may be compounded by neighborhood-level barriers [6]. Gender also interacts with the contextual neighborhood. Women report more depression and anxiety compared to men [25]. Gender minority groups, including noncisgender and nonbinary groups, may be exposed to more stressors such as discrimination and victimization, which could exacerbate the negative effects of disordered environments [15]. Similarly, due to racial and ethnic disparities, many populations are more likely to reside in neighborhoods characterized by greater social and physical risk factors resulting from historical and structural inequalities and to face considerable barriers to accessing mental health services [1, 20].

Despite the importance of neighborhood environments, only a small number of large-scale epidemiological studies have comprehensively examined these relationships across the U.S. Most prior research has been geographically restricted to urban areas or based on small, homogeneous samples, limiting the large and diverse population. Furthermore, studies that have explored neighborhood effects often focus solely on mental health outcomes, without considering utilization of healthcare as a critical dimension of well-being. Also, there are gaps in knowledge regarding the connection between accessibility and sustainability of treatment for mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, or PTSD, and neighborhood disorder. To our knowledge, no study has been conducted to examine the associations of Neighborhood Risk Factors with Mental Health service utilization by using the All of Us dataset, which represents data from diverse populations across the U.S.

The objective of our study is to investigate the association of perceived neighborhood disorder with mental health care utilization using two outcomes in particular: (1) ongoing visits with a provider for anxiety, depression, or PTSD, and (2) ongoing medication or treatment use for these conditions.

Methods

For this study, data were obtained from the NIH’s All of Us Research Program (https://www.allofus.nih.gov/). This is a national cohort study that provides a wealth of self-reported survey data collected from a diverse pool of participants historically underrepresented in biomedical research. A variety of providers and health centers share efforts in enrollment across the United States. Enrollment began with informed consent to access electronic medical records (EMR) and an in-depth demographic survey called “The Basics”. Subsequently, biological samples were collected and biometrics recorded. Some participants also completed optional surveys, such as “Personal and Family Health History” (PFHH) and “Social Determinants of Health” (SDOH). Our analyses used responses collected by these three surveys. Relevant PFHH and SDOH survey questions collecting self-reported history of mental health conditions and neighborhood assessment are included in supplementary materials. The analytic cohort was limited to participants who reported a diagnosis of anxiety, depression, or PTSD in their lifetime.

Figure 1 illustrates the inclusion criteria for the analytic cohort. Available survey data in All of Us data version 7 included varying amounts of demographic data for 413,457 participants collected from 05/31/2017 to 06/30/2022. Of these respondents, 314,384 did not complete the Neighborhood Disorder Scale within the SDOH survey. Because demographic data such as age, income, education, insurance status, and Social Deprivation Index (SDI) value—derived by All of Us from data collected by the U.S. Census American Community Survey (ACS) and adjusted to comport with participant privacy protocol permitting only the first three digits of participant zip codes to be accessible—may vary with time and changes in residence, we limited the analytic cohort to SDOH and PFHH survey responses collected within 365 days following “The Basics” demographic survey. This criterion excluded 55,256 who responded to the SDOH survey more than 365 days after “The Basics” demographic survey. Of 43,817 participants with SDOH survey responses within the allowable timeframe, an additional 4,344 did not respond to the PFHH survey, with an additional 32 excluded from analysis due to completing the PFHH survey more than 365 days after “The Basics” demographic survey was completed. An additional three participants had no available Social Deprivation Index (SDI) value and were omitted from analysis. Our remaining analytic cohort consisted of 39,438 participants with SDOH and PFHH survey responses.Fig. 1Flow diagram showing inclusion criteria. SDOH: perceived neighborhood disorder

The Neighborhood Disorder Scale was administered as part of the SDOH survey. The measure assesses overall neighborhood disorder by asking participants to respond to 13 prompts describing physical and social neighborhood characteristics; physical and social characteristics may be analyzed separately with subscale scores. Physical disorder was assessed from responses to six items: “There is a lot of graffiti in my neighborhood”, “My neighborhood is noisy”, “Vandalism is common in my neighborhood”, “There are a lot of abandoned buildings in my neighborhood”, “My neighborhood is clean”, and “People in my neighborhood take good care of their houses and apartments”. Social disorder was assessed by evaluating responses to “There are too many people hanging around on the streets near my home”, “There is too much drug use in my neighborhood", “There is too much alcohol use in my neighborhood”, “I’m always having trouble with my neighbors”, “There is a lot of crime in my neighborhood”, “My neighborhood is safe”, and “In my neighborhood, people watch out for each other”. Responses were scored such that higher values reflect greater neighborhood disorder with values from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Responses to four items (“My neighborhood is clean”, “People in my neighborhood take good care of their houses and apartments”, “My neighborhood is safe”, “In my neighborhood, people watch out for each other”) were reverse-coded, from 1 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree). A mean subscale score was calculated separately for physical items and social items, and an overall mean score was calculated for neighborhood disorder. One statement was not administered within the SDOH survey–"the police protection in my neighborhood is adequate”–which was included in the validated Perceived Neighborhood Disorder Scale [27], originally published in 1999. No data were collected by the SDOH survey for this item. Responses from SDOH were considered for analysis only if dated within 365 days following “The Basics” response.

Covariates

Demographics

Sex assigned at birth, gender identity, race, ethnicity, highest level of education attained, annual household income, and insurance status were collected in the All of Us “Basics” demographics survey. Cisgender participants were identified as those with concordant birth sex and gender identity. Those with missing sex or gender were coded as “Missing” and included in analyses. Date of birth for each participant was collected during consent and used to calculate age at the time of “The Basics” demographic survey response. Age (years) was further divided into the following categories: 18–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74 and 75 years and above.

Mutually exclusive racial and ethnic identity binary variables were created; first, all participants who endorsed “Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish” were coded as Hispanic/Latino/a/e/x; next, all non-Hispanic/Latino/a/e/x participants were coded as Multiracial if they endorsed multiple racial identities. The remaining participants were single endorsers of racial identity. Endorsers of “Asian”, “Middle Eastern or North African”, “American Indian or Alaska Native”, “Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander”, or “None of these fully describe me” were coded in a single category to avoid small group sizes in accordance with All of Us dissemination protocol which does not allow reporting for small cell sizes less than 20. Remaining identities of “Black, African American, or African” or “White” were coded as endorsed. Those with missing race and ethnicity were coded as “Missing” and included in analyses.

Categorical variables representing annual household income and education were coded as endorsed. Insurance status was also coded as endorsed, with multiple selections coded into a “Multiple/Other insurance” category level. Those with missing education, income, or insurance status were coded as “Missing” and included in analyses.

Social Deprivation Index (SDI) values were obtained for each participant via query within the All of Us Workbench. These values were estimated using six data points collected by the U.S. Census American Community Survey by Zip Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA): the proportion of residents with assisted income, proportion with high school education, proportion with no health insurance, proportion living in poverty, proportion of housing which is vacant, and median income. In order to protect participant privacy and prevent reidentification, All of Us makes available only the first three digits of the participant zip code; SDI values represent this same level of granularity. Participants with missing SDI were omitted from analyses.

The analytical cohort consisted of participants who: (1) completed the 13-item Neighborhood Disorder Scale (within the SDoH survey) within 365 days following “The Basics” demographic survey, (2) responded to the Personal & Family Health History (PFHH) survey within 365 days following “The Basics”, and (3) were not missing SDI.

Data management and analysis

Data analysis was conducted within multiple Jupyter notebooks using R software version 4.4.0 and stored within the virtual environment provided by the All of Us Researcher Workbench. All data were derived from Controlled Tier Dataset V7.

We assessed group differences within the analytic cohort with Chi-Square tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests, and two-sided t-tests after stratifying two ways: first, we stratified by whether there was ongoing care for anxiety, depression, or PTSD; we then stratified by whether there was current treatment or medication for anxiety, depression, or PTSD. Summary statistics for continuous scale scores were reported as means, standard deviations, medians, minimum and maximum values, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Bootstrap confidence intervals with 1,000 replications were calculated for Cronbach's alpha using the “ltm” package in R to assess the reliability. Generalized Variable Inflation Factor (GVIF) values were calculated using the “car” package in R.

Coded responses to the Neighborhood Disorder Scale (NDS) were right-skewed and not normally distributed, confirmed by a one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (D (39,438) = 0.121, p < 0.001). To facilitate interpretability, quartiles were identified among cohort scale score means and used to form a four-category variable, with levels defined by the 25th percentile (NDS 1.00–1.24), and 25th to 50th (NDS 1.25–1.49), 50th to 75th (NDS 1.50–1.99) and ≥ 75th (NDS 2.00–4.00) percentile ranges.

Two distinct binary logistic regression models estimated the association of neighborhood disorder and the probability of (1) concurrent provider care, and (2) concurrent medication or treatment. Covariates were included in the multivariable model to align with previous literature. Throughout these analyses, a 2-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Within this cohort of 39,438 participants, Cronbach's alpha showed acceptable reliability for the 13-item Neighborhood Disorder Scale (α = 0.912, 95% CI 0.911–0.914), as well as the six items of the physical disorder subscale (α = 0.843, 95% CI 0.839–0.846) and the seven-item social disorder subscale (α = 0.873, 95% CI 0.870–0.875).

Of 39,438 participants, 2.03% did not identify as cisgender, and more than half (60.93%) were cisgender women. Participant age ranged from 18 to 75 years and older, with the most populous age category 18–44 years (34.11%) and the least populous 75 and older (8.17%). Discussion

This study explored the association between perceived neighborhood disorder and having mental health care, using data from the NIH All of Us Research Program. Consistent with our hypothesis, we observed that higher neighborhood disorder was significantly linked to higher odds of ongoing visits to a healthcare provider for continued mental health support and continuation of current treatment. These results provide evidence that neighborhood-level risk factors have a significantly associated with having treatment and taking medication for anxiety, depression, and PTSD, and among diverse U.S. populations.

Our results aligned with previous studies, which found that residents in vulnerable neighborhoods with high levels of physical and social disorders were more prone to utilize the service for mental health problems. Chronic exposure to environmental and disordered environments may serve as barriers to continuity of care by reducing feelings of safety, eroding social support, and fostering mistrust [12, 23, 29].

Age-related differences in mental health care utilization were also evident. Younger adults (18–44 years) were significantly more likely to report both ongoing care and current treatment. Participants aged 75 years and older were 80% less likely to be in care compared to the youngest group. Several explanations may account for these age-related patterns. Younger adults may be more likely to experience mental health challenges due to social, emotional, and occupational stressors, as well as greater awareness and reduced stigma in seeking care [32, 33]. Older adults, in contrast, may prioritize physical health needs, face generational stigma toward mental illness, or encounter barriers such as mobility limitations and limited availability of age-appropriate mental health services [18]. These findings highlight the need for targeted behavioral and therapeutic interventions for both younger and older adults.

Our results on the influence of gender differences were also consistent with prior research. Cisgender men were significantly less likely than cisgender women to be in care or on treatment for anxiety, depression, or PTSD. This aligns with a large body of literature showing that women report higher rates of depressive symptoms and are more likely to seek professional help [7, 10]. Biological, psychosocial, and cultural factors all contribute to this disparity, including gender norms that discourage men from expressing vulnerability or seeking support. On the other hand, noncisgender and nonbinary participants were more likely than cisgender women to report ongoing provider visits and treatment. Minority stress theory may explain this finding, as gender-diverse individuals experience elevated stress from stigma, discrimination, and social exclusion, leading to increased mental health needs. Encouragingly, higher engagement with providers may reflect growing recognition of these needs within healthcare systems, though structural barriers co-exist.

Racial and ethnic disparities were also apparent in the findings of the study. Non-Hispanic White participants were more than two times more likely to report ongoing provider visits and current treatment. These differences mirror longstanding inequities in mental health service utilization across racial and ethnic groups. Prior research shows that Hispanic/Latino communities face barriers, including limited access to affordable care, language differences, cultural stigma surrounding mental health, and systemic inequities in healthcare delivery [13]. These findings underscore the importance of culturally responsive interventions and policies aimed at reducing racial and ethnic disparities in mental health care.

Household income influences receiving mental health care [37]. In our sample, lower odds of ongoing care (OR 0.39) and having medication/treatment (OR 0.45) were linked to higher income ($150,000 or more). This opposite result might be due to variations in the severity of illness, with those with higher incomes possibly having less severe symptoms, thereby needing less medical attention. On the other hand, individuals with higher incomes might be better able to manage their own health by having access to telehealth, private support, and other resources that are not included in the dataset. Lastly, unmeasured confounding or model specification may have an impact on this pattern. Participants with Private/Employer/Union insurance demonstrated substantially higher utilization of mental health care, highlighting the critical role of comprehensive insurance coverage in reducing financial and structural barriers to treatment [36]. This pattern underscores the importance of distinguishing between access to care and need for care when interpreting service utilization outcomes.

In our study, higher SDI scores were associated with lower odds of seeing a doctor/provider, indicating that residents of more socially deprived neighborhoods face barriers to engaging in mental health care. Higher social deprivation may reflect underlying social risk factors such as lack of investment in the community and access to resources that would improve community safety and quality of life reduced availability of mental health services, which may decrease the likelihood of utilizing care despite potential need [19]. These results are consistent with previous literature showing that socially vulnerable populations also have worse health outcomes and decreased access to care. For instance, Xi et al. [38] demonstrated that higher social vulnerability is associated with poorer overall and mental health service utilization, although these social vulnerability indices are seldom used prospectively as predictors of treatment engagement [38]. Similarly, socially vulnerable individuals tend to show higher prevalence of severe mental health disorders and lower access to treatment [35].

Overall, this study highlights the complex interplay between neighborhood conditions and receiving mental health care services. Both perceived neighborhood risk factors and social deprivation, along with key sociodemographic factors, were associated with patterns of ongoing care and treatment for anxiety, depression, and PTSD.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of data from the All of Us Research Program, which offers a large and diverse national dataset that includes populations historically underrepresented in biomedical research. This provides a rare opportunity to investigate neighborhood risk factors in relation to mental health care utilization across demographic groups. Additionally, our study examined both ongoing provider care and current treatment, offering a comprehensive view of mental health service utilization.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all measures were based on self-reported survey data, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Additionally, this study may be subject to selection bias due to the inclusion of participants with complete data. To assess the potential impact of this restriction, we compared model estimates from the analytic cohort (Tables 1, 2, 3) with results from a broader sample of survey respondents with complete demographic data (Supplementary Tables 7–9). The findings were generally consistent, suggesting that selection bias may not substantially affect the observed associations.

The cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to infer causality between neighborhood disorder and mental health care utilization. Moreover, while our analytic cohort was large and diverse, some subgroups were aggregated to comply with All of Us reporting guidelines, which may obscure more nuanced patterns. Furthermore, we did not account for geographic-level contextual factors, such as actual neighborhood crime rates or availability of mental health services, and lack of clinical details such as illness severity, duration of illness, treatment modality and number of comorbid diagnoses, which could further clarify the observed associations.

Although the All of Us Research Program includes a large and demographically diverse cohort, the analytic sample in this study represents a subset of participants who completed specific survey components. Because survey weights were not applied, the findings are not interpreted as nationally representative, and potential selection bias may limit the generalizability of the results.

Implications and future directions

Our findings highlight the importance of considering neighborhood environments when developing strategies to improve mental health service utilization. Efforts to enhance neighborhood conditions by increasing city and state investment to improve the quality of life and resource accessibility. At the same time, targeted approaches that address disparities across sociodemographic groups, including age, gender, race/ethnicity and income, remain essential. Future research should further examine the mechanisms linking neighborhood risk factors to care utilization, including the role of social risk factors and access to resources, as well as potential effect modification across population subgroups.

The study will lay the foundation for future longitudinal studies to establish causal pathways between neighborhood disorder and mental health care engagement. Linking self-reported perceptions with objective neighborhood-level data (e.g., census data, crime statistics, geospatial measures) could provide a more comprehensive understanding of environmental influences. Additionally, qualitative research may further add new knowledge for elucidating the pathways that explain quality of life in individuals in disordered neighborhoods and their perspectives on barriers and facilitators to care.

Conclusion

Overall, our study demonstrates that neighborhood disorder is significantly associated with a greater likelihood of ongoing provider visits and current treatment for anxiety, depression, and PTSD. These findings highlight the importance of considering environmental risk factors alongside demographic characteristics when addressing mental health disparities. By integrating neighborhood-level interventions with strategies to reduce inequities across age, gender, and racial/ethnic groups, public health systems can move closer to ensuring equitable access to mental health care for all. These findings underscore the importance of addressing neighborhood environments when developing strategies to improve equitable mental health care utilization.
