Molecular mechanisms of bone metastasis in breast cancer based on transcriptomic and microbiomic analysis
Authors: Dihui Zhang, Manli Long, Lingcui Meng, Yongjin Li, Bolai Chen, Dingkun Lin, Guoyi Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone metastasis (BM) in breast cancer affects patient prognosis, but its molecular mechanisms and relationship with the gut microbiome are not well understood. This study aims to explore gene expression and gut microbiome differences between BM and non-bone metastasis (BNM) patients, which could shed light on cancer progression and metastasis.MethodsWe utilized a multi-omics approach, integrating transcriptomic and microbiomic data. Bioinformatics techniques including differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, protein–protein interaction network analysis, and LDA effect size analysis were applied. We also constructed miRNA regulatory networks and gene–gene interaction networks to identify key genes and microbial functions involved in BM.ResultsThe analysis identified significant upregulation of genes such as IBSP, PROM1, and IDO1 in BM patients. miRNA analysis suggested that let-7 family members might regulate these genes and influence cancer progression. Gene–gene network analysis revealed a synergistic role for genes like THBS1 and ITGB3 in tumor progression. Regarding the gut microbiome, BM patients exhibited enriched pathways related to arachidonic acid metabolism, steroid hormone synthesis, and thyroid hormone synthesis, potentially impacting immunity and metabolism. Additionally, Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection pathways were significantly enriched, indicating a possible role in BM.ConclusionThe study highlights distinct gene expression and gut microbiome differences between BM and BNM patients. HPV infection may play a crucial role in BM development, offering new potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early diagnosis and treatment of BM in breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10552-025-02057-5.Keywords: Breast cancer, Bone metastasis, Transcriptomics, Microbiomics

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies among women worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in females [1]. Despite advancements in early screening and therapeutic approaches, the overall survival rate for breast cancer has improved. However, patients with distant metastases [2], particularly those with bone metastasis (BM), continue to have a relatively poor prognosis [3]. BM is one of the most common forms of breast cancer metastasis, with approximately 70% of patients with advanced breast cancer developing it [4]. These metastases not only lead to severe bone pain and fractures but also adversely affect patients' quality of life and survival [5].

The biological mechanisms underlying BM in breast cancer are both intricate and multifaceted. Breast cancer initially arises from genetic mutations in mammary epithelial cells, which can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation [6]. As the tumor progresses, tumor cells then invade the surrounding stroma and vasculature, marking the first step toward entering the bloodstream [7]. The occurrence of BM is a complex biological process involving tumor cell migration, alterations in the bone microenvironment and evasion of the host immune system [8]. Research has demonstrated that extracellular matrix remodeling within the tumor microenvironment [9], activation of signaling pathways and inflammatory responses play critical roles in tumor metastasis [10]. However, existing studies still fall short of elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms of breast cancer BM, particularly concerning the interactions between the tumor microenvironment and the host microbiome.

In recent years, the relationship between gut microbiota and the onset, progression, and metastasis of various cancers has garnered considerable attention. Gut microbiota may play a crucial role in cancer progression by modulating the host’s immune system, metabolic functions, and inflammatory responses [11]. Specifically, microbes can influence immune cell function through the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, thereby affecting inflammatory responses and tumor immune surveillance [12]. Additionally, the metabolism of bile acids by gut microbiota, resulting in secondary bile acids, has been associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer [12]. Furthermore, microbes can promote cancer cell dissemination by modulating the host’s immune system and inflammatory responses [13]. Previous studies have primarily focused on the differences between primary breast cancer and bone metastases, with limited exploration of the distinctions between bone metastases and other metastatic sites [14]. Moreover, these studies have not integrated transcriptomic data with gut microbiome analysis.

This study aims to identify key genes associated with breast cancer BM and non-bone metastasis (BNM) by conducting a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic and gut microbiome data. We seek to uncover potential connections between differential gene expression and gut microbiome functions in breast cancer BM. Through these analyses, we aspire to reveal critical regulatory mechanisms involved in breast cancer BM, ultimately providing novel targets and strategies for future diagnosis and treatment.

In this research, we first identified key genes associated with breast cancer BM through differential expression analysis and subsequently performed functional enrichment analysis on these genes. Additionally, we explored potential interactions between microbial functions and host genes through a functional analysis of the gut microbiome. Notably, when comparing the gene expression profiles and functional operational taxonomic unit (OTU) analysis of patients with breast cancer BM to those BNM patients, the Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection pathway was significantly enriched in both groups. This finding suggests that HPV infection not only affects the occurrence of BM at the transcriptomic level but may also influence breast cancer progression by modulating the composition and function of the gut microbiota. Finally, we compare these findings with existing literature to enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of breast cancer BM and propose directions for future research.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Gene expression data from the GEO databases (GSE14017, GSE175692, GSE147322) and BioProject (PRJNA804967) were collected and preprocessed for this study. GSE14017 contains 8 BM and 28 BNM breast cancer samples. GSE175692 contains 26 BM and 67 BNM samples with differential gene expression analysis after the removal of low-quality samples. GSE147322 contains 7 BM and 33 BNM samples and is primarily used for model testing. And PRJNA804967 contains 22 BM and 32 BNM samples, with quality-controlled FASTQ files for gut microbiome analysis. The data have been deposited in GEO, accession number and are publicly available as of the date of publication. All data were completely anonymized and approved by the Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from the patients.

Differential expression analysis

For differential gene expression analysis, GSE14017 and GSE175692 were used in the study. For the GSE14017 dataset, 374 significantly differentially expressed genes were identified using the Limma package (version 3.60.3) combined with an empirical Bayesian approach and controlling the false-positive rate by the Benjamini–Hochberg (B&H) method, setting a threshold of log2Fold Change > 1 and p.adj < 0.05. For the GSE175692 dataset, 73 significantly differentially expressed genes were identified using the DESeq2 (version 1.44.0) and the negative binomial distribution model with p-values adjusted by the B&H method, setting the same threshold.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene IDs were converted to Entrez IDs using the org.Hs.eg.db package and the ‘bitr’ function. GO enrichment analyses were performed via enrichGO, applying the B&H method to correct for multiple hypothesis tests, with the p-value and q-value thresholds set at 0.05, and demonstrating the top 10 most significant pathways. Subsequently, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed by enrichKEGG, applying the same B&H correction and thresholds, with each pathway containing at least 10 genes, and demonstrating the top 10 significant KEGG pathways.

PPI network analysis

The differentially expressed genes were input into the STRING database (12.0), Interactions with an interaction score of 0.7 or higher were screened and clustered using k-means, set to cluster into 4 classes to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, which was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape. To identify key hub genes within the network, we utilized the CytoHubba plugin, selecting those with a CytoHubba score exceeding 3. The hub genes common to both datasets were then retained for further analysis.

miRNA prediction

Hub genes and shared differentially expressed genes were submitted to miRWalk for prediction, filtering interaction relationships common across three databases: TargetScan, miRDB, and miRTarBase. The filtered gene–miRNA interaction data were then imported into Cytoscape for network visualization.

Predictive modeling and characteristic importance analysis

We constructed a predictive model using a feature matrix derived from the expression levels of shared differentially expressed genes and the filtered hub genes across two expression matrices. Various machine learning techniques, including logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, random forest, gradient boosting trees and XGBoost, were employed to evaluate their predictive performance in distinguishing BM from BNM. Finally, we extracted key features from the models (e.g., critical genes or microorganisms) and provided biological interpretations.

Gut microbiology analysis

The quality-filtered FASTQ files were processed and analyzed using QIIME 2. The feature table was denoised and constructed using the DADA2 plugin, and subsequently clustered by the closed reference clustering method of the VSEARCH plugin to generate the clustered feature table and representative sequences. The representative sequences were classified using QIIME 2's classifier (silva-138-ssu-nr99-classifier.qza). The final feature table, classification information, and phylogenetic tree were exported, and the feature table was converted from BIOM format to TSV format. α-diversity analyses were performed using the R package microeco to assess species richness and evenness within samples. β-diversity analyses were performed by PCoA and PLS-DA analyses to compare differences in microbial community structure between samples. Differential abundance analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify microbial species that differed significantly across conditions. Finally, functional prediction and association analysis were performed using the Tax4Fun2 tool.

OTU clustering and species annotation

OTU clustering analysis was performed using QIIME 2 to process high-throughput sequencing data and perform diversity analysis. Microbial OTU clustering clusters sequences based on 97% similarity using the tool USEARCH (v7). Microbial communities were classified into different OTUs by sequence similarity to analyze complex microbial ecosystems in more detail. After clustering, the SILVA database was used for species annotation, and precise classification and functional inference were achieved by sequence comparison with 16S, 18S, and 28S rRNA genes in the SILVA database.

Gene expression and methylation analysis

The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle) is utilized for research purposes. UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) is a tool for analyzing the methylation status and expression patterns of biomarkers.

Screening and molecular docking of small molecule drugs

The CellMiner database (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/home.do) was utilized to identify relevant drugs for the model genes. Protein structures were obtained from UniProt, while small molecule structures were sourced from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) for docking in CB-Dock2. The structure with the lowest Vina score (kcal/mol) from the structure-based blind docking was selected in CB-Dock2 and imported into PyMOL (Version 3.1.0) to validate the molecular docking of small drug molecules with protein macromolecules.

Result

Identification of key differentially expressed genes associated with bone metastasis in breast cancer

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of differentially expressed genes between the BM and BNM groups in the GSE14017 and GSE175692 datasets (Fig. 1). In the GSE14017 dataset, 374 differentially expressed genes were identified, in which 350 genes were upregulated and 24 were downregulated in the BM group compared to the BNM group. Similarly, in the GSE175692 dataset, 73 differentially expressed genes were recognized, with 28 upregulated and 45 downregulated in the BM group relative to the BNM group. These results provide a clear depiction of the differential gene expression between the BM and BNM groups. The genes significantly upregulated in BM patients compared to BNM patients include IBSP, PROM1, and ME1, which may be closely associated with the occurrence and progression of BM (Fig. 1A). Besides, IBSP and WIF1 are also significantly upregulated in BM patients, further supporting the potential role of these genes in BM (Fig. 1B). There is a high degree of consistency in the expression trends of certain genes in the BM group across both datasets, particularly IBSP, which shows significant upregulation in both datasets, indicating its critical role in BM. Furthermore, other genes such as FOXC2 and PCK1 are also represented in both datasets, although the degree of differential expression varies. Overall, these results indicate that specific gene expression changes are consistent among different breast cancer patients with BM, suggesting that these genes may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for BM.

Molecular function and pathway enrichment based on GO and KEGG

Functional annotation and pathway analysis were conducted on the significantly enriched genes within the GSE14017 and GSE175692 datasets. In the GSE14017 dataset, the enriched genes were predominantly associated with extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, connective tissue development, and the response to transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling. Additionally, they exhibited notable enrichment in functional categories such as collagen binding and cell-substrate adhesion, highlighting their involvement in tissue architecture and matrix interactions that are implicated in tumor progression (Fig. 2A). In the GSE175692 dataset, the enriched genes are primarily involved in tumor immune response and inflammation. The enriched biological processes include external encapsulating structure organization, mesoderm development, and response to lipopolysaccharide, indicating the genes’ association with inflammation and tissue development. Additionally, these genes are closely linked to G protein-coupled receptor binding, growth factor receptor binding, and metalloendopeptidase activity, suggesting their potential roles in signal transduction and protein degradation (Fig. 2B). In summary, while GSE14017 emphasizes tissue architecture and matrix interactions, GSE175692 highlights immune and inflammation-related signaling pathways, revealing the unique roles of different components within the tumor microenvironment. However, overall consistency is maintained (Figure S1).

Functional enrichment analysis. A, B GO enrichment analysis; C, D KEGG enrichment analysis

Next, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the two sets of differentially expressed genes. The GSE14017 and GSE175692 datasets showed enrichment in 20 and 40 pathways, respectively. Notably, pathways such as ECM–Receptor Interaction, PI3K-Akt Signaling Pathway, Human Papillomavirus Infection, Proteoglycans in Cancer, TGF-Beta Signaling Pathway, Hippo Signaling Pathway, and Transcriptional Misregulation in Cancer were significantly enriched in both datasets. These pathways play a crucial role in regulating tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and immune evasion, providing valuable insights into tumor mechanisms and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

The pathways unique to GSE14017, such as Prostate Cancer, MAPK Signaling Pathway, and Adherens Junction, predominantly focus on the molecular mechanisms of specific cancer types, as well as cellular adhesion and signal transduction pathways (Fig. 2C). In contrast, GSE175692 shows enrichment in cancer-related immune and inflammatory response pathways (Fig. 2D), including the Wnt Signaling Pathway, Breast Cancer, mTOR Signaling Pathway, and TNF Signaling. These reflect the complex interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment, emphasizing the intricate relationship between the tumor and the host immune system. Consequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes from two independent datasets, GSE175692 and GSE14017, revealed significant pathway concordance (Figure S2). Notably, 10 pathways enriched in the GSE175692 dataset were also identified in GSE14017. The overlap across most pathways suggests that these pathways may hold shared biological significance in disease progression and therapeutic response.

Key genes and gene–miRNA networks providing insights into mechanisms and therapeutic targets

Through differential analysis, a total of 9 common differentially expressed genes (OLFML2B, IBSP, SCARA5, ENPP2, BMP2, CHAD, CXCL13, PROM1, and SFRP2) were identified across both datasets. To identify key hub genes within the network, we utilized the CytoHubba plugin and selected genes with a CytoHubba score greater than 3, yielding 39 genes. We then retained the hub genes common to both datasets for further analysis. Ultimately, 34 genes were selected for subsequent analysis.

We further analyzed the intricate network interactions among shared differentially expressed genes, hub genes, and miRNAs, offering valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of cancer (Fig. 3). Within this network, genes such as THBS1, ITGB3, and TGFBR2 were linked to multiple miRNAs, suggesting that these genes may be co-regulated by various miRNAs and hold significant biological relevance. Additionally, certain miRNAs (hsa-let-7c-5p, hsa-miR-3666) may influence distinct target genes, potentially playing key roles in regulating diverse biological processes or disease mechanisms. In particular, the let-7 family of miRNAs typically functions as tumor suppressors. Abnormal expression of these miRNAs in cancer may promote tumor cell proliferation and survival, thereby driving tumor progression. Therefore, analyzing the expression patterns of these miRNAs can provide critical insights for early cancer diagnosis and prognosis, and lay the foundation for miRNA-based therapeutic strategies.

Network of Gene and miRNA Interactions. Circular nodes represent genes or proteins (THBS1, ITGB3, TGFBR2), while diamond-shaped nodes denote miRNAs (hsa-let-7b-5p, hsa-miR-29b-3p)

The figure also illustrates direct interactions between genes, such as the connection between THBS1 and ITGB3, suggesting that these genes may collaborate in tumor progression through direct protein–protein interactions or signaling pathways. This gene–gene interaction highlights the complexity of molecular networks in cancer and offers a new perspective for exploring gene synergy, which may aid in identifying novel therapeutic targets. In summary, the gene–miRNA interaction network not only reveals key regulatory nodes in cancer progression but also indicates potential molecular targets. These findings provide new avenues for cancer diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Further exploration of the relationships between these genes and miRNAs is expected to advance the discovery and application of novel molecular mechanism-based cancer therapies.

Construction of predictive models

Model validation was conducted using a 20% test set (BNM: 9, BM: 7) derived from the full dataset (BNM: 44, BM: 35). Both the XGBoost and Gradient Boosting models achieved an AUC value of 1.00, indicating the classification performance on the training set. Similarly, the Random Forest model also demonstrated strong discriminatory power with an AUC of 0.98. Meanwhile, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model yielded a commendable AUC of 0.87, though slightly lower than that of the ensemble models. In contrast, the AUC values for Logistic Regression and Decision Tree were 0.83 and 0.82, respectively, with the Decision Tree’s simplicity likely contributing to its reduced accuracy (Fig. 4A). In summary, ensemble learning models (such as XGBoost and Gradient Boosting) outperformed traditional single-classifier models on this dataset, delivering superior results.

Construction of Predictive Models. A ROC curves of six different models, the closer the AUC value under the curve is to 1, the stronger the model’s discriminative ability. B ROC curve of the independent test set

Testing the XGBoost model on an independent external dataset (BNM = 33, BM = 7) revealed an accuracy of 0.85 and an AUC of 0.87, with a relatively narrow confidence interval. This indicates the model’s strong capability in distinguishing between positive and negative samples in classification tasks, effectively capturing positive instances even with reduced false positives. Moreover, the model demonstrated high stability, with consistent performance across different samples (Fig. 4B).

Identification of key metastasis-associated genes

To identify key genes, we employed the XGBoost algorithm to train the model and generated a ranking of feature importance (Fig. 5A). Among the 34 feature genes analyzed, 30 were assigned non-zero importance scores, with 19 previously reported in the literature to be associated with tumor metastasis. IBSP emerged as the most significant gene, with an importance score nearing 0.08, indicating its substantial contribution to predicting the target variable. PROM1 [15] and IDO1 [16] followed, with importance scores of approximately 0.07 and 0.06, respectively. Overall, the top five features—IBSP, PROM1, IDO1, CCR5, and PDGFRA—were critical to the model’s predictive performance, with markedly higher importance values than other features, all of which have been shown to be linked to cancer metastasis. At the lower end of the importance scale, the features SFRP2 and HGF exhibit scores close to zero, indicating their negligible contribution to the model’s predictive performance. This may suggest that these features provide limited information for this specific predictive task or possess lower utility compared to other features.

Using STRING, a network was constructed for key genes by filtering interactions with a score above 0.7, followed by k-means clustering into four groups (Fig. 5B). Genes such as TNF, CXCL8, and CXCL12 occupy central positions in the network, exhibiting close interactions with other genes. These genes represent viral protein interactions related to cytokines and their receptors, underscoring their pivotal roles in inflammatory responses and immune regulation. Genes such as MMP3, MMP9, and MMP7, which are associated with elastogenesis and matrix metalloproteinases, are closely linked to tumor invasion and metastasis, positioned in the outer ring of the network and interconnected. In contrast, genes like LEF1 and TWIST1, which are involved in the regulation of muscle tissue development, are more peripheral in the network. Central nodes, such as TNF and HGF, exhibit strong interactions with numerous other nodes, potentially acting as critical regulatory hubs.

Alpha and beta diversity analyses of microbial differences

The diversity data analysis of two distinct groups (BM and BNM) across four different metrics (Chao1, Ace, Shannon, Simpson) revealed that the p values for all four α-diversity indices exceeded 0.05 (Figure S3), indicating no significant differences in species richness or diversity between the BM and BNM groups. We conducted a β-diversity analysis between the two groups. In the PCoA plot, the BM and BNM groups exhibit partial overlap but also show discernible separation (Fig. 6A). This suggests some differences in the overall structure of the microbial communities between the groups, although these differences may not be highly significant. Despite the overlap, a trend of intergroup separation is still observable. In the PLS-DA plot, the separation between the BM and BNM groups is more pronounced, particularly along the P1 axis (Fig. 6B). The groups display clear separation in the PLS-DA space, with minimal overlap, indicating a stronger distinction between their microbial communities.

Differential microbial community composition and key taxa

The LEfSe analysis reveals that taxa significantly enriched in the BM group include Clostridia_vadinBB60_group and Anaerotruncus, among others. The taxa predominantly enriched in the BM group are anaerobes primarily associated with the production of short-chain fatty acids. Meanwhile, the BNM group is notably enriched with species linked to inflammation and pathological conditions, such as periodontitis and intestinal inflammation.

Functional and pathway differences in microbial communities

The analysis of microbial functional differences between the BM and BNM groups revealed several gene functional units with significant variation (Fig. 7A). For instance, 5-hydroxybenzimidazole synthase exhibited a marked difference between the two groups (p = 9.12e-4), with a significantly higher proportion in the BM group. In contrast, the two-component system, OmpR family response regulator RpaA, showed a significantly higher proportion in the BNM group (p = 8.06e-4). Additionally, genes such as acetyl-CoA decarboxylase/synthase complex subunit gamma and peptide-methionine (R)-S-oxide reductase also demonstrated notable differences between the groups, suggesting that these genes may perform distinct biological functions under specific conditions or environments [17, 18].

Differential analysis of functions and pathways in microbial communities. A Differences in relative abundance of gut microbial function between the BM and BNM groups. B Analysis of intergroup differences in microbial functional pathways

To further elucidate the functional differences in microbial pathways, we analyzed the relative abundance disparities between BM and BNM group samples. Several functional pathways exhibited significant differences between the groups, with “arachidonic acid metabolism” and “thyroid hormone synthesis” being markedly enriched in the BM group [19, 20], while other pathways were more active in the BNM group (Fig. 7B). These findings suggest potential distinctions in metabolic and signaling pathways between the BM and BNM group samples.

Integrated transcriptomic and microbiome analysis reveals HPV pathway enrichment in breast cancer metastasis

We conducted a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis of gene expression profiles and associated functional OTUs in breast cancer patients with and without BM. In the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, the HPV infection pathway was significantly enriched in both groups (Fig. 8, S4). This finding suggests that this pathway may influence BM not only at the transcriptomic level but also through the gut microbiome. Although HPV is typically associated with epithelial-origin cancers, such as cervical cancer, its role in breast cancer remains unclear. Our results indicate that HPV-related signaling pathways may have a potential functional role in different clinical subtypes of breast cancer. In particular, the enrichment of this pathway in the context of both BM and BNM may reflect the critical role of HPV infection in the complex interactions between tumor cells and their microenvironment, thereby impacting the invasiveness and metastatic capacity of breast cancer.

Analysis of gene expression and methylation levels

The expression levels of IDO1 and PROM1 in various tumors and tissues were analyzed using the CCLE database (Fig. 9A, B). The results indicated significant differences in their expression status, suggesting a potential relationship with the immune escape mechanisms of tumors [16]. Furthermore, the differential expression of IDO1 and PROM1 across different tumors and tissues offers important molecular targets for the study of treatment options for breast cancer and other malignancies.

Analysis of expression differences in different tumor spectrums and different clinical stages. A Expression levels of IDO1 in different tumor profiles; B expression levels of PROM1 in different tumor profiles; C IDO1 methylation level; D IBSP methylation level; E PROM1 methylation level

Gene methylation status was further analyzed. The results indicated that the methylation status exhibited significant changes as breast cancer progressed from normal tissue to various cancer stages (Stage 1 to Stage 4). In IDO1, the methylation levels in normal samples were lower than those in all cancer stages and varied between stages, suggesting that gene methylation levels are closely associated with cancer progression (Fig. 9C). PROM1 further corroborated this trend, demonstrating that the methylation levels in normal samples were lower, while the values for cancer stages were significantly higher, particularly in Stage 3 (Fig. 9E). In the IBSP, methylation levels in normal samples also differed from those in Stage 2 and Stage 3 (Fig. 9D). These results imply that changes in gene methylation across different developmental stages of breast cancer reflect the genetic and epigenetic alterations experienced by tumor cells during progression, which may be closely related to tumor invasiveness, metastasis, and response to treatment.

Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

In addition, we performed drug sensitivity analysis. In this study, we analyzed the correlation between gene expression of different molecules (IBSP, IDO1, PROM1) and drug IC50 values in breast cancer cells, revealing the close relationship between these molecules and chemotherapy resistance. The results showed that all molecules exhibited significant correlations (correlation coefficients greater than 0.4, p < 0.001), suggesting that the increase in gene expression levels is closely related to the enhancement of drug tolerance (Fig. 10A–P). In particular, the expression of IDO1 in several compounds showed a consistent trend of elevated IC50 values, suggesting that IDO1 may play a key role in drug resistance in breast cancer (Fig. 10E–N).

Discussion

Firstly, through differential expression and functional enrichment analyses, we utilized shared differentially expressed genes from two datasets and hub genes identified via PPI network analysis as features for our predictive model. This approach revealed key genes involved in breast cancer BM, including IBSP, PROM1, and IDO1. IBSP, which encodes bone sialoprotein, is highly relevant to BM due to its consistent role in bone formation [21, 22]. The elevated expression of PROM1 and IDO1 is closely associated with increased invasiveness and metastasis in breast cancer, corroborating previous research findings [15, 23]. Moreover, other critical genes such as CCR5 and CXCL8 significantly influence the tumor microenvironment, particularly in relation to inflammation and cell migration [24–26]. Aberrant expression of these core genes may be closely linked to cancer invasiveness and metastasis, making them, along with their regulated miRNAs, potential new therapeutic targets and biomarkers. Based on these findings, the intricate network map among differential genes, hub genes, and miRNAs reveals that several miRNAs (hsa-let-7b-5p, hsa-miR-106b-5p, hsa-miR-485-5p) regulate multiple genes, with the let-7 family of miRNAs, in particular, often functioning as tumor suppressors [27]. The aberrant expression of these miRNAs in cancer may facilitate tumor cell proliferation and survival, thereby driving tumor progression [28]. Therefore, analyzing their expression patterns could provide crucial information for early cancer diagnosis and prognosis, laying the foundation for miRNA-based therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the interaction between THBS1 and ITGB3 in the network diagram suggests that these genes may collaborate in tumor progression through direct protein interactions or signaling pathways [29, 30]. This gene–gene interaction highlights the intricate molecular networks in cancer, offering new insights into the synergistic gene effects and potentially aiding in the discovery of novel therapeutic targets.

In cancer metastasis research, identifying genes associated with tumor invasion and dissemination is of paramount importance. TWIST1, a crucial regulator of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), is closely linked to the metastasis of various solid tumors [31]. Members of the matrix metalloproteinase family, such as MMP3 and MMP9, facilitate tumor spread and metastasis by degrading the extracellular matrix [32]. Additionally, genes such as TGFBR2 and EGF influence cancer progression and metastasis indirectly through their involvement in critical signaling pathways [33, 34].

In the functional analysis of gut microbiota, we observed significant differences between the BM and BNM groups across multiple metabolic pathways. Notably, pathways such as arachidonic acid metabolism [19], steroid hormone synthesis [35], and thyroid hormone synthesis [20] were markedly upregulated in the BM group. These metabolic pathways may facilitate the BM of breast cancer cells by influencing the host’s immune response and metabolic state. Active thyroid hormone synthesis is often associated with increased cancer invasiveness and metastasis. For instance, in models of lung cancer, small cell lung cancer, and other malignancies, T4 activates non-genomic pathways through the integrin αvβ3 receptor, leading to increased cell proliferation and enhanced angiogenesis, thereby promoting cancer metastasis [36]. Additionally, microbial functions enriched in the BM group, such as 5-hydroxybenzimidazole synthase and the acetyl-CoA decarboxylase/synthase complex subunit gamma, may further increase tumor invasiveness by modulating host metabolism and stabilizing the microenvironment [17, 18]. 5-hydroxybenzimidazole synthase is a pivotal enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of cobalamin (vitamin B12). Elevated levels of cobalamin are associated with poor prognoses in cancer, particularly among patients with hepatic metastases [37–40]. The LEfSe analysis revealed significant enrichment of groups such as Clostridia_vadinBB60_group and Anaerotruncus in the BM group, which are primarily involved in short-chain fatty acid production and the maintenance of gut health [41, 42]. They may play a crucial role in a healthy intestinal environment, supporting gut barrier function and anti-inflammatory responses. In contrast, the BNM group shows significant enrichment of taxa such as Lactobacillus and Porphyromonas, which are associated with inflammation and pathological conditions such as periodontal disease and intestinal inflammation [43, 44]. This may indicate that the presence of a microbiome environment in BNM may be linked to disease or metabolic dysregulation.

Based on these findings, we hypothesize that interactions between gut microbiota and host genes may collectively influence the metastatic patterns of breast cancer. In particular, gut microbiota may indirectly affect the migration and invasion of tumor cells by modulating inflammatory responses and metabolic pathways [45, 46]. Furthermore, the association between differential gene expression and microbial functions suggests that gut microbiota not only play a crucial role in maintaining host immune homeostasis but may also be pivotal in metabolic regulation during the BM of breast cancer [47, 48]. In the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of gene expression profiles and associated functional OTUs in BM and BNM, the HPV infection pathway was significantly enriched. While HPV is commonly linked to epithelial cancers, such as cervical cancer, its role in breast cancer remains unclear [49]. The results indicate that HPV infection-related signaling pathways may influence different clinical subtypes of breast cancer, particularly in the context of bone and non-bone metastases. Previous pathway analyses showed that BM patients were enriched in pathways such as arachidonic acid metabolism and thyroid hormone synthesis, which may promote bone metastasis by influencing immune response, tumor cell proliferation, and migration [50]. In particular, thyroid hormone synthesis is closely associated with cancer metastaticity and may exacerbate bone metastasis by enhancing the migratory capacity of tumor cells [51]. In addition, the HPV infection pathway was enriched in both BM and BNM patients, suggesting that HPV may be involved in the metastatic process of breast cancer by altering the immune microenvironment or epithelial cell function. Therefore, HPV infection may become a new research direction to provide potential targets for the treatment of bone metastasis.

The impact of gut microbiota on cancer is complex. It is important to note that the findings indicate that beneficial flora, such as Clostridia and Anaerotruncus, are enriched in patients with BM and may influence intestinal health through the production of short-chain fatty acids or other immunomodulatory pathways. However, alterations in these microbial communities are not only associated with health status but may also affect cancer metastasis through interactions with tumors [52]. For instance, these microorganisms contribute to the maintenance of a stable microenvironment by modulating the immune response and intestinal barrier function, thereby promoting the development of bone metastasis. Consequently, the gut microbiota of BM patients not only reflects a healthy gut state but may also play a contributory role in the metastatic process. In contrast, Lactobacillus and Porphyromonas, which are enriched in patients with breast cancer BNM, are more likely to be associated with inflammatory and pathological states. Notably, Lactobacillus has been shown to play a role in intestinal inflammation, immune evasion, and other processes [53]. The enrichment of these microorganisms may correlate with immune response, metabolic status, or treatment outcomes. Further investigation into the treatment regimens of patients with BNM could enhance our understanding of whether differences in these microbiota are associated with treatment, comorbidities, or alterations in the immune system. Overall, while research suggests that patients with BM may possess a healthier gut microbiota, treatment, underlying diseases, or other confounding factors may also influence the composition of the microbiota. Future studies should take these factors into account and control for treatment-related variables to gain deeper insights into the relationship between gut microbiota and breast cancer BM.

In addition, we analyzed the expression specificity and methylation levels of the genes using CCLE and UALCAN [54, 55]. Our findings suggest that these genes may influence BM and represent potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, we evaluated their relevance to drug development and conducted preliminary validation through molecular docking, providing insights for future therapeutic strategies. We elucidated the complex relationship between key genes and gut microbial functions during breast cancer BM, providing new biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer BM. However, there are some limitations in this study. Although we identified potential associations between genes and microbial functions using bioinformatics methods, these associations still require verification through further experimental research. In addition, the limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of the results. Therefore, future studies should aim to increase the sample size and integrate multi-omics data to delve into the specific interaction mechanisms between genes and microbes in cancer metastasis. This is particularly important for further exploring the interplay between microbes and cancer-related genes in the context of immune regulation and metabolic modulation.

Conclusion

This study uses a multi-omics approach to uncover differences in gene expression and gut microbiota functionality between breast cancer patients with BM and BNM. Key genes like IBSP, PROM1, and IDO1 are upregulated in BM patients, indicating their role in cancer invasiveness. miRNA analysis, especially the let-7 family, highlights regulatory impacts on cancer progression, while gene interactions, including THBS1 and ITGB3, emphasize their role in tumor progression. BM patients gut microbiome shows upregulation in pathways related to arachidonic acid and hormone synthesis, contrasting with inflammation-associated pathways in BNM patients. Notably, the HPV infection pathway is enriched in both groups, suggesting a potential link with BM. These findings provide potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for breast cancer BM, guiding future research into molecular mechanisms and personalized treatments.
