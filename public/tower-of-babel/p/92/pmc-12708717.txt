Comparison of robotic-assisted and laparoscopic-assisted anorectal pull-through for anorectal malformations: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Daiwei Zhu, Zebing Zheng, Chengyan Tang, Xingrong Xia, Yuan Gong, Lu Huang, Qing Du, wankang Zhou, Zeping Li, Yu Liao, Yuanmei Liu, Zhu Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn this study, we aimed to compare the efficacy of robotic-assisted anorectal pull-through (RAARP) and laparoscopic-assisted anorectal pull-through (LAARP) in treating children with anorectal malformations (ARMs).MethodsSixty five children with ARMs who underwent surgical treatment in our department between 2018 and 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. Patients were divided into two groups based on surgical approach: 27 cases in the RAARP group and 38 in the LAARP group. The collected data included age and weight at surgery, operation time,intraoperative blood loss,postoperative hospital stay,postoperative complications and postoperative defecation function.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in age at surgery, weight at surgery, operative time, or postoperative hospital stay (P > 0.05). the intraoperative blood loss in the RAARP group were significantly lower than that in the LAARP group (P < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed in the complication rates between the two groups (P > 0.05). The total hospitalization cost in the RAARP group were significantly higher than LAARP group (P < 0.05). The RAARP group demonstrated significantly better postoperative defecation function, including voluntary bowel control and soiling, compared to the LAARP group (P < 0.05), However, no significant difference was observed in the incidence of constipation (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe application of RAARP in the treatment of intermediate/high-type anorectal malformations with rectourethral bulbar fistula demonstrates safety and efficacy, effectively reducing intraoperative blood loss while maintaining postoperative defecation function comparable to or better than LAARP, with satisfactory early-term outcomes.RAARP also represents a safe and feasible option for the treatment of low-type fistulous anorectal malformations, warranting consideration for clinical application.

Anorectal malformations (ARMs), the most prevalent congenital anomalies of the digestive tract in children, require diverse surgical approaches depending on their anatomical subtypes. Although the Wingspread classification (1988) categorized ARMs into high, intermediate, and low types-with cloacal malformations and other rare variants classified separately as special types [1], this complex system demonstrates limited clinical utility in guiding surgical decision-making.The international Krickenbeck classification, established in 2005, categorizes ARMs according to fistula presence and type, thereby offering improved guidance for surgical approach selection. This classification built upon the landmark posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP) developed by De Vries and Peña in 1982, which represented a transformative advancement in the management of complex ARMs cases [2, 3]. Subsequently in 2000, Georgeson et al. introduced laparoscopic-assisted anorectoplasty (LAARP), utilizing minimally invasive techniques to reduce surgical trauma while protecting the pelvic neural and muscular structures, resulting in improved postoperative defecation function [4]. Compared with PSARP, LAARP demonstrates clear advantages including reduced invasiveness and quicker recovery.

However, with the advent of robotic surgical systems, robotic-assisted anorectal pull-through (RAARP) combines the advantages of minimally invasive surgery while incorporating cutting-edge surgical techniques and instruments. The robotic system, with its high-definition 3D imaging and enhanced stability, has been widely applied in pediatric surgery [5], providing patients with more treatment options.To date, few studies have compared RAARP versus LAARP for ARMs treatment outcomes. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of these two minimally invasive surgical approaches for ARMs, providing evidence to support the wider clinical application of robotic surgery while offering more surgical options for ARMs.

Materials and methods

Materials

Data from 81 patients with ARMs who were treated at the Department of Pediatric Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University from 2018 to 2023 were collected. Patients were allocated to either the LAARP or RAARP group according to the surgical approach ultimately selected by the families after receiving comprehensive information about procedures. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University approved the study (approval number: KLL-2024-700), and written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of each patient.The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with intermediate/high anorectal malformations (including rectobulbar, rectovesical, rectoprostatic, or rectovestibular fistulas) who underwent surgical correction.The exclusion criteria were as follows: ①Patients with rectovestibular fistula underwent PSARP.②Incomplete clinical data.s③Severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities.④Rare anorectal anomalies (e.g., cloaca, pouch colon, rectal atresia).Ultimately, 65 children were included in this study.Per the Krichenbeck classification [6], the LAARP group included 12 rectobulbar fistulas, 8 rectovesical fistulas, 13 rectoprostatic fistulas, and 5 rectovestibular fistulas, whereas the RAARP group consisted of 9 rectobulbar fistulas, 6 rectovesical fistulas, 9 rectoprostatic fistulas, and 3 rectovestibular fistulas (Table 1). Methods

Therapeutic approach: Patients with rectobulbar fistula, rectovesical fistula, or rectoprostatic fistula underwent a three-stage surgical protocol. The neonates initially underwent transverse colostomy, followed by anorectoplasty three months later as the second-stage procedure.Closure of the transverse colostomy was performed as the third-stage procedure 2–4 months following anorectoplasty.Among patients with rectovestibular fistula, one neonate underwent transverse colostomy during the neonatal period due to severe bowel obstruction and abdominal distension, consequently requiring a three-stage surgical approach,remaining patients underwent primary anorectoplasty at 3 months of age.

Operative procedure: RAARP: A 0.8 cm incision was created at the superior umbilical margin for 8 mm trocar insertion as the camera port, followed by pneumoperitoneum establishment (8–10 mmHg). Bilateral 8 mm working trocars were then placed approximately 5 cm lateral to the umbilicus. Finally, the robotic arm were introduced and secured from the cephalad position relative to the patient.The surgeon manipulated a Maryland dissector with the left hand and monopolar hook with the right hand. The anterior and bilateral walls of the rectum are dissected using an 8 mm robotic monopolar hook between the proper rectal fascia (also known as the serosa layer above the peritoneal reflection) and the longitudinal muscular layer of rectum. The posterior wall is dissected right next to the rectum, between the proper rectal fascia and the anterior sacral fascia.With the help of the 10×magnification and 3D camera, the plasma membrane layer and longitudinal rectus muscle were easily separated to avoid damaging the Denovillers fascia, presacral fascia, and perirectal neurovascular plexus. The dissection extends down until reaching the fistulous tract (potentially located at the bladder neck, bulbar urethra, prostatic urethra, or vestibular region). The identified fistula was precisely sutured using 5-0 absorbable suture material before being surgically divided.The procedure then transitioned to the perineal phase. Under guidance of an anorectal low-frequency electrical stimulator, the center of the external anal sphincter was identified and a 1.2 cm longitudinal incision was made. The external sphincter was dissected vertically while simultaneous laparoscopic observation confirmed contraction of the striated muscle complex center during electrical stimulation.A dilator was introduced through the perineal incision, passing through the center of the striated muscle complex into the pelvic cavity. Sequential dilation was performed up to a 12-Fr dilator to enlarge the perineal tunnel. The distal rectum was then pulled through this tunnel and the striated muscle complex center to the anal verge.The rectal wall was secured to the anal sphincter with interrupted sutures, followed by tension-free anastomosis of the rectal terminus to the anal skin using 5-0 absorbable sutures. The reconstructed anal opening was calibrated to approximately 1.2 cm in diameter. After releasing the pneumoperitoneum, all abdominal incisions were closed. LAARP: A 0.5 cm incision was made at the superior umbilical margin and bilateral umbilical levels for insertion of 5 mm trocars to establish optical and working ports. Pneumoperitoneum was created and maintained at 8–10 mmHg.The rectum was fully mobilized to expose the fistulous connection (located at either the bladder neck, bulbar urethra, prostatic urethra or vestibular region), which was then suture-ligated with 5-0 absorbable material and divided.The procedure then proceeded to the perineal phase using standard PAARP technique.

Outcome measures:The following parameters were recorded for both groups: age at surgery, weight at surgery, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative hospital stay. Postoperative complications, including wound infection/dehiscence, rectal mucosal prolapse, rectal retraction, anal stenosis, urethral diverticulum,and recurrent fistula, were statistically analyzed.

Follow-up and defecation function evaluation:Anal dilation was commenced at 2 weeks postoperatively and maintained for 3–6 months. The follow-up period ranged from 2 to 6 years (mean 3.9 years). Functional outcomes were evaluated using the Krickenbeck classification system, which specifically assesses voluntary bowel control, soiling, and constipation. Since rectovestibular fistula represents a low-type malformation with inherently better functional prognosis compared to intermediate/high-type anomalies, and given the limited number of low-type cases in this study, patients with rectovestibular fistula and those under 3 years of age were excluded from the analysis.The functional evaluation was ultimately performed in 33 LAARP cases and 23 RAARP cases.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as (mean ± SD) and compared using independent Student’s t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as M (P25, P75) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequency counts (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s chi-square tests. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

ARMs represent the most prevalent congenital gastrointestinal anomalies in children, occurring in 1:5000 to 1:1500 live births with male predominance [7].The clinical presentation and surgical management of ARMs differ according to the specific type.For ARMs cases with high fistulas such as rectovesical fistula, the transabdominal approach is mandatory for repair, which is why most surgeons prefer LAARP. Previous studies have demonstrated its advantages in managing high fistulas with greater operative convenience, minimized tissue trauma, and postoperative defecation function comparable to or better than the Peña procedure [8–10].The surgical management of rectourethral fistulas remains controversial, with the PSARP technique originally described by De Vries and Peña [3] continuing to be regarded as the preferred approach by most pediatric surgeons.However,Rintala and Pakarinen’s [11]clinical series documented persistent fecal soiling and incontinence in 35% of patients during long-term follow-up after PSARP for rectourethral fistula repair.

Georgeson et al. [5] first described LAARP in 2000, highlighting its advantages including minimal invasiveness, reduced trauma to both abdominal and perianal regions, and preservation of the anal sphincter complex. These technical benefits have led to its increasing clinical adoption. The laparoscopic approach for rectovestibular fistula remains controversial. Compared to PSARP, LAARP offers advantages including reduced surgical trauma, lower risk of wound infection, decreased perianal scarring, and reduced postoperative constipation [12, 13]. However, the technique requires deeper pelvic dissection with potential vaginal injury risk and may lead to postoperative rectal mucosal prolapse. Comparative studies [14] have demonstrated that while LAARP achieves equivalent long-term functional outcomes to PSARP in rectovestibular fistula repair, this approach is associated with a significantly higher incidence of postoperative rectal mucosal prolapse.

Despite its demonstrated advantages, laparoscopic repair of ARMs remains technically challenging and is associated with several well-documented complications, including urinary diverticula formation, rectal mucosal prolapse, and anal stenosis [15]. The restricted pelvic dimensions in infants pose significant technical challenges for laparoscopic management of low fistulas, where constrained surgical access and limited visualization increase both intraoperative bleeding risks and potential iatrogenic injury to adjacent urethral or vaginal structures.RAARP not only retains the advantages of laparoscopic surgery but also provides additional benefits. The high-definition 3D visualization facilitates precise fistula identification and ensures accurate suture placement at the fistula origin, thereby minimizing residual fistula tissue and preventing urinary diverticulum formation. Furthermore, the flexible and stable robotic arm enable meticulous dissection of perirectal fascia, sphincter complexes, and neurovascular structures in the deep pelvis, significantly reducing potential damage to the pelvic floor sphincter system and neural components.

In this study, all procedures were performed using minimally invasive techniques with comparable postoperative recovery periods. The RAARP group demonstrated marginally longer operative times compared to LAARP, potentially attributable to robotic system setup requirements.With increasing surgical experience and accumulated case volume in robotic procedures, we anticipate that operative times will ultimately become shorter than those of LAARP. The advantages of 3D vision and flexible robotic arm can reduce the time needed for pelvic dissection. RAARP demonstrated significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss compared to LAARP, attributable to the enhanced surgical precision afforded by robotic-assisted dissection techniques. Previous studies have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of RAARP for ARMs with rectovesical and rectourethral fistulas, showing distinct advantages in fistula identification, resection, and suturing [16]. In the present study, due to the limited operating space and restricted maneuverability of laparoscopic instruments in LAARP, handling low fistulas was particularly challenging. Given the unique anatomical relationship between the urethra and the distal rectum, excessive dissection risked urethral injury, while insufficient dissection led to residual fistulas, potentially resulting in urethral diverticula. Indeed, one case of postoperative urethral diverticulum occurred in the LAARP group, likely attributable to excessive fistula remnant.In contrast, during robotic surgery, the high-definition 3D visualization enhanced by bladder neck suspension and magnified endoscopic views provided clear anatomical planes. The stable, flexible robotic arms enabled maximal separation of the rectum and fistula until complete deformation was achieved, allowing thorough resection and secure suturing. Consequently, no urethral diverticulum occurred in the RAARP group postoperatively. Although intergroup comparison showed no statistical significance, these findings further demonstrate the safety and technical advantages of robotic surgery in fistula management.To date, no published studies have reported the application of RAARP for rectovestibular fistula.Based on our institutional experience with laparoscopic and modified Peña procedures for rectovestibular fistula, we have successfully implemented RAARP for this condition. The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications, and postoperative follow-up revealed no instances of vaginal injury or fistula recurrence, demonstrating its safety and feasibility.

Regardless of fistula type in ARMs, the fundamental principles of treatment involve: precise central positioning of the rectal terminus within the striated muscle complex, preservation of sphincteric musculature integrity during dissection, and meticulous protection of pelvic neural structures. This is crucial for long-term defecation function in children. However, Accurate identification and localization of the striated muscle complex center within the constricted pelvic anatomy of infants presents a significant surgical challenge. The LAARP is significantly constrained by limited visualization and restricted instrument maneuverability within the confined pelvic space, presenting notable technical challenges. With robotic assistance, the integration of high-definition 3D vision and stable, flexible arm with enhanced stability provides optimal conditions for precise surgical manipulation within confined anatomical spaces, this technological advantage enables precise anatomical localization of the striated muscle complex center. Guided by perineal anorectal low-frequency electrical stimulation, a pelvic tunnel can be established without disrupting the striated muscle complex, enabling precise central placement of the rectal terminus within this anatomical structure. This reconstruction of normal anorectal muscular relationships is critical for achieving optimal postoperative bowel function in pediatric patients.

In this study, the Krickenbeck scoring system was used to evaluate postoperative defecation function. There was a statistically significant difference in voluntary bowel control and soiling between groups: more children in the RAARP group had voluntary bowel control and fewer experienced soiling compared to the LAARP group. Although constipation rates did not differ significantly.Current evidence demonstrates superior overall defecation functional outcomes with RAARP compared to LAARP. Future large-scale, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are required to validate these findings.Both RAARP and LAARP procedures were associated with mild postoperative rectal mucosal prolapse, potentially attributable to excessive pelvic rectal dissection. Future technical modifications may incorporate posterior rectal wall fixation to the sphincter complex to mitigate this complication.

Comparative analysis with LAARP confirms the safety and efficacy of RAARP for the management of Rectourethral fistula or Rectovesical fistula.RAARP demonstrates distinct advantages over LAARP in managing low fistulas, including precise deep pelvic rectal dissection, accurate fistula identification and repair, and superior preservation of the striated muscle complex and pelvic nerves. These technical benefits confirm the safety and feasibility of RAARP for low fistulas such as rectovestibular fistula.RAARP demonstrates satisfactory early postoperative outcomes in the management of ARMs, with superior anorectal functional results compared to LAARP.However, the current study is limited by its relatively small sample size and short-term follow-up. Future multicenter investigations with larger cohorts and extended observation periods are warranted to evaluate the long-term efficacy of RAARP.
