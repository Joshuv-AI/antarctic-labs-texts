Immersed Halide Perovskite‐Based Electrochemical Cells for Stable Solar Water Splitting: Achievements, Opportunities, and Prospects
Authors: Wooyong Jeong, Juwon Yun, Gyumin Jang, Chang‐Seop Jeong, Jooho Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, halide perovskite materials have attracted significant research interest in photoelectrochemical cells as promising photoabsorbers due to their superior optoelectronic properties. However, their instability under environmental conditions remains a major obstacle to the development of stable water‐splitting devices. This review thoroughly examines the growing array of encapsulation strategies that have accelerated the integration of perovskite materials into water‐splitting systems. In addition, various approaches to extending device lifetime while maintaining high performance are discussed in detail. Recent advances that render perovskite‐based water‐splitting systems a viable source of low‐cost green hydrogen are also highlighted. Finally, current scientific challenges, emerging concepts, and future research directions aimed at enhancing both the performance and operational stability of perovskite‐based electrochemical cells are introduced.Keywords: halide perovskites, photoelectrochemical cells, solar water splitting, stability

Introduction

In recent years, halide perovskites have emerged as one of the most promising materials in the fields of optoelectronics and renewable energy.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] These materials, typically represented by the chemical formula ABX3 (where A is a monovalent cation, B is a divalent metal cation, and X is a halide anion), exhibit unique and exceptional properties. Their high absorption coefficients, tunable bandgaps, long charge carrier diffusion lengths, and facile solution‐processability have positioned halide perovskites as leading candidates for applications such as photovoltaics, light‐emitting diodes, and photodetectors.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] More recently, halide perovskites have been extensively investigated for photoelectrochemical (PEC) water‐splitting applications, where they function as efficient light absorbers to drive chemical reactions, offering a sustainable and efficient pathway for chemical fuel generation.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] Halide perovskites are particularly well suited for PEC systems owing to their excellent optoelectronic properties, including efficient sunlight absorption and the generation of long‐lived charge carriers with minimal energy loss.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] Also, their tunable conduction and valence band positions can be aligned to match the redox potentials of water, facilitating direct conversion of solar energy into chemical fuel.

However, despite their favorable optoelectronic properties, practical implementation is hindered by significant stability challenges.[ 20 ] Halide perovskites degrade under environmental stressors such as moisture, oxygen, ultraviolet radiation, and heat, which pose a major barrier to their deployment.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] A critical issue in PEC applications is electrolyte permeation, which compromises the ionically bonded halide perovskite structure and leads to material dissolution. To mitigate these effects, various encapsulation strategies have been investigated, including epoxy sealing, deposition of metal or metal oxide layers, attachment of thick metal foils, and the application of carbon‐based coatings.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] These encapsulation techniques have significantly enhanced the stability of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes, effectively addressing the immediate challenges associated with their use in PEC systems. Nevertheless, the implementation of such encapsulation structures raises questions about whether perovskite‐based devices can be accurately classified as PEC systems. This controversy arises from the difficulty in clearly distinguishing between PEC and photovoltaic–electrolysis (PV‐EC) systems, highlighting the need for a precise and universally accepted definition of encapsulated perovskite‐based PEC devices.

Following the establishment of encapsulation strategies, research efforts have shifted toward interface engineering, similar to approaches used in photovoltaic systems, and catalyst engineering.[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] Although these strategies have led to notable improvements in both performance and stability, the stability of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes remains relatively inferior, prompting continued efforts to overcome this critical limitation. While recent reviews on perovskite‐based PEC systems have predominantly emphasized device performance and architectural design, systematic discussions on long‐term stability strategies and future perspectives are still limited.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] In this review, we examine whether perovskite‐based photoelectrodes can be definitively classified as PEC devices, while introducing strategies developed to enhance their stability. We begin by exploring the fundamental definition of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes, whose configurations gradually transition from PEC to PV‐EC structures due to the implementation of protective layers and modifications in the connection between the catalyst and absorber layer. By analyzing the configurations of various perovskite‐based photoelectrodes reported in the literature, we conclude that these systems lie along a continuum between PEC and PV‐EC architectures. In this context, we propose the general term “immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells” to describe devices that exhibit dual characteristics of both PEC and PV‐EC systems. This review proceeds to discuss recent advancements aimed at improving the stability of perovskite‐based electrochemical devices. We then explore state‐of‐the‐art strategies and emerging concepts that offer innovative solutions to enhance both device stability and efficiency. By highlighting these developments, we aim to provide a roadmap for researchers to address persistent challenges and unlock new opportunities for halide perovskites in energy and chemical conversion applications. Ultimately, this review seeks to offer a comprehensive understanding of the current landscape of halide perovskite research for immersed water‐splitting systems, while inspiring novel strategies to advance the field.

Theory and Principles of Solar Water Splitting

Understanding the thermodynamic requirements for hydrogen production via solar water splitting is essential. Under standard conditions, the conversion of one molecule of H2O into H2 and ½O2 entails a free energy change (ΔG°) of 237.2 kJ mol−1. Step 1 is dependent on the choice of semiconductor materials and involves a trade‐off between the bandgap and light absorption capabilities. The light‐harvesting efficiency of a semiconductor is primarily governed by its bandgap: a smaller bandgap enables the absorption of a greater number of photons, thereby generating more charge carriers. However, since photovoltage is determined by the quasi‐Fermi level splitting of electrons and holes at the interface under illumination, an excessively small bandgap can reduce the photovoltage due to diminished separation of these levels. In addition, the absorption efficiency (𝜂 absorption) is mainly influenced by the thickness and morphology of the semiconductor, as well as intrinsic physical properties such as the absorption coefficient, carrier mobility, and diffusion length.[ 39 ] It is therefore crucial to consider the material's properties and bandgap in relation to its ability to absorb a broad solar spectrum, ensuring efficient charge carrier generation while maintaining sufficient photovoltage.

Step 2 is the predominant contributor to photovoltage losses during the semiconductor‐based solar water‐splitting process. Conventional photoelectrodes, typically consisting solely of semiconductor materials, are immersed in an electrolyte and covered with a thin protective layer and a catalyst. This configuration facilitates the formation of a semiconductor–liquid junction (SCLJ), where the equilibration of chemical potentials between the redox couples in the electrolyte and the semiconductor's Fermi level generates an electric field capable of separating photogenerated electron–hole pairs (Figure 2 ).[ 40 ] However, severe surface states within the material can induce Fermi level pinning, resulting in photovoltage and performance losses. These surface states originate from discrepancies between the surface and bulk properties of the semiconductor, such as dangling bonds and surface defects, which introduce additional energy levels within the bandgap and promote nonradiative recombination. To mitigate this issue, thick, wide‐bandgap semiconducting materials or metal layers are often employed to enhance device performance and stability by forming a “buried junction,” a configuration analogous to that used in PV devices.[ 41 ] This structure enables the passivation of semiconductor surface states and improves band alignment, thereby minimizing photovoltage loss. In buried junction photoelectrodes, photovoltage is generated within the absorber layer, which consists of solid‐state layers such as a buffer layer, an electron transport layer (ETL), or a hole transport layer (HTL) (Figure 2).[ 40 ] The magnitude of this photovoltage is independent of the SCLJ, as the absorber (semiconductor) does not directly contact the electrolyte.[ 42 ] For example, Cu2O, a promising and extensively studied photocathode material, exhibits a significant conduction band offset with the TiO2 protective layer, resulting in considerable photovoltage loss.[ 43 , 44 ] Inserting a Ga2O3 buffer layer at the Cu2O/TiO2 interface can mitigate this band offset, thereby increasing the photovoltage. Moreover, state‐of‐the‐art PEC cells based on Si and GaAs also incorporate buried junctions, with the illumination region decoupled from the catalytic region (denoted as the active area).[ 45 , 46 ] Despite the absence of direct contact between the absorber layer and the electrolyte in buried junction photoelectrodes, these devices are still widely classified as PEC systems. Therefore, 𝜂 separation/transport can be enhanced through interface engineering by incorporating a buried junction.

Finally, 𝜂 charge transfer is intrinsically linked to catalytic activity. Electrochemical reactions, including the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER), require large overpotentials due to their sluggish kinetics in which various strategies, such as catalyst design, surface modification, and electrolyte optimization, have been introduced to enhance reaction efficiency and reduce overpotential.[ 40 , 47 ] This results in a substantial mismatch in timescales between the rapid photocarrier generation and transport processes (occurring on the picosecond to microsecond scale) and the comparatively slow electrochemical reactions (proceeding on the millisecond to second scale).[ 48 , 49 ] Such a discrepancy can lead to charge accumulation at the semiconductor/electrolyte interface, adversely affecting both performance and long‐term stability during continuous operation.[ 50 , 51 ] To address this, it is necessary to reduce overpotentials and enhance the kinetics of electrochemical reactions, thereby shifting the onset potential in a more favorable direction. To prevent misunderstanding, the distinction between “photovoltage” and “onset potential” must be clarified. Although the definition of onset potential varies across studies, it is generally considered the x‐intercept of the photocurrent density versus potential curve, marking the initiation of the electrochemical reaction. Onset potential is influenced by both the photovoltage and the overpotential of the electrochemical reaction, making it a broader concept encompassing the photovoltage. Notably, even if a semiconductor generates a high photovoltage, a large overpotential can still result in a low onset potential, indicating the additional voltage required to reach a specific current density. Therefore, to lower the overpotential and enhance the onset potential, it is essential to select efficient and compatible catalysts tailored to the specific electrochemical reactions and operating pH conditions, thereby optimizing overall device performance.

Based on the above design principles, extensive efforts have been devoted over the past decades to improving the performance of both photocathodes and photoanodes. However, under standard conditions, the theoretical maximum photovoltage that a semiconductor can generate is ≈400 mV less than its bandgap;[ 52 ] therefore, a bandgap exceeding 1.6 eV is required to achieve overall water splitting using a single semiconductor absorber. This constraint reinforces the persistent challenge of balancing the trade‐off between bandgap and light absorption. To overcome this limitation, multiple‐absorber systems, such as tandem structures with vertical alignment and coplanar structures with parallel alignment, have been employed to simultaneously achieve sufficient photovoltage and high photocurrent for unbiased water splitting. Recent tandem configurations based on III–V semiconductor materials, such as GaInP/GaInAs photoelectrodes, have achieved record STH efficiencies of up to 19.3% under AM 1.5G illumination.[ 53 ] In addition, multi‐junction tandem structures incorporating Si and GaAs have demonstrated similarly high STH efficiencies. Despite these advancements, cost and scalability remain major obstacles to practical implementation. Conversely, STH efficiencies in various low‐cost material combinations, such as Sb2S3 or BiVO4 as photoanodes and Cu2O or Si as photocathodes, remain below 10%.[ 54 , 55 ] For photoanodes, BiVO4 suffers from poor material properties including short carrier diffusion length, low mobility and lifetime, resulting in poor charge separation efficiency.[ 56 ] Although Sb2S3 exhibits better intrinsic properties than BiVO4, it is highly vulnerable to photocorrosion and experiences severe carrier recombination due to intrinsic defects.[ 57 ] In the case of Cu2O, although Ga2O3 has been used to improve band alignment and enhance onset potential and performance, interfacial photocarrier trapping remains a major factor limiting overall efficiency. Si photocathodes also exhibit poor onset potentials owing to their narrow bandgap (1.1 eV), limiting their practical application.[ 58 ] As a result, these limitations collectively hinder the practical deployment of PEC water‐splitting systems for efficient hydrogen production. Therefore, it is imperative to introduce and develop promising absorber materials capable of supporting efficient and scalable hydrogen generation.

Halide Perovskites as an Absorber for Water Splitting Devices

Desirable Optoelectronic Properties

Halide perovskites with the chemical formula ABX3 have emerged as a transformative class of materials in photovoltaics, garnering significant attention due to their exceptional intrinsic physical and optoelectronic properties.[ 59 ] In this structure, the A‐site is occupied by a monovalent cation, which may be an organic species such as methylammonium (MA+) or formamidinium (FA+), or an inorganic cation such as cesium (Cs+) or rubidium (Rb+). These cations can also be judiciously mixed based on their ionic radii to stabilize the perovskite lattice, thereby enhancing structural tolerance and enabling tunable material properties. The B‐site hosts a divalent metal cation, typically lead (Pb2+) or tin (Sn2+), both of which are heavy elements whose relativistic effects significantly influence the electronic structure of the perovskite. The X‐site is occupied by halide anions, chloride (Cl−), bromide (Br−), or iodide (I−), allowing for fine‐tuning of the bandgap and optical characteristics through compositional control. This compositional flexibility enables the tailoring of the material for specific optoelectronic applications, including solar cells and light‐emitting devices.

One of the standout features of halide perovskites is their strong spin–orbit coupling (SOC), induced by the relativistic effects of the heavy B‐site metal cations (Pb2+ or Sn2+),[ 12 ] which possess large atomic masses that enhance the interaction between electron spin and orbital motion. This interaction significantly alters the electronic band structure, as SOC induces energy band splitting that reshapes the band edges and reduces the bandgap of halide perovskites.[ 13 ] The resulting narrower bandgap improves light absorption and facilitates efficient charge carrier transport within the perovskite material, making SOC a critical factor in their performance in photovoltaic devices. In addition, halide perovskites possess a direct bandgap, distinguishing them from many conventional semiconductors.[ 14 ] Direct alignment between the valence and conduction bands eliminates the need for phonon‐assisted transitions, enabling efficient optical transitions in which electrons can be directly excited from the valence band to the conduction band upon photon absorption. In addition, halide perovskites exhibit a high density of states at the band edges, providing numerous electronic states for electrons and holes near the conduction and valence bands.[ 15 ] This elevated density increases the probability of photon absorption, contributing to the exceptional light‐harvesting efficiency that underpins their success in solar energy conversion. Beyond their optical advantages, halide perovskites also possess remarkable charge transport properties that enhance their suitability for photovoltaic applications.[ 16 , 17 ] Long carrier diffusion lengths, often exceeding 1 µm, have been consistently demonstrated, allowing photogenerated electrons and holes to travel substantial distances before recombination.[ 18 ] Furthermore, these materials exhibit low recombination rates, minimizing energy losses and improving charge collection efficiency in devices.[ 19 ] These attributes, combined with their tunable bandgap and strong light absorption, make halide perovskites exceptionally well‐suited for a wide range of optoelectronic applications.

Based on these extraordinary properties, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved remarkable milestones in recent years. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs has reached 27.0%, which is remarkably close to the record of 27.8% held by silicon heterojunction solar cells.[ 60 ] Furthermore, perovskite–perovskite tandem cells have achieved efficiencies approaching 30.1%, while perovskite–silicon tandem cells have recorded efficiencies up to 34.9%, indicating that PSCs became strong contenders to silicon‐based solar cells in the near future.[ 60 ] Moreover, the solution processability of halide perovskites enables low‐cost manufacturing and scalability, offering a significant advantage over the energy‐intensive fabrication processes required for silicon‐based counterparts.[ 61 , 62 ] This rapid advancement in efficiency and integration into photovoltaic technologies underscores both the intrinsic advantages of perovskite materials and the intensive research dedicated to optimizing device architectures and fabrication techniques.

Limitations of Halide Perovskite Materials

Despite their high performance and commercial potential, halide perovskites face several critical challenges that must be addressed to enable widespread deployment. The inherent use of lead (Pb) in most halide perovskite materials raises persistent concerns regarding toxicity. This issue becomes increasingly critical under prolonged operation, during which not only Pb but also iodine components tend to leach or degrade, posing potential environmental and safety risks.[ 63 ] Recent studies have predominantly focused on substituting Pb with less toxic alternatives such as Sn.[ 64 , 65 ] In addition, emerging strategies aim to encapsulate the entire perovskite layer to suppress elemental loss altogether, thereby enabling post‐use recycling of the intact material.[ 66 ]

Another key problem is their limited stability, as they are highly susceptible to degradation under external stimuli.[ 20 , 21 ] Exposure to moisture, heat, electric bias, and UV radiation accelerates decomposition, ion migration, and phase segregation, all of which severely impair device performance and longevity. To mitigate these stability issues, researchers have employed composition, interface, and strain engineering strategies aimed at enhancing the durability of PSCs.[ 67 , 68 , 69 ] Composition engineering, in particular, involves tailoring the chemical structure of the perovskite to improve its intrinsic stability. For example, mixed‐cation systems (e.g., MA+/FA+/Cs+ triple cation), mixed‐halide compositions (e.g., I−/Br−), and all‐inorganic formulations (e.g., CsPbI x Br3− x ) can be precisely tuned to optimize both stability and optoelectronic performance.[ 70 ] Interface engineering focuses on optimizing the boundaries between the perovskite layer and adjacent charge transport layers. By minimizing interfacial defects, this approach reduces pathways for ion migration and suppresses defect formation, thereby improving overall device stability. Key techniques include surface passivation with small molecules or polymers, and the fabrication of 2D/3D heterostructures, which is formed by incorporating 2D cations to 3D perovskite surface, effectively neutralizing surface trap states and facilitating favorable band alignment.[ 71 ] Strain engineering, often considered an extension of interface engineering, employs similar processing techniques to further stabilize the perovskite structure by suppressing lattice dimensional variations.[ 72 ] Halide perovskites are particularly susceptible to lattice expansion or compression under light exposure or thermal cycling,[ 73 , 74 ] leading to mechanical stress at interfaces that may cause delamination, cracking, bandgap shifts, or the generation of defect states detrimental to optoelectronic performance. By modulating the strain state through the use of passivation agents, both the structural integrity and electronic properties of the perovskite can be preserved under operational conditions.[ 72 ] Collectively, these engineering strategies have significantly improved the durability of halide perovskite materials, with operational stabilities now approaching 10000 h in photovoltaic applications.[ 75 ]

Nevertheless, the stability afforded by current strategies remains insufficient for the application of perovskite materials as PEC photoelectrodes, where the material must be directly immersed in an electrolyte to facilitate electrochemical reactions. Even limited electrolyte permeation can compromise the ionically bonded perovskite structure, leading to structural collapse and dissolution, thereby rendering the material unsuitable for sustained PEC operation.[ 76 ] As a result, preventing electrolyte permeation becomes a critical priority, surpassing other degradation mechanisms, for the effective deployment of perovskite materials as PEC electrodes.

Encapsulation Strategies for Perovskite‐Based Photoelectrodes

In the preceding section, the superior optoelectronic properties and stability challenges of halide perovskites were discussed, with particular emphasis on degradation caused by electrolyte permeation during electrochemical reactions in water‐splitting devices. To mitigate this degradation pathway, epoxy resin has been widely employed as a blocking layer in perovskite‐based photoelectrode studies due to its excellent mechanical strength, chemical inertness across a wide pH range, and cost‐effectiveness.[ 12 , 77 ] In practice, epoxy resin inevitably comes into contact with the charge transport layer or top electrode of the photoactive area; therefore, careful consideration must be given to the fabrication process to avoid undesirable interfacial reactions or delamination, which will be further discussed in the final sub‐section. However, epoxy resin is intrinsically insulating, which limits its direct use on the photoactive region. To overcome this limitation, epoxy is typically applied to encapsulate the PEC device, excluding the active area, thereby preventing water ingress while preserving photoelectrochemical performance. For the active area, encapsulation materials must simultaneously provide electrical conductivity and serve as effective barriers to electrolyte penetration. To this end, various conductive encapsulation strategies have been developed to protect the photoactive region of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes without compromising device performance.

Thin Metal Layer

In the early stages of research, thin layers of metals and metal oxides were employed as conductive encapsulation materials to inhibit water permeation and enhance the stability of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes, owing to their straightforward application process (Figure 3a). For instance, thin evaporated nickel (Ni) films were used as physical barriers to prevent moisture intrusion into perovskite photoanodes, providing limited operational durability of less than 10 h.[ 78 ] Reisner and coworkers later introduced a fusible Field's metal, an InBiSn alloy with a low melting point of 62 °C, as an encapsulation material to protect the perovskite layer from water exposure. This low‐temperature process enabled facile deposition while minimizing thermal damage to organic components and adjacent layers.[ 10 , 79 ] Following this development, several research groups adopted Field's metal to achieve operational stability of up to ≈15 h.[ 80 , 81 ] As an alternative strategy, Kim et al. applied a thin titanium dioxide (TiO2) layer via atomic layer deposition as a protective coating. However, this approach yielded limited stability, with operational durations of only around 2 h.[ 25 ] While these methods offer simplicity and some degree of effectiveness, the use of thin metal/metal oxide layers may cause damage to the perovskite layer during the encapsulation process due to thermal stress or penetration of liquid‐phase materials. In addition, these approaches are insufficient to fully prevent water permeation. Therefore, more advanced and precisely engineered conductive encapsulation strategies are required, which are discussed in the following section.

Thick Physical Barrier‐Metal Foil (Ti and Ni) and Graphite

To effectively prevent water permeation into the perovskite layer, thick metal foils and graphite have been employed as protective layers for perovskite photoelectrodes (Figure 3b). Ti foil offers chemical stability in acidic environments, while Ni foil provides both catalytic activity and stability under alkaline conditions. Graphite, with its excellent electrical conductivity and chemical inertness across a wide pH range, serves as a versatile encapsulant for various operating environments. However, these encapsulating materials cannot form direct contact with metal electrodes such as Au, Ag, and Cu, due to the difficulty of attaching thick protective layers to electrode surfaces.[ 29 ] Consequently, an additional conductive adhesive is required to ensure effective electrical connectivity. The choice of conductive materials is critical, as they can substantially influence the fill factor and overall performance of the device, thereby necessitating careful material selection to optimize both efficiency and stability. Zhang et al. first reported a perovskite photocathode encapsulated with Ti foil using silver paste as a conductive adhesive, which demonstrated limited operational durability of 12 h.[ 82 ] This degradation is presumably due to the protic organic solvents in silver paste, which may penetrate and damage the perovskite film during the encapsulation process. To mitigate this issue and reduce stability loss at the perovskite–Ti foil interface, eutectic gallium–indium alloy (EGaIn) has been introduced as an alternative conductive medium.[ 27 ] As a result, the perovskite photocathode exhibited prolonged device stability, maintaining 60% of its initial photocurrent density (t 60) over 54 h. However, liquid‐phase materials can still penetrate and degrade perovskite films, leading to insufficient long‐term stability. To address this issue, Moon's group employed solvent‐free carbon powder as an electrical conductor between the perovskite layer and Ni foil, achieving a remarkable improvement in stability and extending the device lifetime to 225 h (t 70) under continuous 1‐sun illumination, with minimal performance degradation.[ 31 ] As an alternative protective approach, Poli and coworkers first introduced a cost‐effective and commercially available graphite layer combined with mesoporous carbon to encapsulate the perovskite. Building on this concept, Mohite's group reported the use of a conductive adhesive barrier (CAB) in place of carbon‐based conductors.[ 11 ] The CAB consists of a bilayer structure, wherein each layer serves a distinct function: the first is a barrier layer composed of a conductive, inert, and impermeable material such as graphite, designed to shield the photovoltaic component from environmental exposure; the second is a conductive adhesive layer, formulated with a polymer matrix and conductive fillers (e.g., carbon or metal), which ensures strong adhesion to the photovoltaic component while maintaining electrical connectivity. This configuration enabled the perovskite photocathode to achieve prolonged stability over 60 h (t 90) without significant performance loss. While these strategies effectively mitigate water permeation and enhance device longevity, they are often limited by high material costs and the complexity of fabricating thick metal foil‐based structures (Figure 3c).

Catalyst‐Decoupled Lateral Geometry

In perovskite‐based photoelectrodes, photogenerated carriers are typically extracted toward the top electrode and transferred vertically to the catalyst to drive electrochemical reactions, while the bottom electrode is connected to the counter electrode via Cu wiring. However, this configuration necessitates complex encapsulation procedures that may degrade the perovskite light absorber, charge transport layers, or electrodes during the sealing process. To overcome these limitations, a simplified architecture has been developed in which photoelectrochemical reactions occur at a physically separated bottom electrode, while the perovskite layer is entirely sealed with epoxy (Figure 3c). This geometry is commonly referred to as a “catalyst‐decoupled lateral configuration.” In this structure, the catalyst is laterally connected to a separate bottom electrode, allowing the perovskite to be encapsulated without the need for conductive sealing materials. Chen et al. first introduced a laterally decoupled catalyst design in an n‐i‐p perovskite‐based photocathode, achieving spatial separation between light absorption and electrochemical reactions to enhance device stability.[ 28 ] Their architecture employed a bottom‐electrode working configuration that minimized direct electrolyte exposure while maintaining efficient charge transport. Building on this concept, Park et al. developed a dual‐perovskite photocathode integrated with a laterally decoupled HER catalyst.[ 77 ] This configuration enabled the demonstration of a wireless artificial leaf system with an 11% STH efficiency and stable operation for over 85 h without external wiring. These findings suggest that perovskite‐based photoelectrodes can be fabricated more easily using only epoxy encapsulation by reconfiguring the catalyst position.

Nevertheless, this architecture also presents several limitations. The complete spatial separation between the illumination and catalytic areas inevitably results in a longer lateral charge transport pathway, leading to increased lateral resistance. A direct comparison between this lateral resistance and the resistance in vertical architectures (particularly those employing metal foils with conductive encapsulation layers) has not yet been performed, but such analysis would be crucial for understanding the trade‐offs. Furthermore, the physical separation of the catalyst leads to increased device form factor compared to conventional vertical structures, which is undesirable, especially when considering large‐scale implementation. From a stability perspective, the bottom substrate, such as fluorine‐doped tin oxide (FTO) or indium‐doped tin oxide (ITO), may be exposed to the electrolyte during electrochemical operation, thereby compromising the long‐term durability of the device. To prevent chemical degradation of the bottom substrate, careful selection of a stable electrolyte based on the substrate's Pourbaix diagram or implementation of a protective layer is essential.

Practical Considerations in Epoxy Encapsulation

Caution regarding epoxy resin encapsulation is well‐supported by previous PSC literature, particularly due to the degradation risks associated with the curing process. Epoxy resins are typically classified into three types: UV‐cured, thermally cured, and hot‐melt, each presenting distinct risks to damage the perovskite layer. UV‐cured epoxies often incorporate photo‐initiators and polar functional groups that can chemically react with perovskite absorbers or organic HTLs, leading to accelerated degradation and performance loss.[ 83 ] For instance, Wong‐Stringer et al. investigate into the use of solution‐processible polymer interlayer (polyvinylpyrrolidone) which acts as a buffer layer between the epoxy and PSC, mitigating direct degradation induced by UV‐curable epoxy.[ 84 ] Thermally cured and hot‐melt epoxies can not only cause heat‐induced degradation of the photoactive area, but they also release volatile byproducts during high‐temperature curing process. The fumes chemically interact with the perovskite or adjacent charge transport layers, causing structural degradation or interfacial delamination. Dong et al. report detrimental effects on PSC performance even at lower thermal curing temperature of 80 °C due to outgassing of epoxy.[ 85 ] Furthermore, these devices operate under corrosive electrolyte environments, thus their chemical stability of the cured encapsulant must be carefully considered. Polyamide‐based hardeners offer slow and controllable curing kinetics, which result in low‐exothermic crosslinking reactions at room temperature and form densely crosslinked networks with enhanced resistance to chemical degradation.[ 86 ] This makes them suitable for applications involving strongly acidic or alkaline electrolytes. These findings underscore the importance of selecting and engineering epoxy resin with minimal chemical reactivity and thermal stress to ensure long‐term stability of perovskite‐based devices, particularly under immersed operating conditions.

Defining Perovskite‐Based Water Splitting Devices

Given their encapsulated architecture, there remains ongoing debate as to whether photoelectrodes incorporating perovskite absorbers should be classified as classical PEC cells. This divergence in opinion likely arises from the ambiguous definitions surrounding PEC and PV‐EC systems. PEC devices are characterized by a monolithic structure in which the electrode and semiconductor are seamlessly integrated, enabling both light absorption and electrochemical reactions to occur directly within the electrolyte without the need for external wiring. In such systems, the Fermi level of the semiconductor equilibrates with the redox couple in the electrolyte.[ 40 , 87 , 88 ] By contrast, PV‐EC devices exhibit a segmented architecture, with distinct photovoltaic and electrochemical components connected via external wiring, thus separating the environments for light absorption and electrochemical reactions.[ 40 , 87 , 88 ] In PV‐EC configurations, the semiconductor's Fermi level does not equilibrate with the electrolyte, and catalysts are often fabricated separately and later integrated into the electrochemical component. The absence of Fermi level equilibration between the perovskite and electrolyte, along with the use of separately attached catalysts, positions perovskite‐based photoelectrodes more closely with PV‐EC architectures. However, photoelectrodes featuring a buried junction, where the semiconductor does not directly contact the electrolyte (see Section 2), are still classified as PEC devices. Notably, state‐of‐the‐art Si and GaAs photoelectrodes that incorporate spatial separation between the illumination area (semiconductor) and catalyst (electrolyte), allowing for independent catalyst deposition, are widely recognized as PEC systems.[ 45 , 46 ] Therefore, perovskite‐based photoelectrodes have often been regarded as PEC devices in the literature (see Table 1 and Table 2 ), due to their buried junction configurations and monolithic integration without external wiring. Nevertheless, despite their structural similarities to classical PEC systems, the operational principles of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes align more closely with PV‐EC architectures, underscoring the need to establish a clearer distinction between these two classifications.

Classification and designation of perovskite‐based PV–EC device architectures.

It is important to note that PEC and PV‐EC configurations should not be viewed as strictly distinct categories, but rather as points along a continuum of structural transformation in geometrical design.[ 89 ] Perovskite‐based photoelectrodes gradually evolve from PEC to PV‐EC architectures in response to the need for protective layers and strategic catalyst placement. Figure 4a illustrates a conventional PEC cell in which the catalyst is directly deposited onto the semiconductor without a protective layer, forming an SCLJ. The introduction of a protective charge transport layer results in a buried junction, where the photovoltage is determined by the Fermi level difference between the semiconductor and the protective layer, independent of the SCLJ (Figure 4b). To further enhance stability, the architecture may incorporate a thick, laterally aligned catalyst (Figure 4c) or a vertically aligned catalyst (Figure 4d), both of which inhibit electrolyte penetration while maintaining electrical contact with the semiconductor. In the final stage of this progression, the conductive pathway is extended to position the photoelectrode entirely outside the electrolyte with the use of external wiring, thereby forming a conventional PV‐EC configuration (Figure 4e). Perovskite‐based photoelectrodes typically adopt intermediate forms, such as those shown in Figure 4c,d, exhibiting dual characteristics of both PEC and PV‐EC systems. As a result, these devices cannot be definitively classified within the traditional PEC or PV‐EC categories, highlighting the need for a distinct classification. To better reflect their hybrid nature, we propose the term immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells (or devices). This terminology will be used in the following discussion.

Gradual geometric transition from PEC to PV–EC configurations, illustrating various photoelectrode designs based on catalyst geometry. a) Conventional PEC configuration. b) PEC device incorporating a catalyst and protective layer, enabling perpendicular charge transport to the electrolyte. c) Laterally aligned catalyst deposited on the protective layer to obstruct electrolyte contact. d) Vertically aligned catalyst integrated via a perpendicularly extended conductor. e) Further elongation of the conductor displaces the photoelectrode entirely from the electrolyte, representing a traditional PV–EC configuration.

Strategies for Enhancing the Stability of Immersed Perovskite‐Based Electrochemical Devices

Previous studies on perovskite‐based photoelectrodes have predominantly focused on encapsulation techniques to prevent electrolyte–perovskite contact, thereby improving system stability (Table 3 ). Compared to PSCs, immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical systems benefit from the high specific heat capacity of water, which helps mitigate thermal degradation, while moisture and oxygen‐induced degradation can also be suppressed through encapsulation. However, despite these protective strategies, the operational lifetime of immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical devices remains significantly limited, rarely exceeding 300 h, far shorter than the >9000 h reported for PSCs under continuous illumination.[ 31 , 90 ] This suggests that encapsulation failure is no longer the primary degradation pathway, and that instability arises from intrinsic material limitations and electrochemical operational conditions. In this section, strategies beyond encapsulation aimed at enhancing the stability of immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells are introduced. These include conventional approaches used in PSCs, such as improving perovskite film quality and mitigating defects through interface engineering. Furthermore, recent research emphasizes identifying degradation mechanisms unique to electrochemical operation, distinct from those in PV systems, and proposes novel strategies to address these instabilities by improving electrochemical reaction kinetics.

Enhancing Perovskite Film Quality

During immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical operation, the absorption layer must continuously generate charge carriers without undergoing degradation; thus, maintaining high‐quality and stable absorption layers is critical for enhancing the overall stability of perovskite‐based electrochemical cells. Similar to strategies employed to improve PSC stability, various studies have focused on passivating perovskite grain boundaries and incorporating 2D perovskite layers to enhance film quality. Jeong et al. developed a method for fabricating high‐quality perovskite films by submerging as‐coated films into a cyclohexylammonium iodide (CHAI)‐containing antisolvent bath, which significantly enhanced crystallinity and effectively passivated grain boundaries, resulting in a more stable perovskite layer (Figure 5a).[ 33 ] The stability of perovskite‐based electrodes was further improved through the integration of a 2D perovskite layer; after 24 h of operation, the immersed perovskite‐based anode retained 88% of its initial photocurrent density, and after 175 h, it maintained 62% of its initial performance under neutral conditions (Figure 5b).[ 91 ] In addition to the introduction of a 2D perovskite layer, Hansora et al. reported Cl‐doped FAPbI3 film, where acetylcholine chloride was used as a Cl dopant to stabilize the α‐phase of FAPbI3 with Cl‐doped SnO2 and enhance long‐term device stability. As a result, the device demonstrated remarkable operational durability, maintaining 99% of its initial photocurrent density over 140 h of continuous operation in 1 m KOH (pH 14) (Figure 5c).[ 92 ] These findings underscore the importance of grain boundary passivation, 2D perovskite layer incorporation, and halide doping in preserving the structural integrity of perovskite films, thereby ensuring long‐term stability under operational conditions. Interface Engineering

In perovskite materials, defects act as charge‐trapping sites and significantly influence their bonding characteristics. Numerous studies have shown that defect concentrations at the interface between the perovskite and charge‐transport layers are approximately two orders of magnitude higher than those within the perovskite bulk.[ 93 , 94 ] In particular, deep‐level interfacial defects severely hinder device performance and stability by trapping carriers. Therefore, interfacial defect passivation and efficient charge extraction are essential for ensuring durable device operation. Yun et al. first demonstrated that the extent of electron extraction at the perovskite/SnO2 interface governs the operational stability of perovskite photoanodes.[ 31 ] They introduced a mixture of polyethylenimine ethoxylated (PEIE) and chlorobenzenesulfonic acid (CBSA) to modify the SnO2/perovskite interface (Figure 6a). This mixed CBSA+PEIE treatment effectively passivated defects in both SnO2 and the perovskite layer, adjusted band alignment, reduced defect density, and enhanced conductivity, thereby facilitating improved carrier extraction. As a result, the interface‐engineered immersed perovskite‐based anode operated for 42 h at 1.23 VRHE under 1 sun illumination, demonstrating markedly enhanced stability compared to the untreated control device, which operated for only 12 h (Figure 6b). Similarly, Lee's group employed glycidyltrimethylammonium chloride (GTMCl) treatment to engineer the SnO2 interface in immersed perovskite‐based anodes, achieving improved operational stability by suppressing nonradiative recombination (Figure 6c,d).[ 32 ] In another approach, Lee et al. utilized a cross‐linking strategy involving trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMTA) at grain boundaries, combined with a 2D perovskite layer at the interface, achieving a notable operational lifetime of 255 h under mildly basic conditions (Figure 6e,f).[ 95 ] This strategy not only passivated grain boundaries but also suppressed ion migration through TMTA cross‐linking, significantly enhancing long‐term durability. While the chiral 2D perovskite layer contributes to spin‐selective charge transport via the CISS effect, it is important to note that the observed enhancement in operational stability may also be influenced by the cross‐linking strategy. The cross‐linked structure plays a critical role in suppressing ion migration and enhancing the mechanical integrity of the perovskite layer under continuous illumination and bias. Although all samples exhibit an initial decline in photocurrent density, likely due to early surface reconstruction or trap state reorganization, the cross‐linked MV‐2D/CT‐3D architecture shows markedly improved long‐term stability over 255 h of operation. This suggests that while the cross‐linking stabilizes the perovskite interface, the CISS effect further improves spin‐selective reaction pathways and reduces recombination losses. These studies collectively underscore the pivotal role of interface engineering in mitigating instability and advancing the practical deployment of perovskite‐based electrochemical devices. However, the interface engineering strategies introduced in this section are largely similar to those previously developed for PSCs and thus offer limited novelty. Moreover, they are not specifically tailored for application in immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells.

Facilitating the Kinetics of Electrochemical Reactions

The strategies for improving perovskite film quality and interface engineering parallel those employed to enhance the operational stability of PSCs. However, these approaches do not fully address the fundamental difference between perovskite‐based electrochemical devices and PSCs. A key distinction lies in the manner in which photogenerated charges are consumed. In PSCs, photocarrier generation occurs on the picosecond timescale, followed by rapid extraction within nanoseconds to microseconds via an external circuit.[ 48 ] In contrast, although photocarrier generation and separation are analogous in electrochemical systems, charge consumption occurs through much slower reactions, on the millisecond to second timescale, at the electrolyte interface.[ 49 ] This significant mismatch in timescales leads to charge accumulation at the electrolyte interface, generating an internal electric field that drives ion migration and phase segregation within the perovskite layer.[ 31 , 50 , 51 ] To mitigate these degradation pathways, it is essential to accelerate electrochemical reaction kinetics, enabling rapid carrier extraction and reducing charge accumulation in the device. Park's group first demonstrated the impact of catalyst selection on the operational stability of an immersed perovskite‐based anode.[ 24 ] A nickel foil (NF) coated with a three‐dimensional nickel and nickel–iron (hereafter NF.3D Ni.NiFe) catalyst significantly outperformed the bare NF‐encapsulated anode (Figure 7a). The NF.3D Ni.NiFe catalyst exhibited a reduced overpotential of 300 mV at 10 mA cm−2 and a Tafel slope of 52.5 mV dec−1, along with greater stability compared to NF (Figure 7b,c). While the NF‐based perovskite device retained only 50% of its initial photocurrent after 16 h, the NF.3D Ni.NiFe‐based device maintained over 50% of its initial photocurrent density for 48 h (Figure 7d). Similarly, Yun et al. demonstrated the critical role of catalyst choice in enhancing operational stability.[ 31 ] Specifically, the use of a NiFe‐layered double hydroxide (NiFe‐LDH) catalyst, integrated with a Ni foam substrate and Ni foil encapsulation (perovskite/Ni foil/NiFe‐LDH@Ni foam), significantly outperformed the reference anode encapsulated with Ni foil alone. While the Ni foil‐only configuration exhibited limited stability, retaining ≈60% of its initial photocurrent density after 42 h, the NiFe‐LDH‐based anode demonstrated exceptional durability, maintaining 70% of its initial photocurrent density at 1.23 VRHE for over 225 h in 1 m KOH (pH 14). This enhanced stability is attributed to the NiFe‐LDH catalyst's ability to efficiently extract photogenerated holes during the sluggish OER, thereby reducing internal charge accumulation. By accelerating hole extraction, the catalyst suppresses ion migration and phase segregation within the perovskite layer, contributing to improved long‐term device performance. Similar to the previous case, Hansora et al. exhibited stability enhancement by employing NiFeOOH as an OER catalyst instead of bare Ni foil. Notably, NiFeOOH suppressed the rapid decline in stability during the initial operation phase.[ 96 ] In addition, several other studies have demonstrated further improvements in stability through the incorporation of high‐performance electrocatalysts.[ 32 ]

The importance of electrochemical reaction kinetics in suppressing perovskite degradation is further evidenced by the introduction of alternative oxidation reactions with faster kinetics than OER. OER is widely recognized for its high overpotential and sluggish kinetics, primarily due to its four‐electron transfer process.[ 97 ] Moreover, its performance is highly pH‐dependent, with reactivity decreasing significantly in neutral or acidic electrolytes as the hydroxide ion concentration diminishes. Consequently, replacing OER with an oxidation reaction characterized by a lower oxidation potential and faster kinetics is highly desirable. To address this, Yun et al. employed the iodide oxidation reaction (IOR) as an alternative anodic process.[ 98 ] IOR involves a rapid two‐electron transfer and exhibits fast kinetics even in neutral electrolytes, with a lower thermodynamic potential of 0.954 VRHE at pH 7 (Figure 7e). Chronoamperometry measurements showed that an immersed perovskite‐based anode employing IOR maintained ≈90% of its initial photocurrent density at 0.6 VRHE for over 225 h in a neutral 0.5 m KPi electrolyte containing 0.5 m KI (pH 6.5) (Figure 7f). In contrast, an OER‐based anode experienced a rapid decline to 30% of its initial current density within 2 h and continued degrading over 12 h at the same potential. These results indicate that faster electrochemical reaction kinetics at the electrolyte/catalyst interface significantly enhance operational stability. The improved catalytic activity reduces charge accumulation caused by the temporal mismatch between picosecond‐scale photocarrier generation and microsecond‐ to second‐scale electrochemical reactions. Therefore, enhancing electrochemical reaction kinetics is essential for achieving long‐term stability in immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical systems.

Future Research Directions

Degradation Mechanism of Perovskite‐Based Electrochemical Devices

Despite extensive efforts involving encapsulation, interface modification, and catalyst engineering, the operational stability of perovskite‐based electrochemical devices remains significantly inferior to that of PSCs.[ 95 , 99 ] This disparity suggests that the degradation mechanisms in perovskite‐based electrochemical devices are fundamentally distinct and proceed more rapidly than those in PSCs. In PSCs, charge accumulation primarily results from the buildup of photogenerated carriers at the interface between the charge transport layer and the perovskite absorber.[ 100 ] By contrast, in perovskite‐based electrochemical devices, charge accumulation predominantly occurs at the catalyst/electrolyte interface, differing substantially in both timescale and spatial distribution.[ 31 , 98 ] These differences strongly imply that the degradation pathways in the two device types are markedly divergent. A comprehensive understanding of degradation in perovskite‐based electrochemical devices is essential to inform the rational design of systems that collectively suppress these processes and enhance device longevity.

To achieve this, in situ and operando characterization techniques are indispensable. While ex situ surface analyses can provide valuable insights, they require the removal of encapsulation layers, a process that can introduce mechanical and chemical damage, thereby compromising the validity of subsequent measurements.[ 101 ] Therefore, nondestructive approaches such as drive‐level capacitance profiling (DLCP) and in situ photoluminescence (PL) are necessary for real‐time monitoring of the device's internal state. Moreover, charge accumulation at the catalyst/electrolyte interface has emerged as a principal driver of degradation in perovskite‐based electrochemical devices. As a result, the perovskite layer beneath the catalyst deteriorates at a substantially different rate compared to the illuminated region, suggesting the occurrence of lateral degradation. Monitoring the lateral propagation of this degradation across the device is thus critical for a holistic understanding of the degradation mechanisms in perovskite‐based electrochemical systems.

Based on these insights, we successfully elucidated the degradation mechanism of perovskite‐based electrochemical cells.[ 102 ] Our findings reveal that perovskite‐based electrochemical anodes undergo a charge accumulation‐induced failure pathway, wherein charge buildup at the catalyst/electrolyte interface accelerates iodine migration toward the HTL/perovskite interface. This, in turn, initiates defect formation at the HTL/perovskite interface located directly beneath the electrochemically active region. Furthermore, iodine species that migrated toward the HTL/perovskite interface in the active region were found to propagate laterally, eventually affecting the illumination area. These results clearly demonstrate that perovskite‐based water‐splitting devices follow a distinct, electrochemically driven degradation mechanism, offering valuable guidance for the design of more robust perovskite‐based electrochemical systems. Furthermore, these findings highlight the increased importance of developing ion‐capping or protective interface treatments to effectively suppress iodine migration, beyond conventional interface engineering strategies. This underscores the need for targeted approaches specifically designed to address electrochemically driven instability in perovskite‐based systems.

Photothermal Effect

Addressing and monitoring the impact of charge accumulation during real‐time device operation is critical to maintaining the performance and stability of perovskite‐based electrochemical devices. One conventional approach involves increasing the temperature of the electrolyte; however, this method is inefficient due to the high specific heat capacity of water, which demands considerable energy input to produce meaningful thermal effects. Also, elevating the entire system can significantly compromise the stability of perovskite absorbers, especially in systems incorporating organic‐based charge transport layers. An alternative strategy, photothermal modulation via near‐infrared irradiation, offers localized heating specifically at the catalyst surface, thereby enhancing reaction kinetics without significant energy loss.[ 103 ] This targeted thermal activation can effectively mitigate charge accumulation within the device, contributing to improved operational stability. Furthermore, applying photothermal modulation to state‐of‐the‐art catalysts such as Pt or RuO2 can enhance electrochemical kinetics, resulting in additional gains in efficiency. Collectively, this approach presents a practical and energy‐efficient solution to the stability limitations inherent in perovskite‐based electrochemical systems. Photothermal modulation also enables dynamic tuning of reaction rates during operation, opening the possibility for real‐time feedback in adaptive electrochemical systems (i.e., operando analysis). By selectively adjusting the intensity, wavelength, or spatial focus of near‐infrared irradiation, localized thermal environments can be modulated on demand, allowing for precise control over electrochemical kinetics. This tunability offers a promising route toward monitoring device‐internal stability in response to varying kinetics of electrochemical reactions.

Furthermore, photothermal activation not only accelerates electrochemical reaction kinetics but also facilitates bubble detachment from the catalyst surface. Recent studies have demonstrated that impaired bubble release or accumulation on the catalyst leads to performance degradation and accelerates device failure.[ 104 ] By generating localized thermal gradients, photothermal heating induces Marangoni convection, which reduces bubble size and promotes their rapid detachment from the catalyst surface.[ 105 , 106 ] This dual functionality, enhancing reaction rates while actively managing gas bubble dynamics, represents a robust strategy for improving catalyst stability and overall device durability. When advanced photothermal modulation techniques are combined with optimized catalyst/electrolyte interfaces, perovskite‐based electrochemical devices are expected to exhibit prolonged operational lifetimes and enhanced performance.

From a practical standpoint, most photothermal systems require external laser sources or Fresnel lenses, which increase the overall balance‐of‐system costs at the industrial scale. This has often led to concerns regarding the practical feasibility of photothermal systems. However, recent studies have highlighted that designing the catalytic area to exceed the illumination area is necessary to avoid catalytic limitations during operation.[ 107 ] The integration of photothermal effects can efficiently address this challenge by not only allowing the use of smaller catalyst loadings but also maintaining efficient mass transport, without requiring an external convection devices (e.g., magnetic stirrers, rotating disk electrodes). Considering these factors, photothermal systems may offer practical and economic advantages at the industrial level, contrary to the common perception that they are inherently cost‐prohibitive.

Spin‐Dependent Electrochemistry

At the subatomic level, an emerging approach to further enhance the performance and stability of electrochemical devices involves manipulating the spin state of photogenerated charge carriers. Spin‐polarized carriers can influence the formation of reaction products and facilitate charge transfer during electrochemical processes, particularly in spin‐sensitive multi‐electron reactions such as the OER. To control the spin state of these carriers, magnetic fields can be integrated into electrochemical systems. Recent studies have shown that applying external magnetic fields significantly improves electrochemical reaction performance by promoting spin alignment of reaction intermediates, thereby lowering kinetic barriers.[ 108 , 109 , 110 ] In addition to external fields, magnetic materials, possessing intrinsic magnetic moments due to the spin and orbital angular momentum of electrons can also serve as spin‐polarizer. Materials such as ferromagnetic CoFe2O4 or paramagnetic NiFe‐based layered double hydroxides have demonstrated enhanced catalytic activity and stability under magnetic influence.[ 111 ] This area of research, known as spin‐dependent electrochemistry, has garnered increasing recent attention for its potential to enhance both the efficiency and stability of water‐splitting systems, particularly by exploiting the spin‐sensitive nature of multi‐electron electrochemical reactions.

Chiral‐Induced Spin Selectivity Effect

In 1999, Naaman and coworkers reported a novel strategy for spin control, known as the chirality‐induced spin selectivity (CISS) phenomenon.[ 112 ] The CISS effect is a spin polarization process that occurs when electrons traverse chiral materials such as helical molecules, or chiral metal–organic frameworks. As charge carriers move through these chiral structures, the inherent lack of inversion symmetry generates an effective spin–orbit coupling, which gives rise to a helical potential. This leads to a centripetal force perpendicular to the electron velocity, analogous to an internal magnetic field arising from the coupling between the spin of electron and its linear momentum.[ 113 ] The direction of this effective field and thus the resulting spin polarization is governed by the handedness of the chiral material and can be described using Fleming's left‐hand rule. Consequently, one spin orientation is energetically favored while the opposite is suppressed, leading to spin‐selective transmission. The favored spin orientation is governed by the chiral axis of the molecule or material, determined by its handedness and the linear momentum of the charge carriers.

Perovskite Water Splitting Devices Employing CISS Effect

Recently, chiral 2D perovskites have garnered significant attention in the field of spin‐dependent electrochemistry due to their exceptional spin‐selective properties.[ 114 ] These materials exhibit asymmetric lattice distortions within the inorganic framework, induced by chiral organic cations, which break inversion symmetry and lead to enhanced spin–orbit coupling in their electronic band structures.[ 115 , 116 ] This strong coupling facilitates efficient spin filtering of photogenerated carriers, enabling high degrees of spin polarization. As a result, chiral perovskites are emerging as promising candidates for next‐generation, spin‐controlled, solar‐driven water‐splitting systems. Incorporating a CISS‐active layer not only polarizes charge carriers but also suppresses undesired side reactions, such as H2O2 formation, thereby enhancing the stability. By mitigating photocorrosion and improving charge carrier lifetimes, such strategies pave the way for efficient and durable solar‐to‐hydrogen conversion platforms.

The OER, characterized by sluggish kinetics and high overpotentials due to its multistep four‐electron pathway, remains a key bottleneck in overall water‐splitting efficiency.[ 117 , 118 , 119 ] The CISS effect can enhance OER by polarizing spins of charge carriers to favor the formation of triplet O2 while suppressing diamagnetic H2O2 as a by‐product.[ 120 , 121 ] The spin‐sensitive nature of OER arises from a spin‐state transition involving a shift from the diamagnetic singlet reactant (H2O/OH−) to the paramagnetic triplet product (O2), in competition with an alternative pathway that yields diamagnetic H2O2 (Figure 8 ). CISS‐mediated spin polarization thus enables selective reaction pathways by reducing spin‐forbidden transitions and promoting favorable intermediate spin states. Based on this principle, Moon's group developed a dual spin‐control system integrating both chiral 2D and 3D perovskites, where the 3D perovskite serves as a broadband light absorber while a naphthyl ethylamine‐based chiral 2D perovskite layer functions as a spin polarizer (Figure 9a).[ 91 ] This dual spin‐control architecture achieved a high spin polarizability of 75%, effectively reducing charge recombination and promoting triplet oxygen evolution (Figure 9b). Notably, the Faradaic efficiencies for hydrogen and oxygen evolution increased from 91.40% to 98.78% following the introduction of the spin‐polarizing layer (Figure 9c), underscoring the potential of chiral perovskites in enhancing the performance of solar‐driven water‐splitting devices. Furthermore, a molecular cross‐linking strategy was recently employed to improve the structural stability and spin‐filtering ability of chiral 2D perovskites (Figure 9d).[ 95 ] By incorporating mixed chiral/achiral cation combinations, the resulting 2D perovskite system suppressed ion migration and sustained spin‐polarized charge transfer under operating conditions. This design led to exceptional operational durability, maintaining OER performance over 255 h (Figure 9e). This approach effectively suppressed ion migration and enhanced spin‐polarized charge transfer, both of which are critical for achieving durable and efficient perovskite‐based OER systems.

Nevertheless, a key unresolved question concerns the transport of spin‐polarized carriers from the chiral perovskite layer to the catalyst interface. While the CISS effect can generate significant spin polarization within the perovskite film, it remains unclear how effectively this polarization is preserved across multiple junctions, including the electron transport layers, electrolyte interface, and the catalyst itself. Spin decoherence and scattering events may attenuate the spin polarization before reaching the catalytic active site, thereby limiting the practical impact of CISS‐driven selectivity. Recent studies have shown that directly inducing chirality within the catalyst layer can also achieve spin‐selective OER enhancement, suggesting that local spin polarization at the reaction site may be more robust.[ 91 , 122 , 123 ] Thus, future research should not only focus on optimizing spin generation within the light absorber but also address spin transport mechanisms and explore direct integration of chiral features into catalytic layers for more effective spin control.

All‐Inorganic Perovskites

Developing robust perovskite absorber layers with high resistance to ion migration and phase segregation under charge accumulation conditions represents a transformative strategy for significantly enhancing overall device stability. In this regard, all‐inorganic perovskites containing a single Cs+ A‐site cation offer markedly superior photophysical and chemical stability, making them strong candidates for the absorber layer in water‐splitting cells.[ 124 ] Unlike the vulnerable organic components in conventional perovskites, which readily decompose under external stimuli, inorganic cations are less reactive and help preserve structural integrity under stressed conditions.[ 125 ] Indeed, prior studies have employed all‐inorganic CsPbBr3 as an absorber layer for water‐splitting anodes, demonstrating improved stability.[ 24 , 99 ] However, CsPbBr3 is not considered an optimal absorber due to its wide bandgap, which limits performance, and its requirement for high‐temperature annealing.[ 126 ]

CsPbI3 has emerged as a highly promising material for water‐splitting devices, as CsPbI3‐based PSCs have achieved a PCE exceeding 21%, the highest reported among all‐inorganic perovskite solar cells.[ 127 ] This exceptional performance is attributed to its optimal bandgap of ≈1.68–1.73 eV, which is well‐suited for efficient solar energy harvesting.[ 128 ] In addition, CsPbI3 requires a relatively low annealing temperature of around 200 °C, offering a significant advantage over other all‐inorganic perovskites that typically demand higher processing temperatures.[ 129 ] The use of a single halide composition also mitigates phase segregation issues, thereby enhancing its suitability for stable device operation.[ 130 ] Despite these advantages, CsPbI3 suffers from poor phase stability at ambient temperature.[ 126 , 130 ] This instability stems from the small ionic radius of the Cs+ cation, which introduces micro‐strain within the perovskite lattice. This strain promotes tilting of the corner‐sharing octahedra in the black phase toward an edge‐sharing configuration characteristic of the non‐photoactive yellow phase. Because the yellow phase lacks photovoltaic functionality, maintaining the black phase is critical to ensuring long‐term operational stability in CsPbI3‐based devices.[ 131 ] To overcome this limitation, strain engineering strategies have been developed to enhance the phase stability of CsPbI3.[ 132 , 133 ] By targeting lattice micro‐strain with suitable passivators, octahedral tilting is suppressed, thereby inhibiting the phase transition and stabilizing the photoactive black phase. It is important to note that strain engineering in CsPbI3 differs from that applied to conventional perovskite PSCs. In traditional systems, strain engineering primarily addresses tensile and compressive strains introduced during cooling or under thermal stress due to mismatches in thermal expansion coefficients between the perovskite and the charge transport layer.[ 72 , 74 ] In contrast, CsPbI3 requires consideration of both conventional thermal strain and internal micro‐strain arising from cation size mismatch. Thus, the efficacy of strain engineering in CsPbI3 must be critically assessed by accounting for these distinct sources of strain. Incorporating strain‐engineered CsPbI3 as the absorber material holds significant potential to accelerate the development of durable, high‐efficiency perovskite‐based electrochemical devices.

The CsPbI x Br3− x composition, which partially incorporates bromine, also holds potential as an absorber material for immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells. Partial substitution of iodide with bromide can significantly enhance the phase stability compared to CsPbI3, potentially enabling more stable operation under electrochemical conditions. However, CsPbI x Br3− x also presents challenges, most notably severe halide segregation.[ 134 ] This issue becomes particularly pronounced under operation in electrochemical operations, where charge accumulation can exacerbate halide migration. To mitigate this, strategies such as A‐site cation doping with Rb⁺ and the incorporation of halide migration inhibitors such as ionic liquids may be required.[ 134 , 135 , 136 ] If such conditions are met, CsPbI x Br3− x could serve as a highly promising absorber candidate for advancing the performance and durability of immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical systems.

Conclusion and Outlook

Solar energy‐driven technologies for hydrogen production via water splitting have been extensively investigated, as they offer clean and cost‐effective alternatives to conventional fossil fuel‐based hydrogen generation. To accelerate the deployment of solar hydrogen production, this review comprehensively examines halide perovskites as absorber materials for stable solar water‐splitting devices, emphasizing their underlying principles, key challenges, and future potential. The theoretical background and working mechanisms of halide perovskite‐based water‐splitting systems are discussed, with a focus on their exceptional optoelectronic properties. As efficient light absorbers, halide perovskites are promising candidates for PEC applications; however, their inherent instability under environmental conditions remains a critical limitation. To address this issue, various encapsulation strategies aimed at enhancing stability and extending device lifespan are thoroughly examined. Nevertheless, ambiguity persists in the classification of perovskite‐based photoelectrodes due to their encapsulated architecture, raising questions about whether they conform to the definition of a classical PEC cell. To resolve this, the review introduces the term immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical cells (or devices), which more accurately reflects their hybrid nature. Recent advancements aimed at improving stability are also explored, including strategies to enhance perovskite film quality, engineer interfacial properties, and mitigate charge accumulation. In addition, emerging concepts such as spin‐dependent electrochemistry, which exploits spin‐selective transport properties; the photothermal effect, which enhances reaction kinetics and bubble dynamics; and the development of all‐inorganic perovskites with superior thermal and chemical resistance, are elaborated.

Enhancing the stability of perovskite materials is particularly vital for the commercialization of photoelectrochemical applications, as current materials remain insufficiently stable for long‐term operation. Beyond the adoption of all‐inorganic perovskites, the chemical composition, dimensionality, and crystallinity of the perovskite absorber significantly influence the operational stability of water‐splitting devices. Moreover, stability strategies involving refinement of device architecture and the development of innovative encapsulation techniques warrant further investigation. Understanding degradation mechanisms under various external stimuli, insights already well established in the perovskite photovoltaic field, including theoretical modeling, can aid in achieving more durable immersed perovskite‐based water‐splitting devices. In parallel with stability improvements, mitigating the toxicity of lead is essential to reduce environmental impact. Strategies such as minimizing toxic components, employing ion doping, and constructing heterojunction architectures are expected to play key roles in advancing environmentally friendly perovskite‐based water‐splitting technologies. Overall, halide perovskites align well with the goals of sustainable water splitting due to their high solar‐to‐hydrogen conversion efficiencies, low‐temperature and low‐cost fabrication processes. Such attributes can highly facilitate the development of scalable, efficient, and eco‐friendly hydrogen production systems powered by sunlight.

For the practical commercialization of immersed perovskite‐based electrochemical devices, it is essential to conduct comprehensive studies in real‐world operating conditions and large‐area scalability. While several recent reports have demonstrated device operation under realistic conditions with enlarged active areas, the current level of development remains insufficient for practical commercialization.[ 33 , 96 , 137 , 138 ] These pioneering studies underscore the pressing need to address not only performance and operational durability, but also system‐level integration and scalability—key challenges that must be overcome to achieve commercial viability. Furthermore, high‐performance immersed perovskite‐based water‐splitting devices are anticipated to pave the way for emerging fields, given the wide applicability of electrochemical cells. Advances in halide perovskite materials and encapsulation techniques have laid a solid foundation for the development of stable solar water‐splitting systems, supporting a broad range of applications in solar energy conversion. As previously discussed, the potential of (photo)electrochemical systems extends beyond hydrogen generation via water splitting. In addition to solar‐driven water splitting and CO2 reduction, the synthesis of value‐added chemicals—including oxidants, biomass‐derived compounds, and complex organic molecules such as H2S2O8 and 2,5‐furandicarboxylic acid—demonstrates the system's versatility. Despite the significant progress made in immersed perovskite‐based water‐splitting devices, several scientific and commercial challenges remain, including cost, scalability, and toxicity. Addressing these challenges will be essential for realizing sustainable, large‐scale deployment of halide perovskite‐based systems in future green hydrogen economies.
