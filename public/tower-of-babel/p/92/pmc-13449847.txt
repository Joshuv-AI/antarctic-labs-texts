The central role of hyperandrogenism in driving metabolic dysfunction in polycystic ovary syndrome: from pathophysiological mechanisms to targeted therapies
Authors: Jinghui Xu, Bing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome is a prevalent endocrine disorder wherein hyperandrogenism plays a central pathogenic role that extends beyond its reproductive manifestations. This review systematically elucidates the pathophysiological mechanisms through which hyperandrogenism, acting in a vicious cycle with insulin resistance, drives systemic metabolic dysfunction. We synthesize evidence demonstrating that hyperandrogenism promotes organ-specific lipotoxicity and insulin resistance. This contributes to visceral adiposity, hepatic steatosis (and its progression to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease), and skeletal muscle dysfunction. Furthermore, we explore the detrimental effects of hyperandrogenism on cardiovascular integrity and gut microbial homeostasis. Based on this mechanistic framework, we evaluate current therapeutic strategies, drawing a key distinction between metabolic-focused interventions (e.g., lifestyle modifications, insulin-sensitizing agents) that disrupt the hyperandrogenism-insulin resistance cycle, and androgen-lowering therapies (e.g., combined oral contraceptives) that primarily alleviate symptoms with limited metabolic benefits. We conclude that redefining hyperandrogenism as a primary metabolic driver is crucial for developing targeted strategies that mitigate the long-term metabolic risks associated with polycystic ovary syndrome.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-age women, with a prevalence of 6% to 21% globally, varying by region and diagnostic criteria [1, 2]. This complex and heterogeneous condition manifests through Hyperandrogenism (HA), ovarian dysfunction, and Polycystic Ovarian Morphology (PCOM). To further characterize the condition, researchers have identified four distinct PCOS phenotypes: (A) HA, oligoovulation/anovulation (OA), and PCOM; (B) HA and OA; (C) HA and PCOM; and (D) OA and PCOM [3]. Notably, recent clinical evidence highlights a distinct metabolic disparity between these groups. Specifically, Phenotypes A, B, and C—which represent the hyperandrogenic spectrum—demonstrate a significantly more adverse metabolic profile than the non-hyperandrogenic Phenotype D [4].

Reproductively, PCOS is characterized by imbalanced luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone (LH/FSH) ratios, androgen excess, aberrant follicular development, and abnormal ovulation, leading to reduced fertility and an elevated miscarriage risk [5]. However, the metabolic implications are increasingly recognized as the primary determinants of long-term health. PCOS is often associated with obesity, Metabolic Syndrome (MS), Insulin Resistance (IR), Hyperinsulinemia (HI), hepatic steatosis, and dyslipidemia, heightening the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [6].

PCOS pathogenesis centers on two distinct yet synergistic features: HA and IR. Extensive evidence underscores that HA is a driver of metabolic dysregulation, not merely a byproduct of reproductive dysfunction [7]. HA is present in approximately 60–80% of women with PCOS, whereas IR occurs in 65–70%, often independent of obesity [8, 9]. First, compensatory HI resulting from IR directly stimulates ovarian and adrenal androgen production while suppressing hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis, consequently increasing free androgen levels [10]. Conversely, excess androgens impair insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues. Studies show that HA disrupts insulin signaling in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue (AT), promotes visceral fat accumulation, and induces chronic low-grade inflammation [11].

Chronic exposure to this metabolic-endocrine environment disrupts homeostasis in key metabolic organs. Specifically, HA contributes to AT dysfunction, promotes hepatic lipid accumulation (potentially progressing to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, MASLD), and can impair pancreatic β-cell function over time through lipotoxicity and glucotoxicity [12]. Consequently, women with PCOS have a significantly elevated risk of developing T2DM and CVD compared with age-matched controls, highlighting the need to recognize HA as a key metabolic driver beyond its reproductive manifestations [13].

While HA and IR form a bidirectional, self-perpetuating feedback loop, this review posits that HA serves as a primary pathophysiological driver of metabolic dysfunction, rather than a secondary consequence of reproductive abnormalities. This is particularly evident in hyperandrogenic phenotypes (e.g., Phenotypes A, B, and C), where androgen excess acts as a pathogenic catalyst, driving a more severe metabolic trajectory compared to the non-hyperandrogenic Phenotype D. By disrupting insulin signaling pathways and altering AT architecture, HA transforms IR from a manageable metabolic state into a state of systemic dysregulation. Consequently, HA is not merely a hallmark of reproductive dysfunction but also a critical determinant of the metabolic severity of PCOS.

This review therefore aims to systematically examine the pathophysiological effects of HA on key metabolic organs—including AT, liver, skeletal muscle, and pancreas—and its interplay with IR. Based on this mechanistic foundation, we will critically evaluate current therapeutic strategies, with a specific focus on a metabolic-first approach that disrupts this vicious cycle to mitigate the long-term metabolic risks.

Profile of androgens in women

In reproductive-age women, the principal androgens—dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), androstenedione (A4), testosterone (T), and dihydrotestosterone (DHT)—are primarily synthesized in ovarian theca cells and the adrenal zona reticularis [14]. Within the circulation, the majority of testosterone is bound to SHBG and albumin. Only the small fraction of free testosterone (FT) (< 1%) is biologically active and capable of activating intracellular androgen receptors (ARs) [15]. Pathogenesis of hyperandrogenism in polycystic ovary syndrome

The etiology of HA in PCOS is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay between intrinsic ovarian dysfunction, neuroendocrine defects, and metabolic aberrations. Among these, the IR is not merely a comorbid condition but a central pathogenic driver of HA in PCOS [10, 17]. HI exacerbates HA via two primary mechanisms. First, through a direct pathway in the ovary, insulin functions as a gonadotropin-like hormone. It binds to insulin receptors or insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptors on theca cells. There, it synergizes with luteinizing hormone (LH) to enhance steroidogenic enzyme activity and directly stimulate androgen synthesis. Similarly, insulin enhances adrenal androgen secretion. Second, via an indirect pathway in the liver, HI suppresses SHBG synthesis. This reduction in SHBG binding capacity disproportionately increases bioactive FT [18, 19]. Ultimately, IR acts synergistically with neuroendocrine defects described below, creating a self-perpetuating vicious cycle.

Complementing this metabolic dysfunction is a profound neuroendocrine dysregulation, primarily presenting as a persistently elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) ratio. Emerging evidence identifies the hypothalamic neuropeptide kisspeptin as a key mediator of this imbalance. Elevated kisspeptin levels in women with PCOS increase gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse frequency [20, 21]. This rapid GnRH pulsatility preferentially stimulates LH over FSH synthesis and secretion. Excess LH then stimulates ovarian theca cells, promoting excessive androgen production [22]. Excessive androgenic activity disrupts negative feedback mechanisms, perpetuating chronic anovulation and androgen excess [23, 24]. While elevated kisspeptin and neurokinin B (NKB) signaling within the arcuate nucleus are key mediators of this imbalance, HA acts as a critical feedback agent that entrenches this dysfunction. Chronic androgen excess desensitizes the GnRH pulse generator to progesterone-mediated negative feedback, thereby sustaining elevated GnRH and LH secretion. Furthermore, elevated levels of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) — which correlate positively with both LH pulse frequency and testosterone concentrations—may act as an additional central stimulator of GnRH activity [25].

Beyond these upstream stimulatory factors, intrinsic abnormalities in steroid synthesis within the steroidogenic machinery are key contributors to HA. Studies consistently show enhanced CYP17A1 activity—the rate-limiting enzyme in androgen biosynthesis—in both ovarian theca cells and adrenal tissue of women with PCOS. Follicular dysfunction further compounds this cycle. Specifically, follicular arrest leads to the accumulation of small antral follicles. These follicles exhibit premature luteinization and heightened sensitivity to LH [26, 27]. Additionally, adrenal dysfunction plays a distinct role, characterized by the overproduction of 11-oxygenated androgens [28, 29]. These adrenal-derived androgens are often elevated in PCOS, exhibit testosterone-comparable bioactivity, and disrupt hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis feedback regulation [30].

The variability and co-existence of these metabolic, neuroendocrine, and enzymatic aberrations underscore that HA is rooted in a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. Although genetic studies show significant familial clustering of HA, suggesting a heritable predisposition to these defects, environmental factors—especially obesity—strongly modulate the phenotype [31, 32]. Studies show significantly elevated androgen levels in obese versus lean women with PCOS, even after adjusting for insulin sensitivity [33, 34]. This suggests that excess AT actively modulates androgen metabolism, exacerbating PCOS severity.

Impact of hyperandrogenism on metabolic organs and systems

Core metabolic organs

Adipose tissue

AT comprises white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT). WAT, the predominant form, stores energy, whereas BAT generates heat via thermogenesis [35]. While WAT functions as a site for extra-gonadal androgen bioconversion, it is also a critical target for androgenic action. HA fundamentally alters AT distribution and function, driving a transition towards a metabolically malignant phenotype. First, HA induces a pathogenic shift in fat distribution. Fat distribution patterns vary between genders, influenced significantly by androgen levels. In men, fat is predominantly distributed in the abdomen and viscera, characterized by a higher proportion of visceral fat and a lower proportion of subcutaneous fat. This 'masculinization' of AT distribution—characterized by a shift from gynoid to android fat mass—is a hallmark of the hyperandrogenic spectrum (Phenotypes A–C) [36]. Elevated serum levels of T and A4 are associated with an increased android/gynoid fat mass ratio in women with PCOS, indicating a link between androgen levels and AT distribution [37]. The study by Mansour et al. further highlights the significant relationship between androgens and fat distribution. They observed that elevated androgen levels in women with PCOS were positively associated with specific obesity indicators, such as waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio, which effectively reflect central fat distribution.

Androgens also modulate AT functionality by affecting adipocyte size and gene expression, inhibiting the proliferation and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and preadipocytes. This inhibition prevents AT expansion through cell proliferation, leading to compensatory adipocyte hypertrophy and subsequent AT dysfunction [36]. Specifically, adipose hyperplasia—the recruitment of new, small, insulin-sensitive adipocytes—is a healthy mechanism for energy storage. In contrast, androgen-driven inhibition of this process forces AT to rely on pathological hypertrophy. This hypertrophic expansion increases mechanical strain, restricts oxygen diffusion, and ultimately triggers local hypoxia and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [38]. Consequently, the shift from healthy hyperplasia to maladaptive hypertrophy is a key driver of systemic IR and metabolic dysregulation in hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes. In PCOS sheep models, prenatal androgen exposure impairs the differentiation of adipose precursor cells, leading to compensatory hypertrophy of adipocytes in the subcutaneous AT of adult PCOS sheep. This is accompanied by increased expression of pro-inflammatory factors including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)​ and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which may exacerbate IR [39]. Additionally, prenatal androgen exposure suppresses mitochondria-related gene expression such as mitochondrial translation elongation factor Ts, leading to reduced energy metabolism. In visceral AT, this manifests as an imbalance between fat storage and lipolysis, further exacerbating metabolic disorders [40].

Further, AT in women with PCOS exhibits pronounced IR. It also shows altered gene expression in abdominal subcutaneous adipose stem cells (ASCs). These changes are positively correlated with serum androgen levels. This correlation suggests a significant impact of androgens on AT insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism during ASC maturation in vitro [41]. However, a significant translational gap persists. It remains unclear whether the primary metabolic defect in humans originates from intrinsic 'programming,' as demonstrated in ovine models. Alternatively, it may constitute a secondary response to the chronic systemic inflammation that characterizes human PCOS.

Regarding BAT, androgen levels inversely affect its activity, as evidenced by decreased skin temperatures in the supraclavicular region, indicative of reduced BAT thermogenesis in women with PCOS. This reduction may stem from androgen-mediated impairment of lipolysis and the downregulation of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) mRNA transcription. Given that UCP1 is a key mediator of thermogenesis, its suppression—combined with altered sympathetic nervous system activity—may exacerbate obesity and metabolic disorders [42].

Overall, a markedly dysfunctional adipose phenotype—characterized by visceral fat accumulation, adipocyte hypertrophy, and reduced BAT activity—is most pronounced in hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes (A–C) [38]. In contrast, AT changes in the non‑hyperandrogenic Phenotype D are less pronounced and generally retain greater responsiveness to conventional metabolic therapies.

Androgens critically influence the distribution and functionality of AT in women with PCOS, driving visceral lipid accumulation, impairing BAT thermogenesis, and disrupting adipocyte differentiation and metabolic homeostasis. These androgen-induced alterations exacerbate IR and metabolic dysfunction. Elucidating the molecular pathways underlying these effects, particularly those involving mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and lipid dysregulation, represents a key avenue for future research. Therapeutic strategies targeting these mechanisms hold promise for mitigating metabolic complications in PCOS.

Liver

The liver, as the body’s central metabolic hub, processes all nutrients absorbed from the intestinal tract, rendering it a focal point in the study of metabolic diseases. Androgens exacerbate hepatic triglyceride accumulation by downregulating microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP) expression in liver, leading to aggravated hepatic steatosis and damage [43]. In sheep models of PCOS, researchers induced metabolic abnormalities through prenatal androgen exposure. The experimental group exhibited significant hepatic cholesterol and triglyceride accumulation alongside mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced oxidative phosphorylation and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity. Androgen exposure upregulates the expression of low-density lipoprotein receptor-related proteins, enhancing hepatic cholesterol uptake [44]. Concurrently, it reduces the expression of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase, a key enzyme in bile acid synthesis, thereby limiting cholesterol conversion to bile acids and exacerbating hepatic cholesterol accumulation. Additionally, androgens induce hepatic steatosis both in vivo and in vitro by inhibiting the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) α signaling pathway [45]. Moreover, hepatic metabolic dysfunction creates a vicious cycle: hepatic steatosis and local IR suppress the synthesis of SHBG. This reduction in SHBG further elevates bioavailable free androgens, aggravating the systemic hyperandrogenic state. While animal models provide detailed molecular explanations—such as the specific inhibition of MTTP and AMPKα—these pathways have not been directly mapped in human liver tissues due to the practical and ethical limitations of obtaining liver biopsies in this population.

MASLD is a typical metabolic complication of PCOS. It is characterized by varying degrees of hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and hepatocellular injury. In advanced stages, it has the potential to progress to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [46, 47]. The prevalence of MASLD in PCOS is significantly higher than in the general population, with women with PCOS being four times more likely to develop MASLD even after adjusting for obesity and diabetes [48]. HA and IR are not only central to PCOS but also serve as independent predictors of MASLD in women with PCOS [49]. FT and free androgen index (FAI) correlate strongly with MASLD incidence and demonstrate a more pronounced association between high androgen levels and MASLD, especially in PCOS patients with elevated obesity or IR levels [50].

Importantly, the risk of liver metabolic disease​is not uniform across the PCOS spectrum [51, 52]. Clinical evidence suggests that individuals within the hyperandrogenic phenotypes are at a significantly higher risk for hepatic steatosis and progression to fibrosis compared to those with the non-hyperandrogenic Phenotype D. This highlights that HA acts as a potent and independent driver of hepatic lipid accumulation, extending beyond the effects of BMI [53].

Recent studies—primarily based on mouse models—suggest an additional mechanism. Beyond direct effects on hepatic AR, androgens can also regulate hepatic gene expression. They disrupt the liver's circadian timing system through epigenetic modifications. These changes exacerbate lipid accumulation and inflammation, promoting MASLD progression [54]. Adolescents with PCOS and higher FAI experience more severe MASLD compared to those with lower FAI [55]. Consequently, screening for MASLD in adolescents with PCOS, particularly those with elevated androgen levels, is recommended.

Although MASLD is highly prevalent in patients with PCOS, it remains frequently under-screened in clinical practice. Current international guidelines utilize Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) as the primary screening biomarker, while abdominal ultrasonography is reserved for diagnosis rather than routine screening [56]. However, this ALT-centric strategy may fail to identify individuals with "normal-ALT" steatosis. Under-screening is particularly pronounced in non-tertiary care settings. In these environments, clinical priorities often favor reproductive symptoms, and advanced imaging—such as FibroScan or Magnetic Resonance Imaging Proton Density Fat Fraction (MRI-PDFF)—is frequently inaccessible [57]. Given the elevated risk of MASLD in this population, liver ultrasound should be integrated into routine metabolic assessments for high-risk women with PCOS. Furthermore, incorporating non-invasive scoring systems like the Fatty Liver Index (FLI) or Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index into standard PCOS care is essential for early risk mitigation [58].

HA exerts a multifaceted impact on liver function in PCOS, promoting hepatic steatosis and advancing MASLD progression. These effects, mediated through direct AR activation, metabolic pathway disruption, and epigenetic modifications, underscore the complex interplay between hormonal imbalances and metabolic liver dysfunction.

Skeletal muscle

Although circulating androgen levels are lower in females than in males, they play a significant role in regulating skeletal muscle function [59]. Their effects are mediated primarily through binding to the ARs. Androgens exert anabolic effects on skeletal muscle; however, these effects are not directly mediated through myofibers. Instead, ARs act primarily in non-myofiber cells, such as mesenchymal progenitors, to regulate skeletal muscle quality rather than mass [60]. Through this mechanism, androgens promote protein synthesis, enhance muscle strength, and support muscle repair and glucose metabolism, thereby influencing insulin sensitivity and overall muscle function [61–63].

In women with PCOS, HA has been shown to impact skeletal muscle characteristics. Studies report that women with PCOS exhibit greater muscle strength and enhanced lower limb explosive force and power compared to women without PCOS, irrespective of body composition [61, 64]. This suggests a potential anabolic effect of androgens on muscle in PCOS, potentially mediated by increased muscle fiber cross-sectional area and oxidative capacity, as observed in androgenized rodent models [65].

However, this structural advantage is compromised by profound skeletal muscle IR. Proteomic studies have revealed upregulation of Perilipin-1, indicative of increased intramuscular fat deposition, and downregulation of type I muscle fiber proteins, which are essential for oxidative metabolism and insulin sensitivity [66]. This shift towards lipotoxicity and diminished oxidative capacity may exacerbate IR.

While androgen excess in hyperandrogenic phenotypes may confer quantitative advantages in muscle mass and strength, it simultaneously impairs muscle metabolic quality. This impairment is characterized by a reduced proportion of oxidative fibers and increased intramyocellular lipid deposition, which are hallmarks of hyperandrogenic phenotypes [66]. These findings suggest that androgen excess significantly modulates the metabolic profile of skeletal muscle in hyperandrogenic PCOS, potentially aggravating the IR that might otherwise be more manageable in non-hyperandrogenic phenotypes. Further research found notable differences in skeletal muscle function and metabolic characteristics between obese and non-obese women with PCOS. Women with PCOS and obesity exhibited higher skeletal muscle mass, primarily due to their increased lean body mass. However, this increased muscle mass did not translate into metabolic improvements. Instead, IR in women with obesity was primarily driven by chronic inflammation and lipid metabolism disturbances originating from AT. In contrast, non-obese women with PCOS displayed skeletal muscle metabolic deficits, which, despite lower muscle mass, were mainly linked to impaired insulin signaling pathways and increased muscle lipid accumulation [67, 68].

Pancreas

Pancreatic β-cells express ARs, which directly regulate insulin secretion. In early PCOS, androgens induce compensatory β-cell hyperfunction to counteract systemic IR. Testosterone binding to β-cell ARs amplifies the insulinotropic effect of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), thereby enhancing insulin secretion [69].

However, this androgen-driven hyperfunction becomes maladaptive over time. Chronic ARs overstimulation, combined with metabolic stress, leads to β-cell exhaustion and dysfunction [17]. This pancreatic dysregulation represents a critical node in the bidirectional vicious cycle between HA and IR. Although IR is a well-established driver of HA—by stimulating ovarian androgen synthesis and suppressing hepatic SHBG production—HA also reciprocally exacerbates IR.

Elevated androgens directly impair insulin signaling in peripheral tissues by disrupting the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathway, which is critical for glucose uptake [70, 71]. This signaling impairment is exacerbated by androgen-induced visceral adiposity and inflammation. Consequently, a pathological feedback loop is established. Androgen excess impairs insulin sensitivity by disrupting the PI3K/Akt pathway, prompting compensatory HI. This HI, in turn, stimulates further ovarian androgen production, ultimately driving progression toward metabolic decompensation and T2DM.

Metabolism-related systems and tissues

Cardiovascular system

Androgens, particularly T, exert dose-dependent effects on vascular smooth muscle and endothelium. At physiological levels, T facilitates vasodilation, predominantly by stimulating nitric oxide (NO) production and modulating potassium and calcium channels [72]. However, in excess, androgens may adversely impact the cardiovascular system. Women with PCOS, particularly those with elevated androgen levels, are at a significantly higher risk of developing CVDs, including Subclinical Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (SCAD), compared to their non-PCOS counterparts [73].

Experimental studies by Horton et al. demonstrated that DHT contributes to cardiovascular risk factors. It does so by downregulating G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) and estrogen receptor α (ERα) in vascular smooth muscle cells. This promotes atherosclerosis in female mice. Histological analysis further revealed reduced aortic vascular collagen content in high-dose DHT-treated groups, indicating structural impacts on cardiovascular tissues and heightened disease risk [74]. Interestingly, in the hyperandrogenic model, collagen content in the aorta decreased, whereas fibrosis in the liver was significantly exacerbated. Androgens impair vascular integrity, making blood vessels more prone to structural damage by suppressing reparative signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells and intensifying OS. In the liver, androgens promote fibrosis progression by amplifying OS, inflammatory pathways, and the epigenetic regulation of fibrosis-associated genes. This phenomenon may result from distinct cellular responses to androgens: vascular smooth muscle cells primarily exhibit functional changes, such as relaxation and contraction, while activated hepatic stellate cells secrete large amounts of extracellular matrix proteins, driving tissue-specific fibrosis [46, 74]. Conversely, a study on T-induced hyperandrogenic PCOS rats showed increased cardiovascular fragility, characterized by reduced vascular elasticity, compromised structural integrity, and heightened susceptibility to stress-induced damage. However, unlike previous studies, this research found no alteration in ER density on vascular smooth muscle, suggesting alternative mechanisms by which androgens may affect cardiovascular function [75].

In Phenotypes A—C, chronic androgen excess represents a crucial cardiovascular stressor. Data from rodent models demonstrate that T induces cardiac hypertrophy and hyperdynamic function, primarily mediated by an imbalance in OS [76]. Furthermore, DHT promotes arterial stiffening by downregulating GPER and ERα expression in vascular smooth muscle cells. This action diminishes the vasculature's estrogenic protection, an effect that occurs independently of changes in blood pressure. Therefore, androgenic status—rather than ovarian dysfunction alone—is an important determinant of vascular morbidity across the PCOS spectrum [74].

Further analysis revealed distinct cardiovascular effects between obese and non-obese women with PCOS. In obesewomen with PCOS, cardiovascular risk was primarily linked to IR and chronic inflammation, evidenced by increased Carotid Intima-Media Thickness, indicating a heightened risk of atherosclerosis. Conversely, in non-obese women with PCOS, cardiovascular risk was predominantly driven by elevated androgen levels. High FT levels in non-obese women with PCOS were significantly associated with Cardiotrophin-1, a marker of impaired myocardial function. This association was independent of BMI, highlighting androgens as critical determinants of cardiovascular risk in non-obese women with PCOS [77].

Gut microbiota

The gut microbiota is a vast and complex microbial ecosystem within the human body. Under homeostatic conditions, it coexists harmoniously with the host and participates in various physiological processes, including nutrient digestion and absorption, energy metabolism, and immune regulation [78]. Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota dysbiosis is a key pathophysiological feature of PCOS, characterized by a close interaction between HA and the gut microbiota [79, 80].

In DHEA-induced rat models of PCOS, Han et al. reported a significant reduction in the phylogenetic diversity of the gut microbiota, indicating decreased α-diversity. At the genus level, the relative abundances of Turicibacter, Anaerofustis, and Clostridium sensu stricto were significantly decreased, suggesting that HA directly induces structural dysbiosis of the gut microbiota [81]. However, the response of the gut microbiota to androgens can vary depending on the experimental model.

The mechanisms through which androgens influence the host via the microbiota involve a complex "microbiota-metabolite-host" axis [82]. Androgen-associated dysbiosis can compromise intestinal barrier integrity, resulting in a "leaky gut" that permits the translocation of endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into the bloodstream. This process triggers systemic, chronic low-grade inflammation. This inflammatory state is a key driver of IR, which subsequently stimulates increased androgen production by the ovaries and adrenal glands, thereby creating a vicious cycle [83].

Additionally, HA disrupts key metabolic signaling molecules, including Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) and Bile Acids (BAs) [84]. HA is associated with a depletion of SCFA-producing genera (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Bifidobacterium); the resulting loss of butyrate compromises gut barrier integrity and blunts GLP-1 secretion, thereby exacerbating IR [85]. Therefore, interventions targeting the gut microbiota, such as probiotics or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), represent promising novel therapeutic strategies for breaking this vicious cycle.

However, the causal relationship between HA and gut dysbiosis should be interpreted cautiously. Studies show significant heterogeneity in sequencing methodologies, leading to inconsistent microbial profiles. Furthermore, the gut microbiota is highly susceptible to key environmental confounders, particularly dietary patterns and geographic location. These factors are often inadequately controlled for in clinical cohorts. Critically, since most current human evidence is cross-sectional, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether dysbiosis drives or results from the hyperandrogenic state in PCOS.

Ovary and endometrium

The ovary is both a primary driver and a metabolic target in the PCOS vicious cycle. Within ovarian theca cells, HI synergizes with LH to augment the expression of steroidogenic enzymes, directly fueling systemic HA. Beyond its role in follicular arrest, HA creates a state of local lipotoxicity and oxidative stress (OS) within the ovarian microenvironment [86]. Recent research posits that HA increases polyol pathway activity in ovarian tissues, leading to an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that triggers apoptosis in granulosa cells and oocytes [87]. This process is further exacerbated by obesity-induced inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) in the follicular fluid, which disrupts the follicular environment and accelerates atresia [88].

PCOS-related endometrial changes manifest as thickening, menstrual irregularities, and implantation failures, attributable to cellular-level hormonal responsiveness alterations, predominantly aberrant responses to androgens and insulin. These changes are intimately linked to endocrine and metabolic imbalances, culminating in impaired proliferation and differentiation of endometrial cells. This impairment adversely affects endometrial renewal and functionality, consequently impacting endometrial receptivity and the implantation process, leading to infertility [89–91]. The evolving impact of HA across the life-course

The metabolic consequences of HA in PCOS do not remain static; instead, they evolve as a dynamic continuum throughout the female lifespan. During adolescence, PCOS can be conceptualized as a syndrome of postpubertal central obesity. Ectopic fat deposition—particularly in visceral and hepatic depots—triggers HI. This HI, in turn, drives HA as a secondary consequence [93]. During this critical window, androgens such as DHEAS amplify metabolic distress. This is evidenced by a strong correlation with a reduced Adiponectin/Leptin (A/L) Ratio—a pivotal biomarker of AT dysfunction. This biomarker emerges in mid-puberty and predicts heightened PCOS risk [94].

Throughout the reproductive years, the HA-IR vicious cycle peaks. During this phase, HA transitions from a biomarker to a driver of multi-organ dysfunction, encompassing conditions such as MASLD and skeletal muscle IR. In the post-reproductive phase, although the risk for metabolic diseases such as T2DM and hypertension becomes increasingly dependent on obesity, HA persists as a defining endocrine feature. This persistence is characterized by elevated FAI and T levels [13].

Notably, even after adjusting for BMI, postmenopausal women with PCOS exhibit significantly lower levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). This finding suggests that the persistent hyperandrogenic state​ of PCOS exerts a direct and independent negative impact on cardiovascular health well beyond the reproductive years [13]. However, studies on how HA affects metabolism in postmenopausal women with PCOS are still limited. There is a lack of systematic research focused on this specific stage to clearly define its metabolic impact.

Treatment of hyperandrogenism in polycystic ovary syndrome

Therapies with significant metabolic improvements

Lifestyle interventions: the cornerstone

Current guidelines endorse lifestyle modifications as the foundational treatment strategy for women with PCOS, advocating for the adoption of a healthy lifestyle, encompassing a balanced diet and regular physical activity [56]. Specifically, a diet comprising 40% carbohydrates, 30% fats, and 30% proteins is recommended. It is advised to minimize processed carbohydrates and saturated fats. At the same time, increasing the intake of high-protein, fiber-rich foods, and polyunsaturated fatty acids—notably omega-3—helps mitigate HA by diminishing IR [95, 96]. Individualized dietary plans tailored to metabolic profiles and preferences are crucial for long-term success. Interventions such as the Mediterranean diet, Low-Carbohydrate diets, and Calorie restriction have shown promising results in improving metabolic outcomes and reducing HA. Additionally, regular physical activity, including aerobic exercises and resistance training, complements dietary changes by enhancing insulin sensitivity and reducing visceral lipid accumulation [64].

Studies have highlighted the efficacy of calorie-restricted diets (CRDs) in lowering androgen levels, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and regulating glucose metabolism. This dietary approach also promotes hepatic SHBG production and decreases FT levels through weight and fat mass reduction [97]. The ketogenic diet (KD) has emerged as a promising treatment modality for PCOS, with evidence showing its effectiveness in curbing weight gain and reducing high androgen levels and IR. A study demonstrated that an 8-week KD regimen adjusted the gut microbiome and metabolites, implicating a potential mechanism through which KD influences sex hormone metabolism in PCOS [98]. Moreover, Cincione et al. observed significant improvements in LH/FSH ratio, total testosterone (TT), FT, and SHBG levels. These improvements occurred in women with PCOS and overweight or obesity following a 45-day KD intervention. These findings provide substantial support for KD as a therapeutic strategy. However, the absence of data on long-term outcomes and the mechanisms by which KD benefits PCOS necessitate further investigation [99].

Time-restricted eating (TRE) has emerged as a notable dietary intervention strategy. As a circadian-aligned intervention, TRE regulates the timing of food intake rather than solely its quantity. Early TRE (eTRE; for example, restricting intake to an 8-h window such as 08:00–16:00), especially when combined with probiotics or moderate caloric restriction, demonstrates particularly beneficial effects on metabolic health [100]. Clinical studies indicate that eTRE significantly improves the homeostatic model assessment for IR (HOMA-IR), and decreases visceral adiposity [101]. Mechanistically, extending the overnight fasting period promotes hepatic glycogen depletion and stimulates fat oxidation. Furthermore, TRE may upregulate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and restore gut microbial diversity [102].

These effects provide a potential mechanism for interrupting the metabolic vicious cycle associated with conditions like PCOS.

Physical activity is also a critical component of PCOS management, with recommendations suggesting 150 to 300 min of moderate-intensity or 75 to 150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise weekly [56]. Research demonstrates that both continuous and intermittent aerobic training effectively improve metabolic and hormonal profiles in women with PCOS. Continuous aerobic training significantly reduces T levels (P < 0.001). Intermittent aerobic training also reduces T levels (P = 0.019) and further lowers the FAI (P = 0.037). These results emphasize the critical role of exercise in improving HA [103]. For women with obesity and PCOS, both continuous and intermittent training significantly reduce HA. Continuous aerobic training significantly reduces TT levels (P < 0.001), consistent with its metabolic benefits. Intermittent training, particularly high-intensity interval training, improves hormonal regulation by reducing T levels (P = 0.019), increasing SHBG by 22% (P = 0.002), and decreasing the FAI (P = 0.01). These changes are especially effective in mitigating HA in women with PCOS and obesity [104, 105].

Given the metabolic and endocrine diversity within the PCOS population, individualized lifestyle intervention plans are paramount. Furthermore, sustaining lifestyle changes poses a significant challenge, requiring continuous self-management and motivation for long-term adherence.

Insulin sensitizers

Metformin, a widely utilized insulin sensitizer in PCOS management, enhances peripheral tissue sensitivity to insulin and curtails hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis [106]. While its impact on androgen levels can be variable, its primary anti-androgenic mechanism is mediated through the alleviation of HI, which subsequently increases hepatic SHBG synthesis and reduces ovarian androgen secretion. Given the limitations of monotherapy, recent therapeutic strategies recommend combining metformin with anti-androgen agents such as spironolactone [107] or flutamide [108]. This combination therapy produces synergistic effects: metformin targets the underlying metabolic dysfunction, whereas anti-androgens directly inhibit androgen signaling.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) foster insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release. They also improve IR, decrease appetite and food intake, slow gastric emptying, and promote weight loss [109]. Their efficacy in diabetes treatment has extended to PCOS, showing promise in reducing obesity, IR, and androgen levels [110]. Combining GLP-1RAs with metformin appears to enhance the reduction of HA. Current evidence positions GLP-1RAs as superior to metformin in reducing visceral adiposity and normalizing menstrual cycles in women with obesity [111].

While GLP-1RAs do not directly target androgen synthesis, they exert indirect anti-androgenic effects by enhancing insulin sensitivity and optimizing the metabolic profile in individuals with PCOS.

Next-generation multi-target incretin therapies surpass single GLP-1RAs in clinical efficacy, primarily by driving more profound weight reduction. These agents include glucagon-like peptide-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GLP-1/GIP) co-agonists (e.g., tirzepatide) and GLP-1/glucagon receptor (GLP-1/GCGR) co-agonists (e.g., survodutide) [112]. By promoting greater AT reduction than mono-agonists, these therapies more effectively alleviate systemic IR. This weight loss-induced metabolic improvement significantly reduces compensatory HI [113]. Thus, these agents represent a potent strategy for ameliorating HA by extensively resolving obesity-related metabolic dysfunction.

Additionally, the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) agonist pioglitazone reduces androgen levels in PCOS rats through a distinctive mechanism. On one hand, pioglitazone stimulates hepatic SHBG synthesis by upregulating the expression of hepatocyte nuclear factor-4α (HNF-4α). On the other hand, it activates PPAR-γ in liver and muscle tissues, which enhances insulin sensitivity in these tissues.

This improvement in IR subsequently diminishes the suppressive impact of HI on hepatic SHBG synthesis, thereby decreasing the levels of circulating free androgens [114]. Furthermore, activation of the PPAR-γ pathway alleviates the chronic inflammatory state associated with PCOS, thereby improving IR and indirectly reducing androgen levels [115].

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 and 2 (SGLT1/2) inhibitors regulate glucose homeostasis by modulating intestinal and renal glucose (re)absorption. Clinical studies indicate their potential in alleviating HI and HA in PCOS, showing significant reductions in A4 and DHEAS levels without notably affecting FT. These findings suggest that SGLT1/2 inhibitors represent a promising therapeutic avenue for PCOS [116].

Therapies for androgen reduction with limited metabolic benefits

Combined oral contraceptive pills

Combined oral contraceptive pills (COCPs), comprising estrogen and progestin, are widely utilized for androgen-lowering therapy in patients with hyperandrogenic PCOS. Estrogen enhances SHBG production, thereby reducing free androgen levels, while progestin diminishes ovarian androgen secretion by suppressing LH secretion [117]. Certain progestins also offer anti-androgenic benefits by competitively binding to ARs and inhibiting 5-α reductase activity [118]. However, from a metabolic perspective, COCPs present a therapeutic trade-off. While effective for symptom control, they do not address—and may potentially aggravate—the underlying metabolic dysfunction. Research indicates that oral contraceptive use may not improve—and could potentially exacerbate—glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in women with PCOS [119, 120].

Recent international guidelines endorse COCPs for managing hirsutism and/or irregular menstrual cycles in reproductive-age adults with PCOS, suggesting their consideration in adolescents with PCOS for symptom relief. Despite this, the heterogeneity of PCOS symptoms and the paucity of evidence on specific COCP formulations necessitate cautious individualized treatment [56]. Research indicates the efficacy of COCPs in mitigating HA and menstrual irregularities in PCOS [121]. Notably, combining COCPs with metformin has shown a more pronounced decrease in FAI and DHEA, though TT and FT levels remained unchanged [122]. While COCPs effectively reduce androgen levels, the increased risk of venous thrombotic events (VTE) warrants careful consideration. This risk arises from the hypercoagulable state induced by supplemental estrogens, particularly ethinyl estradiol. Forslund et al. highlighted that ethinyl estradiol/cyproterone acetate (EE/CPA) combinations may offer superior HA reduction but advised against their first-line use due to heightened VTE risk [123]. Moreover, early treatment with oral contraceptives (OCPs) elevates platelet microparticles (PMPs) associated with VTE risk, although prolonged use sees this risk revert to baseline, indicating a need for further mechanism-based studies [124]. OCPs have also been implicated in increased expression of inflammatory markers, such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), TNF-α, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), potentially exacerbating metabolic dysregulation in PCOS [125].

Anti-androgen drugs

Current guidelines suggest considering antiandrogens in conjunction with effective contraception for managing HA (especially hirsutism) in women with PCOS. This recommendation applies particularly when there is a contraindication to or intolerance of COCPs, or if there's a suboptimal response to at least six months of COCPs and/or cosmetic treatments [56]. Antiandrogenic drugs operate through various mechanisms, including competitive inhibition at ARs, exemplified by spironolactone [126] and flutamide [127]; reduction in androgen synthesis or free androgen levels, a role fulfilled by COCPs; and 5-α reductase activity inhibition, as seen with finasteride [128].

Spironolactone is a widely used treatment for HA in women with PCOS, effectively reducing androgen levels. Studies indicate that spironolactone reduces TT levels significantly within 6 to 12 months [129]. When combined with metformin, spironolactone significantly improved BMI and TT levels in women with PCOS, without increasing adverse effects, although it did not notably affect hirsutism scores or gonadotropin concentrations. This underscores the efficacy of combining spironolactone with metformin in PCOS management [107]. Furthermore, Amer et al. investigated nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC) for delivering spironolactone topically in a PCOS rat model. This novel approach reduced hair follicle diameter and density significantly, enhancing drug targeting while minimizing systemic side effects [130].

Although distinguishing between metabolic-focused and "pure" androgen-lowering therapies provides a structured framework for PCOS management, these categories frequently overlap in clinical practice. This interconnectivity is best illustrated by the bidirectional feedback loop between hormonal and metabolic pathways. For instance, lifestyle interventions and GLP-1RAs primarily target metabolic pathways. However, they indirectly exert anti-androgenic effects by reducing visceral fat and mitigating the stimulatory impact of HI on the ovaries.

Conversely, "pure" anti-androgens may yield secondary metabolic benefits by alleviating the inhibitory effects of HA on skeletal muscle glucose uptake. Therefore, these categories represent a functional spectrum rather than a strict dichotomy. Clinical efficacy ultimately depends on the synergistic restoration of both endocrine and metabolic homeostasis.

Supplements and adjunct therapies

Various alternative interventions show promise in reducing HA in women with PCOS by addressing hormonal and metabolic dysregulation. Statins, widely known as 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors, are key treatments for hyperlipidemia and dyslipidemia. They inhibit cholesterol production, a precursor for androgen biosynthesis, and downregulate key enzymes in the androgen synthesis pathway, reducing androgen levels [131]. Although current guidelines do not recommend statins for PCOS management, emerging evidence suggests their effectiveness in reducing HA and alleviating cutaneous symptoms like hirsutism and acne [132].

Inositols, particularly myoinositol (MI) and D-chiro-inositol (DCI), function as secondary insulin messengers, enhancing glucose metabolism and cellular insulin sensitivity [133]. Combining MI and DCI in a 40:1 ratio significantly improves hormonal and metabolic profiles in PCOS by reducing T levels and enhancing insulin sensitivity [134]. Similarly, vitamin D supplementation reduces serum androgen and AMH levels, decreases endometrial thickness, and promotes menstrual regularity and follicular development. These effects are likely mediated by improved IR and the activation of vitamin D receptors in ovarian and endometrial cells [135, 136].

Melatonin provides an additional strategy for managing HA in PCOS. Research suggests that melatonin alleviates HA by acting on melatonin receptor 1 A (MTNR1A) in ovarian granulosa cells. By promoting CYP19A1 expression and modulating the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway, melatonin reduces androgen levels, as shown in animal models of PCOS [137, 138].

Bariatric surgeries, such as gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy, provide significant benefits for women with obesity-associated PCOS. These procedures reduce androgen levels, improve menstrual regularity, and alleviate hirsutism, effectively addressing metabolic and reproductive symptoms of PCOS [139, 140].

Recent evidence highlights gut microbiota modulation as a promising therapeutic strategy for HA in PCOS. Preclinical studies suggest that specific probiotic strains (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) can ameliorate PCOS symptoms by modulating the gut microbiota [141]. The proposed mechanisms include restoring microbial eubiosis, enriching SCFA-producing bacteria [82], reducing circulating LPS levels to alleviate metabolic endotoxemia [142], and modulating bile acid metabolism and intestinal barrier function [82].

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has also been investigated as a potential therapy for PCOS. Animal studies indicate that transplanting gut microbiota from donor rats with PCOS into control group recipients can induce hepatic glucose and lipid metabolic disorders, as well as imbalances in reproductive hormones. Conversely, FMT from healthy donor mice to PCOS model mice shifts the gut microbiota composition towards that of the control group and improves phenotypic abnormalities [143].

Collectively, these interventions expand therapeutic options for managing HA in PCOS, complementing conventional treatments.

Conclusion

In summary, this review delineates the intricate pathophysiology of HA in PCOS, highlighting its role as a central driver of the syndrome's reproductive and metabolic manifestations. The etiology of HA is multifactorial, resulting from the complex convergence of metabolic dysfunction (notably IR), neuroendocrine dysregulation, and intrinsic steroidogenic defects within the ovary and adrenal gland. Importantly, HA is not only a consequence but also an active amplifier of metabolic dysfunction. HA instigates a self-perpetuating cycle by promoting AT dysfunction and visceral fat accumulation, exacerbating hepatic steatosis, and impairing skeletal muscle insulin signaling. These tissue-specific effects are mediated through both direct androgen receptor activation and indirect mechanisms involving chronic inflammation and OS. Collectively, these pathways elevate the long-term risks of T2DM and CVD.

Interventions targeting underlying metabolic dysfunction—including lifestyle modifications and insulin-sensitizing agents provide significant metabolic benefits and indirectly ameliorate HA. In contrast, therapies targeting androgen reduction—such as COCPs and anti-androgen drugs—effectively manage hyperandrogenic symptoms but often have limited or adverse metabolic effects. This disparity represents a critical therapeutic trade-off.

Advancing beyond symptomatic management requires a deeper mechanistic understanding of HA's pathophysiological actions. Future research should prioritize elucidating the molecular pathways by which androgens induce tissue-specific IR, inflammation, and OS, particularly in ovarian, adipose, and hepatic tissues. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing targeted therapies that alleviate HA symptoms while mitigating the long-term metabolic sequelae of PCOS.
