Ethnic Inequalities in Return to Work Post First Stroke: Findings From a Population‐Based Cohort Study in South London
Authors: Amal R Khanolkar, Ajay Bhalla, Eva Emmett, Camila Pantoja‐Ruiz, Charles D A Wolfe, Matthew D L O'Connell, Iain J Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReturn to work post stroke is affected by sociodemographic factors and disability. We explored ethnic differences in employment among White, Black African, Black Caribbean, and other ethnic minority groups at 1 and 5 years post first stroke in a population‐based cohort.MethodsData on individuals with first‐ever stroke were obtained from the South London Stroke Register ([SLSR] patients registered between 1995 and 2025). Analysis was restricted to individuals in employment at the time of first stroke (N=1737, 67% men, mean age: 54 years [SD: 12.1]), with employment status assessed at 1 year and 5 years follow‐up. Associations between ethnicity and employment status (employed, retired or other) were examined using multinomial logistic regression adjusting for a range of sociodemographic (age, sex, study period, socioeconomic deprivation) and clinical factors (eg, stroke severity and management, co‐occurring health conditions, functional indicators) at baseline and follow‐up.ResultsAfter adjusting for baseline factors (socioeconomic deprivation/stroke severity/health conditions), Black African (relative risk ratio [RRR] 0.47 [95% CI 0.29–0.75]) and Black Caribbean (RRR, 0.40, [0.22–0.75]) groups were significantly less likely to be employed at 1 year post stroke than White individuals. At 5 years, only Black African individuals were less likely to be employed (RRR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.30–1.15]). Similar ethnic disparities were observed at 1 year and 5 years in relation to clinical factors assessed at follow‐up.ConclusionsBlack survivors of stroke were ~50% less likely to be employed 1 year after stroke compared with White survivors, with Black African disadvantage persisting at 5 years after stroke. These persistent disparities were not explained by sociodemographic, stroke severity, and functional/health indicators. More research is required to better understand what drives the ethnic disparities in poststroke return to work.Keywords: employment, ethnicity, health inequalities, return to workSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Race and Ethnicity, Disparities, Social Determinants of Health

Research Perspective

What Is New?

Black African and Black Caribbean individuals face disparities in return to work post first stroke compared with White individuals in the United Kingdom; both Black groups experienced significant disparities at 1 year post stroke, Black African individuals continued to experience this disparity at 5 years post stroke, and sociodemographic, clinical, and functional indicators did not explain the observed disparities.

Black African and Black Caribbean individuals face disparities in return to work post first stroke compared with White individuals in the United Kingdom; both Black groups experienced significant disparities at 1 year post stroke, Black African individuals continued to experience this disparity at 5 years post stroke, and sociodemographic, clinical, and functional indicators did not explain the observed disparities.

This is the largest study in the United Kingdom (and wider Europe) to comprehensively examine ethnic inequalities in return to work post stroke using population‐based data from a stroke register.

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

More studies are required to examine potential disparities in return to work in ethnic minority groups not included in this study such as South and East Asian groups; future quantitative studies should examine other factors not typically included in health records and registers such as post‐stroke rehabilitation, access to the labor market, cultural factors, and unconscious bias and ideally be accompanied by qualitative studies to help identify factors that could explain these inequalities.

Cerebrovascular diseases including stroke are the third leading cause of death in the United Kingdom (nearly 30 000 or 5.1% of all deaths in 2023). 1 Although strokes largely affect older people (median age of first stroke being 70 years in men and 76 years in women in England and Wales), a quarter of all strokes occur in working age individuals <65 years of age. 2 The impact of stroke is extremely variable and for a substantial proportion of survivors of stroke, it can be significantly debilitating (stroke is the third leading cause of disability worldwide). 3 , 4 Stroke occurring in working age individuals can have serious short‐ and long‐term consequences including the inability to return to work (RTW). 5 Several studies have shown that stroke severity, cognition, socioeconomic indicators, and physical disabilities are among the strongest factors associated with RTW. 3 , 6 , 7 , 8 In the United Kingdom, nearly a third of individuals experiencing stroke <65 years of age give up working and a further 15% work reduced hours. 9 This can have a serious impact on the financial security of survivors of stroke and their dependents. Furthermore, the inability to RTW has long‐term consequences on self‐esteem, life satisfaction, mental health, and well‐being on both survivors of stroke and caregivers. 10 Stroke is one of the most expensive health conditions in modern society both in terms of cost to treat and loss of productivity resulting in severe economic consequences for society. 11

Ethnic inequalities—that is, differences based on ethnicity including both social and clinical characteristics—in stroke incidence, severity, outcomes, and recovery in high‐income countries are well established. 12 Ethnic minority (EM) groups including Black and South Asian individuals have higher rates of risk factors like hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and high cholesterol predisposing them to increased risk of stroke, which they experience at younger ages relative to White peers. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 EM individuals experience longer door‐to‐needle care, less likely to receive acute and evidence‐based interventions (eg, admission to emergency departments, specialized stroke units, lower rates of revascularization, treatment with thrombolysis) and have poorer functional outcomes, with findings varying between countries and ethnic groups. 16 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Ethnic inequalities in poststroke treatment and care have been further confirmed in systematic reviews. 23 Crucially, a recent systematic review including data on nearly 12 500 patients with stroke found that across countries, EM individuals consistently report lower health‐related quality of life compared with the dominant ethnic group. 24 Further, EM individuals report worse cognitive decline (like memory loss and confusion) compared with White peers affecting their ability to RTW. 25 , 26 Despite these stark inequalities in risk factors and stroke care/outcomes, some studies show better recovery rates and survival in EM groups compared with White peers, which is partly explained by the younger age of EM individuals. 19

Although several studies have examined clinical and sociodemographic factors associated with RTW post stroke, there is a crucial paucity of data on ethnic differences with conflicting findings. These limited studies indicate EM individuals are less likely to RTW post stroke compared with White peers. 27 , 28 However, studies have been on smaller samples, restricted to comparisons between 2 ethnic groups or short follow‐up times (often <6–9 months post stroke), and limited by selection bias (for example, focusing on individuals in rehabilitation who differ in their ability and frequency in RTW). 27 , 29 , 30 , 31 Further, findings cannot be generalized to the United Kingdom with a different ethnic composition closely tied to history and immigration policy. Until today, only 1 older UK‐based study reported ethnic differences finding that Black individuals are less likely to be employed post stroke with follow‐up limited to 1 year. 27

RTW is crucial to survivors of stroke, their families, and society. The average age at first stroke is decreasing in both sexes and occurring earlier in the life course having a greater impact on working age individuals. 2 It is thus possible that some EM groups who are at higher risk for stroke at younger ages could be more adversely affected by stroke including RTW. EM individuals in the United Kingdom continue to report and experience persisting and substantial health inequities due to health vulnerabilities associated with the inequities of historical racism, poverty, and social deprivation as well as clinical and cultural factors. Despite stroke being the most frequent cause of disability alongside substantial increase in incidence in recent years and severity especially in EM groups, no study in the United Kingdom has comprehensively examined ethnic differences in RTW in survivors of stroke. Further, examining ethnic differences and predictors of RTW in individuals who survive stroke (and other noninjury related conditions) in population‐based cohorts could help inform the UK National Health Service 10‐year plan that aims to focus on community‐based health care and simultaneously reduce health inequalities. 32 This is pertinent for conditions like stroke where care, rehabilitation, and recovery will be dependent on community health centers.

In a population‐based cohort of patients of stroke, we examined survivors who returned to work at 1 year and 5 years following their first stroke by ethnicity and investigated the role of sociodemographic factors and stroke severity in these associations. We also explored contextual factors (like functional indicators and newly developed health conditions during follow‐up) at 1 year and 5 years associated with RTW and whether these explained ethnic differences (ie, differences between EM and majority White groups).

METHODS

Ethical Considerations

The SLSR (South London Stroke Register) has ethical approval from the National Health Service Health Research Authority (Wales REC 1 Research Ethics Committee, reference number: 22/WA/0027), and previously from the ethics committees of Guy's and St Thomas' Hospital Trust, King's College Hospital, Queens Square, and Westminster Hospital (London). Consent is obtained from all living patients before participating in the register, except where patients lack capacity to decide, in which case advice from a consultee is sought in line with the Mental Capacity Act.

Data Availability

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set for academic use should be made to the SLSR team: https://www.kcl.ac.uk/lsm/research/divisions/hscr/research/groups/stroke/index.aspx

Data will be made available after approval of a proposal through the SLSR investigator team, including a signed data access agreement.

Study Design and Participants

The SLSR is an ongoing prospective population‐based cohort study initiated in 1995 and comprising all adults with first stroke when resident in a geographically defined area of South London (total population of 398 555 in 2021) and described previously. 33 , 34 The register uses multiple overlapping notification sources like hospital admissions, neurovascular outpatient clinics, radiology reports, accident and emergency records, and general practitioners' clinics to ensure rigorous monitoring and increased case ascertainment (estimated at 88%). 35 Stroke was verified by a senior study clinician, using the World Health Organization definition of stroke (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] until March 2022, Eleventh Revision [ICD‐11] thereafter). The SLSR covers a geographically defined area within Lambeth and Southwark boroughs in South London, which are ethnically diverse (eg, 25% Black and 8.5% Asian or 47% ethnic minority) and include a large proportion of socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals as per the 2021 UK census. As of April 2025, the SLSR included 8325 individuals with a registered first stroke with data on age, sex, and ethnicity.

Exposure

The main exposure of interest was ethnicity that was self‐identified and assessed at baseline (time of first stroke). Participants could choose from 1 of the following ethnic groups (each of which had more detailed subcategories): White, Black or Black British, Mixed, Asian or Asian British, and other ethnic groups. Based on the ethnic diversity of Lambeth and Southwark boroughs and sample sizes, individuals were categorized into 4 ethnic groups for analysis: White (reference), Black Caribbean, Black African, and all other ethnic minority groups (which included individuals of South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, mixed ethnic backgrounds, and other non‐specified ethnicities, and hereafter referred to as other ethnic groups). In this study, the conceptualization of and nomenclature for ethnicity followed the guidelines recommended by the UK Government and the Office for National Statistics (ONS).

Outcome

The main outcome of interest was self‐reported paid employment status at 1 and 5 years post first stroke. Participants were asked to choose from 1 of the following 7 categories: full‐time employed, part‐time employed, retired, carer (for home/family/dependents), unemployed, unable to work due to disability/ill‐health or unknown. These categories were combined as follows for analysis: employed, retired, and other (carer, unemployed, and unable to work). We included individuals who were retired in a separate category as retirement is common given the older age demographic of patients and survivors of stroke are potentially more likely to retire both due to consequences of stroke and being older. Those with unknown or missing information on employment status at time of first stroke (N=617 of the eligible 8325 individuals) were excluded from analysis. Those missing information on employment status were less likely to be White and more likely to be of other ethnic groups, have a Barthel Index (BI) score <15, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score <13 (moderate/severe stroke), more likely to fail the swallow test but less likely to have ≥2 chronic health conditions (Table S1).

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics included age (<55, 56–65, and >65 years), sex (male or female), and cohort year (1995–2004, 2005–2014, and 2015–2024) at the time of first stroke. Socioeconomic position was based on the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 36 an area‐level socioeconomic position indicator widely used in research and health audits in the United Kingdom. The IMD is a composite measure of relative deprivation at small‐area level, called lower layer super output area, containing a population of ~1500 people. The IMD consists of 7 domains, weighted according to relevance (income: 22.5%, employment: 22.5%, education: 13.5%, health: 13.5%, crime: 9.3%, barriers to housing and services: 9.3%, and living environment: 9.3%). 37 Weighted scores from each domain are combined to create a single composite IMD score that is categorized into quintiles, with quintiles 1 and 5 being the most and least deprived, respectively. Each participant was linked via postcode at first stroke to their respective IMD quintile released most recently before first stroke or 2004 IMD for strokes before 2004. Due to smaller numbers of participants, IMD quintiles 3 to 5 were combined into a single category. Educational level was recorded from 2004 and thus available for a smaller number of individuals (N=4282). Educational level was dichotomized into “lower education” (no formal education, primary, lower secondary education) and “higher education” (upper secondary, postsecondary nontertiary, tertiary education).

Poststroke outcomes like functional independence in performing activities of daily living was measured by the BI, assessed at 7 to 10 days, 1 year, and 5 years post stroke via face to face, telephone interviews, or postal questionnaires. Total BI scores were categorized into <15 (moderate/severe disability) or ≥15 (mild disability). Stroke severity was assessed by the GCS, which assesses an individual's level of consciousness based on 3 aspects of responsiveness: eye‐opening, verbal, and motor responses. Total scores ranged from 3 to 15 that were categorized into <13 (moderate or severe brain injury) or ≥13 (mild brain injury) for analysis. 38 The swallow test (which assesses dysphagia) was an additional marker of stroke severity and dichotomized as pass versus fail. Additional contextual factors such as functional outcomes at 1 and 5 years included the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), which was subdivided into HADS Anxious and HADS Depressed (0–10=normal/mild [noncase] and ≥11=moderate/severe [case]); and the 12‐item Short Form survey, 39 divided into self‐rated mental and physical scores to assess health‐related quality of life. Instrumental activities of daily living in the previous 3 to 6 months was assessed by the Frenchay Activities Index developed for patients recovering from stroke. 40 Total scores (ranged from 0 to 45) were categorized into inactive (0–15) or moderate (16–30)/active (31–45). Need for assistance from another individual was assessed by a single question: “In the last 2 weeks, have you required help from another person for everyday activities (such as making a cup of tea)?” with answers categorized as no versus yes. We binarized continuous variables using clinically validated and established cutoff points to enable comparison with other studies and enhance clinical interpretation.

Stroke management and interventions were assessed by stroke unit admission (stroke unit versus other hospital ward/none) and thrombolysis rates (no versus yes, available from 2004).

Data on chronic health conditions were collected at the time of first stroke (and again at 1 and 5 years post stroke) and included hypertension, high cholesterol, cardiovascular conditions, type 2 diabetes, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, depression, and dementia (full list of conditions listed in Table S2). Information on these conditions were collected from hospital or general practice health records and supplemented by medications at time of first stroke and via self‐report during follow‐up. Each condition was coded as a binary variable (no versus yes). We created a composite indicator of the number of chronic conditions at time of stroke each participant has that was then categorized as no conditions, 1 condition, 2 conditions, and ≥3 conditions. Similarly, we also created variables to indicate the number of new diagnosed chronic conditions between baseline and 1 and 5 years post stroke.

The study sample was restricted to those individuals in paid employment at the time of first stroke and with valid data on age, sex, and ethnicity, N = 1737 (20.9% of the total of individuals registered with first stroke in the SLSR, see Figure S1).

Statistical Analysis

We first assessed ethnic differences in baseline characteristics (age, sex, IMD quintile, stroke severity, care processes, and health conditions), followed by differences in functional indicators at 1 year and 5 ears post stroke. Differences in proportions across categorical variables were examined using chi‐square tests.

Associations between ethnicity and employment status were examined separately at 1 year and 5 years post stroke using multivariable multinomial logistic regression models. We built models that separately adjusted for different sets of confounders as follows: Model 1 (sociodemographic): adjusted for sex, age, cohort period, and socioeconomic deprivation (IMD); Model 2 (stroke severity indicators): BI, GCS, and swallow test; Model 3 (health conditions); Model 4 (care processes): stroke unit admission and thrombolysis; and Model 5: a final model run with mutual adjustment for all covariates (sociodemographic, stroke severity, health conditions, and care processes). All 5 models were adjusted for age (years), sex, and cohort period. As a sensitivity analysis, these models were also run with individuals <65 years to assess whether ethnicity and employment associations differ when restricted to working age individuals only.

We also ran a sensitivity analysis to examine the effect of educational level on ethnicity and employment status associations. We ran 2 models with Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, cohort period, IMD, and educational level and Model 2 additionally adjusted for stroke severity indicators (BI, GCS, and swallow test), health conditions and care processes (stroke unit admission and thrombolysis).

Next, we separately examined independent associations between ethnicity and each contextual factor (ie, factors specifically assessed during follow‐up at time of employment status) at 1 year and 5 years post stroke using multivariable logistic regression models. These models were run to help determine contextual factors associated with ethnicity and to help inform which factors can be included in models with employment as the outcome (described below). Models were run individually between ethnicity and functional indicators like the BI, Frenchay Activities Index, HADS‐Anxious, HADS‐Depressed, need for assistance, health conditions at baseline, and number of newly diagnosed health conditions. Associations between ethnicity and 12‐item Short Form survey physical and mental health scores were examined using multivariable linear regression. All models on associations between ethnicity and contextual factors were adjusted for age, sex, and cohort period.

The final set of regression models examined associations between ethnicity and employment at 1 year and 5 years using multivariable multinomial logistic regression models adjusted separately for contextual factors as follows: Model 1: need for assistance, Model 2: BI and Frenchay Activities Index, Model 3: HADS‐Anxious and HADS‐Depression, Model 4: chronic conditions at baseline and newly diagnosed conditions, Model 5: SF‐12 physical and mental health continuous scores, and Model 6: final model including all predictors from models 1 to 5. All models were adjusted for age, sex, and cohort period. Ethnicity estimates for both sets of regression models were plotted for visualization but only for the employed category for simplicity.

Patients were missing in follow‐up time points either due to death or loss to follow‐up. We examined differences in baseline characteristics of those in employment at time of stroke and loss to follow‐up at 1 year and 5 years, separately.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Table 1 displays the distribution of key baseline characteristics by ethnicity. White individuals were socioeconomically more advantaged (17% in IMD quintile 3–5) compared with Black African (6.7%) and Black Caribbean (9.1%) groups. Functional independence in activities of daily living (ie, BI at 7–10 days post stroke) was worse in all EM groups compared with the White group. However, there were no significant differences in stroke severity as assessed by the GCS or the swallow test. Both Black African (33.2%) and Black Caribbean (34.9%) groups were significantly more likely to have ≥3 chronic conditions at the time of first stroke compared with White peers (24.1%). A substantially higher proportion of Black African (46.5%) individuals were employed at the time of stroke compared with White (17.2%), Black Caribbean (21.1%), and other ethnic (26.4%) groups. At 1 year, smaller numbers of EM individuals were employed (eg, 31% Black Caribbean, 36.7% Black African) compared with White individuals (48.7%) but differences narrowed at 5 years (Table S3).

Figure 1 and Tables S4 and S5 display results from multinomial logistic regression modeling on associations between ethnicity and employment status at 1 year and 5 years post stroke, respectively. Across all models, Black Africans had ~50%, Black Caribbeans ~60%, and other ethnic groups ~40% lower likelihood for employment at 1 year compared with White individuals. In the final model mutually adjusted for all baseline predictors, the Black African group had a relative risk ratio (RRR) 0.47 (95% CI, 0.29–0.75] and the Black Caribbean group a RRR 0.40 (95% CI, 0.22–0.75] for employment. The previously decreased likelihood for employment in other ethnic groups was substantially attenuated (ie, decreased in size) and no longer significant (RRR, 0.91 (95% CI, 0.47–1.75]). Sensitivity Analysis

In models restricted to individuals <65 years (Tables S6 and S7), we observed the same pattern for ethnic differences in employment at both 1 year and 5 years as in models with the full sample. In the mutually adjusted model, Black African (RRR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.31–0.85]) and Black Caribbean (RRR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.22–0.84]) groups had lower likelihood for employment compared with White individuals. In the 5‐year model, only the Black African group were significantly less likely to be employed compared with the White group.

In models restricted to those with information on educational level, we found that individuals with higher education were significantly more likely to be employed at 1 and 5 years post stroke compared with those with low education (Table S11). However, there was little change in the RRR for Black individuals with both Black African and Black Caribbean groups ~50% less likely to be employed at 1 year. At 5 years Black African individuals had a lower likelihood to be employed (RRR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.27–1.09]) with 95% CIs just crossing 1.

There were some significant differences in baseline characteristics between individuals excluded (lost to follow‐up) and those included in analyses (Table S12). At both 1 year and 5 years, individuals included in analyses were more likely to be from recent years (2015–2025), admitted to stroke unit, passed swallow test, and have a mild brain injury (GCS ≥13). There were small differences in distribution of ethnic groups with slightly higher proportions of Black Africans and Black Caribbeans in the included sample. There were no significant differences by education and sex.

DISCUSSION

This analysis of a large multiethnic population‐based study with long‐term follow‐up (5 years) including survivors of stroke employed at the time of first stroke found consistent evidence for ethnic inequalities in RTW that persisted even after considering several established factors like stroke severity, functional ability, and mental health associated with RTW. Key findings are that Black African and Black Caribbean individuals had ~50% lower likelihood for employment at 1 year post stroke compared with White individuals. However, at 5 years post stroke, only Black African individuals were significantly less likely to be employed compared with White individuals though findings were not statistically significant in mutually adjusted models. We also found evidence for substantial ethnic inequalities in a range of contextual factors at 1 year and 5 years post stroke in individuals who were employed at the time of first stroke (eg, requiring assistance and worse mental and physical health). However, adjustment for these contextual factors did not attenuate the observed ethnicity–employment associations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study's main strengths include the population‐based design, prospective identification of stroke cases, a multiethnic population living in a large inner city with substantial variation in socioeconomic deprivation and standardized follow‐up of all patients. This is one of the largest studies to date examining ethnic differences in RTW including at different time points post stroke. Furthermore, a detailed case mix and several clinical and evidence‐based processes (eg, thrombolysis, admission to stroke unit) are further strengths.

Main limitations include missing data on some clinical and sociodemographic factors. Furthermore, we were unable to examine other factors that are known to influence RTW like family/partner status, educational level, type of occupation, benefits payments that are either not collected in the SLSR or available for a limited period. Living conditions might affect RTW but there was limited change in living conditions of survivors of stroke in our study sample precluding us from examining this comprehensively. 8 EM individuals face discrimination in accessing the labor market, which can be further heightened after disability, but we were unable to account for discrimination and stigma as these data are not collected in the SLSR. We did not examine RTW in common EM groups in the United Kingdom like individuals of mixed ethnicity and South Asian ancestry due to small numbers. Loss to follow‐up led to a significant proportion of missing data on functional outcomes. Given that the SLSR is set in inner London, findings may not be generalizable to rural areas in the United Kingdom. Due to smaller number of participants, we were unable to examine ethnic differences in full‐time and part‐time employment status and how this differs between pre‐ and post stroke. The estimates for Black African individuals were no longer significant in mutually adjusted models at 5 years that could be due to loss in statistical power because of the reduction in sample size.

The SLSR does not collect information on facilitators and barriers to RTW post stroke (eg, patient motivation, financial incentives, or workplace adaptations/programs). Lastly, employment status was collected at specific time points of 1 year and 5 years post‐stroke and we do not collect the exact time of RTW. Thus, we could study associations only at fixed follow‐up periods after stroke. However, analysis at fixed time points enables us to use all available data at each time point and exclude those with missing data (but those missing data at 1 year were eligible to be included in the 5‐year analysis).

Comparison With Existing Literature

A recent umbrella meta‐analysis of 39 studies (including ~54 000 participants) found that 50.9% (95% CI, 44.3–57.5) RTW to work after stroke. 3 Direct comparisons are problematic due to differences in study design, recruitment of participants, follow‐up, and sample sizes. The small number of published studies that have reported ethnic differences further limit comparisons but our findings are in line with studies from the United States and New Zealand that found EM groups are less likely to be employed compared with White individuals even after accounting for most clinical and sociodemographic factors. 28 , 29 , 41 However, a US study found that differences between Mexican American and non‐Hispanic Whites were no longer apparent after adjusting for stroke severity in contrast to our findings. 29 We found that estimates for the Black African group were no longer statistically significant in mutually adjusted models at 1 year and 5 years, but this is likely due to reduction in sample size (with 95% CIs just crossing 1). Comparisons with existing studies are difficult due to the wide variation in factors adjusted for and duration of follow‐up. Only 1 UK study, also using data from the SLSR, examined ethnic differences in stroke but included a smaller sample of 2900 individuals with first stroke between 1995 and 2004 and limited to 1‐year follow‐up. This study reported that Black individuals were significantly less likely to RTW at 1 year post stroke. We add to this study by including a substantially larger study population with first stroke recorded between 1995 and 2025, longer follow‐up, including more baseline and contextual factors, and identifying significant differences between Black African and Caribbean individuals. 27 Despite several studies and systematic reviews on RTW post stroke, the paucity of studies on ethnic differences is perplexing given that stroke in more common and severe in many EM groups. This is further exemplified in a recent systematic review on ethnic differences in RTW of individuals who experienced illness; only 2 studies on ethnic differences in RTW post stroke were included. 42

The substantial and persisting inequalities in RTW post stroke observed in our study is a cause for concern, especially in the light of increasing incidence in stroke overall and in EM groups in particular. 15 Previous studies have found that socioeconomic indicators, multimorbidity, markers of stroke severity and functional status are strong predictors of RTW post stroke. Although these factors vary substantially between ethnic groups, it is surprising that accounting for them in models did not attenuate the observed estimates for the Black African and Caribbean groups, as expected. The persisting differences in employment observed in the Black African group at 5 years is likely due to their younger age compared with White individuals, allowing them more time to continue to experience disadvantage before retirement. This contrasts with Black Caribbean individuals who were the same age as White individuals and no longer experience this disadvantage at 5 years as they are perhaps more likely to retire. Stroke is a health condition of disparities and when considered as a continuum, ethnic disparities exist from prevention to treatment and recovery. 43 Although some of these disparities have been accounted for in our study, we are unable to examine the impact of ethnic discrimination, unconscious bias, patient‐centric and ethnic‐specific rehabilitation, institutionalized inequities in education, access to the job market, and networking among other factors. Previous studies have shown that quality and duration of early life education explain some of the observed ethnic differences in later‐life cognitive functioning including memory, which is relevant in survivors of stroke. 44

Further studies are required to investigate these and other yet identified factors including mediators on the pathway between stroke and employment and how these associations might differ between ethnic groups. The UK Equality Act 2010 mandates that individuals with disability due to long‐term illness including conditions like stroke cannot be discriminated against and reasonable allowances should be made for individuals RTW and those seeking new work. However, it is not clear the extent to which work‐based programs facilitate RTW for individuals with stroke and whether this is equitable across ethnic groups, different occupations and health conditions. There could also be inequalities in stroke survivors who are able to return to full‐time work post stroke. These need to be explored in future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we found substantial and persistent ethnic inequalities in employment following stroke that cannot be explained by the conventional factors known to predict RTW. In a population of survivors of stroke employed at the time of first stroke, Black African and Black Caribbean survivors of stroke were ~50% less likely to be employed 1 year after stroke compared with White survivors. Black African individuals experienced disadvantage persisting to 5 years though findings were not statistically significant in mutually adjusted models. These disparities remained after accounting for stroke severity, functional ability, socioeconomic deprivation, multimorbidity, and mental health—pointing to unmeasured structural and systemic barriers that urgently require investigation and policy response. Further studies are needed to better understand the pathways and mechanisms between ethnicity and employment, including social and cultural factors, and potential differences in wider society to employing different groups of people after stroke.
