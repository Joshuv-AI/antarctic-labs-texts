Biological Evaluation of Cleavable Linkers in Exatecan-Based Antibody–Drug Conjugates: A Comparative Study of DXd and Exo-Linker Platforms
Authors: Tomohiro Watanabe, Yusuke Iwai, Natsuki Shikida, Jason T. Stofleth, Tomohiro Fujii, Takuya Seki, Kazutaka Shimbo, Yutaka Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a transformative class of cancer therapies that combine the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the cytotoxicity of potent drug payloads. This study presents the development and evaluation of a novel linker platform designed to enhance ADC stability and pharmacokinetics by addressing the limitations associated with traditional cleavable linkers. Using trastuzumab conjugated with a payload linker consisting of this platform and exo-EVC-Exatecan (APL-1082), we examined key parameters, including in vivo efficacy and pharmacokinetic profiles in rat models, to directly compare it with the clinically validated trastuzumab-deruxtecan (T-DXd, Enhertu). The resulting ADC demonstrated superior stability and maintained drug-to-antibody ratios (DAR) with reduced aggregation and hydrophobicity compared to T-DXd, suggesting an improved pharmacokinetic profile. Additionally, combining APL-1082 with AJICAP site-specific conjugation technology enabled the production of high-DAR ADCs, achieving a DAR of 10 with promising homogeneity and physicochemical properties. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of this novel linker as a versatile platform for next-generation ADCs, offering enhanced stability, efficacy, and expanded therapeutic possibilities.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have gained prominence as a next-generation class of cancer therapies that integrate the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the cytotoxic power of potent drug payloads. − By linking a cytotoxic agent to an antibody that targets specific tumor antigens, ADCs enable direct, precise drug delivery to malignant cells, reducing collateral damage to normal tissues and significantly enhancing the therapeutic index. This targeted approach allows for a higher maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and reduces the minimum effective dose (MED), ultimately expanding the therapeutic window. These attributes underscore the potential of ADCs in redefining oncological treatments, making them a compelling option for a wide range of malignancies. Currently, 11 ADCs have received FDA approval, and approximately 210 are currently undergoing clinical evaluation. A noteworthy advancement in ADCs was achieved in 2019 with the approval of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd, commercialized as Enhertu), a blockbuster ADC with high efficacy against HER2-positive cancers, including breast and nonsmall cell lung cancer. Significantly, T-DXd demonstrated activity even against tumors with ultralow HER2 expression, underscoring the versatility of deruxtecan-based ADCs for various oncologic indications. T-DXd comprises an anti-HER2 antibody (trastuzumab) conjugated to an exatecan-derived payload (DXd) via an interchain native cysteine linkage, yielding a high drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of 8. Although high-DAR ADCs have historically posed challenges related to aggregation and suboptimal pharmacokinetics (PK) owing to hydrophobicity, the DXd payload and carefully optimized tetrapeptide linker (Gly-Gly-Phe-Gly, GGFG) have successfully mitigated these issues. This linker-payload configuration, known as the DXd platform, has since been established as a robust technology platform supporting multiple DAR8 ADCs currently in clinical trials.

However, despite the efficacy and stability of the DXd platform, several potential intrinsic challenges remain, particularly concerning the GGFG tetrapeptide linkers. Initially developed for cathepsin-specific cleavage in tumor microenvironments, the GGFG linker is comprised of hydrophobic amino acids (i.e., Phenylalanine, Glycine). It is a well-established principle in ADC development that increasing hydrophobicity can contribute to challenges such as aggregation and poor pharmacokinetics. While off-target cleavage of the GGFG linker by noncathepsin enzymes has not been extensively reported, recent findings have identified premature cleavage of other clinically approved peptide linkers, such as Val-Cit, within the plasma. , These findings highlight the potential vulnerability of the GGFG linker and raise concerns about off-target metabolism, dose-limiting toxicities, and therapeutic reliability.

To address the limitations observed in conventional cleavable linkers, we developed a new system called the exolinker, designed to address the challenges associated with traditional peptide linkers. This system modifies the structure of linear peptide linkers such as Glu-Val-Cit (EVC) by placing them at the exoposition on the p-aminobenzyl (PAB) group. The Exolinker has several key advantages. Its hydrophilic structure enhances plasma stability, reduces the risk of premature cleavage, and acts as a barrier against unintended payload release. Furthermore, the streamlined design of the exolinker contributes to improved chemistry, manufacturing, and control (CMC) processes, thus simplifying production and quality management. Adjusting the hydrophilic properties of the exolinker to counterbalance the hydrophobic characteristics of the payload allows for modifications that optimize stability and pharmacokinetic performance, making it a useful approach for next-generation ADCs. Although preliminary data on Exolinker ADCs indicate favorable outcomes, comprehensive biological evaluations remain limited. This study compares exolinker-based trastuzumab ADCs and clinically established T-DXd (Figure ), examining key factors, such as physical properties, in vivo efficacy, and pharmacokinetic profiles in rats. Our findings demonstrate that Exolinker ADCs exhibit similar therapeutic efficacy with improved stability and physicochemical properties, suggesting that the exolinker is a valuable option for future ADC designs.

Furthermore, building on the advantageous properties of the exolinker in enhancing the physicochemical characteristics of ADCs, we investigated its application in generating ADCs with an elevated DAR. Specifically, we aimed to produce an ADC with an unprecedented DAR of 10 and subsequently evaluate its stability. The findings regarding this approach are presented in this report.

Experimental Sections

Reagents and Cells

Trastuzumab (Herceptin), a human monoclonal IgG1 antibody, was purchased from Midwinter. The two AJICAP peptide reagents were prepared as previously reported (first-generation and second-generation). T-DXd was purchased from MedChem Express (Manassas, VA, USA). APL-1082 cells were prepared as described previously. All other chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma (USA).

All cell lines (HCC 1954, NCI-N87, PC-3, and SU-DUL-4) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA).

Experimental Procedure for Trastuzumab-Exolinker ADC

Trastuzumab was converted to DAR = 8 ADC as previously described.

Instruments and Analytical Methods

ADC concentration and recovery were measured using a SoloVPE system with the Slope Spectroscopy method.

Hydrophobic Interaction Chromatography (HIC) analysis of ADCs was performed using Tosoh Bio Butyl-NPR 2.5 μm 4.6 × 35 mm column (Tosoh Bioscience), as previously reported.

RP-HPLC analysis of ADCs was performed using AdvanceBio RP-mAb Diphenyl, 2.1 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm column (Agilent), as previously reported.

SEC analysis of ADCs was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC Protein BEH SEC column (200 Å, 4.6 × 300 mm, 1.7 μm), as previously reported.

The theoretical partition coefficient (CLogP) was determined using ChemDraw Professional 23.1.2 (Revvity, Cambridge, MA, USA).

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Assay

The in vitro cytotoxicity assay was performed using a previously reported method.

In Vivo Xenograft Study

NCI-N87 cells (Cat # CRL-5822) were purchased from the ATCC. These experiments were performed using a previously established procedure. Study Arms and Treatments for the NCI-N87. The tumor volumes were monitored, and on the first day (when mean tumor volume reached ∼120 mm3), mice were stratified and placed into 5 treatment groups of (10) mice as outlined in Table S1.

Rat PK Study

Animal Experiments

Animal experiments were cultured by a previously established procedure.

Clinical Observations

The animals were observed daily for clinical signs. Individual body weights were measured on days 0, 7, and 14 on the first day of administration, defined as Day 0.

Total Antibody Analysis

Blood samples were collected at six time points (immediately after administration, after administration, and 1, 3, 7, and 14 h after administration) via the caudal vein. The total antibody concentrations were measured using the double-sandwich ELISA method.

LC–MS Assay for Total ADC Analysis

25 μL of Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin was washed with PBS buffer (pH 7.4). Then, 50 μL of human IgG-Fc fragment antibody (1 mg/mL, PBS buffer, Bethyl Laboratories, Inc., USA) was added. After shaking at room temperature for 2 h, the beads were washed five times with PBS (pH 7.4). Blood samples were added to the beads and shaken for 2 h. The beads were washed five times with PBS buffer (pH 7.4), and 25 μL of elution solution (10% acetonitrile, 1% formic acid v/v in water) was added to each sample. After shaking for 30 min, the eluent was reduced by adding TCEP to a final concentration of 10 mM, and the mixture was incubated at 65 °C for 30 min. The resulting samples were analyzed using a NexeraBio system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). A PLRP-S column (5 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm, 1000 Å, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) was used as the analytical column, maintained at a column temperature of 80 °C. The autosampler temperature was set at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 25 μL. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, whereas mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient conditions for mobile phase B were as follows: hold at 10% for 0 to 0.5 min, increase linearly from 10% to 25% from 0.5 to 1.0 min, and increase linearly from 25% to 35% over the next 9 min. A wash step was performed by increasing mobile phase B from 35% to 98% in 0.1 min, holding at 98% for 5 min, and reducing mobile phase B to 10% within 0.1 min, maintaining it at 10% for 4.8 min. The flow rate was set at 400 μL/min. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted in the positive ion mode with a scan range of m/z 500–6,000, and TIMS was disabled. The end-plate offset and capillary voltage were set to 500 V and 4,500 V, respectively. The nebulizer gas pressure was set to 1.4 bar, the dry gas flow rate was 5.5 L/min, and the dry gas temperature was maintained at 200 °C. All the samples were analyzed with an in-source collision-induced dissociation (isCID) energy of 0 eV. The resulting data were processed and analyzed using Genedata Expressionist software version 16.5.1 (Genedata, Basel, Switzerland). Spectra corresponding to a retention time of 4.0–10.0 min and an m/z range of 600–3,000 were extracted and analyzed using time-resolved deconvolution with the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) algorithm. The minimum and maximum mass settings were 23 and 27 kDa for the light chain and 50 and 58 kDa for the heavy chain, respectively, with a mass step of 1.0 Da. Appropriate smoothing was applied prior to peak detection. After valid feature filtration based on the intensity and detection reproducibility, protein mapping was conducted with a mass tolerance of 250 ppm, incorporating fixed modifications or conjugates for each linker and payload. The peak heights of the top two glycan variants (A2G0F and A2G1F) were used for the heavy-chain DAR calculations. The DAR values of the light and heavy chains were summed and doubled to determine the overall DAR value.

Experimental Procedure for Homogeneous DAR = 10 ADC by AJICAP First-Generation Approach

The DAR of 10 ADC was prepared using a slightly modified procedure from a previously reported method. Trastuzumab was site-specifically modified using the first-generation AJICAP peptide reagent according to an established protocol. The resulting conjugates were reduced using TCEP to yield a fully reduced antibody containing 10 free thiols. A 20 mM solution of APL-1082 in dimethylacetamide (15 equiv) was added to the reduced antibody solution (2 mg/mL in PBSE: 20 mM PBS and 2 mM ethylenediamine) and incubated for 1 h. The crude ADC was purified using a NAP-10 desalting column, yielding a DAR of 10 ADC in a buffer containing 20 mM histidine and 10% trehalose. The final ADC concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL.

Experimental Procedure for Homogeneous DAR = 10 ADC by AJICAP Second-Generation Approach

The DAR of 10 ADC was prepared using slightly modified procedures based on previously reported methods. Trastuzumab was site-specifically converted to an antibody-thiol intermediate (containing two free thiols) using second-generation AJICAP technology according to an established protocol. TCEP (10 equiv) was added to the resulting antibody-thiol intermediate, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The reduced antibody solution (2 mg/mL in PBSE buffer, 20 mM PBS, and 2 mM ethylenediamine) was incubated with a 20 mM solution of APL-1082 in dimethylacetamide (15 equiv). The reaction mixture was incubated for 1 h. The crude ADC was purified using a NAP-10 desalting column to obtain a DAR = 10 ADC in a buffer containing 20 mM histidine and 10% trehalose. The final ADC concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL.

Results and Discussion

For direct comparison with T-DXd, we used trastuzumab as an antibody to reduce the interchain disulfide bonds. This was then conjugated with APL-1082 (Exo-EVC-Exatecan), an Exolinker Exatecan. The DAR was measured using the HIC, confirming a DAR of approximately 8. To compare the hydrophobicity of T-DXd and Exolinker-ADC, we measured the HIC retention time and aggregation rate using Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) (Table ). The ClogP of APL-1082 is −3.0, which is significantly lower than that of deruxtecan (−0.28), indicating a markedly higher hydrophilicity. In alignment with this, the HIC retention time for Exolinker ADC was significantly shorter than that for T-DXd. This retention time was closer to that of the parent antibody trastuzumab, suggesting a more favorable pharmacokinetic (PK) profile.

Summary of Trastuzumab-ADCs

A comparison of biological profiles was performed (Figures and S3). An in vivo xenograft study was performed using the NCI-N87 gastric cancer model to assess the efficacy of the two ADCs. Although the exolinker ADC showed slightly higher tumor growth inhibition (TGI) levels than T-DXd (Table S2), both ADCs demonstrated comparable tumor inhibition with no statistical difference (p > 0.05). The potency of the payload is expected to impact the overall efficacy of the ADC. In the KPL-4 human breast cancer cell line, for example, exatecan has a reported IC50 of 0.9 nM, while DXd has a reported IC50 of 4.0 nM, suggesting a clear potency advantage for exatecan. This highlights the need for further efficacy analyses, such as employing medium antigen expression models, to understand better and optimize the ADC performance. Nonetheless, the exolinker construct showed an in vivo profile comparable (or potentially slightly more efficacious) to the present model.

Biological profile comparison between T-DXd and trastuzumab-exo-EVC-exatecan, (a) in vivo xenograft study using NCI-N87 model, (b) rat PK study: DAR trend analyzed by LC–MS (left), total antibody trend analyzed by ELISA (right).

Additionally, a rat PK study was conducted to evaluate the stability differences between the ADCs (Figure b). We assessed the ADC deconjugation levels using our previously established LC–MS-based analysis (Figures b and S5–S8). Following ADC administration, blood samples were collected at predetermined intervals. The ADCs were pulled down using an immunocapture method with anti-Fc antibodies, followed by LC–MS analysis to determine the DAR over time, thereby allowing the quantification of drug release from the ADC. The DAR of T-DXd decreased by approximately 50% within 7 days, whereas the Exolinker ADC demonstrated greater DAR retention, suggesting superior linker stability. Furthermore, total antibody levels were measured by ELISA, which indicated that both T-DXd and Exolinker ADCs maintained levels comparable to those of trastuzumab. These findings imply that the Exolinker offers enhanced stability relative to the GGFG-linker used in T-DXd, with no negative effects on the ADC clearance rate.

This study demonstrated that the Exolinker can enhance the stability of ADCs, as evidenced by the DAR = 8 construct, where the stability is primarily attributed to the Exoposition tripeptide. The hydrophilic properties of this peptide suggest that it may further support a higher DAR, a notable challenge in achieving homogeneity at levels approaching DAR = 10. To accomplish this, we implemented a combined approach utilizing the native cysteine interchain break method alongside AJICAP site-specific conjugation technology , (Figure ). The AJICAP platform facilitates thiol incorporation at specific lysine residues in the Fc region of the antibody. The AJICAP peptide reagent in the present study specifically modifies Lys248, which, unlike other lysine residues, is not highly exposed despite being sufficiently accessible for peptide interaction. , Notably, Lys248 was similarly shielded by interchain cysteine thiols, suggesting that, even at a high DAR of 10, the hydrophobicity of the payload may not present a significant limitation. Although methods for achieving high-DAR ADCs (DAR >8) have been previously reported, such as attaching payloads to hydrophilic polymers, , peptides, or randomly modifying surface-exposed lysine residues, no clinical validation has been reached. In contrast, our approach allows the site-specific introduction of payload linkers to multiple amino acid residues on the antibody without requiring genetic modification, thus providing a more practical and scalable strategy for ADC development. This method is promising for developing high-DAR ADCs with improved stabilities and pharmacokinetic profiles. The AJICAP first-generation method involves site-specific modification of Lys248 using a peptide reagent. Following this modification, treatment with the reducing agent TCEP cleaved the peptide-antibody linkage (a disulfide bond), leaving a thiol group on the amino group at Lys248. However, this process also cleaves the interchain disulfide bonds of the antibody, resulting in 10 free thiols on the antibody, as confirmed by the Ellman assay. In the original AJICAP first-generation approach, these interchain disulfide bonds were reoxidized to maintain only the thiol at Lys248 in a free state, allowing for the attachment of payload linkers, specifically at Lys248, yielding an ADC with DAR = 2. In this study, we leveraged the high hydrophilicity of the Exolinker by utilizing an antibody intermediate with all ten free thiols intact, foregoing reoxidation of the interchain disulfide bonds (Figure a). The direct conjugation of APL-1082 to this thiol-rich intermediate successfully yielded an ADC with a DAR of 10. In the AJICAP first-generation method, reoxidation of the interchain disulfide bonds leads to increased aggregation of certain antibodies. This is likely due to undesirable side reactions, such as disulfide scrambling or interantibody disulfide bond formation. However, with the DAR = 10 approach, these concerns were eliminated because reoxidation was not required. Additionally, the Exolinker is expected to enhance payload stability, further contributing to the low aggregation rate of the resulting ADC.

In 2023, an improved AJICAP conjugation technology, referred to as the second generation, was implemented to eliminate the TCEP reduction and reoxidation steps. We evaluated this improved manufacturing-validated technology for producing a DAR of 10 ADC (Figure b). In this process, peptide conjugation is followed by hydroxylamine cleavage to generate an antibody-thiol intermediate containing two free thiols. Subsequently, similar to the traditional fully saturated cysteine reduction method, TCEP reduction followed by payload linker conjugation successfully produced a DAR of 10 for the ADCs. Both first- and second-generation technologies demonstrated the ability to produce high-DAR ADCs efficiently. The HIC retention time of DAR = 10 ADC was 9.4 min (Figure S3), comparable to the retention time of T-DXd. This indicates that the hydrophobicity of DAR = 10 ADC is reasonably well balanced, suggesting that it is suitable for in vivo stability and acceptable hydrophobicity. Analytical verification by RP-HPLC indicated a DAR of 9.4, while SEC analysis showed an aggregation rate of less than 3% (Figure S9), highlighting the robust capability of the platform to enhance stability while preserving conjugate homogeneity.

These results strongly suggest that the Exolinker represents a promising ADC linker platform with the potential to broaden ADC applications and establish a versatile foundation for future ADC development.

Conclusion

In summary, this study explored the application of a newly developed exolinker to improve ADC’s stability and pharmacokinetic performance. We synthesized Exolinker ADCs by conjugating trastuzumab with APL-1082 (Exo-EVC-Exatecan) and employed HIC and SEC to confirm a DAR of approximately 8 with lower retention times and aggregation rates than T-DXd. The in vivo efficacy of the Exolinker ADC was assessed using an NCI-N87 xenograft model, which demonstrated tumor inhibition similar to that of T-DXd. In rat PK studies, LC–MS analysis indicated superior DAR retention for the Exolinker ADC over 7 days, suggesting enhanced linker stability. To evaluate the feasibility of a higher DAR, we employed the AJICAP site-specific conjugation technology, enabling thiol incorporation at Lys248 and achieving a DAR of 10. RP-HPLC and SEC analyses confirmed a DAR of 9.4 and an aggregation rate below 1%.

Future directions for this work include biological evaluation of the DAR = 10 ADC and extension of this approach to a broader range of payloads beyond exatecan, such as hydrophilic auristatin analogs, immunostimulatory agents, and PROTAC molecules. These findings demonstrate the capacity of the exolinker to enable stable, high-DAR ADCs and establish it as a versatile platform with significant potential for advancing next-generation ADC therapeutics.
