Orphan Receptor GPRC5B Controls Adult Hippocampal Neurogenesis and Affects Retinoic Acid-Mediated Behaviors
Authors: Wenqi Fu, Rana Alabdali, Alireza Mousaei, Fernanda Daher, Nina Wettschureck, David M MacLean, Manoela V Fogaça, Cesare Orlandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstudied orphan GPCRs lack identified natural ligands, yet understanding their function is critical for therapeutic development. GPRC5B is a brain-enriched, retinoic acid (RA)-induced orphan GPCR. While RA is used to treat severe acne, chronic exposure is associated with depression, likely due to its inhibition of adult hippocampal neurogenesis. Here, we tested whether GPRC5B plays a role in the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying RA-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice by suppressing adult hippocampal neurogenesis. We found that Gprc5b knockout (KO) mice were resilient to RA-induced behavioral effects and that RA increased GPRC5B expression in the hippocampal neurogenic subgranular zone. This correlated with RA-mediated inhibition of adult hippocampal neurogenesis, an effect absent in Gprc5b KO mice, which also exhibited a larger pool of proliferative neuronal stem cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that GPRC5B mediates RA-induced anti-neurogenic effects and depressive-like behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05380-9.Keywords: G protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR), Orphan GPCR, Retinoic acid (RA), Major depressive disorder, Mouse behavior, Adult neurogenesis

Introduction

Preclinical and clinical evidence suggests that retinoic acid (RA), a derivative of vitamin A, is bidirectionally implicated in the pathophysiology of brain disorders [1]. RA deficiency has been associated with impairments in learning and memory [2], whereas RA oversupply or vitamin A intoxication has been linked to the onset of depressive behaviors [3–5]. Specifically, rats deprived of vitamin A for 6 weeks displayed poorer performance in the radial arms maze compared to their normal diet counterpart, and the cognitive impairment observed was fully restored when replaced with a regular diet [6]. Conversely, administration of RA as all-trans-RA (ATRA, the bioactive form of vitamin A) or its stereoisomer 13-cis-RA, both clinically used for the treatment of severe acne, is suggested to induce depressive behaviors in clinical settings [7]. Likewise, in rodents, chronic RA administration promotes HPA axis hyperactivation, stress-related behaviors, and anhedonia [8, 8–16]. Taken together, these data suggest that signaling pathways mediated by RA are closely linked to the etiology of central nervous system (CNS) diseases, especially affective disorders.

Mechanisms of RA-induced depressive-like phenotypes seem to involve the inhibition of adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) [1, 9, 17], a process that involves the production of new neurons in restricted regions of the mature CNS, including the subgranular zone (SGZ) in the hippocampal dentate gyrus [18]. During development, neural stem cells (NSCs) generated from embryonic development in the dentate epithelium migrate and settle at the hippocampal dentate gyrus. Part of these NSCs will eventually give rise to the NSCs in the adult neurogenic niche (Berg et al. 2019). These NSCs can stay quiescent, maintaining self-renewal capacity, until undergoing asymmetric division producing activated neural progenitor cells (NPCs). NPCs further develop into pre-mature neurons positive for doublecortin (DCX) expression, before becoming mature dentate gyrus granule neurons of the molecular layer. Accumulating evidence suggests that deficits in the AHN process can lead to disorders related to learning, memory, and mood [19–23]. Although a clear causal link between the blunting of AHN and the pathophysiology of mood disorders has not been firmly established, emerging studies show that antidepressants are capable of reversing AHN deterioration [24], and AHN deficits are accompanied by depressive phenotypes in both rodents and humans [25].

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are transmembrane mediators of signal transduction in response to extracellular stimuli and are, either directly or indirectly, targeted by FDA-approved antidepressants [26]. Buspirone, for example, directly acts on serotonin receptors, while fluoxetine or selegiline modulate levels of catecholamines within the synaptic cleft indirectly activating monoaminergic GPCRs. Orphan GPCRs (oGPCRs) are those GPCRs whose endogenous ligands remain unidentified or debated. Nonetheless, the neurophysiological function of many oGPCRs has been extensively demonstrated, and efforts to develop drugs targeting these receptors are ongoing [27–33]. Genome-wide association studies and knockout animal data suggest that loss-of-function mutations of oGPCRs or complete loss of certain oGPCRs can lead to severe dysfunction of the brain [34, 35]. G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member B (GPRC5B) is a class C oGPCR that is transcriptionally inducible by RA and is among the most abundant GPCRs in the brain [36]. Importantly, using RNAscope in situ hybridization, we recently showed that Gprc5b is universally expressed in the whole mouse brain, with an enrichment in brain regions relevant for adult neurogenesis such as the SGZ and subventricular zone (SVZ) [36]. The functional role of GPRC5B in the CNS and peripheral tissues has been associated with motor coordination, lymphatic development, smooth muscle cell contractility, and differentiation, as well as inflammatory responses in adipocytes and renal glomerulus [37–43]. Nonetheless, the role of GPRC5B in RA-induced phenotypes remains unexplored.

In this study, we show that the loss of GPRC5B prevents RA-induced depressive-like behaviors and abolishes RA-dependent suppression of adult hippocampal neurogenesis. These results establish the orphan receptor GPRC5B as a molecular brake on adult hippocampal neurogenesis and provide a foundation for understanding its role in mediating RA-driven depressive-like behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Animal Procedures and Genotyping

Global Gprc5b KO mice were generated by germline recombination of Gprc5bfl/fl mice [37]. Gprc5b KO mice and their wild-type littermates were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. Heterozygous breeding pairs were used to generate littermates for testing and a three-primer system was used for GPRC5B genotyping: Gprc5b-F binds upstream of exon 2, 5′-GCTGGAAGGTTTCTCCCTCT-3′; Gprc5b-R binds immediately downstream of the start codon on exon 2, 5′-AAGAGACAACCACCAGACAGG-3′; Gprc5b-R-XL binds the intronic region between exon 2 and exon 3, 5′-CTGCTTCAAGGCTGGGATTA-3′. Therefore, the amplified Gprc5b KO allele produces a band size of 495 bp, while the WT allele produces a band size of 361 bp (the 2 k-bp band was not showing). Mice were group-housed in specific pathogen-free conditions under a 12-h light-dark cycle (6:00–18:00). Experiments were performed during the light phase. Food and water were provided ad libitum. For the ATRA treatment paradigm, 4-week-old young adult mice were injected daily for 28 consecutive days until they reached 8 weeks of age. All procedures were carried out in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. All animal experiments were approved by the University of Rochester Committee on Animal Resources (UCAR).

Drug Administration

For behavioral experiments, all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA, MP Biomedicals) was administered at 10 mg/kg. ATRA or the corresponding vehicle (2% of ethanol in corn oil) was intraperitoneally injected daily to both WT and Gprc5b KO mice for 28 consecutive days between 2 and 4 PM. For experiments to trace lineages of adult-born neurons, a thymidine analog, bromo-deoxy uridine (BrdU; Sigma-Aldrich #B5002) was intraperitoneally injected to P42 naïve male WT and Gprc5b KO mice for 3 consecutive days, once per day, at 100 mg/kg.

Behavioral Experiments

All behavioral experiments were scored blindly in terms of genotypes and/or treatment conditions.

Tail Suspension Test

Mouse tails were taped upside down to a metal bar positioned approximately four-fifths of the way up from the tail base in a semi-transparent cubicle, as described [44]. This setup was designed to prevent interference between mice during the experiments. The mouse behavior was videotaped for 6 min, and immobility was manually scored and analyzed as a measure of passive behavior. A white noise generator (70 dB) was used to block any startling background noise.

Sucrose Splash Test

Under red light, mice were individually placed in empty standard mouse cages and a fresh 10% sucrose solution was squirted onto the dorsal coat, as described [45, 46]. The viscous solution elicited grooming behaviors. The time spent grooming during the 6-min video recording was analyzed as an indicator of self-care and motivation. A white noise generator (70 dB) was used to block any startling background noise.

Sucrose Preference Test

The 5-day, four-night sucrose preference test was used to assess anhedonia in mice, as described [47]. On day 1 afternoon, adaptation 50-ml tubes (one with 50 ml of plain water and the other with 50 ml of 1% sucrose) were placed in home cages where mice were group-housed. On day 2, in the afternoon approximately at the same time, the position of the two tubes was switched. On day 3, the tubes were swapped again. On day 4, these mice were single-housed and provided with a pre-weighted pair of water and sucrose tubes individually. The following morning, the tubes were collected carefully and weighed to calculate the liquid consumption. The sucrose preference was defined as the percentage of the sucrose consumption to the total consumption of the water and sucrose.

Open Field Test

The spontaneous exploratory behavior of mice was recorded within an open field arena for 5 min, as described [48]. The arena contains a square platform (40 × 40 cm) and four walls made of acrylic gray matte finish with a height of 30 cm. The center zone was defined as the center square (20 × 20 cm) and the edge as the remaining area in the arena. Low light conditions (80–110 lux) were used to avoid light-induced stress, and a white noise generator (70 dB) blocked any startling background noise. GoPro HERO8 cameras were used to record each mouse in open field videos, with the following settings: FPS = 60, resolution = 1440, lens = linear, hyper smooth = ON, and low light = ON. Total distance traveled, mean velocity, and ratio of time that the animal spent in the center over edge were analyzed using EthoVision software.

Locomotor Test

The locomotor test was used to measure levels of both motor activity and anxiety-like behaviors. The measurements were performed in a sound-attenuating cubicle enclosed chamber (27.3 × 27.3 × 20.3 cm). The chamber was equipped with three-dimensional infrared photobeams to track animal movements in the X, Y, and Z axes (Med Associates Inc., St. Albans, VT). Light beam breaks were recorded for 60 min and aggregated into 5-min bins to gauge ambulatory distance and time spent in predefined zones. The center zone was defined as the inner square of the chamber (15.7 × 15.7 cm). The edge zone was defined as the space between the outer (25.6 × 25.6 cm) and the inner square of the chamber. The total ambulatory distance indicated the overall motor activity. The center-over-edge distance ratio was calculated to evaluate anxiety levels.

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) Test

The elevated plus maze apparatus contained two open arms (no sides, 34.9 × 6.07 cm), two enclosed arms (with sides, 34.9 × 6.07 × 19.13 cm), and a center square platform (6.1 × 6.1 cm). The apparatus was made of black plexiglass, placed at a height of 93.73 cm above the ground. At the beginning of the test, each mouse was placed at the center of the platform and allowed to spontaneously explore the maze for 5 min [49]. The session was recorded by a ceiling-mounted camera. The recorded videos were later analyzed to obtain the number of entries into, and the time spent by each mouse in the open arms, enclosed arms, and center platform by a scorer blind to the experimental conditions using The Observer XT (Noldus, Leesburg., VA).

Marble Burying Test

The marble burying test was conducted in a cage similar to the mouse home cage, where 5-cm corn-cob bedding was gently overlaid by 20 marbles of the same color in a layout of 4 × 5 in an equidistant arrangement. The mouse was then placed gently at the center and allowed to spontaneously explore for 30 min [50]. A white noise generator (70 dB) was used to block startling background noise interference. The number of marbles was scored as buried when more than two-thirds of the surface area was below the bedding.

Stress-Induced Hyperthermia (SIH) Test

The test started with the first measurement of the mouse basal temperature (T1) with a digital rectal thermometer. After an interval of 15 min, a second rectal temperature was measured (T2). The delta temperature (T2–T1) was calculated and reported as an index of stress-induced hyperthermia.

Pose Estimation and Behavioral Clustering

To extract pose information from recorded open field videos, a ResNet-50-based neural network was trained in DeepLabCut (DLC) 2.3.9 [51, 52] on 395 manually labeled video frames with 7 keypoints, using all default settings and 500,000 iterations, resulting in training error and testing error of 1.63 px and 2.44 px, respectively. Pose data were then imported to Keypoint-MoSeq to perform behavioral clustering using an unsupervised learning algorithm [53]. The number of latent dimensions was set at 4 based on the principal component analysis (PCA) with a confidence cut-off of 0.4 for DLC data as per error calibration. A κ value of 105 was used for the initial fit of the autoregressive hidden Markov model and 2 × 104 for the full model to aim for syllable durations of approximately 400 ms as recommended [53]. The actual mean syllable duration was 494 ms. Finally, we compared the composition of syllables between groups using a custom Python script. Because these are compositional data (parts of a whole that sum to a constant), direct statistical comparison between them can yield false results; therefore, we performed a centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation on frequency values and compared them using a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; 4) on Aitchison distances (Euclidean distances of CLR-transformed proportions). P-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

RNAscope and Fluorescence Microscopy

General procedures of RNAscope were previously described [36]. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation followed by decapitation. The brains were swiftly extracted, embedded in a mold filled with Tissue Tek O.C.T. compound (Sakura, # 4583), and rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen. Sixteen µm coronal sections were obtained through cryosectioning of fresh-frozen brains and mounted onto Superfrost Plus microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In situ hybridization was performed using the RNAscope multiplex fluorescent reagent kit v1 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, #323133) to detect target mRNA at the single cell level. Probes for mouse Gprc5b (Mm-Gprc5b-O1-C1; #1187201-C1), mouse NeuN (Mm-Rbfox3-C3; #313311-C3), and mouse doublecortin (Mm-Dcx-C2; #478671-C2) were designed and purchased from the manufacturer, with the procedure conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Probes were conjugated to the fluorophores Alexa 488, Atto 550, and Atto 647. For low-magnification overview images of brain sections, the RNAscope multiplex fluorescent reagent kit v2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, #323100) was used with the Opal 690 fluorophore (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, FP1497001KT) assigned to the Mm-Gprc5b-C1 probe for signal amplification. DAPI was applied for nuclear counterstaining. Images were captured using the all-in-one fluorescence microscope Keyence X-800E with a 10× objective for stitched overview images and a 40× objective for images used to quantify expression and statistical analysis. Consistently, all images used for direct comparisons were taken with identical illumination intensities for corresponding channels.

A frequency distribution analysis was used to quantify the expression of Gprc5b mRNA in the stem cell niche of the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus. Double staining of Gprc5b and Dcx mRNAs enabled the quantification of the receptor in Dcx+ adult neural progenitors. Four sections were sampled from three animals treated with chronic retinoic acid versus chronic vehicle, and two to four images per section were taken for the following four fluorophores/fluorescent dyes: DAPI, Alexa 488, Atto 550, and Atto 647. Nuclei in brain sections were identified by DAPI staining and used to create a mask twice the area of DAPI with FIJI software (SciJava Consortium). Hybridizations with Dcx and Rbfox3 were used to label adult neural progenitors and mature neurons, respectively. For neuronal population distribution analysis, we developed a MATLAB script that determines the relative frequency of neuronal populations that express a certain amount of Gprc5b mRNA. Gprc5b expression was defined as the mean gray value of Gprc5b+ puncta in twice the area of a DAPI mask recognized by FIJI software. The frequency distribution histograms obtained from MATLAB analysis (script will be provided upon request) are organized in bins that incorporate all the cells expressing defined increments reported as bin width in each figure legend. We defined the total number of bins as 50. To evaluate the effect of chronic ATRA treatments on the expression levels of Gprc5b in mature neurons or neural progenitors, the percentage of Gprc5b-positive cells was scored by the FIJI quantification macro we created. To compare the Gprc5b frequency of distribution in Dcx+ neural progenitors, we conducted the Wilcoxon-rank sum two-sided test using the abovementioned MATLAB script (available upon request).

Western Blot

Mouse brains were dissected with an acrylic rodent brain matrix for coronal sectioning (Stoelting, #51380). From each brain region of interest, a sterile biopsy 2 mm puncher with a plunger was used to extract tissues from individual 2 mm coronal slices (INTEGRA). Both left and right symmetrical 2 mm punches were retrieved from the hippocampus (HP), nucleus accumbens (ACB), and hypothalamus (HY). For the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), a single 3 mm punch was retrieved to obtain an equal amount of protein for the immunoblotting analysis. Brain punches were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use. One milliliter of immunoprecipitation buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 1% Triton X-100, complete protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor mixture) was used to homogenize 80 mg of tissue of interest. Protein concentration was quantified against a calibration curve using the Pierce™ 660 nm Protein Assay Kit (Pierce; #22662). Equal amounts of protein per sample were diluted in a 4X sample buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl (pH = 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% Glycerol, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, trace amount of Bromophenol Blue) and loaded onto 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad; #4561096) for SDS-PAGE analysis. Our in-house generated rabbit polyclonal antibodies anti-GPRC5B were used at 1:5000. Specificity was previously validated [36]. Mouse anti-GAPDH antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:30,000 (Sigma-Aldrich, #MAB374).

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

Tissue processing and immunohistochemistry were performed on free-floating mouse brain sections. Mice were anesthetized with a solution of 10 mg/kg xylazine and 100 mg/kg ketamine in saline, followed by transcardiac perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then phosphate-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) using a pump system (MasterFlex L/S). Subsequently, brains were removed and post-fixed in phosphate-buffered 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C. Brains were sunk in a 30% sucrose solution for cryoprotection. Brains were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (O.C.T.) for cryosectioning into 30-µm coronal sections with a Leica cryo-microtome. Sections were stored in cryoprotectant media (30% sucrose and 30% ethylene glycol in PBS) at −20 °C or used immediately. Brain sections were transferred to a 24-well plate filled with cryoprotectant solution. Brain sections were then washed 3 × 10 min each in PBS before a 2-h incubation in blocking buffer (2% BSA+ 0.35% Triton-X100 in PBS) under room temperature. After blocking, brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies solubilized in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used included rabbit anti-DCX (1:500, Cell Signaling; #14802), rabbit anti-Ki67 (1:400, Cell Signaling; #9129), rat anti-BrdU (Abcam, 1:200; #ab6326), mouse anti-NeuN (Cell Signaling, 1:500; #94403), and rabbit anti-active Caspase3 (BD Pharmingen, 1:400; #570524). After incubation overnight with the primary antibodies, the epitope-primary complex was revealed by biotinylated secondary antibodies and ABC kit (Vector; #PK6101) with diaminobenzidine (DAB, Sigma-Aldrich; #D4293) or fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, goat anti-rat 647 #A-21247; goat anti-mouse 594 #A-11032; and goat anti-rabbit 488 #A48282). To denature DNA allowing BrdU antibodies to bind, brain sections were preincubated in 2 N HCl at 37 °C for 30 min followed by three times 1-min washes in sodium borate solution (pH = 8.5) to neutralize before incubation with the primary antibody. Double immunolabeling of BrdU and NeuN colocalization was checked 14 days post-BrdU injections to examine dividing stem cells/neurons in the dentate gyrus differentiation. Similarly, colocalization of BrdU and NeuN was examined as indicative of long-term neuron survival after 42 days since the initial birthmark with the exogenous BrdU incorporation. Brain sections from littermate mice not injected with BrdU were used as negative controls.

The Fiji plug-in cell counter was used to score the number of marker-positive cells with the punctate signals from Ki67 or BrdU. DCX immunostaining signal localized to the neuronal soma was used primarily as the determinant. DCX, Ki67, and BrdU signals were eventually normalized to the area of the entire dentate gyrus surface area. The “Polygon” function in Fiji was used to include the inner layer of the subgranular zone and close the two edge points to form a triangle covering the entire dentate gyrus.

Cell cultures, Total RNA Extraction, and RT-qPCR

SHSY-5Y neuroblastoma cells were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of MEM (Gibco; #11965-092) and F12 medium (Gibco; #11765054) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biowest; #S181), non-essential amino acids (Gibco; #11140-050), and penicillin 100 units/ml and streptomycin 100 µg/ml (Gibco; #15140-122) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were treated with 1 µM ATRA or vehicle (1 µM DMSO) for 24 h before they were collected in RNAase-free conditions. Total RNA was extracted from the cell homogenate using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen; #15596026) according to the manufacturer’s manual. RNA in the aqueous phase was further purified using RNeasy spin columns (Qiagen), and its concentration was measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was conducted using qScript cDNA SuperMix (QuantaBiol; #95048) starting from 700 ng of total RNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Analysis of RNA expression was performed on a QuantStudio 3 (Applied Biosystems) with TaqMan probes under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min. Three biological replicates and two technical replicates for each sample were used. A total of 10 ng of each sample was used in each real-time PCR. The expression ratio of the target genes was calculated using the HPRT1 (IDT Primetime: hGPRC5B, Hs.PT.58.38841806; HPRT1, Hs.PT.58v.45621572) as reference using the 2−ΔΔCT method [54].

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-way ANOVA along with Tukey’s post hoc tests were used for comparing multiple means from more than two groups. Student’s t-test was used to compare means of two groups, except for Wilcoxon-rank sum test used to compare frequency of distribution of Gprc5b. Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.2.3). Wilcoxon-rank sum test was conducted using MATLAB (R2024a Academic Use). Z emotionality (Z score) was calculated as described [55]. This is to integrate mobility of tail suspension test, percentage of sucrose preference of sucrose preference test, and grooming time of sucrose splash test into a single dimension. For each test, individual Z score was calculated by z=x-μσ, where x represents individual value, µ the mean of the control group, and σ the standard deviation of the control group. Average of the three tests were then calculated as the individual emotionality score.

Results

Naïve Gprc5b KO Mice Exhibit a Mild Antidepressant-Like Phenotype

To test our hypothesis that GPRC5B plays a role in mediating chronic RA-induced depressive-like behaviors, we first generated a global Gprc5b KO mouse line by germline expression of Cre recombinase targeting the second exon bearing the start codon of the Gprc5b gene (Fig. 1a). The effective ablation of GPRC5B expression was confirmed at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels, respectively, by genotyping, fluorescent RNAscope in situ hybridization, as well as immunoblot (Fig. 1b-–e). Next, we conducted a battery of behavioral assays consisting of the tail suspension test (TST), sucrose splash test (SuST), and sucrose preference test (SPT) to examine coping-, motivational-, and anhedonia-like behaviors. Following the chronology of testing, we observed a trend towards an antidepressant-like behavior suggested by the decreased immobility during TST in KO compared to WT when both sexes were pooled (p = 0.0812, Fig. 1f). However, no significant differences in immobility between WT and KO mice during TST were observed in male and female mice analyzed separately. Moreover, we report a significant increase in the grooming time of female Gprc5b KO mice during SuST (Fig. 1g). Grooming time during SuST remained comparable between WT and KO for male and sex-pooled data (Fig. 1g). Importantly, we observed an elevated sucrose preference in male Gprc5b KO mice compared to their WT littermates, while no significant differences were observed between female WT and KO as well as between WT and KO pooled mice (Fig. 1h). We further analyzed our data adopting a z-score to combine indexes of coping, motivation, and anhedonia-like behaviors and found that the ablation of GPRC5B significantly elevated the z-emotionality score in both female and male mice, analyzed separately or in combination, indicative of antidepressant-like effects (Fig. 1i). To obtain a more comprehensive characterization of Gprc5b KO mice behavior and rule out possible confounding effects, we performed a series of tests to assess locomotor behaviors and anxiety-like behavior (Supplementary Fig. 1). We report no significant differences between WT and Gprc5b KO mice in locomotor activity over time, total distance traveled, and jump counts in male and female groups. Additionally, we probed anxiety-like behaviors in WT and Gprc5b KO mice using a battery of tests including the elevated plus maze, marble burying test, center over edge ratio in the open field test, and stress-induced hyperthermia test (Supplementary Fig. 2). This dataset showed a significant reduction in the time spent in the open arms in female Gprc5b KO mice and in the combined analysis, while all the other parameters measured were not different between WT and Gprc5b KO for both male and female mice.

A computational approach using the software package DeepLabCut [51, 52] was used to extract pose information from open field recordings. To bring the data into context, an unsupervised learning algorithm from the package Keypoint-MoSeq [53] was applied to the pose data to find and cluster distinct behaviors or syllables (SupplementalFig. 3a). No significant differences were detected in the overall composition of behavioral syllables of naïve WT and Gprc5b KO animals using permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, see the “Materials and Methods” section). Tests were run on pooled WT and Gprc5b KO (F(1,33) = 0.729, R2 = 0.022, p = 0.533, permutations = 4999) as well as WT and Gprc5b KO groups separated by sex (F(3,31) = 0.585, R2 = 0.054, p = 0.846, permutations = 4999) with no significant effect in either case (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Loss of GPRC5B Occludes Chronic Retinoic Acid-Induced Depressive-Like Behaviors

GPRC5B was originally found to be a retinoic acid-inducible receptor in several cell lines [56]. We first confirmed this data by quantifying Gprc5b mRNA levels after treating neuroblastoma SHSY-5Y cells with 1 µM ATRA for 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, in vivo studies have shown that chronic ATRA administration in mice induces depressive-like behaviors [8, 8–16]. To replicate these findings, we performed daily i.p. injections with 10 mg/kg of ATRA in naïve C57BL/6 mice throughout 28 days and assessed mouse behavior using SPT, SuST, TST, and OFR (Supplementary Fig. 5a). We found that chronic administration of ATRA did not reduce sucrose preference in the SPT compared to the vehicle group in both male and female mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Previous literature reported mixed observations regarding the effect of chronic retinoic acid administration on SPT [8–10, 12–14, 57, 58]. We then tested the same cohort of mice in SuST to examine stress-sensitive reward behaviors. Chronic ATRA decreased grooming time dramatically in SuST for males, females, and in the combined analysis, suggesting no health deterioration due to chronic injections (Supplementary Fig. 5c-d). Additionally, ATRA increased the immobility time in the TST in the male group and in the combined male and female group (Supplementary Fig. 5e-f). Importantly, body weight gain was not significantly different between vehicle and ATRA groups in both female and male cohorts during the 4 weeks of injections (Supplementary Fig. 5 g), suggesting no health deterioration due to chronic injections. Taken together, our results replicate previous findings that ATRA induces depression-related behaviors in mice and suggest that male mice may be more sensitive to these effects.

We then subjected our male Gprc5b KO mice and their wild-type littermates to chronic treatment with ATRA or vehicle, following our established protocol with the exclusion of SPT. Again, we observed that chronic retinoic acid administration induced a significant reduction in the grooming time in SuST in wild-type mice (Fig. 1j). Strikingly, we observed that the loss of GPRC5B completely occluded the response to chronic ATRA (Fig. 1j). Similarly, we found a significant increase in TST immobility time induced by ATRA that was not displayed by Gprc5b KO mice (Fig. 1k). TST immobility time in vehicle-treated Gprc5b KO mice was significantly lower than in vehicle-treated wild-type littermates (Fig. 1k). We did not observe any anxiety-related phenotype induced by ATRA treatments suggested by a comparable time spent in the center zone in the open field test among the four groups (Fig. 1l). Importantly, we also did not observe any effect of chronic ATRA treatments on locomotor behaviors in both wild-type and Gprc5b KO mice ruling out possible confounding phenotypes (Fig. 1m). Overall, our data suggest that GPRC5B plays a significant role in mediating the depressive-like phenotypes induced by chronic administration with ATRA.

Chronic Exposure to Retinoic Acid Increases Expression of Gprc5b in the Niche of Adult Hippocampal Neurogenesis (AHN)

Considering that RA controls GPRC5B expression in several cell lines, we hypothesized that chronic treatment in mice could up-regulate its expression in brain regions relevant to the pathophysiology of major depression disorders. Therefore, we collected tissue punches from the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), ventral striatum (vSTR), hippocampus (HP), and hypothalamus (HY) from WT mice chronically treated with vehicle or ATRA and performed western blot analysis using specific antibodies that we previously validated [36]. Our results, obtained from male mice, showed that total protein levels of GPRC5B were not significantly altered in mPFC, vSTR, HP, and HY after chronic treatment with ATRA (Fig. 2a-b). We reasoned that western blot analysis does not enable cellular resolution and we previously reported an enrichment of Gprc5b mRNA expression in the main brain areas of adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) [36]. Therefore, we took advantage of RNAscope single-cell resolution to examine Gprc5b expression levels in response to chronic ATRA treatment in neuronal progenitors present in the hippocampal subgranular zone. Here, we adopted probes against Rbfox3 (NeuN) as a marker of mature neurons and Dcx (doublecortin) as a marker of immature neurons (Fig. 2c-d 2c–d). As expected, we found that chronic administration of ATRA significantly reduced the number of Dcx-positive cells (Fig. 2e). Intriguingly, we showed that after 28 d of chronic ATRA administration, the percentage of cells expressing both Gprc5b and Dcx significantly increased from 87.12% ± 2.15% to 92.48% ± 1.28% (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, we examined how chronic ATRA administration affected Gprc5b expression levels in Dcx-positive cells. Our frequency distribution analysis revealed a significant up-regulation of Gprc5b expression after chronic ATRA treatments compared to control mice treated with vehicle (Fig. 2g). To extend our study, we applied the same analysis to mature neurons that we identified as expressing NeuN mRNA using RNAscope. This analysis showed that the number of NeuN-positive cells was not affected by chronic ATRA treatments (Fig. 2h). Similarly, the percentage of Gprc5b-positive mature neurons (NeuN-positive) was also unaffected (Fig. 2i). Finally, our frequency distribution analysis of Gprc5b expression in NeuN-positive cells did not reveal a significant effect due to ATRA (Fig. 2j). Additionally, a similar analysis has been performed in the CA1 region of the hippocampus, where the expression of Gprc5b is lower than that observed in DG. These results again showed a lack of Gprc5b regulation by ATRA treatments in NeuN mature neurons (Supplementary Fig. 6). Altogether, these results indicate that chronic ATRA treatments significantly increased Gprc5b expression in premature neurons but not in mature neurons in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus.

Ablation of GPRC5B Blocks Chronic Retinoic Acid-Mediated Inhibition of AHN

Our observation that Gprc5b levels are significantly modulated by ATRA in the AHN niche led us to hypothesize that genetic ablation of GPRC5B could have an impact on adult hippocampal neurogenesis. In agreement with previous reports [9], we found that chronic treatments with ATRA induced a significant reduction in DCX+ neuroblast density in WT mice (Fig. 3a-b). Interestingly, chronic ATRA did not reduce the number of DCX+ cells in Gprc5b KO mice (Fig. 3a-b). To test whether Gprc5b KO mice lack a reduction in DCX+ cell density in the SGZ after chronic ATRA treatment is due to a larger reservoir of Ki67+ activated but not quiescent neural stem cells, we conducted immunohistochemical labeling of this endogenously expressed proliferation marker [59]. We found that chronic treatments with ATRA did not significantly affect the number of premature neurons expressing Ki67 (Fig. 3c–d). We also observed a lower number of Ki67-positive cells in the vehicle-treated Gprc5b KO mice, while chronic ATRA produced a trend (p = 0.052) toward an increased number in the Gprc5b KO mice (Fig. 3c-d). Taken together, these findings indicate that although GPRC5B KO mice have fewer immature neuronal progenitors (Ki67+), the differentiation and/or maturation processes into neuronal precursors (DCX+) are more efficient compared to WT mice. Furthermore, chronic ATRA administration did not suppress adult neurogenesis in mice lacking GPRC5B expression.

GPRC5B Genetic Ablation Promotes NSC Differentiation and Long-Term Survival of Adult-Born Neurons in the SGZ

Given that the loss of GPRC5B hinders the capability of ATRA to reduce the number of DCX+ neuroblasts, we hypothesized that GPRC5B could serve as a molecular brake of neurogenesis. Therefore, GPRC5B ablation could accelerate the neural differentiation from NSC to DCX+ premature neurons. To test this hypothesis, we adopted the classical paradigms of proliferation and long-term survival of BrdU/NeuN labeling [60, 61] (Fig. 4c). At 14 days post-injections (dpi), male Gprc5b KO mice exhibited a significantly higher number of BrdU+/NeuN+ cell density, suggesting that the ablation of GPRC5B promotes short-term survival of newborn neurons (Fig. 4a and d). Strikingly, at 42 dpi, while WT mice displayed close to zero BrdU+/NeuN+ cell density, their Gprc5b KO mouse counterparts sustained a BrdU+/NeuN+ cell density comparable to what was measured at 14 dpi (Fig. 4b and d–e). In the adult hippocampus, neurogenesis is counterbalanced by apoptosis, particularly during the progenitor-to-neuroblast transition [62, 63]. Microglia help regulate this process by clearing apoptotic cells, ensuring that only properly integrated neurons survive. We quantified cell death in hippocampal sections from WT and Gprc5b KO mice and did not find any difference in the level of apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 7a-d). Moreover, the deletion of Gprc5b did not induce a significant alteration in the overall hippocampal DG morphology (Supplementary Fig. 7e-g). The evidence suggests that the loss of GPRC5B prevents the chronic ATRA-induced reduction in DCX+ cell density by increasing the rate of NSC differentiation, albeit from a smaller NSC pool. Furthermore, data from 6 weeks post-BrdU injections indicate that the greater number of DCX+ immature neurons in Gprc5b KO mice originated from a smaller pool of Ki67+ NSCs that exhibited prolonged survival compared to their WT littermates.

Discussion

GPRC5B is a brain-enriched orphan GPCR that concentrates in adult neurogenic niches including the SGZ of the hippocampal dentate gyrus, but its function remains to be elucidated [36, 56, 64]. We here produced a comprehensive behavioral characterization of a global Gprc5b KO mouse line. Our observations related to the viability of these mice confirmed previous reports of a partial lethality before weaning age [40, 42]. Although a previous report suggested that Gprc5b KO mice have reduced spontaneous activity 1 h before light onset, with overall similar spontaneous activity in both light and dark cycles, and increased total distance traveled and center-over-edge time in the open field test [40], our results demonstrated an overall locomotor phenotype resembling that of wild-type mice. Our results further explored depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors in naïve Gprc5b KO mice and suggest a possible role of Gprc5b in counteracting the development of depressive-like phenotypes. GPRC5B transcriptional inducer, RA, when administered chronically, leads to suppression of adult neurogenesis and depressive-like behaviors in rodents [8, 8–16]. This is surprising since RA is a main component applied to generate specific types of neurons from either embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) including NSCs in vitro [65–67]. AHN inhibition by RA or stress is a process that has been associated with learning and affective-like phenotypes in rodents [9, 17, 68, 69]. Although a causal link between alterations in adult neurogenesis and the development of depressive symptoms is still debated, it has been shown that recovery of deficits in adult neurogenesis is required for antidepressants to exert their effects [70–74]. Additionally, many correlative studies suggest a link between stress-inhibited adult neurogenesis and the development of depressive-like phenotypes in animal models [60, 75–77]. Therefore, in this study, we tested the hypothesis that GPRC5B plays a role in the molecular and cellular mechanisms of chronic RA-induced depressive-like behaviors. Consistent with this hypothesis, we report that ablation of GPRC5B in mice completely blocks RA-induced depressive-like behaviors, whereas a mild antidepressant-like phenotype is found in Gprc5b KO naïve mice. Intriguingly, we also observed that Gprc5b KO mice subjected to daily vehicle injections exhibited a more pronounced reduction in immobility time in the TST compared to naïve mice. Since repeated injections can serve as a mild chronic stressor, potentially increasing immobility in WT mice, these findings suggest that the Gprc5b KO strain may possess stress resilience. Exploring this role of GPRC5B will require further studies. Furthermore, chronic RA treatments significantly increased Gprc5b mRNA expression in DCX-expressing cells of the adult SGZ neurogenic niche, and our histological analysis revealed that ablation of GPRC5B protects mice from chronic RA-induced inhibition of adult neurogenesis. Finally, a longitudinal study using BrdU/NeuN double immunostaining unveiled that GPRC5B suppresses neural differentiation and long-term survival.

Chronic administration of RA induces depressive-like behaviors in rodents. This is corroborated by several preclinical studies using different administration protocols, sex and age of rodents, and different endpoint behavioral tests [8, 8–16]. However, there is also a limited number of reports claiming that chronic RA treatments do not induce measurable depressive-like phenotypes [57, 78]. In the current study, we introduced SuST in addition to TST to further validate the behavioral effects of chronic RA treatments. Still, using our ATRA administration protocol, we could not detect significant effects in the SPT. Changes in sucrose preference in response to ATRA have not been consistently reported in the literature, an effect likely related to the restricted expression of RA receptors across neuronal circuits [79–82]. Finally, the present study confirms the significance of using young adult mice when examining the effects of RA. Notably, most of the studies that reported RA did not induce depressive-like behaviors utilized adult mice aged 3–6 months, whereas those that observed such behaviors used young adult mice around 1–2 months old, in alignment with the clinical administration timeline of RA in patients with severe acne. In summary, our results align with previous studies, as we observed a significant increase in immobility in TST and a significant reduction in grooming time in the SuST following ATRA administration in young adult mice. Additionally, we provide novel perspectives by reporting that young adult female mice phenocopy behavioral responses observed in their age-matched male counterparts in the SuST.

Our immunohistochemistry studies confirm that chronic RA administration suppresses adult hippocampal neurogenesis, reducing the number of early post-mitotic migrating DCX-positive neurons, which correlates with the induction of depressive-like behaviors in mice [9, 69]. Additionally, we found that chronic RA does not significantly affect the number of Ki67-positive stem/progenitor cells. Neurogenesis is a multi-step process including type 1 cell proliferation through type 2 cells differentiated to post-mitotic type 3 migrating neuroblasts. Given that Ki67 expression is restricted to type 1 proliferating cells, our results indicate that chronic RA is ineffective on type 1 stem/progenitor cells, while it suppresses type 3 neuroblasts that are migrating and post-mitotic. This aligns with previous observations that the number of Ki67+ neural stem cells is not altered in postmortem hippocampal tissues from MDD patients compared to age-matched controls [83]. One possible explanation is that the adult hippocampal pool of stem cells is spared from the effects of RA. Once they commit to neurons, astrocytes, or oligodendrocytes, i.e., in the early postmitotic maturation phase, they become susceptible to RA likely by increasing the expression of RA receptors [84]. Overall, our data suggest an effect of RA on differentiation and not on proliferation. A thorough analysis of how RA signaling controls adult hippocampal neurogenesis will require the development of better temporal-resolved and refined markers for understanding this multi-step process. In this direction, recent high-throughput single-cell transcriptomic studies could assist in the selection of markers that will lead to a more precise cell identity definition [85]. A provocative possibility is that GPRC5B expression occurs exclusively during post-differentiation stages, rather than during early stem cell maturation, thereby preventing RA from regulating this process. This hypothesis warrants further investigation through single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. Our study suggests that loss of GPRC5B promotes cell differentiation and enhances long-term neuronal survival, whereas it does not affect stem cell proliferation. Intriguingly, one study suggests that depletion of GPRC5B renders embryonic cortical progenitors unable to develop into neurons [86]. Overall, GPRC5B appears to be involved in the cellular mechanisms regulating neurogenesis, with potential implications for therapies targeting depression, neurodegenerative disorders, and brain injury repair. However, a limitation of our studies using global genetic ablation of GPRC5B is the inability to delineate the cell type-specific contributions of GPRC5B expressed within different cell populations of the SGZ and in circuitries that converge here.

The crosstalk between retinoid signaling and GPCR signaling is understudied [87–89]. Consistent with previous publications, our qPCR results confirm that in neuroblastoma cells, RA significantly up-regulates the expression of the orphan GPCR Gprc5b. Additionally, our RNAscope data revealed that in vivo, Gprc5b is co-expressed with Dcx in neural progenitors and that chronic RA administration selectively increases Gprc5b expression in the hippocampal DG immature neuroblasts. These data support a role of GPRC5B in mediating RA-induced inhibition of adult neurogenesis. Considering that our analysis adopted a global Gprc5b KO mouse, future studies will explore the cell-autonomous role of GPRC5B in regulating these cellular aspects of adult hippocampal neurogenesis. Our analysis also suggests that GPRC5B controls signaling pathways involved in the process of proliferation and/or differentiation affected by RA signaling. Future deorphanization studies and elucidation of GPRC5B signaling properties will facilitate our understanding of the crosstalk between RA signaling and GPCR signaling. We previously showed that GPRC5B overexpression does not elicit measurable G protein activation using several luciferase reporter assays [90]. Similarly, the Presto-Tango assay did not show a constitutive recruitment of β-arrestin [91]. These findings suggest that GPRC5B either exhibits constitutive activity below detectable levels or does not engage traditional GPCR transducers. The latter hypothesis has been proposed to explain challenges in deorphanization endeavors [92]. Notably, two independent groups identified Fyn kinase, a member of the Src family kinases, as an interacting partner of GPRC5B and a potential non-canonical transducer of GPRC5B signaling [93, 94]. Recently, the transcriptional co-activator Yes1-associated protein 1 (Yap1), a classical target of this family of kinases, has gained attention as a key promoter of the transition from quiescence to activation of adult hippocampal neural stem cells [95]. The Hippo-Yap pathway is an evolutionarily conserved kinase cascade that regulates the activity of its effector proteins, including Yap1, causing their cytoplasmic retention and degradation [96]. Several cellular processes converge on the regulation of this kinase cascade, including G protein signaling [97, 98]. GPRC5B control over the Hippo-Yap pathway in SGZ neuronal progenitors could thus represent a molecular mechanism underlying RA-dependent responses that will require further investigation. Signaling mechanisms involving GPRC5B interaction with ligand-activated GPCRs are also emerging and provide alternative routes for GPRC5B to control downstream effectors that could be involved in the regulation of adult hippocampal neurogenesis [37, 39].

In conclusion, our study identifies the orphan receptor GPRC5B as a functional mediator of RA-induced behavioral phenotypes. Additionally, at the cellular level, it further places GPRC5B as a negative regulator of stem cell differentiation and adult-born neuronal survival in the hippocampus, a role that can possibly underlie RA-induced behavioral effects observed (Fig. 5). Ultimately, unlocking the role of this orphan GPCR could be of significant therapeutic interest in the development of novel antidepressants, in therapies for neurodegenerative diseases, as well as brain injury interventions. Future studies may focus on perturbations of GPRC5B specifically in neural progenitors in the SGZ to confirm a causal relationship between depletion of GPRC5B in newborn adult neurons and depressive-like behaviors in mice.
