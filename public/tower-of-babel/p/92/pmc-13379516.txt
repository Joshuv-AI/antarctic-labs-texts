Use of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL to Evaluate Health‐Related Quality of Life following Unilateral Subthalamic Deep Brain Stimulation for Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Sarah A Brinkerhoff, Frank Robinson, Roy C Martin, Sandra Morales, Christopher L Gonzalez, Christopher P Hurt, Melissa Wade, Gary Cutter, Gitendra Uswatte, Harrison C Walker, Victor A Del Bene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN DBS) improves Parkinson's disease (PD)‐related motor symptoms, but how STN DBS impacts various aspects of patient‐perceived health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) is less clear. The Patient‐Reported Outcome Measurement Instrument (PROMIS) and Quality of Life in Neurological Disorders (Neuro‐QoL), National Institutes of Health computer adaptive inventories, are T‐scored to a normative reference sample, and assesses 16 domains of a patient's HRQoL.ObjectivesThis cross‐sectional study evaluated the convergent validity of a novel PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate measure, the construct validity of the aggregate and 16 PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL domains, and the responsiveness of these measures to change after STN DBS.MethodsTwenty eight people with PD completed the PROMIS, Neuro‐QoL, Parkinson's disease Questionnaire‐8 (PDQ‐8), and the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale part III before surgery for STN DBS and at two follow‐up visits within 6 months after surgery. A Pearson correlation assessed the relationship between PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate and the PDQ‐8, to determine convergent validity. One‐sample t tests compared preoperative scores to normative means. Finally, one‐sample t tests compared preoperative HRQoL and HRQoL after STN DBS.ResultsThe PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate agreed strongly with the PDQ‐8, providing convergent validation. Preoperatively, patients reported significantly worse‐than‐normative values of overall HRQoL, physical function, cognitive function, social health, fatigue, and mental health, providing construct validation. After DBS, the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate, physical function, cognitive function, social health, and sleep improved, demonstrating responsiveness to change of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL.ConclusionsOverall, these findings support the potential integration of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL in clinical and research settings in patients with PD to provide valuable insights into the patient's lived experience during DBS therapy.Keywords: deep brain stimulation, neuropsychology, Parkinson's disease, quality of life, subthalamic nucleus

Patient perception of health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) after deep brain stimulation (DBS) for Parkinson's disease (PD) is a critical aspect of clinical care, but is under‐represented in DBS research. Although the emphasis on motor symptoms is central to PD treatment, the patient's experience is equally important, particularly in an era of precision medicine and individualized treatment. 1 HRQoL domains assessed vary widely. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 There is need for a brief, multi‐domain, norm‐referenced measure of patient‐reported HRQoL that can be completed at clinical visits or self‐administered at home. Such a tool would permit longitudinal tracking of subjective responses to DBS.

The National Institute of Health Patient‐Reported Outcome Measurement Instrument (PROMIS) and the Quality of Life in Neurological Disorders (Neuro‐QoL) batteries are validated tools to assess HRQoL across a range of domains in chronic diseases. They are brief, taking less than 15 minutes each to complete. 10 , 11 The PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL address multiple clinical research needs: they are disease agnostic, minimize participant burden, and are normed to healthy and/or general clinical populations, yielding easily interpretable T‐scores. The PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL data are collected digitally with computer adaptive methods (pen‐and‐paper formats are also available).

Despite their clinical utility in other populations, the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL have been seldom used in patients with PD. On measures other than PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL, patients with PD report mild improvements in HRQoL after subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS. 12 The Neuro‐QoL has been validated in PD 13 and reports from the PROMIS and the Neuro‐QoL converge with other measures. On the PROMIS, patients with PD reported worse physical function, 14 , 15 pain interference, 15 and sleep than population norms or matched controls, 16 consistent with prior research in PD. 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 On the Neuro‐QoL, patients with PD reported worse upper extremity function, 13 , 17 emotional and behavioral dyscontrol, 13 cognitive function, 17 and lower extremity function, 17 also consistent with prior research in PD. 2 , 4 , 6 , 8 , 9 Reports from these other instruments are an array of largely single‐domain self‐report measures without norm‐referenced scores, such as the Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire‐39, Hamilton Anxiety and Depression Rating Scales, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and the Non‐Motor Symptoms Scale. A unified, computer‐based, norm‐referenced, measure of multi‐domain HRQoL would enable simple implementation in clinical settings or, when desirable, patient self‐administration at home.

No study has yet assessed the complete PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL in PD‐DBS surgical patients, or whether the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL can detect changes in HRQoL in people with PD in response to DBS therapy. This study aims to evaluate the validity the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL for assessing preoperative HRQoL and their responsiveness to postoperative unilateral STN DBS changes in HRQoL for people with PD. We tested the following hypotheses: preoperative HRQoL (PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL) will correlate with validated measures of PD motor severity and HRQoL; patients will report worse‐than‐normative HRQoL at preoperative baseline; and patient‐reported symptoms from the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL will be sensitive to DBS‐related changes.

Methods

Patients were eligible if they were between 18 and 70 years old; had a disease duration of 4 or more years; were recommended for unilateral STN DBS as part of routine clinical care from a multidisciplinary committee (neurology, neurosurgery, neuropsychology, and advanced practice providers); showed more than 30% improvement in the Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale III (MDS‐UPDRS III) 18 scores compared to the “off” state (after >12 hours without dopaminergic medication) during a preoperative screening visit; and had a Hoehn and Yahr classification >1, a Dementia Rating Scale‐2 score of ≥130 of 144, and a Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI‐II) score of ≤25. Complete inclusion/exclusion criteria are available at clinicaltrials.gov, NCT03353688.

This analysis was carried out as a subset of data from Subthalamic Nucleus Directional Stimulation (SUNDIAL), a randomized, cross‐over, double‐blind clinical investigation funded by the National Institutes of Health BRAIN Initiative (NCT03353688; UH3NS100553). Sample size was determined based on the clinical trial primary outcome. Ethical approval was granted by both the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the institutional review board of the University of Alabama at Birmingham, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Surgical Intervention

Our surgical protocol is to treat the more severely affected hemibody with unilateral DBS first, with the option for a contralateral implant later if needed. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 The neurosurgical team implanted directional “1‐3‐3‐1” DBS leads (Boston Scientific Vercise DBS system, Natick, MA, FDA IDE G170063) at the STN with patients awake and under local anesthesia, save for midazolam 1 to 2 mg during placement of the frame. Targeting used standard methods combining intraoperative O‐arm computed tomography (CT) images co‐registered to volumetric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), single‐ or multi‐pass microelectrode recordings, macrostimulation, and trial stimulation with the implanted lead. The surgical procedure is described in detail previously. 19 The interval between lead implantation and device activation averaged 35.2 ± 21.5 days.

Preoperative and Postoperative Visits

Device programming was performed at the University of Alabama at Birmingham outpatient neurology clinic and guided by monopolar review measuring the therapeutic window and motor function from each of the eight contacts on the lead plus each of the three‐contact–pairings in the two middle rows (ie, “virtual rings”). 23 Participants returned for two follow‐up visits to the Human Performance Laboratory and the outpatient neurology clinic at the University of Alabama at Birmingham: visit 1, scheduled for 2 months after device implant, and visit 2, scheduled for 4 months after device implant. At initial device activation, they were randomized to either directional or ring stimulation and returned for testing on this setting for visit 1. At the end of visit 1, they were allocated to the stimulation setting they had not yet received and returned for testing for visit 2. Regardless of stimulation setting, the goal of device programming was to maximize motor improvements without side effects. Participants and research staff were blinded to stimulation setting, save for the nurse practitioner who programmed the device (M.W.).

Outcome Measures

At preoperative baseline, motor symptoms were measured by the MDS‐UPDRS III total score while “off” dopaminergic medications for at least 12 hours. 19 , 24

At preoperative baseline and the two follow‐up visits, participants self‐administered four instruments: the Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire‐8 (PDQ‐8), an eight‐item HRQoL self‐assessment for people with PD adapted from the longer PDQ‐39 25 ; the MDS‐UPDRS II, a 13‐item self‐assessment of motor experiences of daily living 18 ; and the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL. PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL used computerized adaptive testing and results are reported as T‐scores (M (mean) = 50, SD (standard deviation) = 10), 26 , 27 which were normed to the general population and/or a clinical sample (consisting of stroke [n = 209], epilepsy [n = 183]; multiple sclerosis [n = 84], PD [n = 59]; and ALS [n = 18]). All except three domains were normed to the general population: PROMIS sleep disturbance was normed to the general population + clinical sample, and Neuro‐QoL stigma and Neuro‐QoL emotional dyscontrol were normed to the clinical sample.

The 16 subdomains (9 PROMIS, 7 Neuro‐QoL) were separated into nine items describing function (higher T‐scores indicated better HRQoL) and seven items describing symptoms (lower T‐scores indicated better HRQoL). PROMIS function items were: physical function, global mental health, global physical health, and satisfaction with social roles and activities. Neuro‐QoL function items were: cognitive function, upper extremity function, lower extremity function, ability to participate in social roles and activities, and positive affect. PROMIS symptom items were: fatigue, pain interference, sleep disturbance, anxiety, and depression. Neuro‐QoL symptom items were: stigma and emotional dyscontrol. Ten missing subdomains scores were imputed from 2772 data points (0.36% missing).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were completed in R. 28 , 29 A priori significance was set at α = 0.05. Family‐wise error‐correction is described below. Tests were run separately for the PDQ‐8, MDS‐UPDRS II, the 16 subdomains, and a calculated PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate measure, which was calculated by reversing the scale for symptoms measures so that higher scores always indicated better HRQoL and averaging the 16 subdomains. This aggregate measure acted as an exploratory composite of disease‐neutral HRQoL.

First, we assessed the convergent validity of the PROMIS + Neuro‐QOL aggregate using Pearson correlations with preoperative PDQ‐8, MDS‐UPDRS II, and MDS‐UPDRS III scores. Pearson r values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 denoted small, medium, and large effects, respectively. 30

Next, we assessed the construct validity of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QOL domain scores. We used one‐sample t tests to determine if our sample had worse preoperative HRQoL than the normative populations. For the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL outcomes, the sample mean was compared to T = 50. For the PDQ‐8, the sample mean was compared to 17% (associated with a Hoehn and Yahr score of ≥1 31 , 32 ). For the MDS‐UPDRS II, the sample mean was compared to a score of 13 (associated with moderate or severe PD). 33

Finally, we assessed the responsiveness of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QOL to DBS. We calculated the average of the visit 1 and visit 2 scores to get a single postoperative score for each participant. We first tested if the change in levodopa daily equivalent dose (LEDD) affected the change from preoperative score to postoperative score in the PDQ‐8, the MDS‐UPDRS II, and the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate. If LEDD change significantly affected responsiveness of any of the overall HRQoL measures, we covaried for LEDD change across all measures using analysis of covariances. If LEDD change did not affect responsiveness, we used one‐sample two‐sided t tests to determine if postoperative scores were significantly different from preoperative scores. Responsiveness was primarily assessed by the results of the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)/t tests, but we also computed one‐sided binomial tests to determine if a significant proportion of the sample improved regardless of the magnitude of improvement. For the HRQoL items that significantly improved after DBS, we calculated the percentage of patients who achieved a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) change from preoperative to average postoperative. For the PDQ‐8 we used an MCID of −5.94, 34 for the MDS‐UPDRS II we used an MCID of −3.05, 35 and for the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL items, we used an MCID of T = 5. 36 , 37

After determining responsiveness, we sought to understand the effect of preoperative state on the magnitude of change after DBS. We regressed postoperative scores on preoperative scores and compared the slope of the relationship between preoperative and postoperative scores to y = 1. If the slope was not different from 1, then preoperative HRQoL did not affect the magnitude of change after DBS. If the slope was significantly less than 1, individuals with worse preoperative status experienced greater improvements.

P‐values were adjusted with a Benjamini‐Hochberg adjustment for multiple comparisons, and those with a false discovery rate (FDR) q < 0.05 were considered significant. The FDR correction was done on each set of the nine tests of function subdomains and the seven tests of symptom subdomains, for the convergent validity analysis, responsiveness analysis, and analysis of the relationship between preoperative and postoperative scores. We report both corrected and uncorrected scores in the supplemental material, as the clinical use of these measures is conducted at the individual level, not at a group level after statistical corrections.

Construct Validity: Participants' Preoperative PROMIS and Neuro‐QOL Scores Compared to Normative Samples

Participants reported poor overall HRQoL before DBS surgery, evidenced by worse‐than‐normative PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate (p = 0.003) and PDQ‐8 scores (P = 0.012, Cohen's d = 0.52) (Figs. 3 and 4). They also reported lower HRQoL on many items of function (Fig. 3): physical function (P < 0.001, d = −0.79), upper extremity function (P < 0.001, d = −1.37), lower extremity function (P = 0.019, d = −0.47), cognitive function (P < 0.001, d = −1.33), global physical health (P = 0.005, d = −0.58), global mental health (P = 0.012, d = −0.51), and social satisfaction (P < 0.001, d = −1.15). Symptom‐wise, participants had worse Fatigue than normative populations (P < 0.001, d = 0.91) (Fig. 4). Participants' positive affect and well‐being was better than a normative sample (P < 0.001, d = 0.72) (Fig. 3). LEDD change from preoperative to postoperative did not affect responsiveness of the PDQ‐8, the MDS‐UPDRS II, or the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate score. Therefore, one‐way t tests were used to assess the responsiveness of all outcomes. Patients reported improvements after DBS surgery in the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate (P = 0.002, d = 0.35) (Fig. 5), the PDQ‐8 (P = 0.005, d = −0.44) (Fig. 6), and the MDS‐UPDRS II (P < 0.001, d = −0.64) (Fig. 6). Discussion

Patient‐reported outcomes from PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL offer critical insights into both motor and non‐motor symptoms from PD and should be integrated into precision medicine treatment plans. Population‐normed and easily administered, these measures capture the subjective experiences of PD‐related symptoms and everyday function. Several observations from our study in patients undergoing unilateral STN DBS support the broader clinical implementation of PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL. First, aggregate HRQoL scores correlated with validated measures of PD motor severity and HRQoL, thereby supporting the convergent validity of our aggregate calculation. Second, patients with PD reported low HRQoL versus population norms before DBS surgery, supporting the construct validity of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QOL. Finally, improvements in HRQoL, particularly in domains of motor, social, and cognitive function, were reported after unilateral STN DBS, supporting the responsiveness to change of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QOL.

Aggregate PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL scores correlated preoperatively with the PDQ‐8 and the MDS‐UPDRS II, which are both widely used disease‐specific measures for characterizing HRQoL and patient‐perceived experience of activities of daily living. The aggregate PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL also correlated with the MDS‐UPDRS III—considered the gold standard of motor function. This aggregate measure aligned well with disease‐specific self‐assessment of QoL and reflects the established relationship between HRQoL and motor symptom severity in people with PD. 38 , 39 , 40 Advantageously, our calculated aggregate score—while agreeing with validated measures of health in patients with PD—also reflects domains of health not fully measured by the PDQ‐8 or the MDS‐UPDRS II, such as sleep, anxiety, and fatigue. This aggregate PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL was designed to be exploratory omnibus composite measure to capture overall HRQoL from the 16 domains in the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL instruments. We validated this composite against the PDQ‐8 and the MDS‐UPDRS II to establish overall validity of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL items in capturing PD‐related issues and subsequently explored the responsiveness to DBS of the individual scales. In creating the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate, we did not have data to indicate weighting any scale(s) above others, and therefore, weighted each of the 16 scales equally.

Before unilateral STN DBS surgery, PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL captured low HRQoL. Patients reported not only low overall HRQoL (PDQ‐8 and the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate), but also lower‐than‐normative physical function, upper extremity function, lower extremity function, and global physical health. Using a T‐score of 40 (1 SD below the population mean) as the threshold for impairment, 64% of patients reported poor upper extremity function, compared to only 21% reporting poor lower extremity function and 14% reporting poor overall physical function. Because only approximately 16% of a normally distributed population would be expected to score this low, the markedly higher rate in poor upper extremity function indicates clinically meaningful impairment and suggests that the Neuro‐QoL upper extremity domain is more sensitive to PD than the PROMIS physical function domain. Nowinski and colleagues13 reported similar T‐scores of patient‐reported lower extremity function on the Neuro‐QoL. In addition to statistically low preoperative physical function, people with PD report lower cognitive function, global mental health, social satisfaction, and higher feelings of fatigue. Approximately one‐third of our sample self‐reported low preoperative cognitive function and one‐fourth reported low social satisfaction, similar to the prevalence reported by prior studies. 8 , 13 However, in our sample, fatigue affected 37% of our participants lower than the estimated 50% prevalence in PD. 41

This is among the first studies to report that some domains of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL are sensitive to change after unilateral STN DBS. People with PD reported improvements in overall HRQoL (PDQ‐8, MDS‐UPDRS II, and the PROMIS + Neuro‐QoL aggregate) approximately 4 months after unilateral STN DBS, regardless of whether they received directional or ring stimulation. Our findings are consistent with prior literature suggesting that people with PD without significant preoperative depression can judge their own HRQoL 42 and that unilateral STN DBS improves HRQoL. 4 , 43 Our findings also demonstrate that unilateral STN DBS stimulation setting does not have differential effects on HRQoL (Figs. S1 and S2), which is relevant for clinicians making hardware decisions. Self‐reported physical function and upper extremity function both significantly improved after unilateral STN DBS, consistent with MDS‐UPDRS III improvements, and 43% of the sample achieved MCID in self‐reported upper extremity function within 4 months post‐DBS. The PROMIS physical function item and the Neuro‐QoL upper extremity function item are both sensitive to change in motor function after DBS, as well as change because of PD progression. 17 Interestingly, self‐reported cognitive function also significantly improved after unilateral STN DBS and 43% of the sample achieved self‐reported MCID, despite objective cognitive function not changing (pooling left and right STN DBS together) after unilateral STN DBS, 24 , 44 and actually worsening after left‐hemisphere DBS. 24 More work is needed to assess the dissociation between objective and subjective cognitive function after STN DBS. Self‐reported mood symptoms remained stable postoperatively; however, the preoperative scores (anxiety T = 53.5 and global mental health T = 46.7) were within 1 SD from the population mean, therefore, these findings do not discount that STN DBS may have a mild positive effect on mood. 44 , 45 , 46

Surprisingly, the PD patients in this study reported higher levels of positive affect and well‐being before surgery than a normative sample without neurological injuries despite reporting worse function and symptoms than the normative sample. One possible explanation of this finding is that patients experienced heightened positive affect in anticipation of receiving DBS implantation. Another possibility is that this finding speaks to the ability of people to find meaning in the face of adversity. 47 Others have reported higher‐than‐expected levels of positive affect and well‐being in other populations with neurological injuries. 48 As discussed in the limitations section, we excluded patients with high BDI‐II scores, so there was a potential for a selection bias against patients with severe depression.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations. First, the power analysis to determine sample size was designed to assess the efficacy of directional versus ring unilateral STN DBS on motor outcomes as part of the parent study; therefore, the sample size (n = 28) may be underpowered to detect statistical significance. Second, our selection criteria excluded patients with elevated depression (>25/63) on the BDI‐II to remove the potential confounding effect of mood symptoms in the primary motor outcomes of the DBS randomized cross‐over trial. Therefore, it is possible that in a sample with a wider range of depression scores, PROMIS may be more sensitive for identifying depression than we report here. Third, our sample was not racially or ethnically diverse. A multi‐center study of 8514 patients with PD found that African American, Asian, and Hispanic patients with PD report worse HRQoL than white patients with PD on the PDQ‐39, 49 suggesting limited generalizability of this study and a caution toward interpreting that all people with PD have unremarkable depression, anxiety, feelings of stigma, and emotional dyscontrol. Fourth, our sample did not report deficits in gait, mobility, or balance at the group level before surgery, likely because of the young age (57 ± 7.2 years) and relative earliness in disease state (Hoehn and Yahr off‐medicine = 2.1 ± 0.4). Therefore, the lack of improvements in group‐level patient‐reported mobility and balance in our sample following unilateral DBS is unsurprising. Fifth, we conducted this study during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, which may have affected the completion of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL during follow‐up visits. Further investigation revealed that 8.1% of the in‐person visits and 6.8% of the telehealth visits had incomplete PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL forms, so in‐person or telehealth administration did not adversely affect the rates of completeness. However, confounding effects of the pandemic on HRQoL in people with chronic disease cannot be ruled out. Finally, although people with PD tend to recalibrate their assessments of past changes in HRQoL based on their current state, 50 our time frame (~4 months after DBS implant) was shorter than the hindsight recalibration period (1 year), and the evidence of significant group‐level improvements suggest a more‐accurate self‐reflection of HRQoL.

These limitations underscore the need for continued research into self‐reported symptoms in patients with PD and after DBS that consider diverse patients and methodologies, longer observation periods, and investigations in varied global contexts to gain a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between PD and patient‐reported health outcomes. This study also has several specific strengths. As part of the larger randomized crossover trial, our sample was well‐characterized and studied over multiple visits after DBS implantation. Additionally, clinicians and patients were both blinded to the DBS settings during each of the two follow‐up visits.

Conclusions

These findings from 28 patients in a single‐center study support potential clinical integration of the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL in movement disorders clinics to enhance our understanding of the effects of DBS therapy for PD and other movement disorders on the lives of our patients. These assessments can be administered in waiting rooms or at home between clinic visits and offer patient‐centered evaluations of HRQoL that complement the growing precision medicine emphasis in neurology and psychiatry. The forms are computer‐adaptive and, therefore, do not introduce an item order effect, and can also be completed quickly—9 minutes for the PROMIS and 11 minutes for the Neuro‐QoL short forms administered in this study with scores automatically calculated by the software 10 , 11 — comparable to the 10‐ to 15‐minute assessment time plus 5 to 7 minutes of rater calculation and data entry for the PDQ‐39. 51 Additionally, the PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL are disease agnostic and may be completed by all patients presenting to a movement disorders neuropsychology clinic. These measures identified areas of concern in HRQoL in PD, correlated with baseline motor disability and a PD‐specific HRQoL measure, and were responsive to changes in HRQoL following unilateral STN DBS. By complementing traditional assessments like the MDS‐UPDRS and PDQ‐8, PROMIS and Neuro‐QoL provide valuable insights into the patient's lived experience—underscoring that the patient is the ultimate arbiter of treatment success.
