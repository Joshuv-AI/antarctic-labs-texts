Perineal approach with penile invagination for one-stage oral mucosa graft urethroplasty in adults with hypospadias-associated strictures
Authors: Manuel Hevia, Ana Dominguez, Agustin Fraile, Javier Saenz-Medina, Rafael Rodríguez-Patrón, Eduardo Martin, Luis Cordoba, F Javier Burgos-Revilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrethral strictures in adults with a history of childhood hypospadias repair represent a complex reconstructive challenge due to altered anatomy, deficient spongiosum, and frequent use of penile skin in prior repairs. The aim of this study was to prospectively evaluate the anatomical, functional, and patient-reported outcomes of single-stage oral mucosa graft (OMG) urethroplasty performed through a perineal approach with penile invagination in adults with hypospadias-associated urethral strictures.MethodsThis prospective, single-center study included adult patients undergoing single-stage dorsal onlay OMG urethroplasty via a perineal approach with penile invagination (2022–2024). Outcomes were assessed using objective measures (urethral caliber and uroflowmetry) and urethral stricture surgery-patient-reported outcome measures (USS-PROMs). Anatomical success was defined as the ability to pass a 16 Fr catheter without resistance and no need for secondary urethral procedures. Functional success was defined as maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) >12 mL/s. Complications were graded according to Clavien-Dindo.ResultsEleven patients were included. Median age was 54 [interquartile range (IQR), 45–64] years and median follow-up was 16 (IQR, 13–25) months. Median stricture length was 6 (IQR, 5–7) cm. Meatal location was glanular or subcoronal in most patients, with one mid-shaft meatus; no concomitant meatal reconstruction was performed. Median urethral caliber improved from 5 (IQR, 4–6) to 16 (IQR, 12–16) Charriere (Ch), and median maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) from 6 (IQR, 4–8) to 16 (IQR, 12–19) mL/s. Anatomical success was achieved in 6/11 patients (55%), whereas functional success was observed in 10/11 (91%). Median USS-PROM symptom score improved from 17 (IQR, 16–18) to 3 (IQR, 2–5). Four patients (36%) developed penile hematoma, two with associated wound dehiscence; all were managed conservatively (Clavien-Dindo I). No urethrocutaneous fistula occurred.ConclusionsSingle-stage dorsal onlay OMG urethroplasty using a perineal penile invagination approach provides high functional improvement and patient satisfaction in selected adults with hypospadias-associated strictures, while avoiding penile incision and with a low rate of clinically relevant complications.Keywords: Hypospadias, urethral stricture, urethroplasty, oral mucosa graft (OMG), penile invagination

Highlight box

Key findings

• Single-stage dorsal onlay oral mucosa graft urethroplasty through a perineal approach with penile invagination achieved high functional success and patient satisfaction in selected adults with hypospadias-associated strictures.

• The technique avoided penile skin incision and no urethrocutaneous fistula occurred during follow-up.

What is known and what is new?

• Adult urethral strictures after childhood hypospadias repair are surgically challenging due to altered anatomy and compromised penile tissues. Staged reconstruction is traditionally recommended in many complex cases.

• This study describes a single-stage reconstructive alternative using penile invagination through a perineal approach while preserving penile skin integrity and future reconstructive options.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Careful patient selection is essential when considering single-stage reconstruction in this population.

• Functional outcomes and patient-reported satisfaction should be considered alongside anatomical patency when evaluating urethroplasty success.

Introduction

In adults with urethral strictures following childhood hypospadias repair, reconstructive surgery is particularly challenging due to altered anatomy, deficient spongiosum, and the frequent use of penile skin as part of the neourethra during prior repairs (1-4). These patients often present years or decades after the initial surgery with long, complex strictures located in the penile or penobulbar urethra, frequently after multiple previous interventions, which significantly compromises tissue quality and surgical outcomes (1-3,5-7).

Re-entering the penile shaft and incising previously reconstructed urethral segments may further impair local vascularity and has been associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, particularly urethrocutaneous fistula formation (2-4,7). In this context, several authors have emphasized the importance of preserving penile tissues and minimizing additional dissection in patients with a history of failed hypospadias repair (3,4,7).

Perineal approaches with penile invagination or inversion have been described as effective techniques to access the anterior urethra while avoiding penile skin incisions, mainly in the setting of complex anterior or panurethral strictures (8-11). These techniques allow wide urethral exposure through a single perineal incision and have been associated with satisfactory outcomes in selected patients, while preserving penile skin integrity (8-10). However, despite the theoretical advantages of avoiding penile incision, the application of this approach specifically to hypospadias-associated urethral strictures, where penile skin may constitute part of the neourethra, has been only sparsely reported and remains poorly characterized in the literature (4,7).

Outcome assessment after urethroplasty in this population is further complicated by the lack of standardized definitions of success. Previous series have used heterogeneous anatomical and functional endpoints, limiting meaningful comparison between studies (12,13). Current guidelines and contemporary reviews emphasize the need for objective anatomical assessment, commonly defining failure as the inability to pass a flexible endoscope or catheter of approximately 16 Charriere (Ch) without resistance, regardless of symptom severity (12-14).

At the same time, there has been increasing recognition of the importance of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in urethral stricture surgery (USS-PROMs), as anatomical patency alone does not fully capture patient satisfaction or functional improvement (15,16). Validated instruments such as the USS-PROM, including its Spanish version, have been developed to standardize patient-centered outcome reporting and are increasingly recommended in both clinical practice and research settings (15,17).

The present study was therefore designed as a prospective evaluation of single-stage oral mucosa graft (OMG) urethroplasty performed through a perineal approach with penile invagination in adult patients with hypospadias-associated urethral strictures. By deliberately avoiding penile skin incision and re-entry into previously reconstructed urethral segments, this technique aims to reduce the risk of urethrocutaneous fistula while preserving future reconstructive options.

The primary objective of the present study was to prospectively evaluate the anatomical and functional outcomes of single-stage OMG urethroplasty performed through a perineal approach with penile invagination in adult patients with hypospadias-associated urethral strictures.

A secondary objective was to assess patient-reported outcomes following reconstruction using validated PROMs collected preoperatively and postoperatively. Given that anatomical patency alone does not fully capture the patient’s perception of surgical success, symptom improvement, or quality of life, the integration of PROMs was considered essential to provide a comprehensive evaluation of treatment effectiveness in this complex cohort.

Additional objectives included the prospective assessment of perioperative and postoperative complications, classified according to standardized criteria, and the evaluation of the need for secondary procedures during follow-up. Particular attention was paid to complications traditionally associated with hypospadias-associated reconstructions, such as urethrocutaneous fistula, restenosis, and diverticular formation. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-1-0004/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This was a prospective, observational, single-center study conducted at University Hospital Ramón y Cajal, a tertiary referral center for reconstructive urology in Madrid, Spain. Adult patients undergoing urethral reconstruction for urethral stricture disease related to childhood hypospadias repair were consecutively enrolled between January 2022 and September 2024. Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of the last outpatient visit.

Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) history of hypospadias repair during childhood; (III) diagnosis of anterior urethral stricture confirmed by retrograde urethrography and/or urethroscopy; and (IV) planned single-stage urethral reconstruction using an OMG through a perineal approach with penile invagination. Patients were considered eligible only if the stricture was deemed suitable for single-stage reconstruction based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) obliterative strictures not amenable to graft augmentation (patients with a caliber <4 Ch were excluded and managed with staged reconstruction); (II) extensive lichen sclerosus involving the urethra or penile skin; (III) severe residual penile curvature requiring additional corrective procedures; and (IV) inability to complete follow-up assessments or PROMs.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the University Hospital Ramón y Cajal review board (No. 13/22). All patients provided written informed consent.

Preoperative evaluation

All patients underwent a standardized preoperative evaluation including detailed medical history, physical examination, urine culture, and uroflowmetry. Stricture location, length, and extent were assessed by retrograde urethrography combined with voiding cystourethrography. Urethroscopy using a small-caliber ureteroscope (4 Ch) was performed preoperatively or intraoperatively to further characterize the stricture, assess luminal caliber, and evaluate urethral plate quality, as previously described (18). Urethral luminal caliber was initially assessed by traversing the stricture with a 4 Ch small-caliber ureteroscope. Subsequently, sequential ureteral catheters (4–7 Ch) were gently advanced across the narrowed segment, and the largest catheter that could be passed without resistance was recorded as the functional urethral caliber. Caliber was reported in Charrière units.

Meatal location was systematically documented and varied among patients as a consequence of prior childhood hypospadias repair. The meatus was located at the glans or subcoronal level in most cases, while one patient presented a mid-shaft meatus.

Patient-reported outcomes were prospectively collected using validated PROMs, including the USS-PROM, administered preoperatively (17). The same instrument was repeated at last follow-up to allow longitudinal comparison. Questionnaires were administered and collected in person by the treating surgeon to ensure completeness and consistency of responses.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by experienced reconstructive urologists using a standardized technique.

Patients were placed in the lithotomy position. Initially, the urethral meatus was inspected and a guidewire and a ureteral catheter were inserted to delineate the urethral lumen (Figure 1A,1B). A midline perineal incision was performed to expose the bulbar urethra. In some cases, the absence of the bulbospongiosus muscle was observed due to a previous perineal urethrostomy that had been used in cases of hypospadias reconstruction (Figure 1C).

(D) Penile invagination through the perineal incision, allowing access to the penile urethra without penile skin incision. (E) Unilateral dissection of the urethra with identification of the neourethra facilitated by the ureteral catheter. (F) Dorsal or dorsolateral urethrotomy extended to healthy proximal urethral tissue.

Penile invagination was then carried out, allowing access to the penile and penobulbar urethra through the perineal incision without performing any penile skin incision. This maneuver enabled adequate urethral exposure while avoiding re-entry into previously reconstructed penile tissues (Figure 1D).

A meticulous unilateral dissection was performed while preserving the vascular supply of the previously used flaps. Given that the absence of the corpus spongiosum complicates identification of the neourethra, placement of a ureteral catheter was helpful in facilitating its identification during dissection (Figure 1E). In patients with prior hypospadias repair, vascular anatomy is frequently altered and the corpus spongiosum may be deficient. In this context, preservation of vascularity does not rely on identification of a single macroscopic pedicle but rather on minimizing circumferential dissection and avoiding direct manipulation of the neourethra.

Dissection was intentionally performed laterally and away from the neourethral segment to reduce the risk of disrupting residual subdartos and periurethral microvascular networks, which are not individually identifiable intraoperatively. No intraoperative signs of urethral plate ischemia were observed.

An initial dorsal or dorsolateral urethrotomy was performed until healthy proximal urethral tissue was reached (Figure 1F). Urethrotomy was extended approximately 1 cm beyond the proximal and distal margins of the diseased segment into healthy urethral mucosa. A preserved urethral plate was defined as a viable, pliable, and well-vascularized tissue strip allowing tension-free dorsal onlay graft fixation (Table S1).

Subsequently, a dorsal meatotomy was carried out to connect the meatal incision with the penile urethral incision. The meatus and distal urethra were calibrated using a 26 Ch metal dilator (Figure 2A).

(B) Initial fixation of the oral mucosa graft to the meatus with interrupted sutures. (C) Introduction of the graft through the meatus and retrieval through the perineal incision. (D) Urethra-to-graft anastomosis performed with interrupted absorbable sutures. (E) Quilting sutures and tubularization of the urethra over a catheter. (F) Final aspect after layered closure of the urethra and perineal wound, with the penis returned to its anatomical position. Ch, Charriere.

OMGs were harvested from the inner cheek using standard techniques by a dedicated surgical team working in parallel, then defatted and tailored to the stricture length.

The graft was initially secured to the meatus with three interrupted sutures (Figure 2B) and subsequently introduced through the meatus and retrieved through the perineal incision (Figure 2C). Urethra-to-graft anastomosis was performed using interrupted 4/0 absorbable sutures (Figure 2D). Quilting sutures were placed, and the urethra was tubularized over a 20 Ch catheter (Figure 2E). The graft bed consisted mainly of the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa.

The OMG was placed dorsally and fixed to the underlying bed using multiple quilting sutures to ensure intimate contact and prevent graft displacement or hematoma formation.

Reconstruction was performed using a dorsal onlay technique, in which the native urethral plate margins were sutured to the lateral edges of the graft. The anastomosis of the graft to the native urethra was carried out over a 20 Ch catheter to provide a sufficient caliber to the reconstructed urethral plate.

A 14 Ch silicone catheter was used as the definitive indwelling catheter to avoid excessive tension and ischemia of the neourethra.

The urethra and perineal wound were closed in layers and the penis was returned to its anatomical position (Figure 2F).

Meatal reconstruction was not routinely performed, as it would have required distal urethral reconstruction with grafting and a staged approach. Given the absence of functional obstruction at the meatus and the predominantly cosmetic nature of distal meatal repositioning, additional reconstructive procedures were deferred in accordance with patient preference.

Postoperative management and follow-up

Indwelling catheter duration ranged from 4 to 6 weeks and was individualized according to intraoperative complexity and postoperative local conditions. Prolonged catheterization was preferred in cases with extensive reconstruction, compromised tissue quality, postoperative hematoma, or partial wound dehiscence, as a precautionary measure to reduce mechanical stress on the repair.

Routine postoperative urethrography was not performed prior to catheter removal. Imaging was reserved for selected cases with clinical suspicion of leakage or fistula formation, as most clinically relevant complications were readily detectable on physical examination.

Urethral reconstruction was performed over a 20 Ch catheter to ensure adequate neourethral caliber. Subsequently, a 14 Ch indwelling catheter was used to minimize anastomotic tension and reduce the risk of ischemia while maintaining sufficient urinary drainage.

Prolonged catheterization was therefore used as a protective strategy in this high-risk population rather than as a rigid protocol.

Follow-up visits were scheduled at 4–6 weeks postoperatively, at 6 months, and annually thereafter. The primary outcome assessment was performed at 12 months postoperatively. At this time point, patients underwent clinical evaluation, urethral calibration, uroflowmetry, and completion of USS-PROM. Additional follow-up was conducted thereafter according to clinical practice, focusing on symptom recurrence, flow deterioration, and need for secondary procedures.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was anatomical success, defined as the ability to pass a 16 Ch urethral catheter through the reconstructed segment without resistance at follow-up evaluation, combined with the absence of any need for secondary urethral procedures, including urethral dilatation, direct visual internal urethrotomy, or revision urethroplasty.

Secondary endpoints included functional outcomes assessed by changes in uroflowmetry parameters (peak flow) and PROMs between preoperative evaluation and last follow-up. PROM results were analyzed descriptively due to the exploratory nature of the study.

Postoperative complications were recorded prospectively and classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification (19). Particular attention was paid to complications traditionally associated with hypospadias-related reconstruction, including urethrocutaneous fistula, restenosis, and diverticular formation. The need for any secondary intervention during follow-up was also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Given the limited sample size, statistical analysis was primarily descriptive. Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range or mean and standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 11 adult patients with urethral stricture disease following childhood hypospadias repair were included. Median age at surgery was 54 (IQR, 45–64) years, and median follow-up was 16 (IQR, 13–25) months. Patients had undergone a median of 2 previous urethral surgeries (IQR, 1–3).

Strictures were located in the penile urethra in 9 patients and involved both the penile and bulbar urethra in 2 patients. Median stricture length was 6 (IQR, 5–7) cm. Meatal location was glanular or subcoronal in most patients, with one patient presenting a mid-shaft meatus. No patient underwent concomitant meatal reconstruction.

Median operative time was 130 (IQR, 120–160) min. The median length of the harvested OMG was 7 (IQR, 7–7) cm, with a median graft width of 2 (IQR, 1.5–2) cm. Median catheterization time was 30 (IQR, 30–45) days, and median length of hospital stay was 1 (IQR, 1–1) day. No intraoperative conversions or aborted procedures occurred. All included patients were deemed suitable for augmentation based on preoperative assessment. Baseline and perioperative characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Anatomical success, defined as the ability to pass a 16 Ch catheter without resistance and without need for secondary intervention, was achieved in 6 of 11 patients (55%). Functional success, defined as a Qmax greater than 12 mL/s, was observed in 10 of 11 patients (91%). Five patients (45%) did not meet the criteria for strict anatomical success. Two patients had a residual urethral caliber of 14 Ch, two of 12 Ch, and one of 10 Ch. In three cases, narrowing was located at the proximal anastomosis, while in two cases it appeared to involve the entire grafted segment.

No secondary interventions were required, as all patients were either satisfied with their functional outcomes or declined further surgical treatment. At last follow-up, no clinical progression was observed (Table S2).

Patient-reported outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes demonstrated a marked improvement following surgery. Median USS-PROM symptom score decreased from 17 (IQR, 16–18) preoperatively to 3 (IQR, 2–5) at last follow-up (Figure 3). Quality of life scores improved from a median of 3 (IQR, 3–3) preoperatively to 1 (IQR, 0–1) postoperatively.

Complications

Postoperative complications were observed in a minority of patients. Four patients (36%) developed a postoperative penile hematoma. In two of these patients, the hematoma was associated with partial perineal wound dehiscence. All cases were managed conservatively with local wound care and close outpatient follow-up, without the need for surgical reintervention, blood transfusion, or prolonged hospitalization. Postoperative penile hematoma influenced catheter duration and wound management in selected cases, leading to prolonged catheterization for prudential reasons.

According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, these events were classified as grade I complications. No urethrocutaneous fistula, graft loss, diverticulum formation, or infectious complications were observed during follow-up. No patient required reoperation or additional invasive procedures for complication management. No patients reported postoperative distal spraying, obstructive meatal symptoms, or cosmetic dissatisfaction. No secondary distal procedures were required during follow-up.

Discussion

Reconstruction of urethral strictures in adults with a history of childhood hypospadias repair represents one of the most demanding scenarios in reconstructive urology. These patients frequently present with complex anterior strictures, deficient corpus spongiosum, altered vascular anatomy, and extensive use of penile skin during prior repairs, often as part of the neourethra. Classical hypospadias repair techniques, such as the transverse preputial island flap described by Duckett, as well as other cutaneous flap-based reconstructions, frequently incorporated ventral penile skin into the urethral tube, resulting in fragile tissues and increased susceptibility to fistula formation during secondary reconstruction (20).

The concept of staged urethral reconstruction using free grafts was originally introduced by Bracka, who described a two-stage approach for complex hypospadias repair based on initial graft placement followed by delayed reconstruction (21). This principle was later extrapolated to adult urethral reconstruction, particularly in patients with failed hypospadias repair, and further developed by Barbagli and colleagues (2). Although staged urethroplasty remains an important option, especially in cases with poor urethral plate quality, fistulae, or significant chordee, it is associated with relevant limitations. Some authors have reported graft contraction rates of up to 39% after the first stage, frequently necessitating additional procedures and, in some cases, more than two surgical stages (22). Moreover, the lack of adequate tissue for reconstruction and the need to re-incise previously reconstructed penile skin during the second stage may increase the risk of urethrocutaneous fistula.

In this context, single-stage reconstruction using OMGs has emerged as an appealing alternative in carefully selected patients. However, expectations regarding anatomical success must be realistic. In patients with hypospadias-associated strictures—often older and with long-standing disease—achieving a wide urethral caliber comparable to primary bulbar urethroplasty is not always feasible. Importantly, surgical success should not be defined solely by urethral caliber. Contemporary literature and current guidelines emphasize that anatomical patency alone does not fully reflect functional outcomes or patient satisfaction after urethroplasty (23,24). Patient-reported satisfaction and quality of life are more closely related to symptom control and functional outcomes than to urethral caliber alone (16,23-26).

This concept is supported by our findings, which demonstrate a clear discrepancy between anatomical and functional outcomes. While anatomical success—defined according to widely accepted criteria as the ability to pass a 16 Ch catheter without resistance—was achieved in just over half of patients, functional success based on uroflowmetry and patient-reported outcomes exceeded 90%. These results reinforce the importance of incorporating both objective measures and validated PROMs, such as the USS-PROM, when evaluating outcomes after urethral reconstruction (16,17).

The surgical strategy evaluated in the present study offers several potential advantages. The perineal approach with penile invagination allows full exposure of the penile and penobulbar urethra through a single incision while avoiding penile skin dissection and re-entry into previously reconstructed ventral tissues. Placement of the OMG in a dorsal onlay configuration enables reconstruction on a well-vascularized corporal bed while minimizing manipulation of the ventral urethra, which is particularly relevant in patients in whom ventral penile skin constitutes part of the neourethra. This approach may contribute to the absence of urethrocutaneous fistula observed in our cohort.

In adult patients with a history of childhood hypospadias repair, distal meatal position frequently represents a cosmetic rather than a functional issue. In the absence of distal obstruction, meatal reconstruction often requires additional grafting and a staged approach, thereby increasing surgical burden without a clear functional benefit.

In the present series, the decision to avoid routine meatal reconstruction was based on functional considerations and patient preference, reflecting a tailored, patient-centered approach. Importantly, no patient demonstrated clinically relevant distal obstruction. One patient expressed interest in secondary distal meatal reconstruction for cosmetic reasons, underscoring the importance of individualized counseling and shared decision-making in this complex population.

Based on our experience, a pragmatic decision-making framework can be proposed. Patients with absent or severely scarred urethral plate, obliterative or near-obliterative lumen (below 4 Ch) (12), significant residual chordee, or active urethrocutaneous fistula are better candidates for staged reconstruction. Conversely, patients with a preserved urethral plate, luminal caliber greater than 4 Ch, no significant curvature, and no fistula may be suitable candidates for single-stage dorsal onlay OMG urethroplasty using a perineal approach with penile invagination, achieving acceptable anatomical outcomes and high functional satisfaction (Figure 4). This approach with penile invagination preserves penile skin and vascular supply, facilitating future staged reconstruction if required, without compromising subsequent surgical options.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size is small, reflecting the rarity and heterogeneity of hypospadias-associated urethral strictures in adult patients. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, follow-up duration, although sufficient to assess early and mid-term outcomes, may not capture late recurrences, which are known to occur after urethral reconstruction. PROMs were administered by the treating surgeon, which may represent a potential source of response bias.

Finally, the absence of a comparative control group undergoing staged reconstruction limits direct comparison between surgical strategies. Despite these limitations, the prospective design, standardized surgical technique, and systematic use of validated PROMs provide meaningful insight into the role of single-stage reconstruction in a carefully selected subset of patients with hypospadias-associated strictures.

Conclusions

Single-stage OMG urethroplasty using a perineal approach with penile invagination is a viable reconstructive option in selected adults with hypospadias-associated urethral strictures. This technique enables effective reconstruction while avoiding penile skin incision, providing satisfactory functional outcomes and high patient-reported satisfaction despite variable anatomical patency. Careful patient selection and the use of combined anatomical, functional, and patient-reported endpoints are essential when assessing success in this challenging population.
