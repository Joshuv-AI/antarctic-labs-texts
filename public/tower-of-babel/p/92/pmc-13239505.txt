Explainable machine-learning model for coronary artery disease diagnosis in non-AMI patients: integrating inflammatory and traditional risk factors
Authors: Kexin Yang, Sheng Liu, Chenyang Wang, Siyao Ni, Ludan Bi, Yunxiao Yang, Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and interpret an explainable machine learning (ML) model integrating inflammatory biomarkers and traditional risk factors for the diagnosis of coronary artery disease (CAD).MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 4656 patients undergoing coronary angiography for suspected CAD. Demographic, clinical, biochemical and 12 inflammatory cytokine variables were collected. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression identified 21 candidate features. 10 ML algorithms were trained using cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score. Model explainability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsAmong the evaluated models, the generalised linear model showed balanced performance and was selected as the final model (AUC=0.815). SHAP analysis identified hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, sex, triglycerides and interleukin-6 as the most influential predictors of CAD.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the potential of interpretable ML approaches that combine inflammatory and traditional markers to enhance early CAD diagnosis and inform clinical decision-making.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Inflammation, Angina Pectoris

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, contributing substantially to the global healthcare and economic burden.1 Early and precise diagnosis is essential to improve clinical outcomes and optimise therapeutic strategies. Although coronary angiography (CAG) is considered the gold standard for CAD diagnosis,2 its invasive nature is associated with procedural risks—including arterial dissection, thrombosis and, in rare cases, mortality3—as well as significant financial costs and operator dependency. These limitations restrict its widespread use, particularly in primary care and resource-constrained settings.4 5 This underscores the need for a reliable, cost-effective and non-invasive diagnostic alternative.

Atherosclerosis (AS), the pathological basis of CAD, is not merely a consequence of lipid deposition but is increasingly recognised as a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory process.6 Numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of inflammatory biomarkers—including interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and high-sensitivity C reactive protein (hsCRP)—in the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions.7

In recent years, advances in machine learning (ML) have enabled the construction of predictive models capable of processing large-scale clinical datasets and identifying complex, non-linear patterns. ML algorithms have shown promising results in stratifying the risk of multiple chronic diseases such as pneumonia, diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.8 9 In the field of CAD, several studies have demonstrated that ML-based diagnostic models can outperform conventional regression approaches in both discrimination and calibration.10,14 However, most existing ML models remain focused on conventional risk factors—such as age, sex, blood pressure and lipid levels—while neglecting biomarkers related to inflammation, metabolism and other pathophysiological mechanisms. This limits both their diagnostic performance and biological interpretability.

To minimise the confounding influence of acute inflammatory states and better reflect the stable pathophysiological signatures of CAD, this study excluded patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and focused solely on hospitalised individuals with suspected CAD but without AMI. This approach aimed to improve model generalisability and reduce misclassification due to acute-phase responses.

In this single-centre retrospective study, we systematically integrated 12 inflammation-related serum biomarkers—including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)—alongside traditional cardiovascular risk factors and routine clinical laboratory parameters to develop a series of ML-based diagnostic models for CAD. Moreover, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to enhance model interpretability and facilitate clinical translation. This study provides an interpretable, non-invasive diagnostic strategy for CAD and offers mechanistic insights into inflammation-driven cardiovascular risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Study population

Between 8 August 2021 and 10 November 2023, 4656 patients with suspected CAD were enrolled from the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine, Beijing Anzhen Hospital. All underwent CAG. CAD was defined as ≥50% stenosis in at least one major coronary artery. The exclusion criteria were AMI, severe valvular disease, advanced heart failure, significant arrhythmias, major hepatic or renal dysfunction, malignancy, autoimmune disease, active infection and recent use of anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive drugs. Venous blood samples were collected in the morning after fasting for at least 12 hours, prior to CAG. The samples were centrifuged at 2500 g for 10 min, and the serum was stored at −80°C for subsequent cytokine assays.

Demographic and clinical information

The clinical characteristics and biochemical parameters of each patient were recorded. Quality control samples were processed in a blinded manner, with the intra-assay coefficient of variation <5% and the inter-assay coefficient of variation <10%. Serum cytokines (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, TNF-α, interferon-alpha (IFN-α), IFN-γ) were quantified using a multiplex flow cytometric assay (FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

Data preprocessing and partitioning

Variables with >30% missing values were excluded. The remaining dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and an independent testing set (30%) using stratified sampling, with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility. In the training set, missing values were imputed using the mode for categorical variables and the median or mean (depending on distribution skewness) for continuous variables. For the testing set, imputation was performed using parameters estimated from the training set to prevent data leakage. Subsequently, continuous variables were log-transformed if skewed and standardised (Z-scores). Highly collinear variables (variance inflation factor (VIF) > 5) were removed.

Feature selection

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with L1 regularisation was employed for feature selection. The optimal regularisation parameter (λ) was determined through repeated fivefold cross-validation, combined with the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to address class imbalance and improve robustness. Two feature sets were retained: λmin (minimum cross-validation error) and λ₁se (one SE above λmin), balancing model complexity and generalisability.

Model development

10 ML models were developed: logistic regression, random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbours (KNN), C5.0 decision tree, adaptive boosting, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), generalised linear model (GLM), naive Bayes and neural network (NN). Training and optimisation were performed in R using grid search with repeated fivefold cross-validation. Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search with repeated cross-validation; the tuning ranges and settings for each model are provided in online supplemental table S1 to support reproducibility.

Model evaluation and interpretation

Model performance was evaluated in the independent testing set using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP values (fastshap package). Features were sequentially added according to SHAP importance to examine incremental AUC contributions, and DeLong’s test was used for pairwise AUC comparisons to identify a concise diagnostic predictor set.

Regarding demographic and clinical features, significant differences were observed between the CAD and non-CAD groups across multiple variables. The CAD group had a markedly higher proportion of male patients than the non-CAD group (76.1% vs 59.4%, p<0.001), and also demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension (HTN, 63.4% vs 20.3%), diabetes mellitus (DM, 38.2% vs 14.8%), hyperlipidaemia (HLP, 79.3% vs 45.4%) and smoking history (47.0% vs 28.6%) (all p<0.001). No statistically significant differences were noted in age (p=0.307), body mass index (p=0.578) or alcohol consumption (p=0.451).

In laboratory tests, the CAD group exhibited significantly higher levels of haemoglobin (p=0.002), platelet distribution width (PDW, p<0.001), blood glucose (p<0.001), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, p<0.001), triglycerides (TG, p=0.006), urea (p=0.006), creatinine (Cr, p=0.003), total calcium (tCa, p<0.001) and cholinesterase (ChE, p<0.001) compared with the non-CAD group. Conversely, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, p=0.005), non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C, p=0.049) and total CO₂ (TCO2, p<0.001) levels were higher in the non-CAD group, while the anion gap (AG) was significantly elevated in the CAD group (p<0.001).

Regarding inflammatory and immune markers, the CAD group demonstrated increased serum levels of IL-2 (p=0.001), IL-4 (p=0.027) and IL-17 (p=0.002), whereas IL-6 (p=0.009), IL-8 (p<0.001) and IFN-α (p<0.001) were significantly reduced compared with the non-CAD group. No statistically significant differences were observed in hsCRP, TNF-α or IL-1β.

Stepwise SHAP-Based feature inclusion and model optimiSAtion

Stepwise SHAP-guided feature inclusion was applied to the five top-performing models, with AUC calculated at each step and inter-model differences assessed using the DeLong’s test (figure 3). Online supplemental figure S1 summarises, for the five models included in the SHAP-guided stepwise feature-inclusion analysis, the optimal AUC and the minimum number of SHAP-ranked features required to reach the performance plateau. XGBoost showed consistently superior performance (AUC=0.844, 20 variables), followed by RF (AUC=0.835) and SVM (AUC=0.822). GLM and GLMNET achieved slightly lower AUCs but maintained competitive accuracy with fewer variables (17 and 16, respectively), indicating greater parsimony. Balancing performance and interpretability, GLM was selected as the primary diagnostic model, with its AUC, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score evaluated across increasing numbers of features (figure 3).

Global model interpretability

Global interpretability of the final GLM was assessed using SHAP values. The mean absolute SHAP summary plot (figure 4) ranked HTN, HLP, sex, DM and TG as the top predictors, followed by smoking history, LDL-C, Alb, Lp(a) and UA. The beeswarm plot (figure 4) shows feature distribution and directionality, while dependence plots (figure 4) reveal non-linear effects. Higher TG, age and tCa were associated with increased CAD likelihood, whereas elevated LDL-C, UA, LDH and TCO₂ were inversely associated.

Discussion

CAD remains a leading cause of death worldwide. Although CAG is considered the gold standard for diagnosis, its invasiveness and high cost limit its widespread clinical application.3 This study aimed to develop an auxiliary diagnostic model based on non-invasive blood biomarkers to identify CAD among patients without AMI.

By systematically comparing 10 ML models, we evaluated their diagnostic utility in differentiating CAD from non-CAD in a non-AMI patient population. Complex models such as C5.0, RF and XGBoost showed superior accuracy and sensitivity, with XGBoost reaching a sensitivity of 0.967 and an accuracy of 0.924, indicating strong ability to identify CAD. However, these models generally exhibited low specificity, leading to increased false-positive rates among non-CAD patients. In clinical practice, this may result in unnecessary invasive procedures for non-CAD individuals, thereby increasing healthcare resource use and psychological burden.

In contrast, although the GLM slightly underperformed some complex models in terms of AUC (0.815) and accuracy (0.810), it achieved a better balance between sensitivity (0.820) and specificity (0.711), along with a high F1 score (0.887). This performance makes the GLM more practical for clinical use, enabling effective identification of high-risk patients while reducing misclassification, and thus making it suitable for diagnostic scenarios that require a balance between safety and resource efficiency.

A key advantage of the GLM lies in its structural transparency and interpretability.15 The contribution of each variable to the diagnostic outcome can be directly inferred from the model coefficients, facilitating clinical interpretation and integration into practice. Compared with complex ‘black-box’ models, the GLM integrates more seamlessly with existing risk scoring systems such as the Framingham Risk Score and Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) and exhibits a lower risk of overfitting and better generalisability across populations.16,18 Given the absence of statistically significant differences in AUC among the five selected models in pairwise DeLong comparisons, the final model selection was guided by interpretability, clinical transparency and relatively balanced overall performance. Accordingly, the GLM was ultimately selected as the final auxiliary diagnostic model in this study.

To enhance interpretability and clinical trust, we applied SHAP to provide both global and individual-level insights into the model’s decision-making mechanisms.19 This approach allows quantification of feature importance and visualisation of the direction and magnitude of their effects, improving the model’s transparency and clinical acceptability.20

The SHAP analysis reaffirmed the importance of classical cardiovascular risk factors—HTN, HLP, male sex, DM and smoking—which showed strong positive contributions to CAD diagnosis. These findings are consistent with longstanding epidemiological evidence and reinforce the clinical plausibility of the model’s core features.

Meanwhile, SHAP also revealed the complex roles of inflammatory factors in CAD. IL-6, IL-8 and IFN-α were retained after LASSO variable selection, with IL-6 ranking 14th among the top 17 features in stepwise feature analysis, still contributing to the model output. However, because our endpoint was defined by angiographic stenosis and we did not have functional assessment or plaque imaging data, we could not determine whether these inflammatory signals were more closely related to lesion severity, plaque vulnerability or treatment-modified biomarker profiles. Future studies integrating coronary physiological assessment (fractional flow reserve/instantaneous wave-free ratio, FFR/iFR) and plaque imaging (intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)/optical coherence tomography (OCT) or coronary CT angiography) may better clarify the relationship between inflammatory biomarkers and high-risk plaque phenotypes.

Notably, IL-6 showed a negative SHAP value distribution, indicating that higher IL-6 levels were paradoxically associated with a lower predicted probability of CAD. A similar inverse association was observed for LDL-C, where elevated levels corresponded to decreased model-predicted probability of CAD.

This counterintuitive pattern may reflect the masking effect of prior treatment. Statins, commonly prescribed for patients at high cardiovascular risk, are known to reduce both LDL-C and systemic inflammatory markers such as IL-6.21 In such cases, patients with established CAD may present with artificially suppressed biomarker levels, leading the model to interpret these reductions as reflecting prior high risk rather than inherently low risk. Thus, the model may leverage these ‘treated’ biomarker profiles as indirect indicators of underlying CAD.

This finding underscores the need to interpret certain biomarker trends—particularly IL-6 and LDL-C—in the context of prior treatment history. Misinterpretation of treatment-induced biomarker suppression as a low-risk physiological state may lead to under-recognition of disease in real-world settings. Additionally, such directional anomalies may be influenced by statistical factors, including multicollinearity, variable interactions or sample imbalance. Although the GLM is robust and interpretable, its linear structure inherently limits the capacity to capture complex non-linear relationships among features.22

Beyond conventional risk indicators and inflammatory biomarkers, this study highlights the diagnostic relevance of Lp(a), a genetically determined atherogenic lipoprotein, in the final GLM model. Lp(a) consists of an LDL-like particle containing apoB-100 covalently bound to the highly polymorphic apo(a) domain, and its plasma levels are predominantly regulated by the LPA gene, with marked inter-individual variation and strong heritability.23 24 A robust body of evidence, including multiple large-scale epidemiological and Mendelian randomisation studies, supports the role of elevated Lp(a) as an independent risk factor for ASCVD.25 26

For instance, findings from the Copenhagen City Heart Study, which followed 9300 individuals for a decade, demonstrated that Lp(a) concentrations of ≥120 mg/dL were associated with nearly a fourfold increase in myocardial infarction risk and a 35% absolute rise in 10-year event probability.27 Moreover, consensus statements by the European Atherosclerosis Society emphasise that Lp(a)-associated risk persists even after optimal control of traditional risk factors, representing a substantial component of residual cardiovascular risk.28 Lp(a) not only promotes AS but also has prothrombotic effects and is therefore considered an independent cardiovascular risk factor beyond traditional lipid parameters. In this study, SHAP analysis demonstrated a significant contribution of Lp(a) to model diagnosis, highlighting its clinical value in identifying individuals at high genetic risk and supporting the ability of ML to capture complex etiological mechanisms.

Another noteworthy finding of this study is the identification of serum potassium as a key diagnostic variable, implicating the broader cardio-kidney-metabolic axis in CAD pathophysiology. Potassium homeostasis is essential for maintaining myocardial electrical stability, and its dysregulation is common among patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Moreover, serum potassium levels may be influenced by the use of medications such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, all of which are widely prescribed in cardiovascular care.

CKD is not only a frequent comorbidity of CAD but also an independent risk factor that exacerbates AS and endothelial dysfunction through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress, chronic inflammation and vascular calcification.29 Data from the US Renal Data System 2020 report indicate that cardiovascular mortality increases progressively with declining renal function, suggesting a graded and bidirectional relationship between the renal and cardiovascular systems.30 The association between serum potassium levels and CAD risk observed in this study suggests that this marker not only reflects the disordered cardio-renal interaction but may also serve as an early signal to identify high-risk patients.

These results highlight the strength of ML-based diagnostic frameworks in uncovering non-obvious but clinically meaningful associations. Unlike traditional rule-based models, data-driven approaches such as the GLM, when guided by feature selection algorithms and interpretability tools such as SHAP, can reveal multisystem interactions that contribute to disease expression, thereby supporting a more holistic understanding of CAD pathogenesis.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-centre study; although we performed internal validation (repeated cross-validation and an independent testing set), the model has not been validated in an independent external cohort, and its transportability remains to be confirmed through prospective multicentre external validation. Second, obstructive CAD was defined by ≥50% stenosis on CAG without physiological assessment (FFR/iFR) or plaque characterisation (IVUS/OCT), so we could not evaluate associations with high-risk plaque features. Third, the analysis included only a limited set of haematological and inflammatory biomarkers, and key clinical tests such as electrocardiography and echocardiography were not included as candidate predictors. In addition, the potential impact of medications such as statins or ACE inhibitors on biomarker levels was not accounted for, which may have confounded SHAP-based interpretations. Finally, the non-CAD sample was relatively small; although stratified splitting and SMOTE were used to mitigate class imbalance, residual imbalance may affect the stability of model performance and feature importance estimates.

Conclusions

This study developed and evaluated a ML-based diagnostic model leveraging routinely available blood biomarkers to assist in the early identification of CAD in patients without AMI. The GLM demonstrated favourable diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.815; accuracy: 0.810; sensitivity: 0.820; specificity: 0.711), with the added advantage of clinical interpretability.

SHAP analysis identified HTN, HLP, sex, diabetes and smoking as the most influential predictors, while also highlighting the diagnostic relevance of chronic inflammation and metabolic-related markers. This model offers a non-invasive, cost-effective and explainable tool to support clinical decision-making in CAD diagnosis. Future studies should incorporate prospective, multicentre validation and integrate additional multimodal data sources to enhance clinical utility.
