High-risk influencing factors associated with physical inactivity and gender differences among Chinese university students in 2024
Authors: Liang Chen, Pei-jun Wei, Hao-jie Zuo, Ding-you Zhang, Hu Lou, Shi-hai Yu, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis cross-sectional study aimed to identify high-risk influencing factors associated with higher odds of physical inactivity among Chinese university students using data from the CPAHLS-CS. It further explored gender differences in these associations to provide evidence for developing differentiated intervention strategies. This cross-sectional study aimed to identify factors associated with IPAQ-derived low physical activity (LPA) among Chinese university students using data from the CPAHLS-CS and to examine whether these associations differed by gender.MethodA cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among 35,696 university students from general higher education institutions across China in 2024 using stratified cluster sampling. The questionnaire was utilized to assess the students’ PA levels and collect data on demographic, psychological, behavioral, and health indicators. PA level was classified into low, moderate, and high categories according to the IPAQ scoring protocol. For binary logistic regression, LPA was used as the outcome variable, with LPA coded as 1 and moderate/high PA coded as 0. Chi-square tests, Spearman correlation analysis, sex-stratified binary logistic regression, and pooled logistic regression models with gender-by-predictor interaction terms were used to examine factors associated with physical inactivity and to formally test whether these associations differed by gender.ResultsThe overall prevalence of IPAQ-derived LPA was 68.48%. When calculated within gender groups, the prevalence of LPA was 55.0% among male students and 85.5% among female students. .When calculated within each gender group, the prevalence of IPAQ-derived LPA was higher among female students than among male students. Regression analysis showed that low self-efficacy was the strongest factor associated with higher odds of LPA across genders (male students OR = 1.997, 95% CI: 1.806–2.207; female students OR = 2.169, 95% CI: 1.949–2.413). Myopia and smartphone addiction were identified as common factors associated with higher odds of LPA across genders. Depressive symptoms were a positive predictor of inactivity for both males and females. Formal interaction testing in the pooled model confirmed significant gender differences for anxiety severity and BMI. In the pooled interaction model, the associations of anxiety severity and BMI with low physical activity differed by gender. Interaction analyses showed that anxiety severity was associated with lower odds of LPA among female students, whereas higher BMI was associated with higher odds of LPA primarily among male students.ConclusionThe issue of IPAQ-derived LPA among Chinese university students is severe and exhibits marked gender differences. Female students are more susceptible to IPAQ-derived LPA, while the risk for male students is more closely associated with weight perception. Low self-efficacy was identified as a key high-risk influencing factor associated with higher odds of LPA, whereas depression, smartphone addiction, and myopia were core associated factors across genders. Formal interaction analyses indicated that anxiety severity and BMI were associated with physical inactivity in a gender-dependent manner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28549-w.

Introduction

Physical inactivity has been identified by the World Health Organization as the fourth leading risk factor for chronic diseases [1]. However, the PA levels of Chinese university students remain generally low [2], particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic, and have further declined. In recent years, China has attached great importance to university students’ health. The “Healthy China 2030” Plan Outline identifies university students as a key target group of the National Fitness Program, reflecting the strategic priority placed on improving the health of the younger generation [3]. This focus is essential for strengthening comprehensive national strength and ensuring sustainable future development [4]. Therefore, by adopting a student-centered approach, we can offer personalized health promotion programs for university students [5], which is essential for genuinely improving the physical health of Chinese university students.

Relevant studies indicate that gender plays a moderating role in influencing PA [6]. Men and women exhibit differences in certain socio-cognitive factors [7], which lead to significant gender disparities in the factors contributing to physical inactivity among university students [7]. In terms of overall PA levels, female university students show a lower participation rate in physical activities [8]. Research indicates that men are more likely to engage in aerobic exercise, strength training, or competitive sports, whereas women often prefer low-intensity or recreational activities. This distinction highlights the differing preferences in PA types between genders [9].However, existing health promotion interventions often adopt a one-size-fits-all approach, failing to account for these distinct preferences [10]. For instance, campus-based PA programs frequently emphasize competitive sports and team-based activities, which tend to align more closely with male preferences, while neglecting to provide adequate low-intensity or recreational options that might better engage female students [11]. This distinction highlights the differing preferences in PA types between genders [12]. In terms of socio-cultural factors, gender role expectations impose implicit constraints on sports participation. Men are often encouraged to demonstrate strength and competitiveness through physical exercise [13], while women may limit their outdoor activities due to concerns related to body image [14], social evaluation [15], or safety considerations [16]. Existing broad-based health promotion interventions may demonstrate limited efficacy in increasing PA within this demographic [17]. Numerous studies suggest that the determinants of physical inactivity are not uniform across genders, yet many existing studies treat university students as a homogeneous group, thereby overlooking gender-specific mechanisms. Uniform intervention programs such as standardized PE curricula, general health education campaigns, or campus-wide activity initiatives often fail to account for gender-specific preferences, psychological barriers, and socio-cultural constraints [18]. As a result, such interventions may be less engaging for female students or fail to address motivational and confidence-related barriers, thereby limiting their effectiveness.

Psychological factors also play an important role in PA among university students [19]. Relevant studies have shown that college students’ physical exercise can positively predict their emotional regulation ability [20]. Depressive symptoms, such as diminished motivation, heightened fatigue, and reduced interest, can directly contribute to a decrease in an individual’s PA levels, thereby establishing a reciprocal relationship between mental and physical health [21]. Depression diminishes an individual’s motivation and energy, thereby increasing the likelihood of sedentary behavior and subsequently reducing engagement in PA [22]. Research findings suggest that anxiety constitutes a critical determinant influencing PA among university students. Symptoms of anxiety not only adversely affect students’ mental health [23] but also exert both direct and indirect effects on their PA patterns and behaviors [24]. Self-efficacy denotes an individual’s conviction in their capacity to effectively execute tasks or behaviors. Moreover, extensive research has demonstrated a significant correlation between self-efficacy and levels of PA [25]. Individuals exhibiting high levels of self-efficacy are more inclined to engage in and maintain regular exercise [26], as well as to manage stress more effectively through the adoption of healthy PA regimens [27]. In contrast, low self-efficacy is strongly associated with a tendency toward physical inactivity [28]. Hence, it can be inferred that psychological interventions are essential for examining the determinants of physical inactivity among Chinese university students.

In addition to psychological factors, physiological and behavioral indicators are also associated with PA levels. Students exhibiting abnormal Body Mass Index (BMI) frequently demonstrate decreased engagement in PA, which may be attributed to physical constraints [29], diminished self-confidence [14], or adverse prior experiences [30]. Students who are overweight or obese may demonstrate diminished performance in physical activities as a result of increased physical burden, heightened cardiovascular strain, and an elevated risk of joint injuries, factors that can subsequently reduce their motivation to engage in exercise [31]. Likewise, students classified as underweight based on their BMI may experience challenges, including reduced endurance, diminished muscular strength, and increased susceptibility to fatigue, which can adversely affect their performance and engagement in physical activities [18]. Moreover, visual impairments constitute an additional important factor influencing physical inactivity among university students in China [32]. Visual health concerns are increasingly prevalent within this demographic, with pertinent surveys revealing that more than 70% of university students suffer from impaired vision [33]. Research shows an association between the degree of myopia and reduced PA among university students [34]. The extensive proliferation of smartphones and their engaging functionalities has resulted in individuals, particularly younger demographics, dedicating substantial durations to screen-based activities [35]. Excessive smartphone use can lead to the development of smartphone addiction, which is characterized by a pronounced dependence on the device, the emergence of withdrawal symptoms, interpersonal conflicts, and difficulties in emotional regulation, among other associated problems [36]. Nearly half of university students show signs of smartphone addiction [37]. The most direct impact of excessive screen time is the encroachment on time that could otherwise be used for physical exercise. Young students spend large amounts of their free time on activities like using mobile phones, engaging with social media, and watching videos, resulting in a significant decrease in the time available for PA each day [38]. These findings suggest that physiological and behavioral factors jointly contribute to physical inactivity among university students. It requires exploration from multiple perspectives and the study of multi-dimensional intervention strategies to effectively encourage young people to adopt a positive and healthy active lifestyle.

While scholars from various fields have accumulated a relatively rich body of research on the influencing factors of PA, a comprehensive review reveals several notable limitations in the existing literature regarding study populations, theoretical perspectives, and methodological approaches. In terms of study populations, research has predominantly prioritized the formation of exercise habits in children and adolescents [39] or rehabilitative activities for middle-aged and elderly populations with chronic diseases, often focusing on the adverse effects of LPA levels [40]. In contrast, there has been relatively insufficient attention paid to the university student population, which is in a critical transitional period. Regarding research content, existing literature often analyzes from a single dimension, emphasizing either the built environment and facility accessibility [41] or concentrating on curriculum design and institutional arrangements [42]. Existing studies often focus on a single dimension, such as the built environment or institutional arrangements, and rarely integrate personal, behavioral, and psychological factors [18].More importantly, existing studies rarely provide empirical evidence on how multiple factors jointly influence PA through gender-differentiated pathways, leaving a critical gap in understanding the mechanisms underlying physical inactivity among male and female university students. Therefore, based on the CPAHLS-CS, this study examined PA levels among Chinese university students, identified factors associated with IPAQ-derived LPA, and tested whether these associations differed by gender. To strengthen the theoretical grounding of this study, we used the Social Ecological Model as a broad organizing lens and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior model as the operational analytic framework. The Social Ecological Model emphasizes that physical activity is shaped by multiple interacting influences rather than by isolated individual attributes. In the present study, this perspective is particularly useful for framing gender not merely as a demographic control variable, but as a socially embedded moderating factor that may alter how psychological, physiological, and behavioral characteristics are associated with physical inactivity.

The COM-B model was used more specifically to operationalize the measured variables and to translate empirical findings into intervention targets. Within this framework, BMI and visual health status were conceptualized as indicators of physical capability; self-efficacy was conceptualized as reflective motivation; depression and anxiety severity were conceptualized as affective or automatic motivational factors; and smartphone addiction tendency was conceptualized as a competing behavioral opportunity that may displace time and attention from physical activity. Gender was not treated as a COM-B component, but as a moderator that may condition the associations between COM-B-related factors and physical inactivity.

Therefore, the two frameworks served different but complementary purposes: the Social Ecological Model provided the broader rationale for examining gender-sensitive and multidimensional determinants of physical inactivity, whereas COM-B provided the operational structure for classifying variables and interpreting intervention implications. By integrating these frameworks, this study aimed to move beyond a descriptive list of correlates and to identify which capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related factors were most strongly associated with physical inactivity, and whether these associations differed by gender.

Method

Study participants

The respondents of this survey are current students of regular higher education institutions in mainland China. The list of such institutions refers to the National List of Regular Higher Education Institutions (as of June 20, 2024) published by the Ministry of Education.

Sampling procedure

This study adopts stratified, cluster, and multi-stage sampling methods to select the survey subjects.

Determine the sampling scope

To ensure the representativeness of the monitoring sample, three sampling cities (prefecture-level cities) were selected in each province (autonomous region or municipality directly under the central government), and samples were drawn from each of these selected cities. The sampling scope covers prefecture-level cities within provinces and autonomous regions. Among these, the provincial capital city of each province is directly designated as a “Type I” sampling city. The selection principle for the other two sampling cities is as follows: one city with a medium level of urban development is selected as a “Type II” sampling city, and one city with a relatively lower level of urban development is selected as a “Type III” sampling city. For municipalities directly under the central government, a random cluster sampling method is adopted during sampling, while still meeting the requirement of selecting three sampling cities. Stratification and sample size

For this survey, the population is categorized into different groups based on gender (male, female) and academic year, resulting in a total of 16 sample groups (e.g., first-year male students, first-year female students, etc.). The minimum sample size for each group is set at 45 individuals. The planned total sample size for each province (autonomous region, municipality directly under the central government) is 1,080 individuals. Nationwide (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan), the projected total sample size is 33,480 individuals. The survey was conducted in September 2024 using electronic questionnaires, administered by an administrative class. In total, 36,756 valid questionnaires were actually collected. These valid responses came from 192 regular higher education institutions across 25 provincial-level administrative regions. Sampling weights were not applied because the sampling procedure was not based on a complete national sampling frame with known selection probabilities. Consequently, the analyses were conducted on the observed sample, and the findings should be interpreted as associations within a large multi-region institutional sample rather than weighted national estimates. By institution type, the included provincial-level regions were: Beijing, Henan, Jiangsu, Guangxi, Shaanxi, Inner Mongolia, Jiangxi, Heilongjiang Hubei, Hunan, Chongqing, Liaoning, Jilin, Zhejiang, Anhui, Shandong, Guangdong, Yunnan, Guizhou, Shanghai, Hebei, Gansu, Shanxi, Ningxia, Qinghai. These regions were selected to ensure broad geographic coverage across eastern, central, western, and northeastern China, while also considering the feasibility of institutional collaboration and data collection. Within each participating city, institutions were selected through collaboration with local educational institutions and research partners. Eligible institutions included both undergraduate universities and vocational colleges, institution type was adjusted for in all multivariable models. 89.3% of respondents were from undergraduate institutions (including independent colleges) and 10.7% from higher vocational colleges. Institutions were invited to participate if they had the administrative capacity to organize student recruitment and implement standardized data collection procedures. Therefore, institution selection was not based on a probability sampling framework, but efforts were made to include institutions with different educational types and geographic locations.

Variable measurement

The CPAHLS-CS includes both physical health and mental health components. The physical health section collects data on students’ vision health, height, and weight (for calculating BMI). The mental health section primarily assesses students’ levels of depression, anxiety, self-efficacy, and smartphone addiction. The average time to complete the entire questionnaire is 10 min and 39 s. All established measurement scales used have passed validity tests for the Chinese college student population. ① Level Of Depression

The depression status of university students was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-Revised (CESD-R) [43, 44]. The scale was originally developed by Richard Radloff in 1977 and later adapted into a brief version. It consists of two dimensions: self-acceptance and self-evaluation, comprising a total of 9 items. It uses a 4-point scoring system, ranging from “not at all” (0 points) to “almost every day” (3 points). Higher scores on the items indicate more severe depressive symptoms in the respondents. The overall scale demonstrates good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.85. In terms of validity, its correlation with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) is 0.76, indicating sound reliability and validity [44]. It can well reflect depressive symptoms and has good reliability and validity among Chinese university students. The results of confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the scale demonstrated good fit indices in the sample of university students in this study ( χ²/df = 2.68, RMSEA = 0.072, CFI = 0.945, NFI = 0.938, IFI = 0.946, GFI = 0.929. All indices met the acceptable fit criteria, indicating that the scale has good construct validity.

② Anxiety Level

In the present study, anxiety among university students was assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). This scale was developed by Spitzer et al. and constitutes a component of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) [45]. The GAD-7 scale comprises seven items, which assess the following: (1) Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge; (2) Being unable to stop or control worrying; (3) Worrying too much about different things; (4) Having trouble relaxing; (5) Being so restless that it is hard to sit still; (6) Becoming easily annoyed or irritable; (7) Feeling afraid as if something awful might happen. The scale employs a 4-point scoring system: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater severity of anxiety symptoms. The GAD-7 scale has good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.905. Furthermore, the reliability and validity of the GAD-7 scale have been validated in multiple studies involving the Chinese college student population [46, 47]. It is effective in screening for generalized anxiety disorders and assessing the severity of symptoms. A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted on the college student sample in this study. The results showed that the scale had a good model fit ( χ²/df = 2.45, RMSEA = 0.068, CFI = 0.952, NFI = 0.947, IFI = 0.953, GFI = 0.936). The GAD-7 has demonstrated acceptable reliability, validity, and measurement invariance in Chinese university student samples. In the present study, confirmatory factor analysis also indicated acceptable construct validity [48].

③ BMI

The Body Mass Index (BMI), as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), was employed as the assessment criterion for overweight and obesity. During the questionnaire survey, participants were required to report their height and weight based on measurements from their most recent university or personal health examination. According to the BMI calculation formula, BMI = weight/height², calculate the individual’s BMI value. For the Chinese college student population, this study employed the BMI scoring table from the National Student Physical Health Standard for assessment. The normal BMI range for male students is 17.9–23.9, while for female students it is 17.2–23.9. A BMI ≤ 17.8 for males or ≤ 17.1 for females is classified as underweight, and a BMI ≥ 24.0 is classified as overweight/obesity.

④ Self-efficacy

The General Self-Efficacy Scale was shortened from the original 20-item version to 10 items. This study adopted the Chinese version of the General Self-Efficacy Scale, which was jointly developed by Jianxin Zhang and Ralf Schwarzer in China. The scale demonstrates satisfactory reliability and validity [49]. The scale consists of 10 items and uses a 4-point Likert scale for measurement. The final score is calculated as one-tenth of the sum of the scores on all 10 items, ranging from 1 to 4 points. A higher total score indicates a higher level of general self-efficacy in the respondents. The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) has a cutoff score of 2.5; scores below this threshold suggest a lower level of general self-efficacy. An analysis of its reliability and validity revealed an internal consistency coefficient of Cronbach’s α = 0.87, a test-retest reliability of r = 0.83 (p < 0.001), and a split-half reliability of r = 0.82 (n = 401, p < 0.001) [50]. The results indicate that the GSES demonstrates good reliability, which is consistently observed in its application among university students [51]. The results of confirmatory factor analysis indicate that the fit indices of the scale in the sample of this study met the acceptable criteria (χ²/df = 2.81, RMSEA = 0.075, CFI = 0.939, NFI = 0.932, IFI = 0.940, GFI = 0.925), demonstrating ideal construct validity. The scale is suitable for assessing self-efficacy among Chinese university students [52].

⑤ Smartphone Addiction Tendency

The Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS) was used to assess the level of smartphone addiction tendency among university students. This scale was developed by Chinese scholars, including Jie Xiong et al. [53]. The MPATS comprises four dimensions: withdrawal symptoms (negative physiological or psychological reactions when not engaged with mobile phone activities), salient behavior (the centrality of mobile phone use in thoughts and actions), social comfort (the role of mobile phone use in interpersonal communication), and mood modification (emotional changes caused by mobile phone use). The scale consists of 16 items; each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points). The total score ranges from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward smartphone addiction tendency and lower scores [53]reflecting a lower propensity for addiction. The factor loadings for the four dimensions ranged from 0.51 to 0.79, with a cumulative variance explanation rate of 54.3%. The results of confirmatory factor analysis supported the suitability of the four-factor model. The results of confirmatory factor analysis indicate that the model has good applicability in the sample of this study (χ²/df = 2.73, RMSEA = 0.070, CFI = 0.942, NFI = 0.935, IFI = 0.943, GFI = 0.928). The overall scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.83, while the coefficients for the four subscales ranged from 0.55 to 0.80. Test-retest reliability for the full scale was 0.91, with subscale values ranging from 0.75 to 0.85. These findings indicate that the Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale possesses good reliability and validity for use with university students [54].

①Physical Activity (PA) Level

The short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess the PA levels of university students. This study employed the Chinese version translated by Qu Ningning et al. [55]. Questionnaire results were calculated according to the International PA Questionnaire’s scoring methodology for activity levels [56]. The data were processed to calculate individual weekly PA levels (MET-min/w). The weekly level for each intensity of PA was computed as follows: the MET value assigned to that activity × weekly frequency (d/w) × daily duration (min/d). The sum of the three intensity levels yielded the total PA level. Individual PA levels were then assessed based on established grouping criteria and categorized into three grades: low, moderate, and high, representing the individual’s PA grade. In this study, participants’ PA levels were assessed by inquiring about their exercise intensity, duration, and frequency over a specified period. Scores were calculated according to a predefined scoring protocol and categorized into three levels: Based on the IPAQ scoring protocol, total weekly PA was calculated in MET-min/week and categorized into three levels: LPA, moderate PA, and high PA. In this study, low physical activity (LPA) refers specifically to the IPAQ-derived LPA category. Although PA level was originally classified into three categories according to the IPAQ scoring protocol, the primary objective of the present study was not to model the full gradient of PA intensity, but to identify high-risk influencing factors for IPAQ-derived LPA among university students. Therefore, LPA was defined as the risk outcome, whereas MPA and VPA were combined as the reference category representing non-LPA. This categorization is consistent with the risk-oriented focus of the study and facilitates the interpretation of odds ratios for identifying students at elevated risk of IPAQ-derived LPA. The short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess PA. Total weekly PA was calculated in MET-min/week and categorized into low, moderate, and high PA according to the IPAQ scoring protocol. In the present study, the outcome variable was IPAQ-derived low physical activity (LPA). For binary logistic regression, LPA was coded as 1, whereas moderate and high PA were combined and coded as 0.All intraclass correlation coefficients for the PA dimensions within this scale exceeded 0.7 [55] and have undergone reliability and validity testing globally, demonstrating good psychometric properties among university students [57]. In accordance with the IPAQ scoring protocol, PA records were screened for implausible values before classification. Activity episodes shorter than 10 min were not counted, because the IPAQ protocol defines eligible PA as activity performed for at least 10 continuous minutes. In addition, cases with unrealistically high total reported daily PA duration were excluded. After data cleaning, total PA was calculated as the sum of walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity MET-minutes/week, and participants were categorized into low, moderate, or high PA levels. Also, combining MPA and VPA may reduce information regarding differences between moderate and vigorous activity. However, because both MPA and VPA represent activity levels above the low-activity category, their combination provides a more parsimonious and public-health-oriented contrast between students with and without IPAQ-derived LPA. It effectively reflects the levels of PA. A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted on the college student sample in this study. The results showed that the scale demonstrated good fit indices (χ²/df = 2.57, RMSEA = 0.069, CFI = 0.948, NFI = 0.941, IFI = 0.949, GFI = 0.933). All indices met the acceptable criteria, indicating good construct validity, and the scale is suitable for assessing PA levels among Chinese university students.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data processing was primarily conducted using SPSS 26.0, Excel, and OriginPro 2025 software, with the main steps divided as follows:

The data collected via the Wenjuanxing platform were initially processed using Excel. To ensure data quality, rigorous data cleaning was performed after questionnaire collection. Invalid responses—such as those containing logical inconsistencies, missing key information, abnormal age entries, duplicate submissions, or irregular completion times—were excluded. To further address the potential bias associated with self-reported measures, several quality-control procedures were implemented before statistical analysis. First, the questionnaire was completed anonymously to reduce social desirability bias, and standardized instructions were provided before data collection. Second, responses with missing key variables, duplicate submissions, logical inconsistencies, abnormal age entries, or irregular completion times were excluded. Third, PA data derived from the IPAQ-short form were processed according to the standard IPAQ scoring protocol. Reported PA bouts lasting less than 10 min were recoded as zero, and respondents with implausibly high total daily PA duration exceeding 960 min were excluded. MET-minutes/week were then calculated using the standard MET values for walking, moderate-intensity PA, and vigorous-intensity PA. These procedures were applied to reduce the influence of implausible PA reporting and improve the comparability of IPAQ-derived PA estimates. Height and weight were self-reported based on students’ most recent physical examination records. Although this approach may reduce random recall error compared with unaided estimation, BMI remains subject to reporting bias. Similarly, visual health status, depression, anxiety, and smartphone addiction were assessed using self-reported questionnaire items or validated scales; therefore, these measures should be interpreted as screening indicators rather than clinical or objectively measured diagnoses. Ultimately, 35,696 valid questionnaires were retained from the initially collected 36,756, meeting the minimum sample size requirement as designed for the study.Multicollinearity diagnosis was performed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each predictor variable to assess model stability and ensure the reliability of regression coefficient estimates as well as the accuracy of model prediction validity.Chi-square tests were employed to examine differences in PA levels by gender across demographic indicators, visual health status, BMI, depression levels, anxiety levels, smartphone addiction tendency, and self-efficacy. The strength of these associations was assessed using Cramer’s V coefficient [58].Because several variables were categorical or ordinal, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to examine bivariate associations among PA level and the included predictors. For nominal categorical variables, correlation results were interpreted only as preliminary descriptive associations and were not used as evidence of causal or independent effects. The main inferential analysis was based on logistic regression. 5)A binary logistic regression was used, with physical inactivity coded as 1 and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity as 0. Sex-stratified models were first fitted for males and females separately. A pooled model including all participants then tested interactions between gender (1 = male, 0 = female) and each predictor: visual health, depression severity, anxiety severity, BMI, smartphone addiction tendency, and self-efficacy. Gender differences were assessed via interaction p-values. The data were analyzed separately by gender to examine the predictive relationship between independent variables (visual health status, BMI, depression level, anxiety level, smartphone addiction tendency, and self-efficacy) and the dependent variable (PA level). Grade was included as an additional covariate in the multivariable models. An odds ratio (OR) of 1 indicates no statistical association with insufficient PA. An OR > 1 indicates that the variable is associated with higher odds of IPAQ-derived LPA, whereas an OR < 1 indicates that the variable is associated with lower odds of IPAQ-derived LPA. In interpreting ORs, both statistical significance and effect size magnitude were considered. Given the large sample size, small ORs may reach statistical significance but may have limited practical significance. Therefore, ORs close to 1.00 were interpreted as modest associations rather than strong risk effects. Because of the cross-sectional design, ORs were interpreted as measures of association rather than evidence of causality. To ensure that the theoretical framework was operationalized rather than used only descriptively, all measured predictors were mapped onto the COM-B model before interpretation. BMI and visual health status were classified as physical capability-related factors; self-efficacy was classified as a reflective motivation-related factor; depression and anxiety severity were classified as automatic motivation-related factors; and smartphone addiction tendency was classified as an opportunity-related factor because excessive smartphone use may displace time, attention, and situational opportunities for PA. Gender was treated as a moderator rather than as a COM-B component, while grade was included as a covariate. This mapping was used to structure the interpretation of regression findings and the development of intervention implications. The influence of the above variables is considered statistically significant only when the associated p-value is less than 0.05. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of high-risk influencing factors among students from a large, multi-region, multi-institution sample, rather than as nationally representative estimates for all Chinese university students.

To further address the potential bias associated with self-reported measures, several quality-control procedures were implemented before statistical analysis. First, the questionnaire was completed anonymously to reduce social desirability bias, and standardized instructions were provided before data collection. Second, responses with missing key variables, duplicate submissions, logical inconsistencies, abnormal age entries, or irregular completion times were excluded. Third, PA data derived from the IPAQ-short form were processed according to the standard IPAQ scoring protocol. Reported PA bouts lasting less than 10 min were recoded as zero, and respondents with implausibly high total daily PA duration exceeding 960 min were excluded. MET-minutes/week were then calculated using the standard MET values for walking, moderate-intensity PA, and vigorous-intensity PA. These procedures were applied to reduce the influence of implausible PA reporting and improve the comparability of IPAQ-derived PA estimates. Height and weight were self-reported based on students’ most recent physical examination records. Although this approach may reduce random recall error compared with unaided estimation, BMI remains subject to reporting bias. Similarly, visual health status, depression, anxiety, and smartphone addiction were assessed using self-reported questionnaire items or validated scales; therefore, these measures should be interpreted as screening indicators rather than clinical or objectively measured diagnoses. Ultimately, 35,696 valid questionnaires were retained from the initially collected 36,756, meeting the minimum sample size requirement as designed for the study.

This mapping was used to structure the interpretation of regression findings and the development of intervention implications. Results

Based on the multicollinearity diagnostic results, the variance inflation factors (VIFs) for each predictor variable fall within the range of 1.003 to 1.604, all substantially below the critical threshold of 10. The tolerance values range from 0.623 to 0.997, all surpassing the commonly accepted threshold of 0.1. Notably, the anxiety level exhibits the highest VIF (1.604) and the lowest tolerance (0.623). In contrast, the VIFs for self-efficacy level, smartphone addiction tendency, BMI, visual health, and depression level are all close to unity, with their corresponding tolerances also approaching one. Considering these various metrics, it can be concluded that there is no significant multicollinearity among the independent variables, indicating a relatively independent variable structure that is suitable for subsequent regression analysis.

Descriptive analysis

Table 1 reveals that LPA constitutes the predominant level of PA among Chinese university students, representing 68.48% of the total sample, with a distribution characterized by distinct structural features. Descriptive analyses showed that IPAQ-derived LPA was significantly associated with grade, visual health status, depression severity, anxiety severity, BMI, smartphone addiction tendency, and self-efficacy in both male and female students. All chi-square tests were statistically significant at P < 0.001, with small-to-modest association strengths based on Cramer’s V. Within the myopia group, the proportion of students classified as LPA was higher than that of students classified as MPA or VPA in both males and females. (male: p < 0.001, V = 0.094; female: p < 0.001, V = 0.047). Furthermore, students identified as having smartphone addiction tendency were more likely to be classified within the LPA group (male: p < 0.001, V = 0.134; female: p < 0.001, V = 0.113). A higher prevalence of non-healthy weight status was also observed among students in the LPA category. Additionally, individuals with low self-efficacy demonstrated reduced participation in moderate-to-vigorous PA, with rates of 16% for males and 2.8% for females (male: p < 0.001, V = 0.125; female: p < 0.001, V = 0.104). In the total sample, female students with IPAQ-derived LPA accounted for 54.19% of all participants, whereas male students with LPA accounted for 20.14%. These values represent proportions of the total sample rather than gender-specific prevalence. When LPA prevalence was calculated within each gender group, 85.51% of female students and 47.74% of male students were classified as having IPAQ-derived LPA (The denominator is the total amount calculated separately for each gender).

Binary logistic regression analysis

In the grade-adjusted sex-stratified logistic regression models, low self-efficacy showed the strongest association with higher odds of IPAQ-derived LPA in both males and females (male students: OR = 1.997, 95% CI: 1.806–2.207, P < 0.001; female students: OR = 2.169, 95% CI: 1.949–2.413, P < 0.001). Visual impairment, depression severity, and smartphone addiction tendency were also significantly associated with higher odds of LPA in both genders (all P < 0.001). Anxiety severity was significantly associated with lower odds of LPA only among female students (OR = 0.924, 95% CI: 0.860–0.994, P < 0.05), whereas the association was not significant among male students (P = 0.245). BMI was significantly associated with higher odds of LPA among male students (OR = 1.153, 95% CI: 1.130–1.238, P < 0.001), but not among female students (P = 0.067). Among all variables, low self-efficacy showed the strongest association with higher odds of LPA (male students: OR = 1.997; female students: OR = 2.169). Myopia status, depression severity level, and mobile phone addiction status were associated with higher odds of LPA (OR > 1 for all). Myopia status, depression severity level, and smartphone addiction tendency were statistically associated with higher odds of LPA across genders. However, the magnitude of these associations was modest, particularly for smartphone addiction tendency and depression severity level, and therefore these findings should be interpreted as small but statistically reliable associations rather than strong risk effects.

However, because differences in statistical significance across stratified models do not necessarily indicate statistically significant gender differences, a pooled logistic regression model including gender-by-predictor interaction terms were further conducted.

The interaction term between gender and depression severity was significant. Depression severity positively predicted LPA in females, meaning higher levels of depression corresponded to a greater risk of insufficient physical activity. The negative coefficient of the interaction term suggested that this positive effect of depression was notably attenuated in males. The interaction term between gender and anxiety severity was significant. Anxiety severity was associated with lower odds of IPAQ-derived LPA among female students, whereas no significant association was observed among male students. The interaction term between gender and body mass index (BMI) was significant. BMI showed a weak positive predictive effect on LPA in females, and this effect became even weaker in male, with statistically significant gender differences. The interaction term between gender and smartphone addiction was significant. Smartphone addiction positively predicted LPA among female students (, and the positive coefficient of the interaction term indicated that this positive effect was stronger in males. The interaction term between gender and self-efficacy failed to reach statistical significance, which demonstrated that the negative predictive effect of self-efficacy on LPA had no obvious difference between males and females. The results of the above interaction term test confirm that there are significant gender differences in the effects of visual health, depression, anxiety, BMI and smartphone addiction on insufficient physical activity, while the effect of self-efficacy is relatively consistent. These findings provide direct statistical evidence for the formulation of gender-targeted physical activity intervention strategies.

Figure 2 visualizes the grade-adjusted ORs and 95% CIs from the sex-stratified logistic regression models. Compared with the numerical results in Table 2, the figure highlights the relative magnitude and precision of associations across predictors and between genders. Low self-efficacy showed the largest association with IPAQ-derived LPA in both male and female students, with ORs clearly higher than those of other predictors. Visual impairment, depression severity, and smartphone addiction tendency were consistently associated with higher odds of LPA across genders, although their effect sizes were modest. In contrast, BMI and anxiety severity showed more gender-specific patterns, which were further evaluated using the pooled interaction model in Table 3. Therefore, Fig. 2 complements the regression table by visually emphasizing effect-size hierarchy, confidence intervals, and gender-specific patterns rather than merely duplicating numerical estimates. No such significance was observed for females. After additionally adjusting for grade, the main associations remained largely consistent with the original model. Low self-efficacy remained the strongest factor associated with insufficient PA in both male and female students. Visual impairment, depressive symptoms, and smartphone addiction tendency were also consistently associated with higher odds of insufficient PA across genders. The gender-specific patterns for BMI and anxiety were generally retained: BMI was significantly associated with insufficient PA among male students, whereas anxiety severity was significantly associated with insufficient PA among female students.

Overall, the COM-B-based interpretation indicated that motivation-related factors were particularly salient. Low self-efficacy showed the strongest association with physical inactivity in both male and female students, suggesting that reflective motivation may be a central behavioral pathway. Depression severity and anxiety severity further indicated the relevance of automatic motivational processes, although their patterns differed by gender. Capability-related factors also contributed to physical inactivity, with visual impairment showing consistent associations across genders and BMI showing a more gender-specific pattern. Smartphone addiction tendency represented an opportunity-related factor, indicating that competing digital behaviors may reduce the time and contextual opportunity available for PA. These findings suggest that IPAQ-derived LPA among university students is not attributable to a single domain, but reflects the combined influence of capability, opportunity, and motivation, with gender shaping the strength and direction of some associations.

Discussion

Using data from the CPAHLS-CS, this study identified high-risk influencing factors associated with IPAQ-derived LPA among Chinese university students and examined whether these associations differed by gender. The findings provide a more operationalized theoretical interpretation of physical inactivity by linking measured predictors to COM-B domains within a broader social-ecological perspective. Specifically, the Social Ecological Model helped frame physical inactivity as a multidimensional and gender-sensitive phenomenon, while COM-B enabled the empirical findings to be organized into capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related pathways.

The results suggest that motivation-related factors, especially low self-efficacy, were the most prominent correlates of physical inactivity across genders. Capability-related factors, including BMI and visual health status, also contributed to risk, although BMI showed a more gender-specific pattern. Smartphone addiction tendency reflected an opportunity-related pathway, suggesting that digital behavioral environments may compete with PA by displacing time and attention. Importantly, gender moderated several associations, indicating that the same COM-B-related factor may not operate identically among male and female students. Thus, the theoretical contribution of this study lies not in testing a full structural causal model, but in demonstrating how COM-B domains can be used to organize risk identification and how gender may condition the associations between capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related factors and physical inactivity.

These findings should be interpreted as associations with IPAQ-derived LPA rather than causal determinants of objectively measured sedentary behavior or complete absence of physical activity. Nevertheless, the domain-based interpretation provides a clearer basis for designing gender-sensitive intervention strategies targeting capability enhancement, opportunity restructuring, and motivation activation. Because body image, gender norms, exercise-related coping styles, perceived facility climate, and environmental intimidation were not directly measured in the present study, explanations involving these constructs are presented as plausible hypotheses rather than empirically confirmed mechanisms.

The prevalence and gender differences in insufficient PA among university students

This study substantiates that insufficient PA among Chinese university students is both highly prevalent and marked by significant gender disparities. Specifically, when calculated within each gender group, the prevalence of IPAQ-derived LPA was 85.51% among female students and 47.74% among male students, indicating a substantially higher prevalence among female students. The gender gap observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously. Although the present data showed a higher proportion of IPAQ-derived LPA among female students, this study did not directly measure body image concerns, perceived safety, gender-role beliefs, social evaluation, or perceived exercise-facility climate. Therefore, explanations involving gender norms or environmental intimidation should be regarded as plausible hypotheses rather than confirmed mechanisms.

One possible interpretation, consistent with previous literature, is that gender-related physiological factors [59] and socially [60] constructed gender processes may jointly shape PA participation. For example, gendered expectations regarding sport competence, appearance, and appropriate forms of exercise may influence students’ willingness to participate in different types of PA. However, because these constructs were not assessed in the present survey, the current findings only indicate gender differences in the distribution and correlates of IPAQ-derived LPA, rather than explaining the underlying psychosocial mechanisms.

Accordingly, the present findings suggest the need for future studies to directly measure gender-related psychosocial and environmental factors, such as body image concerns, perceived safety, social evaluation, exercise-facility inclusiveness, and gender-role attitudes. From a practical perspective, universities may consider offering more diverse, inclusive, and preference-sensitive PA opportunities, but the effectiveness of such strategies should be evaluated in future intervention studies.

Low self-efficacy as a high-risk influencing factor associated with insufficient PA

The results of this study demonstrate that low self-efficacy constitutes a significant risk influencing factor for insufficient PA among university students, corroborating the central tenet of Social Cognitive Theory, which posits that self-efficacy critically influences behavioral intention and persistence [61]. A deficiency in self-efficacy may impede the translation of favorable conditions into actual PA behaviors [62]. Specifically, diminished self-efficacy often leads university students to question their own capabilities [63], thereby decreasing their propensity to attempt or sustain PA, which consequently elevates the likelihood of insufficient PA. A comprehensive understanding of self-efficacy’s role across diverse populations and contexts, alongside its integration as a fundamental element in PA promotion strategies, is essential for augmenting PA levels among Chinese university students [64]. These findings indicate that low self-efficacy may be an important statistical marker for identifying students with higher odds of IPAQ-derived LPA. However, because the present study was cross-sectional and did not test intervention components, specific intervention pathways should be interpreted as practical implications rather than evidence-based conclusions derived directly from this dataset.

Future intervention studies may examine whether strategies designed to enhance exercise-related confidence, such as progressive goal setting, supportive feedback, peer support, and improvement-oriented physical education evaluation, can reduce insufficient PA among university students. Such strategies are theoretically consistent with Social Cognitive Theory, but their effectiveness in this population should be confirmed through longitudinal or experimental research.

COM-B domain interpretation of common associated factors across genders

From a COM-B perspective, the common associated factors across genders represented different behavioral domains rather than a single homogeneous risk cluster. Depression severity reflected automatic motivation, smartphone addiction tendency reflected opportunity constraints generated by competing digital behaviors, and visual health status reflected physical capability. This domain-based interpretation helps clarify why these factors may jointly contribute to physical inactivity despite having relatively modest individual effect sizes. Although depression severity, smartphone addiction tendency, and visual health status were statistically significant in the regression models, their ORs were relatively modest. Therefore, these variables should not be interpreted as strong independent risk factors. Instead, they may represent small but meaningful markers within a broader behavioral and psychosocial risk profile, especially in a large sample where statistical significance can be achieved even for small associations. These factors can be interpreted within the COM-B framework as follows: depressive symptoms reflect reduced motivation, smartphone addiction represents environmental constraints on opportunity, and visual impairment may limit physical capability. Depressive symptoms may diminish individuals’ motivation and behavioral engagement in exercise, often manifesting as decreased energy, anhedonia, and lack of motivation, which collectively impair willingness and capacity to participate in physical activities.

Depression severity, smartphone addiction tendency, and visual health status were statistically associated with higher odds of IPAQ-derived LPA in both male and female students. However, the magnitude of these associations was modest, and the present cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions regarding directionality or causality. Therefore, these variables should be interpreted as associated factors or screening markers within a broader psychosocial and behavioral risk profile, rather than as strong independent determinants of insufficient PA.

A possible explanation is that depressive symptoms may be linked to lower motivation, reduced energy, or lower behavioral engagement, which may in turn be associated with reduced PA [65]. Nevertheless, these mediating processes were not directly tested in the present study. Future longitudinal studies should examine whether changes in depressive symptoms precede changes in PA, or whether insufficient PA contributes to subsequent depressive symptoms. Similarly, smartphone addiction tendency and visual impairment were associated with higher odds of IPAQ-derived LPA. One plausible hypothesis is that prolonged screen exposure and reduced outdoor activity may be shared behavioral correlates linking smartphone overuse, visual impairment, and lower PA. However, because screen time, outdoor exposure, and temporal ordering were not directly assessed, the present study cannot confirm a causal pathway or a vicious cycle among smartphone addiction, myopia, and insufficient PA.

These findings may inform future campus health-promotion programs that integrate PA promotion with digital well-being education and vision-health awareness. However, specific strategies, such as screen-time management, outdoor activity promotion, or combined vision and PA interventions, should be regarded as potential intervention directions requiring empirical evaluation rather than as direct conclusions from the current data. Importantly, statistical significance should be distinguished from practical significance. The large sample size increased the statistical power to detect small associations. Therefore, ORs close to 1.00, such as those observed for smartphone addiction tendency, visual health status, and depressive symptoms, indicate limited changes in odds at the individual level. These findings are more useful for population-level risk screening and hypothesis generation than for identifying strong causal determinants.

Gender-specific COM-B pathways: BMI and anxiety severity

The gender-specific findings further demonstrate why gender should be considered within the broader social-ecological framing of PA behavior. BMI and anxiety severity corresponded to different COM-B domains: BMI reflected physical capability, whereas anxiety severity reflected automatic motivation. However, their associations with physical inactivity differed by gender, suggesting that COM-B-related factors are socially patterned rather than uniformly distributed across male and female students. This study identified significant gender differences in the impact of BMI and anxiety levels on insufficient PA. This finding extends beyond the traditional “single risk factor” research paradigm by highlighting the moderating role of gender in the interplay between physiological and psychological factors and PA behavior.

The observed gender differences can be understood through differential distributions of capability, opportunity, and motivation across male and female students. Specifically, elevated BMI was a significant risk factor for insufficient PA only among male students. One possible explanation is that BMI may carry different psychological meanings for male and female students. For men, body evaluation is often closely tied not only to thinness but also to strength, muscularity, and athletic competence [66]. Under gender-role expectations that associate masculinity with physical capability and sport performance, an elevated BMI may be interpreted by some male students as reduced fitness, lower athletic competence, or deviation from an “ideal” athletic body [67], thereby weakening exercise confidence and increasing avoidance of physically demanding activities. This interpretation is consistent with stereotype-based and expectancy-related accounts in sport psychology, which suggest that gender norms influence self-perceptions of competence and subsequent participation behavior.

By contrast, the non-significant association between BMI and insufficient PA among female students may indicate that BMI alone is an inadequate proxy for the body-related processes that shape women’s exercise behavior. A scoping review on body image, PA, and sport concluded that negative body image can act as a deterrent to PA participation, but these body-image processes are multidimensional and not reducible to body weight alone [68]. For women, exercise participation may be shaped more strongly by appearance surveillance, body dissatisfaction, social comparison, and situational body exposure than by BMI perse [69]. Consequently, the predictive validity of BMI as a solitary indicator is relatively limited for this group, suggesting that their motivation to participate in PA may be more strongly driven by social and emotional factors rather than weight control [70]. Therefore, our findings may reflect that BMI functions as a more direct behavioral risk marker for male students, whereas for female students, body-related barriers to PA are likely transmitted through more complex psychosocial pathways that should be measured more directly in future studies, such as body dissatisfaction, social physique anxiety, and exercise-related self-presentation concerns. In addition, the gender-specific pattern observed for anxiety deserves a more theory-informed interpretation [62]. The protective effect of anxiety levels against insufficient PA in female students is a significant and innovative finding of this research, contrasting with the common perception that “anxiety inhibits PA participation“ [71]. However, this result may be interpreted from an emotion-regulation perspective. Specifically, female students experiencing higher levels of anxiety may be more inclined to actively seek coping strategies, among which physical activity serves as an accessible and effective means of alleviating negative emotions. In this context, anxiety does not directly enhance exercise performance; rather, it may indirectly promote greater engagement in physical activity as a self-regulatory behavior aimed at reducing psychological distress. Therefore, the observed positive association between anxiety and PA in female students may reflect a compensatory mechanism of “exercise as coping,” rather than a contradiction to existing theories. Rather than suggesting that anxiety itself is beneficial, our findings may indicate that, among some female students, moderate anxiety is more likely to be translated into health-management behavior, including exercise participation.

From the perspective of health behavior theory, emotional states do not determine behavior directly; instead, their behavioral consequences depend on how individuals cognitively appraise symptoms, regulate distress, and select coping strategies. In this context, some female students may use exercise as a self-regulatory strategy to manage concerns related to mood, health, appearance, or weight control. At the same time, this conversion is unlikely to occur uniformly across genders. Recent research indicates that gender moderates the relationship between motivation, self-efficacy, and PA among university students, with female PA behavior being more strongly linked to ability motivation alongside self-efficacy [72]. This suggests that women’s PA may be especially sensitive to whether exercise is perceived as a feasible and meaningful coping strategy. In other words, anxiety may promote PA only when female students also believe that exercise is achievable, emotionally rewarding, and socially acceptable [73]. Therefore, the present finding should be interpreted with caution. Rather than suggesting that anxiety is inherently beneficial, it highlights the potential role of physical activity as a coping strategy for anxiety among female students. Future interventions may benefit from guiding female students to adopt healthy and structured forms of physical activity as a means of emotional regulation, while avoiding the risk of maladaptive or stress-driven exercise behaviors [74]. The results of this study suggest that interventions targeting female students should reinforce the cognitive guidance that “exercise alleviates anxiety,” promoting low-impact PA forms with high emotional regulation value, such as yoga, Pilates, and dance [71]. This approach can help reduce anxiety levels while promoting PA among female students, thereby enhancing their intrinsic motivation for exercise [75]. Accordingly, interventions for female students may benefit from explicitly framing exercise as an emotion-regulation and self-care strategy, while providing accessible forms of PA with high psychological acceptability, such as yoga, dance, brisk walking, Pilates, and non-competitive group exercise. This may help transform emotional distress into sustainable PA engagement.

Research limitations

This study is characterized by precise sampling methods and a substantial sample size; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal order or causality. Therefore, the high-risk influencing factors identified in this study should be interpreted as factors statistically associated with higher odds of IPAQ-derived low physical activity, rather than as causal determinants. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to clarify the direction and mechanisms of these associations. Second, most variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Although validated instruments and standardized data-cleaning procedures were applied, recall bias, social desirability bias, and reporting errors may still have affected estimates of physical activity, height, weight, visual health status, and psychological indicators. In particular, physical activity was measured using the IPAQ rather than objective tools such as accelerometers or wearable devices. Future studies should incorporate objective physical activity measurements to improve measurement accuracy. Third, although this study included several psychological, behavioral, and health-related variables, residual confounding remains possible. Important factors such as socioeconomic status, major or discipline, urban–rural background, parental education, sleep quality, social support, body image, and campus sports resources were not measured. These unmeasured variables may partly explain the observed associations and should be considered in future multilevel or longitudinal research. Fourth, although the study included a large multi-region and multi-institution sample, the sampling procedure was based partly on institutional collaboration and feasibility rather than a fully probability-based national sampling framework. In addition, upper-year students accounted for a relatively small proportion of the sample, and sampling weights were not applied. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence from a large multi-region institutional sample rather than as nationally representative estimates for all Chinese university students. Finally, the dichotomization of physical activity into LPA versus moderate/high PA facilitated risk-oriented logistic regression analysis but may have obscured differences between moderate and vigorous physical activity. Future studies may use ordinal, multinomial, or trajectory-based models to examine the full spectrum of physical activity levels.

Conclusion

Theoretically, this study contributes by clarifying the complementary use of the Social Ecological Model and COM-B in explaining IPAQ-derived LPA among university students. The Social Ecological Model provided a broad rationale for examining multidimensional and gender-sensitive correlates of LPA. The COM-B model offered an operational structure for classifying these correlates into capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related domains.

Based on the CPAHLS-CS, the study findings indicate that the prevalence of IPAQ-derived LPA among Chinese university students is severe and exhibits distinct gender-specific patterns. Female students showed a higher prevalence of IPAQ-derived LPA than male students. In sex-stratified and interaction analyses, BMI showed a more male-specific association with LPA, whereas anxiety severity showed a more female-specific pattern. Because body image, weight perception, and exercise-related coping were not directly measured, these explanations should be interpreted as hypotheses for future research rather than confirmed mechanisms. Low self-efficacy showed the most pronounced association with IPAQ-derived LPA. Depression, smartphone addiction tendency, and myopia were statistically significant but modest associated factors across genders, whereas the influences of anxiety and BMI demonstrate significant gender differences. These findings should be interpreted as association-based evidence derived from a cross-sectional design, and causal relationships should be further examined in longitudinal studies. Future PA promotion strategies for Chinese university students should focus on enhancing self-efficacy, strengthening mental health support, intervening in smartphone addiction tendency, and reinforcing vision protection. At the institutional level, universities are encouraged to integrate structured PA promotion into the curriculum. In addition, campus environments should be optimized to provide accessible and inclusive exercise facilities and organized activity programs. Specifically tailored strategies for male students should prioritize BMI management and body image perception guidance, promoting competitive and skill-enhancing sports programs. For instance, structured strength-training sessions, sports clubs, and performance-oriented physical education courses. For female students, strategies should emphasize reinforcing the cognitive link between “exercise and emotional regulation,” encouraging participation in social, low-intensity, and aesthetically oriented forms of PA, such as group fitness classes, peer-supported exercise programs, and wellness-oriented activity initiatives. Developing gender-tailored fitness programs and intervention frameworks is essential to address these differentiated needs effectively. Future interventions should be designed based on the COM-B framework, targeting capability enhancement, opportunity restructuring, and motivation activation to effectively promote physical activity among university students. Interventions should be integrated into university governance systems through credit-based incentives, digital health platforms, and continuous monitoring mechanisms, ensuring scalability and long-term sustainability. Future interventions should therefore be designed according to COM-B domains, with strategies aimed at enhancing physical capability, restructuring activity opportunities, and activating motivation, while also considering the gendered social context emphasized by the Social Ecological Model. Such precise and evidence-driven strategies can provide actionable guidance for policymakers and practitioners seeking to effectively address physical inactivity among university students.
