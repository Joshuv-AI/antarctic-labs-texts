Explainable Quantile CNN-LSTM model for uncertainty-aware multi-layer soil moisture prediction in tropical cocoa plantations
Authors: Sarowar Morshed Shawon, Mukter Zaman, Shamala Maniam, Marran Al Qwaid, Md Tanjil Sarker, Tee Yei Kheng, H Y Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of multi-layer soil moisture within the root zone is critical for cocoa plantations, where water availability directly influences root development, nutrient uptake, flowering and yield stability. In tropical systems, strong rainfall variability, heterogeneous soils, and delayed subsurface responses make depth-resolved moisture forecasting particularly challenging. This study proposes an improved Quantile Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)-Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) framework for robust and interpretable multi-layer soil moisture prediction. The model integrates CNN for localized temporal feature extraction with stacked LSTM long short-term memory networks for sequential dependency modelling, while incorporating quantile regression to provide probabilistic forecasts. Autocorrelation-guided lag optimization identified lag-7 temporal window as optimal. Zone 1 data were used exclusively for training and validation, whereas Zones 2 and 3 were reserved for independent testing to ensure spatial generalization and robustness. The proposed model achieved consistently high predictive accuracy across five soil depths (5–105 cm), with an overall average R2 of 0.948, low RMSE (0.39–0.79 across layers), and MAPE generally below 3%. Independent testing in Zones 2 and 3 demonstrated minimal performance degradation, confirming strong transferability under varying field conditions. For uncertainty quantification, quantile regression produced reliable 50 and 80% prediction intervals. Low mean pinball loss values, Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) close to nominal levels, moderate Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW), and favorable Winkler scores indicate well-calibrated and sharp probabilistic forecasts. Explainability analysis using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Integrated Gradients Attribution (IGA) revealed coherent depth-dependent climatic controls, transitioning from short-term atmospheric drivers in shallow layers to slower-varying seasonal influences in deeper horizons. Overall, this research delivers a robust, uncertainty-aware, and interpretable quantile deep learning framework for multi-layer soil moisture forecasting, supporting smart irrigation and climate-adaptive water management in tropical precision agriculture.Keywords: Multi-layer soil moisture, Root zone, Quantile regression, CNN-LSTM, Integrated gradient attribution, SHAP, eXplainable AISubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Soil moisture is one of the most critical biophysical variables governing agricultural productivity, particularly in perennial plantation systems1. In crops such as cocoa (Theobroma cacao), soil moisture directly influences root development, nutrient uptake, flowering cycles, pod formation and overall yield stability2,3. Unlike annual crops, plantation systems persist for decades, during which soil structure, organic matter content and root architecture evolve continuously4,5. In tropical cocoa plantations, soil moisture dynamics are further complicated by dense canopy cover, thick litter layers, heterogeneous root distribution and intense rainfall regimes6. Deep and extensive root systems extract water from multiple soil horizons simultaneously, leading to complex vertical interactions between surface and subsoil moisture, making root-zone moisture the most relevant indicator for irrigation and drought management7,8. Tropical soils often exhibit high permeability, rapid drainage and strong responses to short-duration rainfall events, resulting in abrupt temporal fluctuations. Besides, increase of the average global temperature and reduction in rainfall also creates water stress in plantations, highly impacting yield9,10. Cocoa is particularly sensitive to drought and heat stress, which can significantly reduce flowering, pod development, and yield quality11–13. These characteristics demand advanced predictive approaches capable of capturing vertical, spatial and temporal complexity within the soil profile contributing to the current and upcoming trend of data-driven precision agriculture14,15.

Machine learning (ML) models have gained prominence in agricultural soil moisture forecasting due to their ability to model nonlinear relationships between environmental drivers and soil responses16–18. Techniques such as Linear Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), XGBoost (XGB) have been applied by Settu and Ramaiah to model and test soil moisture and found XGB and RF to achieve the lowest errors (RMSE = 0.018–0.019)19. Arshad et al. also predicted the top-layer (0–10 cm) soil moisture in the arid regions of Pakistan using ML techniques and claimed GB to be more consistent with R2 = 0.88920. Araújo et al. reviewed the integration of ML and IoT in order to detect and predict moisture content in soil and water management21. This leads to the development of more capable soil moisture probes able to capture moisture variability through multiple soil layers22,23. ML, when integrated with the capability of customized soil probes, further advance soil moisture prediction capability24,25 and even more so when data from multiple layers are integrated26. However, in plantation systems like cocoa, the complexity of soil-atmosphere interactions poses significant challenges27,28. Conventional ML approaches rely heavily on engineered features and may struggle to capture temporal dependencies and cross-layer interactions29. Moreover, their performance can degrade when modeling highly dynamic tropical rainfall patterns and long-term moisture retention in deeper soil layers. These limitations highlight the need for more advanced modeling frameworks that can learn hierarchical spatial-temporal representations directly from raw sequential data.

Deep learning (DL) models have demonstrated superior capability in handling complex, nonlinear and high-dimensional time-series data30,31. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) effectively extract localized spatial and short-term temporal features, while Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are designed to model long-term sequential dependencies25,32. Often CNN integrated with GRU33 and even combination CNN-RNN with LSTM34 In plantation soil moisture prediction, where rainfall infiltration, evapotranspiration and root uptake interact across multiple time scales and soil depths, hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures provide a powerful solution35,36. Lü et al. presented Hydrus-1D and CNN-LSTM-attention model to predict root-zone soil moisture and reached R2 of 0.929, suggesting the superiority over traditional ML models36. By combining convolutional feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling, hybrid models can simultaneously capture rapid surface responses to rainfall and gradual subsoil moisture variations34,37. This is particularly advantageous in tropical cocoa systems, where moisture redistribution processes operate across both short and extended temporal horizons. Despite their predictive strength, deep hybrid models are often criticized for their lack of interpretability, which can hinder their adoption in agricultural decision-making38,39.

Beyond point predictions, uncertainty quantification has become increasingly important in soil moisture forecasting, particularly in climate-sensitive plantation systems. Quantile Regression methods enable the estimation of conditional quantiles of the target variable, thereby providing prediction intervals rather than single deterministic outputs for time series predictions40,41. When integrated with DL architectures, quantile loss functions allow the model to learn the distributional characteristics of soil moisture under varying meteorological conditions42. This is particularly relevant in tropical cocoa plantations, where rainfall variability, extreme weather events and heterogeneous soil properties introduce significant uncertainty into moisture dynamics. The relevant researches in this domain lack deep dive into the ability of quantile-based DL models’ ability to capture asymmetric error distributions and extreme moisture conditions, improving drought and waterlogging risk assessment. By providing probabilistic forecasts, quantile approaches enhance decision-making robustness for irrigation scheduling and stress management, thereby complementing deterministic DL predictions and supporting risk-aware precision agriculture.

In agricultural and plantation systems, stakeholders require not only accurate predictions but also interpretable insights that align with agronomic knowledge. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), techniques like LIME and SHAP, address this requirement by revealing how input variables influence model outputs43–45. Integrated Gradients (IG), a gradient-based attribution method, quantifies the contribution of each input feature by aggregating gradients along a path from a baseline to the actual input46,47. In the context of multi-layer soil moisture prediction in cocoa plantations, integrated gradient attributions can identify the relative importance of rainfall, temperature, humidity and previous soil moisture states at different depths. This transparency enables agronomists to validate whether the model captures realistic hydrological and physiological processes.

Despite the rapid advancement of machine learning and deep learning techniques for soil moisture prediction, several important research gaps remain, particularly in plantation agriculture systems. First, most existing studies focus on single-layer or surface soil moisture, with limited attention to depth-resolved multi-layer forecasting within the active root zone of perennial crops such as cocoa. Second, while hybrid deep learning models have shown improved predictive performance, many studies rely solely on deterministic point predictions without rigorous uncertainty quantification, limiting their practical utility under climate variability and extreme moisture conditions. Third, explainability is often treated as a secondary analysis rather than being systematically integrated into model validation, raising concerns about interpretability, multicollinearity effects, and physical plausibility of learned relationships. Furthermore, spatial generalization is rarely evaluated using strict hold-out strategies, making it difficult to assess true robustness beyond the training environment.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an explainable hybrid Quantile CNN–LSTM framework for robust multi-layer soil moisture prediction in tropical cocoa plantations. The model integrates convolutional feature extraction and recurrent temporal modeling with quantile regression to provide calibrated probabilistic forecasts through prediction intervals. A strict spatial hold-out design (training on Zone 1 and independent testing on Zones 2 and 3) is implemented to ensure genuine generalization. In addition, dual explainability analysis using SHAP and Integrated Gradients is incorporated to validate depth-dependent climatic drivers and ensure physical interpretability. Collectively, this research contributes (i) a depth-aware and quantile deep learning based uncertainty quantification framework for cocoa plantation soil moisture prediction, (ii) rigorous spatial robustness evaluation, and (iii) interpretable insights (IGA and SHAP) supporting climate-adaptive precision irrigation in tropical agriculture.

Materials and methods

Study area and materials

This study was carried out in a tropical cocoa plantation located in Bagan Datuk, Perak, Malaysia, a region characterized by persistently elevated temperatures, high humidity, and strong monsoonal influence. The Northeast Monsoon (October–March) brings prolonged rainfall, while the Southwest Monsoon (April–September) contributes to comparatively drier conditions. The soil profile in the plantation transitions from sandy loam in the upper layer to clay loam and clay at deeper layers, enabling strong moisture retention, especially at depths beyond 80 cm. To capture spatial variability, the plantation was segmented into three monitoring zones (1.16, 1.10, and 1.13 acres), each with distinct micro-topographic features.

To systematically monitor soil moisture dynamics, six IoT-enabled multi-depth probes were strategically deployed in each zone using a star configuration to ensure uniform spatial coverage. Each probe captured moisture levels at five depths: 5, 30, 55, 80, and 105 cm, covering the active cocoa root zone (0–150 cm). Probes recorded data at 30-minute intervals, enabling high-resolution tracking of root-zone moisture variations. During dry spells, supplemental irrigation was applied twice weekly to reduce severe moisture depletion in the cocoa root zone. These managed recharge pulses should therefore be considered when interpreting short-term increases in shallow-layer moisture during otherwise low-rainfall periods. Along with soil moisture, key meteorological variables: temperature, relative humidity, rainfall, wind speed, wind gust, solar irradiance and dew point were continuously recorded using co-located environmental sensors. All sensors were calibrated using oven-dried soil samples to obtain true Volumetric Water Content (VWC), resulting in a strong linear calibration curve (R² = 0.957), ensuring consistent conversion of raw sensor outputs into standardized VWC measurements.

Data acquisition was conducted over one year (Training range: 12/07/2018 00:00 to 11/22/2019 23:33). Zone 1 provided a sufficiently long continuous dataset and was consequently utilized for training (90%) and validation (10%), while Zones 2 and 3 (Testing range: 11/22/2019 23:33 to 12/31/2019 23:30) supported independent spatial generalization testing. To reduce noise caused by sensor-level fluctuations, values from the six probes in each zone were averaged. Erratic or missing values were handled through linear interpolation, after which all variables were normalized and synchronized to form a clean multivariate time-series dataset. The temporal descriptors were retained because they are deterministic encodings of daily, weekly, monthly, and seasonal periodicity rather than added learned complexity. They provide calendar-phase information that complements the short lag window.

CNN-LSTM modelling approach

A hybrid deep learning model was developed to integrate 1D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. The CNN component captured local temporal patterns from high-frequency sensor data, while stacked LSTMs modelled long-term dependencies between moisture levels and climatic drivers. The proposed CNN-LSTM was designed as a structured hybrid model rather than a depth-optimization exercise. Standalone CNN and standalone LSTM already isolate local temporal feature extraction and recurrent memory, so the hybrid architecture is built by combining these two mechanisms. Figure 3 shows the architecture of the proposed improved CNN-LSTM model. The architecture consisted of four Conv1D layers followed by five LSTM layers with recurrent dropout (0.1) to prevent overfitting. A dense layer of 128 neurons and a linear output layer generated the final moisture prediction. Check-point was used based on validation loss to maintain model generalization.

The resulting dataset and methodological framework provide a solid foundation for smart irrigation, moisture forecasting, and eXplainable AI-based agricultural decision support in tropical plantation environments. Data correlation analysis

A comprehensive autocorrelation and cross-correlation analysis were conducted across all five soil moisture layers (M1–M5) in the three monitoring zones. The autocorrelation analysis depicted in Fig. 4. The shallow layer (M1) exhibits relatively high autocorrelation at lower lags, reflecting short-term memory driven by rapid atmospheric interactions. However, the coefficients decline progressively with increasing lag, indicating limited long-term persistence. The intermediate layers (M2 and M3) show moderate temporal continuity with gradual decay, while deeper layers (M4 and M5) demonstrate stronger short-term persistence due to soil buffering effects. Nevertheless, none of the layers maintain consistently high autocorrelation across extended lags. The decay patterns and slight fluctuations across zones suggest that soil moisture dynamics are depth-dependent and influenced by nonlinear infiltration, redistribution, and root uptake processes.

Although temporal memory exists, it is neither constant nor purely linear, indicating that simple autoregressive models would be insufficient to capture the complex dynamics under tropical climatic variability. This supports the use of the hybrid CNN-LSTM framework to model both localized temporal features and longer sequential dependencies.

The correlation matrices in Fig. 5 illustrates the key relationships among environmental variables and soil-moisture layers over Zones 1–3. Due to the strong inter-correlation between relative humidity and wind gust were removed immediately after the correlation study and were excluded from all downstream point prediction, quantile estimation, and XAI analyses. Therefore, all reported predictive and uncertainty metrics correspond to the same final reduced feature set. Strong positive correlations are observed among adjacent soil moisture layers, particularly in deeper horizons, reflecting vertical hydraulic connectivity. However, each layer was modelled independently to prevent cross-layer leakage and artificial performance inflation. Among meteorological variables, temperature and solar irradiance exhibit strong positive correlations, typically ranging between 0.85 and 0.86 across the three zones, reflecting their shared diurnal and seasonal patterns. Rainfall demonstrates comparatively weak linear correlations with soil moisture layers, often below 0.30, highlighting its episodic and event-driven influence rather than continuous linear association. Together, the autocorrelation and cross-correlation analyses provide statistical justification for adopting a nonlinear hybrid deep learning architecture, implementing careful feature selection, and conducting depth-wise independent modelling. These analyses directly address concerns regarding temporal stability, potential overfitting, and inflated performance due to correlated predictors, thereby strengthening the methodological foundation of the study.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively assess the predictive performance of the proposed model, multiple regression-based evaluation metrics were employed. These metrics quantify error magnitude, relative accuracy, and variance explanation, providing a balanced assessment of model accuracy and stability across soil depths and zones.

Mean squared error (MSE)

An MSE evaluation yields the estimated value once the errors are squared and averaged. Because squaring each error tends to overemphasize its regressive properties, it is sensitive to outliers. MSE is extensively utilized in regression tasks.

Root mean squared error (RMSE)

By its nature, RMSE is the square root of MSE, and shows the average magnitude of a set of values relative to the variable of interest. For the purpose of interpretation, it is greatly preferred over MSE and even MAPE, which is why it remains popular for regression models.

Mean absolute error (MAE)

As MAE is used to assess the average of all absolute predicted values, it can only be positive or zero. Despite MAE being more lenient on MAE, it is more comprehensible than MSE due to the lack of nonlinear squaring.

Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE)

As a metric, MAPE showcases average values of predicted and real values that are useful for estimation. This measure does allow for greater accuracy relative to the prediction values when actual values differ substantially.

Coefficient of determination (R2)

R² exists within the range of 0 and 1, where 0 indicates no explanatory power, and 1 indicates a perfect fit. It measures the portion of variance in a dependent variable that can be predicted using the independent variables.

Here, is actual value of the target variable for the i-th data point, is predicted value of the target variable for the i-th data point, is mean of the actual values of the target variable and n is total number of data points in the dataset. These metrics are widely employed in assessing the effectiveness of a model in completion of regression tasks. Each and every regression metric comes with its set of pros and cons, and the issue at hand determines which metric is best suited for a given task.

Results

This section presents the predictive performance of the proposed Quantile CNN-LSTM model in a structured and zone-wise manner. Zone 1 data were exclusively used for model training (90%) and validation (10%), while the complete datasets from Zone 2 and Zone 3 were reserved strictly for independent testing. This experimental design ensures that the reported performance in Zones 2 and 3 reflects true spatial generalization rather than re-validation on similar samples.

Zone 1: training and validation performance

Zone 1 served as the model development zone. Selecting appropriate lags is essential to capture the temporal dependencies and memory characteristics of the soil moisture dynamics across different depths (layers). Using multiple lag intervals enriches the feature set and increases the model’s ability to recognize non-linear temporal correlations between layers. Figure 6 shows the heatmap of lag values vs. layers of soil in terms of R2. Lag optimization analysis confirmed that a 7-temporal window (3.5 h, as the data were collected at 30-minute sampling intervals) achieved the best balance between predictive accuracy, stability, and computational efficiency. Although marginal improvements were observed at lag 9, the gains were negligible compared to the increase in sequence complexity. Therefore, lag 7 was selected for all subsequent analyses across zones to maintain methodological consistency.

Heatmap of lag value vs. soil layer in terms of R2 in zone 1.

The empirical selection of the lag-7 temporal window also has a practical and physical interpretation. In tropical cocoa plantations, soil moisture variability is primarily driven by short-term rainfall events, evapotranspiration cycles, and root-zone water uptake. These processes typically influence soil moisture dynamics over short-term rather than over long temporal horizons. In addition, supplemental irrigation in the study site was applied approximately twice per week during dry periods, producing short recharge pulses in the upper soil layers. Consequently, a lag-7 (3.5 h) temporal window provides sufficient context to capture the dominant moisture redistribution and plant water-uptake processes within the cocoa root zone.

Figure 7 illustrates the time-series comparison between observed and predicted soil moisture for all five layers (M1–M5) in Zone 1, along with the 50% and 80% prediction intervals derived from quantile regression. The model closely follows the temporal oscillations across depths, effectively capturing both short-term fluctuations in shallow layers and smoother dynamics in deeper layers. The prediction intervals consistently envelope the observed values, demonstrating reliable uncertainty quantification without excessive widening.

Error metrics including RMSE, MAE, and MAPE remained consistently low, confirming accurate learning of temporal dependencies during training. Importantly, these results establish the model’s capacity to learn depth-specific soil moisture dynamics without cross-layer input leakage.

Independent spatial testing: zone 2 and 3

To rigorously evaluate spatial robustness and transferability, the model trained exclusively on Zone 1 was directly applied to the complete datasets of Zone 2 and Zone 3 without any retraining or parameter adjustment. This strict separation ensures that the reported performance reflects genuine generalization capability.

Prior to subsequent modelling and interpretation, the highly correlated predictors relative humidity and wind gust were removed to reduce multicollinearity, and each soil moisture layer was modelled independently to avoid cross-layer leakage. As illustrated in Fig. 9, lag 7 consistently produced high R² values across all five layers in Zones 2 and 3, similar to the behavior observed in Zone 1. Although lag 9 yielded marginal improvements in certain layers, the gains were negligible and accompanied by increased sequence complexity. Therefore, lag 7 was retained as the optimal and stable temporal window across all zones, reinforcing methodological consistency and robustness.

Under the common lag-7 input formulation, Linear Regression serves as a low-capacity autoregressive-seasonal benchmark, while standalone LSTM provides a nonlinear persistence-aware comparator. Since autocorrelation decays with lag and is not purely linear, the hybrid gain cannot be attributed to simple persistence alone. Linear Regression produced the lowest performance, with an overall average R² of approximately 0.80. Its comparatively high MSE and RMSE values indicate larger squared deviations from observed values, while elevated MAE and MAPE reflect greater absolute and percentage errors. These results confirm that linear models are insufficient for capturing the nonlinear and depth-dependent soil moisture dynamics. XGBoost improved performance, achieving an average R² of about 0.875, with noticeable reductions in MSE, RMSE, and MAE. However, performance declined slightly in deeper layers and independent zones, suggesting limitations in modelling long-term temporal dependencies. The standalone CNN showed moderate improvement over LR but remained less effective than sequence-based models, as reflected in higher RMSE and MAE compared to LSTM. The standalone LSTM achieved stronger results, with an overall average R² near 0.895, benefiting from its ability to model sequential dependencies. Lower MAE and MAPE values indicate improved absolute and relative accuracy compared to CNN and XGB.

The proposed CNN-LSTM consistently outperformed all baselines across every metric. It achieved the lowest MSE and RMSE, indicating reduced variance in prediction errors, and lower MAE and MAPE, demonstrating stable absolute and percentage accuracy. The overall average R² reached 0.948 across all zones, with values up to 0.973 in Zone 1 and remaining above 0.91 in the independent Zones 2 and 3.

These results confirm that integrating convolutional feature extraction with recurrent temporal modelling provides measurable improvements in predictive accuracy, error stability, and spatial generalization for multi-layer soil moisture prediction.

eXplainable AI (XAI): SHAP and IGA insights across all 5 soil moisture layers

To ensure transparent and physically meaningful interpretation, the proposed CNN-LSTM model was analysed using SHAP (Fig. 14) and Integrated Gradients Attribution (IGA) (Fig. 15). Prior to interpretation, highly correlated predictors such as relative humidity and wind gust were removed to reduce multicollinearity, and each soil moisture layer was modelled independently to avoid cross-layer leakage.

SHAP summary plots illustrating feature importance across soil depths: (a–e) correspond to layers (M1–M5), respectively, showing global feature contributions and directional effects.

Integrated gradients attribution (IGA) results: (a–e) represent layers (M1–M5), respectively, showing aggregated feature contributions across depths.

The SHAP analysis (Fig. 14) identifies the most influential predictors and their directional impact across depths. In the shallow layer (M1), short-term atmospheric variables and recent temporal memory dominate, reflecting rapid surface moisture dynamics. In intermediate layers (M2–M3), both meteorological drivers and antecedent moisture memory contribute substantially, indicating delayed infiltration effects. In deeper layers (M4–M5), feature importance shifts toward slower-varying climatic and seasonal descriptors, consistent with buffered subsurface behaviour. Variables linked to higher evaporative demand, such as temperature and solar irradiance, generally show negative contributions to predicted moisture, whereas rainfall-related and moisture-associated variables contribute positively.

To further interpret model behaviour, we compared the Linear Regression (LR) coefficients with the SHAP importance of the proposed CNN-LSTM. Although these two measures are not directly equivalent in scale, their divergence is informative because LR summarizes global linear effects, whereas SHAP reflects feature contributions learned under nonlinear interactions. Across the soil profile, antecedent soil-moisture terms were assigned much stronger and more physically coherent importance by SHAP than by LR; for example, M1 Lag 1 (1.037 vs. 0.125), M3 Lag 1 (0.850 vs. 0.045), and M5 Lag 7 (0.720 vs. 0.015). This pattern indicates that key predictors contribute through nonlinear temporal memory and depth-dependent moisture redistribution, while LR likely underestimates these effects because correlated lagged and climatic variables dilute the linear coefficients. Overall, the comparison supports a physically consistent transition from short-term memory dominance in shallow layers to longer-lag persistence in deeper layers.

The IGA results (Fig. 15) provide gradient-based attribution and closely align with the SHAP findings, confirming the same depth-dependent transition of dominant drivers. The consistency between these two independent explanation methods strengthens confidence in the stability and reliability of the model’s interpretations.

Overall, the agreement between SHAP and IGA, together with refined feature selection, demonstrates that the model’s strong predictive performance is supported by coherent and physically plausible soil-climate relationships across all five moisture layers.

Discussions

This study developed a depth-aware and explainable hybrid CNN-LSTM framework for multi-layer soil moisture prediction under tropical plantation conditions. Autocorrelation analysis confirmed that although soil moisture exhibits temporal persistence, particularly in deeper layers, the decay patterns across lags and zones indicate that the dynamics are neither purely linear nor strictly stationary. This justifies the need for a nonlinear sequential architecture rather than simple autoregressive or linear models. The superior performance of the proposed model over Linear Regression, XGBoost, CNN, and standalone LSTM across all evaluation metrics demonstrates the benefit of integrating convolutional feature extraction with recurrent memory mechanisms to capture both short-term atmospheric responses and delayed subsurface processes. The proposed model parameterizes the depth-specific moisture histories and meteorological response patterns. This helps explain why deeper layers show smoother temporal behaviour and slightly different error characteristics, as clay-rich horizons buffer rapid atmospheric forcing and delay percolation signals. The strong performance at M4 and M5 therefore suggests that the model captures the integrated hydrological effect of soil profile architecture.

A strict spatial separation strategy was adopted, where Zone 1 was used exclusively for training and validation, and Zones 2 and 3 were reserved for independent testing. The model maintained high R² values above 0.94 on average in unseen zones, with consistently low RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. This minimal performance degradation confirms strong spatial generalization and indicates that the model captures transferable soil-climate relationships rather than zone-specific patterns. The consistent selection of lag 7 across zones further supports the presence of a stable temporal memory window for root-zone moisture dynamics. The improvement from Linear Regression (all-zone average R² = 0.801) to the proposed CNN-LSTM (0.948) is substantial, especially under independent spatial testing. Moreover, Fig. 4 shows that autocorrelation decays with lag and varies across depths, indicating that persistence alone cannot explain the observed performance. The time-series comparisons indicate that the model remained responsive during dry-spell interruptions and brief recharge events, particularly in M1–M2 where deviations from seasonal trends appear most abruptly. This behaviour is consistent with the SHAP and IGA findings, which show stronger short-term atmospheric control in shallow layers and greater antecedent-memory influence in deeper layers, further supporting this interpretation by revealing physically meaningful shifts from short-term atmospheric controls in shallow layers to slower climatic controls in deeper layers.

The integration of quantile regression extends the framework beyond deterministic prediction by providing reliable 50% and 80% prediction intervals. Coverage probabilities closely matched nominal levels while maintaining moderate interval widths, demonstrating a balanced trade-off between reliability and sharpness. This probabilistic capability is particularly important under tropical climatic variability, where rainfall irregularity and extreme events introduce substantial uncertainty into soil moisture behaviour.

Explainability analysis using SHAP and Integrated Gradients revealed coherent and depth-dependent feature contributions. Shallow layers were primarily driven by short-term atmospheric forcing, while deeper layers showed stronger influence from slower-varying climatic and seasonal descriptors. The consistency between SHAP and gradient-based attributions, together with the removal of highly correlated predictors and independent depth-wise modelling, strengthens confidence in the stability and physical plausibility of the explanations.

This interpretation is further supported by the comparison between LR coefficients and CNN-LSTM SHAP importance. Predictors that appear weak in LR, particularly antecedent moisture terms at deeper depths, remain highly influential in SHAP, indicating that part of the predictive signal is nonlinear and interaction-driven rather than purely linear. Thus, the gain of the proposed model is not only predictive but also physically informative, revealing a depth-dependent shift from rapid surface control to longer-memory subsurface dynamics.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the proposed framework delivers accurate, robust, and interpretable multi-layer soil moisture prediction, addressing concerns related to temporal dependency, spatial generalization, multicollinearity, uncertainty quantification, and explanation reliability in tropical precision agriculture systems.

Limitations and future works

Despite the strong predictive performance of the proposed framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted within three monitoring zones of a single tropical cocoa plantation. Although these zones exhibit local variability, the model has not yet been validated across different agro-climatic regions, soil types, or crop systems. Therefore, the generalizability of the model to other environments remains to be further examined. Second, the dataset used in this study covers approximately one year of observations. While this duration captures seasonal variability and rainfall events, longer multi-year datasets would allow the model to learn broader climatic patterns and improve robustness under extreme weather conditions. Third, the pre-processing process involved averaging measurements from multiple probes within each zone and applying linear interpolation to handle missing sensor readings. Although these steps help reduce noise and ensure temporal continuity, they may smooth localized variability in soil moisture dynamics. Finally, model interpretation was performed using SHAP and Integrated Gradients. While these approaches provide useful insights into feature contributions, they remain post-hoc interpretability techniques and may not fully capture all complex nonlinear interactions within deep learning models.

The proposed framework establishes a foundation for integration into automated irrigation scheduling systems, enabling depth-specific and uncertainty-aware water management in plantation agriculture. In addition, future research may further extend the uncertainty quantification framework by incorporating multi-quantile training strategies and benchmarking the proposed approach against alternative uncertainty estimation methods under the same strict spatial hold-out protocol. Such comparisons could include probabilistic deep learning or ensemble-based uncertainty quantification techniques. Future research may explore federated learning to enable collaborative multi-site training while preserving data privacy and data ownership. Additionally, the development of time-series foundation models for soil moisture forecasting could be investigated to leverage large-scale, multi-region datasets for improved generalization and adaptability. Transfer learning strategies may also facilitate rapid deployment in new environments with limited labelled data.

Conclusion

This study proposed an explainable and uncertainty-aware hybrid CNN-LSTM framework for multi-layer soil moisture prediction in a tropical cocoa plantation. By integrating convolutional feature extraction, recurrent temporal modelling, lag optimization, quantile regression, and dual-method explainability analysis, the framework aims to address key challenges in depth-resolved forecasting under variable climatic conditions.

Autocorrelation and correlation analyses confirmed that soil moisture dynamics exhibit depth-dependent temporal memory and strong vertical connectivity, yet may not be sufficiently represented by simple linear or autoregressive models. The proposed hybrid architecture consistently outperformed Linear Regression, XGBoost, CNN, and standalone LSTM across all evaluation metrics, achieving an overall average R2 of 0.948 across five depths and three zones. Importantly, the strict separation of training (Zone 1) and independent testing (Zones 2 and 3) suggests promising spatial transferability, with only limited performance degradation in unseen zones.

The integration of quantile regression enabled reliable probabilistic forecasting, with prediction interval coverage closely matching nominal confidence levels while maintaining reasonable interval sharpness. This enhances the practical applicability of the model for climate-sensitive plantation management. Furthermore, SHAP and Integrated Gradients analyses provided consistent, depth-resolved insights into dominant climatic drivers, confirming that the model’s predictions are supported by physically plausible soil–climate relationships rather than spurious correlations.

Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates the potential to provide accurate, robust, and interpretable multi-layer soil moisture prediction, which could support smart irrigation, climate-adaptive water management, and the deployment of explainable artificial intelligence in tropical precision agriculture systems.
