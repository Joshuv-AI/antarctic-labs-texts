Perceptions of adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus about diabetes management and exercise
Authors: Meera Swaminath, Stephanie A. Griggs, James Lukasik, Elias K. Spanakis, Rebecca L. Kinney, Garrett I. Ash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of study:The purpose of this study was to understand the experiences and challenges with diabetes management and physical activity faced by adults with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM), as well as gather feedback on proposed features for a digital health tool, to inform the development of T1DM-tailored interventions.Methods:US-based participants who had T1DM for at least 6 months were recruited online through social media and healthcare platforms. Data were collected through two semi-structured interviews focused on aim (1) understanding lived experiences with managing T1DM during physical exercise and the role of digital tools in exercise routines and aim (2) participant preferences on proposed digital tool features.Results:Thematic data analysis revealed four major themes for aim 1: (1) lifestyle patterns, (2) motivations and barriers to exercise, (3) confidence with technology, and (4) T1DM-specific application with networking and accountability features. For aim 2, four themes were identified: (1) data interface, (2) tailored coaching and diverse representation, (3) T1DM-specific stigma, and (4) prior experience and current diabetes self-management goals.Conclusion:It is essential that a digital health tool for people with T1DM offers personalized exercise support that adapts to real-time glucose measurements. The tool should provide educational content, facilitate peer interaction, and integrate with continuous glucose monitors and insulin pump technologies. By incorporating features specifically designed for the T1DM community, a digital health solution can improve diabetes management and promote an active lifestyle.

Introduction

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) is an autoimmune disease characterized by the destruction of pancreatic beta-cells, which are responsible for the production of insulin (Lucier & Mathias, 2024; Sapra & Bhandari, 2023). Insulin is an essential hormone that plays a role in various physiological processes, including glucose metabolism and the conversion of food into usable energy (Cleveland Clinic, 2022; Lucier & Mathias, 2024). Individuals with T1DM often rely on a comprehensive approach typically including a combination of: (1) lifelong insulin replacement therapy, (2) medical nutrition therapy including dietary nutrition balance, maintaining body weight, and correcting metabolic fluctuations through diet, and (3) exercise therapy to enhance overall health and glucose targets (Lu & Zhao, 2020). However, factors such as snacking, dietary modifications, unplanned physical activities, and the stress and anxiety associated with the management of a complex chronic health issue can lead to fluctuations in glucose. Experiencing severe glucose fluctuations may result in fear of future hypoglycemia (defined as low glucose less than 70 mg/dL) and can lead to increased risk of stress and depression (Buchberger et al., 2016; Cox et al., 1987; DiMeglio et al., 2018; Grigsby et al., 2002). Adults with T1DM are also disproportionately affected by risk factors like hypertension, dyslipidemia, and sedentary lifestyles, all of which can increase rates of morbidity and mortality (Biswas et al., 2015; McCarthy et al., 2016; Vanderniet et al., 2022).

Exercise therapy, through regular and structured physical activity, has been shown to positively impact individuals with T1DM. Benefits include improved achievement of glycemic targets, cardiovascular fitness, increased muscle strength, and serving as a protective factor against depression (McCarthy et al., 2015; Reddy et al., 2019; Yardley et al., 2014). However, challenges arise with the risks of both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia - momentary spikes in glucose levels caused by factors such as insulin dosage, exercise intensity, dietary choices, stress, and sleep patterns. Additionally, the anxiety related to potential future hypoglycemic or hyperglycemic episodes can complicate regular exercise. To mitigate these risks, it is essential for individuals with T1DM to closely monitor their glucose levels before, during, and after exercising, adjusting diet and insulin as necessary. This approach can promote sustained exercise engagement while ensuring safety (Colberg et al., 2016; Riddell et al., 2017; Riddell & Peters, 2023). Unfortunately, stigma and discouragement from peers, family, and healthcare providers regarding physical activity - often stemming from fears about worsening health outcomes and a lack of knowledge about how to monitor physical activity - can deter adults with T1DM from engaging in regular physical activity (Kennedy et al., 2018; Kime et al., 2018; Knight et al., 2016; MacMillan et al., 2016; Quirk et al., 2015; Ryninks et al., 2015).

Advancements in diabetes technologies designed to support people with T1DM have revolutionized diabetes care management. Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) are among key innovations, providing people with T1DM with near real-time feedback on their glucose levels. This immediate data enhances glucose self-monitoring particularly during exercise, and helps individuals recognize trends and patterns in their glucose levels, ultimately guiding their diabetes management (Beck et al., 2019; Friedman et al., 2023). Research has shown CGM technology is effective in lowering the risk of hypoglycemia (Friedman et al., 2023; Moser et al., 2020).

Another significant advancement is the insulin pump. Insulin therapy involves taking multiple daily injections with long and short-acting insulin or infusing rapid-acting insulin through an insulin pump. Given the demanding nature of diabetes management - where people with T1DM must consistently self-monitor their glucose levels and make insulin dose decisions - automatic insulin delivery through an insulin pump can enhance glycemic health and overall health outcomes (Beck et al., 2019). Furthermore, the integration of closed-loop algorithms in these pumps, which automatically adjust insulin delivery based on glucose predictions has been shown to improve glycemic outcomes and overall system effectiveness (Brown et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2021; Fuchs & Hovorka, 2020; Kudva et al., 2025).

Coinciding with innovations in diabetes technology over the past three decades is the development of digital health applications (Elenko et al., 2015; Fleming et al., 2020). Digital health, also termed mHealth or mobile health, is defined by the World Health Organization as "medical and public health practice supported by mobile devices, such as mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants, and other wireless devices (Linwood, 2022; World Health Organization, 2011). Digital health tools often function as a method to close gaps in healthcare management. Digital health applications can be categorized into three distinct groups: (1) applications designed for wellness, (2) stand-alone medical devices, or (3) applications that use data from medical devices to monitor, diagnose, prevent, or treat a condition (Fleming et al., 2020). Commercially available diabetes applications are designed to improve health outcomes and quality of life for people with T1DM by supporting nutrition and weight goals, helping maintain lifestyle modifications, guiding dosing, and supporting glucose monitoring (AADE, 2008; Fleming et al., 2020). Digital health platforms also provide opportunities for providers, other diabetes health professionals, and people with T1DM to connect and provide support to each other. Virtual engagement with professionals knowledgeable about managing physical activity and diabetes benefits individuals, and peer collaboration can increase support and enjoyment around exercise. (Ash et al., 2019; Charpentier et al., 2011; Joensen et al., 2016). The emergence of these platforms is an important step towards providing additional support for people with T1DM.

Technology-based diabetes management tools such as continuous glucose monitors (CGM) and insulin pumps have significantly enhanced diabetes care self-management. Yet, only a third of people with T1DM achieve recommended glycemic targets (A1C < 7% and >70% time in glucose range 70-180 mg/dL) (Foster et al., 2019). Digital health tools hold promise for underserved communities, but poorly designed applications can hinder access to vital health information, especially for those lacking diabetes knowledge and digital literacy (Figueroa et al., 2022). Comprehensive diabetes support tools must be rooted in a thorough understanding of how diabetes interacts with physical activity, addressing both barriers and facilitators to improve care for individuals with T1DM. To fulfil these gaps, our study combined two related aims: the first explored lived experiences and behavioral challenges faced by adults aged 27-65 years with T1DM in diabetes management and physical activity, and the second gathered participant feedback on potential digital health features designed to address those needs. By linking behavioral insights with community feedback, our qualitative descriptive study uniquely provides practical direction for developing digital interventions tailored to adults with T1DM (Sandelowski, 2010).

Human Factors and Ergonomics (HFE) offers a systems-oriented approach for understanding how people interact with technologies while performing complex health-related work. In healthcare settings, HFE emphasizes designing tools and systems that account for users’ cognitive demands, decision-making processes, and contextual constraints to support safe and effective performance. This perspective is particularly relevant for adults with T1DM engaging in physical activity, where individuals must continuously interpret health data, anticipate glucose fluctuations, and adjust behavior in real time while exercising. This study focuses on how individuals with T1DM interact with exercise-related tasks and digital health technologies in their everyday lives.

Problem statement

Adults living with T1DM experience ongoing challenges in balancing safe, regular physical activity with optimal glucose control. While exercise offers substantial physical and mental health benefits, many individuals limit participation due to fear of hypoglycemia, lack of tailored guidance, and inconsistent support from healthcare providers. Current digital health tools often focus on general diabetes management, without fully integrating real-time data or addressing the diverse lived experiences of people with T1DM. Few platforms offer features that promote peer accountability or community engagement which encourage sustained exercise participation. Addressing these gaps is critical to improving health outcomes and enhancing quality of life.

Research design and methodology

Study design, setting, population, sample size

This study followed a qualitative descriptive design within a constructivist/interpretivist paradigm, which provides a straightforward summary of participants’ experiences in their own words and allows for low-inference interpretation of the data. Participants had T1DM for at least 6 months, were 27-65 years old, and lived in the United States. Purposive sampling was used to capture a range of perspectives across gender, age, diabetes duration, and technology use. Recruitment through online diabetes communities and healthcare networks supported variation in participant backgrounds and experiences. Two semi-structured interviews were used to gather data from participants until data saturation was reached (Hennick et al., 2017). The aim of the first interview was twofold: (1) to gain a comprehensive understanding of the lived experience with T1DM in the context of managing T1DM during physical exercise and (2) to explore how their prior experiences with digital tools or technologies impacted their exercise routines (aim 1). For the second interview, the aim was to gain insight into participant preferences and gather their feedback on the proposed features and integrations of a digital application that could fulfill their needs. Our objective was to determine how these enhancements can benefit participants in their journey toward physical exercise (aim 2).

This study was informed by principles from Human Factors and Ergonomics and human-centered design. The two-interview structure was intentionally used to examine how adults with T1DM manage physical activity in daily life and how digital health tools may support or hinder that process. Interview questions for Aim 1 were designed to elicit how participants plan exercise, respond to glucose fluctuations, and adjust behaviors based on prior experience and available information. Interview questions for Aim 2 focused on usability, clarity, and perceived fit of proposed features with individual routines, preferences, and levels of diabetes management experience.

The interview guide was developed based on these study aims and informed by prior research on diabetes self-management and physical activity. Questions were reviewed to ensure that they were clear, relevant, and aligned with the study objectives.

Data management and collection procedures

Exemption was obtained from the Yale University Institutional Review Board (#2000032779). Participants were recruited from July 2022 to January 2023 via social media platforms, including diabetes support groups on Facebook. The data reported from this study was drawn from in-depth semi-structured interviews (Appendix). Initially, a Qualtrics survey was sent to applicants to provide self-reported demographics, perceptions of T1DM management and physical exercise, and high-level preferences regarding proposed digital application features. These surveys were used to provide context and tailor the interviews as needed.

Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim using Zoom transcript, de-identified, checked for accuracy against recordings, and then organized and coded using NVivo R1 (2020) for Mac. Interview data were analyzed and coded for themes using qualitative content analysis (Graneheim & Lundman, 2004). Codes were derived through inductive coding beginning during the data collection process, and the interview guide was modified as needed to facilitate the emergent design. Interviews for Aim 1 and Aim 2 each lasted between ~45 to ~60 minutes, and data collection continued until informational redundancy was reached and theoretical saturation was achieved (Hennink et al., 2017).

Strategies of scientific merit

Whittemore et al. (2001) criteria were used to determine validity with four main strategies of trustworthiness including credibility (internal validity), transferability (external validity), dependability (reliability), and confirmability (objectivity) throughout the systematic process. To enhance credibility, data were collected via audio recording. Field notes and verbatim transcripts enhanced the reliability. A thick description of methods was reported to a second researcher, who made an effort to purposively recruit and select participants from varied backgrounds (transferability). To maintain objectivity (enhance confirmability), an audit trail, memos, conceptual mapping, and raw data were shared among the research team to validate the findings and confirm the themes. The coding/analysis team also included a member who had lived experience with T1DM. This perspective was used to contextualize interpretations and ensure that emerging themes accurately reflected participants’ experiences.

Data analysis procedures

First, transcripts were read in their entirety to get a sense of the whole. Next, meaning units were derived directly from the data, condensed, and abstracted into themes using NVivo R1 (2020) for Mac. Two coders used a conceptual coding method to develop the final coding structure by synthesizing 1,360 codes into a final coding structure of 59 codes until consensus was reached (Saldaña, 2025). Field notes, memos, and conceptual mapping were used to refine the themes and subthemes.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Among the 12 participants: 10 were 30-50 years old, 3 were newly diagnosed (7-14 months), 9 used an insulin pump, 4 identified as male, and 8 identified as non-Hispanic white (Tables 1A and 1B). Six had HbA1C above the 7.0% threshold, 5 had a household income <$60,000 per year, and 8 had a 4-year college degree.

Aim 1: life experience with T1DM management, technology, and connection to physical exercise

When gathering life experiences with diabetes management, technology, and the connection to physical exercise, four major themes emerged, including lifestyle patterns, motivations and barriers to exercise, confidence with prior technology use, and a need for a T1DM-specific networking and accountability digital application. These four major themes and key subthemes are presented in Table 2 including exercise, diet and sleep profiles, motivation to exercise, influence and barriers to exercise, overstimulation, and application support. Subthemes were reported to illustrate the breadth of participant experiences and preserve the richness and variability of participant perspectives.

Theme 1: lifestyle patterns

Participants focused on lifestyle patterns of exercise, diet, and sleep, including exercise frequency, type, duration, number of meals, highest caloric meal, and the positive effects of good sleep. Most reported exercising at least 3-5 times per week - engaging in physical activities like walking, hiking, weight training, and cycling typically for 30-40 minutes at medium intensity. They rated their overall confidence in planning activities at a 7 out of 10, although some participants reported feeling that a low level of fitness hindered regular physical activity. Regarding diet, participants generally consumed two or three meals daily, with the highest caloric intake at dinner. They relied on personal experience and partial support from healthcare providers for dietary and exercise decisions. Decision-making regarding diet, exercise, and glucose adjustments were at times self-managed, through "trial and error" and experience with the condition. Sleep was established as particularly important for their daily routine, with poor sleep leading to feelings of fatigue and irritability, while sleeping well enhanced physical and emotional health.

Theme 2: motivations and barriers to exercise

Motivations and barriers to exercise and the influence of family on exercise were discussed across the interviews. Several motivations to exercise were described, including better general and emotional health, weight management, and feeling better overall. When asked about motivations and perceived benefits of exercise, participants stated that they were motivated to exercise to maintain their overall "health," "weight," and to "feel better." They often experienced improvements in their mood, "less frustration and anxiety," "glucose regulation," and "better control of insulin resistance" when they engaged in physical exercise.

For general health motivations, one participant stated, "I always am able to be really fit physically, you know, that was always a goal for me" (P6). Weight management was a motivation noted by one participant who stated, "keeping my body weight down motivates me to exercise (P2), while another stated, "insulin is what makes people fat. So if I could take less insulin by working out, exercising, walking, then it stands that I would lose weight, which is a goal of mine" (P5). To illustrate the motivation for emotional health, one participant stated, “I think that on the days that I am able to exercise, I feel calmer. I feel like the tension that's all through my neck from sitting at a computer all day or working out, like I feel like I can relieve it more (P11).”

Participants' families and friends also influenced their diet and willingness to exercise regularly. Those who came from active families described themselves as someone who "take[s] their health seriously" (P2) and felt encouraged to make exercise a "lifelong habit." Other participants, whose families were more "food-focused" and "sedentary," were less motivated to exercise. For instance, one participant stated the following: “Meals and food are like the center of everything. And I'm a very big believer, like we eat dinner together, we will have a meal together. And I feel like we spend so much time focused on food, rather than activity" (P11).

Interestingly, a small subset of participants mentioned that family inactivity and perception of unhealthy genetic predispositions motivated them to exercise and become "role models" for their family. To illustrate one participant stated, “everybody looks up to me like a role model now. My brother and sister saying you doing it. I could do it too” (P4). Similarly, another participant stated, "I think my motivation is really looking at my youngest one and knowing I want to be healthy for him” (P11).

When discussing barriers to exercise, many participants, despite reporting well managed diabetes, cited the risk of hypoglycemia as a significant barrier to regular exercise. They likened hypoglycemia to a "cloud" hindering their ability to exercise, with remarks like "I can't jump on a bike if my sugars reading at 90, because then I'm gonna have to stop and eat something while I'm working out." This need to eat to counteract low glucose was reported to feel "counterproductive" to their weight management goals despite exercising and that "more food always means more weight." Most participants stated they almost always avoided exercise when they thought that their sugar was low. One participant recounted a severe hypoglycemic episode, stating that "the EMTs said they had never seen anyone with a 17-blood glucose before…if I lived alone, I'd probably be dead" (P5).

In contrast, the risk of hyperglycemia was reported to not influence decision-making around exercise; only one participant expressed that they felt "more concerned about the long-term damage that diabetes can do if I'm constantly running high" (P3). Other barriers to exercise included low fitness levels, busy work schedules, and interruptions from major life events such as pregnancy or childcare along with comorbid health conditions such as COVID-19, eating disorders, depression, irregular menstrual cycles, and cancer. One participant illustrated this impact, "I've got severe neuropathy. Oh, and I'm missing my pinky on my right hand. So there's complications. I don't have a lot of power in my hands and have frozen shoulders. Neuropathy in my legs and feet" (P10).

Theme 3: confidence with technology

Participants generally had high access to technology, social media platforms, and diabetes management tools in their daily lives and reported feeling comfortable using devices like insulin pumps and CGMs. Commonly used tools include laptops, computers, smartphones, and smartwatches, such as Fitbits and Apple Watches, and various applications for communication, social media platforms such as Facebook and YouTube, and diabetes management software such as MySugr, Dexcom Clarity, and Tidepool.

Most reported favoring diabetes technology, with one participant stating that "using automated insulin delivery systems have unlocked things that just before were not possible." Users particularly appreciated Dexcom CGMs, for their "discreet" and continuous monitoring capabilities which made them feel "confident" and "normal." One participant stated: "Not having to pull something out. Something you're trying to ignore, pretend it isn't there, and having to pull out of your pocket to look and stuff, it's a real pain in the butt. Whereas looking at it on a phone is simple, and it's right, it looks normal” (P9).

However, some participants also reported overstimulation at times and expressed a desire to decrease their screen time. One stated, "I really tried to detach even if it's for a few minutes or a conversation or whatever because I don't want to be continuously like glued to a device when there's an outside world” (P12).

Theme 4: T1DM-specific application with networking and accountability

When asked about their intended goals with a T1DM application, participants mentioned wanting the application to be specific to those with T1DM, providing timely reminders to exercise, and opportunities to use the platform collaboratively with peers. Many suggested that the app should be "Type 1 specific", well-integrated with diabetes management devices and created by someone with T1DM.

Specifically, one participant commented that it would be helpful if the app that was developed by somebody "who either has diabetes or gets diabetes could be really appealing to me because a lot of diabetes is a burden of carrying what I go through on a day-to-day basis. Without, you know, most people who don't have it don't really know” (P2).

Additionally, participants also mentioned wanting it to push reminders to prompt or plan exercise based on user activity and provide "positive reinforcement." Finally, participants wanted the app to provide opportunities for support and advice-sharing by connecting individuals with T1DM to one another, thereby increasing "community" and "accountability." To illustrate this, one participant suggested that "it could be like a social app, you know, where like, you track your progress with your friends and you celebrate it or whatever. Like it's more like a community-based app and social interaction app that makes sense for accountability" (P6).

Aim 2: participant preferences and feedback on proposed digital application

In the second set of interviews, participants were first shown some examples of features from an existing commercial application (GlucoseZone™, FitscriptLLC, New Haven, CT) that could meet their suggestions and asked for their reactions and preferences (GlucoseZone 2026). In doing so, four major themes emerged, including data interface, tailored coaching and diverse representation, T1DM-specific stigma, and prior experience and current diabetes self-management goals (shown in Table 3).

Theme 1: data interface

Participants reported highly valuing the integration of real-time data from their diabetes devices, like insulin pumps, with exercise videos, allowing them to adjust their diet and exercise accordingly. They expressed interest in features like historical reports, expressing interest in seeing the overlay data from exercise and non-exercise days to better inform their future workouts. However, many were unfamiliar with heart rate and glycogen depletion metrics, stating they did not understand these statistics. Despite the wealth of available data, participants wanted fewer, more customizable metrics to avoid feeling overwhelmed. One participant noted, "The last thing I need in diabetes is more measurements," emphasizing that an excess of numbers could discourage regular exercise, leading to burnout.

Theme 2: tailored coaching and diverse representation

Participants appreciated that the video library feature and coach-assigned workout videos "catered to people with diabetes." They emphasized the importance of diversity in representation - gender, race, age, and body types - in the videos. Some participants who identified as non-white mentioned that seeing themselves represented might motivate them to exercise, noting that the status quo of T1DM representation tended to be "very white."

This participant shared, "Oh, yes, that type one diabetes is very, if I can be frank is, very white. So, I mean, my whole life, I was diagnosed, when I was five, we go to walks, or we go to like community events, and we're the only people of color there. And it's just like, how, when we cannot be the only people of color in America who have diabetes, so, and luckily, I had my sister too” (P12).

Age representation was also a concern. One participant stated, "For example, I'm going to be 67. I mean, those people looked like maybe they were in their 30s. So, I think the age piece is important” (P8). Another person spoke about seeing different bodies, suggesting that "we don't all need to see perfect, you know, beautiful Beachbody specimens. It's, you know, hey, that guy looks like me. I think that it's nice to see a representation of something you can identify with. Somebody you can identify with” (P10).

One participant suggested that "when there was something in that video when he started out, he was like, my blood sugar's 138. And I was like, oh, well, this guy, like non diabetic generally doesn't have a blood sugar 138. So he's probably diabetic. I was sort of like that, that there was a little connective moment and just being like, the instructor, he himself is diabetic” (P2).

Participants expressed diverse interests in adaptive recommendations and tailored education, such as exercise suggestions, glucose stabilization information, and reminders to hydrate and snack. While some welcomed reminders as motivation, others found them disruptive, stating they felt like constant reminders of their condition. They stressed the need for flexibility, advocating for an opt-out option for recommendations to make the app more user-friendly.

Theme 3: T1DM-specific stigma

Participants expressed a preference for exercising with a coach or in-person, citing past experiences with T1DM that made them feel self-conscious around others without diabetes. One participant emphasized, “Having diabetes isn’t my identity,” and wished to avoid judgment from peers (P9). Others highlighted the stigma surrounding diabetes, making it difficult to share their experiences.

Some participants recounted restrictive experiences due to their parents’ misunderstandings of T1DM, while another noted frequent confusion between Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, leading to mislabeling. Additionally, one participant mentioned that despite a supportive family, they struggled to find effective ways to exercise with diabetes management.

Theme 4: prior experience and current diabetes self-management goals

Participants experienced with diabetes management showed limited interest in features like GPS tracking and Quick Start for exercise guidance, as they felt they already knew how to manage their supplies and understood their body's responses to exercise. They expressed concerns about liability, noting that such features might overlook critical factors like insulin levels and stress.

Participants with more experience with diabetes management showed limited interest in features like GPS and exercise guidance based on starting glucose, as they felt they already knew how to manage their supplies and understood their body's responses to exercise. They expressed concerns about liability, noting that such features might overlook critical factors like insulin levels and stress.

Conversely, those less familiar with exercise welcomed the idea of accessible education and support for finding diabetes-friendly snacks in emergencies. Many participants only saw their endocrinologists every three months and felt their providers lacked a deep understanding of their needs, often neglecting the complexities of diabetes management. They cited that even within these limited visits, their providers spent little time with them and did not "even understand how insulin works" and felt that "as long as you exercise, they [providers] don't care."

Overall, participants felt relatively unsupported by their medical team. When asked about how they envisioned their diabetes management and exercise improving using the application, participants stated that they would have a more positive experience with glucose variability and diabetes management, experience fewer lows and manage their A1C, be able to manageably exercise/increase accountability with exercise, have more awareness of their condition, feel more capable of adapting and making changes based on real-time data, get in better shape and improve their health and fitness, and feel empowered using the tool.

When envisioning improvements through the application, participants hoped it would enhance their glucose stability, reduce lows, better manage A1C, increase accountability in exercise, boost awareness of their condition, and empower them to adapt using real-time data—ultimately improving their health and fitness.

Discussion

This study was designed to understand the lived experiences of adults with T1DM, specifically regarding their management of the condition in relation to exercise, and to determine their preferences for features in a diabetes digital support application. By linking behavioral themes from participants' daily lives with their direct feedback on digital features, these findings illustrate how real-world challenges can inform the creation of more relevant digital health interventions. Overall, participants provided positive feedback on the proposed digital tools, highlighting that a specialized support platform could significantly improve their T1DM management.

We identified three major takeaways from our study. The first was that participants were motivated to exercise but faced barriers such as risk and/or experience with hypoglycemia. Second, participants felt that given the diversity present within the T1DM community, a successful T1DM digital tool should contain features that could be tailored to fit individual needs and lived experiences. Third, individuals recounted how inadequate support from providers and experiences with stigma negatively impacted their quality of life, highlighting gaps where a digital health tool could provide necessary support.

Motivations to exercise were consistent with another study that found that people with T1DM were motivated by the physical benefits of exercise and improvements in body image (Lascar et al., 2014). Despite motivation to exercise, participants still faced barriers to regular exercise, ranging from lack of time and low levels of fitness to diabetes-specific complications such as loss of control over diabetes and fear of hypoglycemia. Not surprisingly, the risk of hypoglycemia was found to be a major barrier across several studies examining barriers to exercise among adults with T1DM (Anderbro et al., 2010; Anderbro et al., 2018; Brazeau et al., 2008; Cigrovski Berkovic et al., 2021; Nefs et al., 2015).

Though risk of hypoglycemia remains a significant barrier to exercise among individuals with T1DM, recent research has found that digital health applications can support individuals dealing with hypoglycemia. A study assessing diabetes digital health applications on the market found that features explored in our study, such as integrations with diabetes devices such as CGMs, meters and wearables, and resources to support clinical decision-making were among the critical features needed to provide adequate diabetes support (Kordonouri & Riddell, 2019). Three other studies noted that advancements in technology and the ability to integrate novel technologies to provide uniquely tailored recommendations via smartphone applications could increase glycemic stability, resulting in fewer hypoglycemic episodes (Cigrovski Berkovic et al., 2021; Moser et al., 2020; Thompson, 2018).

Our study revealed that representation and individualization are key components of a digital support platform, where a tool can be tailored to support individual needs and reflect diverse lived experiences within the T1DM community. One study found that comprehensive and personalized diabetes education was effective when education was provided based on learners' needs and priorities (Cho & Kim, 2023). Differences in diabetes management can also be found across various racial/ethnic groups. For example, racial/ethnic minorities with T1DM were found to have less favorable glycemic outcomes than non-Hispanic whites and were less likely to check glucose or use insulin (Butler et al., 2020). To account for these differences in individuals’ needs, our research highlights a critical requirement for future digital health tools to provide more consistent, individualized guidance.

Furthermore, we found that digital health platforms may be able to support users in areas where they lack adequate support. Our study highlighted that participants felt they received little guidance from their medical providers regarding diabetes management, and that most of their knowledge came from other outlets, such as media, or through trial-and-error. Similarly, Kime et al. (2018) also found that participants reported little support from healthcare providers. One way to counteract limited guidance from providers could be to increase support from peers. Incorporating social and peer support into these tools can enhance self-management and bridge these gaps in professional care (Hazime and Burner, 2024).

In the current digital health landscape, a range of tools have been developed to support people living with T1DM. Apps such as acT1ve, Diactive-1, Enhance-d, Diabetes Cockpit, and the T1D1 app illustrate the growing diversity of digital solutions available (Active Network, 2026, Diaactive-1, 2025; Sestante Analytics AG 2026; Schuster, 2026; TD 2025). These platforms span research-driven interventions, clinically informed tools, and commercially available applications supporting T1DM self-management. The features and priorities identified in our study align with those present in existing tools, while suggesting opportunities to further enhance responsive and individualized support for exercise-related decision-making in T1DM.

However, digital support tools are not systems without limitations. As they utilize a considerable amount of data related to glucose control and physical activity, the end user needs to have a significant knowledge of diabetes. Because these platforms utilize mobile devices, digital literacy and smartphone access are extremely important. Many systems use internet networks to transmit data, making uninterrupted access mandatory. Another potential concern is cybersecurity; if applications and medical devices are compromised, personal information could be manipulated or device functions altered.

Our findings contribute to existing Human Factors and Ergonomics literature by applying a user-centered approach to understanding exercise-related diabetes self-management and the design of digital health tools. The results characterize physical activity for adults with T1DM as cognitively and behaviorally demanding, requiring continuous monitoring, anticipation of glucose changes, and real-time decision-making. These demands influence how individuals plan, engage in, and sustain exercise and help explain why fear of hypoglycemia and uncertainty persist as barriers despite strong motivation to be physically active. By linking insights from participants’ lived experiences with formative feedback on proposed digital application features, this study demonstrates how human factors methods can be used to translate user needs into design considerations. The two-interview structure enabled examination of both the work required to manage exercise with T1DM and the extent to which digital features align with users’ routines, preferences, and levels of experience.

Finally, this work identifies usability and fit-related challenges relevant to human factors design, including information overload, unfamiliar or poorly contextualized metrics, and variability in user preferences related to reminders, coaching, and data presentation. These challenges intersect with broader social and contextual factors that shape quality of life for adults with T1DM. Throughout our study, participants described how past experiences with stigma and discrimination, as well as unsupportive family members or spouses, negatively impacted their willingness to engage in physical activity and their overall well-being. Prior research has similarly shown that adults with T1DM experience stigma in the form of blame, negative social judgment, stereotyping, stigma-by-association with T2DM, exclusion, and discrimination, and that diabetes-related stigma is associated with increased distress and poorer glycemic control (Browne et al., 2014; Hansen et al., 2020). Together, these findings underscore the importance of designing flexible digital health tools that reduce unnecessary burden, accommodate differences in experience, and create opportunities for peer support. By facilitating connection and accountability in a supportive environment, diabetes digital support tools may help reduce loneliness and isolation while supporting safe and effective exercise engagement among adults with T1DM (Joensen et al., 2016).

Limitations

This study had some limitations. The sample size was small, and participants were selected until data saturation, as determined by the study team. It is possible that a larger sample size could have rendered an even greater representation of racial/ethnic backgrounds, genders, and participants from different socioeconomic backgrounds, impacting the current generalizability of findings. Additionally, while this study utilized in-depth Zoom interviews to allow participants to present their perspectives comfortably, it is possible that online mediums of data collection impacted the sharing of stories differently than in-person interviews would have.

Conclusions

The behavioral findings of our study were generally consistent with the barriers, motivators, and facilitators of exercise among T1DM participants in other studies. A major strength of this study is the combination of behavioral insights with practical feedback on proposed features for a digital health tool designed to support exercise and diabetes management. Ultimately, digital health platforms provide an opportunity for public health research to be put into practice, increasing the sustainability of knowledge within communities. By providing individuals with the education needed to make informed decisions regarding their health, digital health tools drive health equity.

Implications and applications

The results of this study provide guidance for the development of digital health interventions that support safe and sustainable physical activity among adults with T1DM. Integrating continuous glucose monitor and insulin pump data into exercise guidance can help users make timely adjustments to minimize the risk of hypoglycemia. Digital platforms should adapt coaching, reminders, and educational content to the individual’s fitness level, glucose patterns, and lifestyle preferences. Ensuring diverse representation in coaching and media content, including variation in age, body type, and racial or ethnic background, can strengthen user trust and motivation. Incorporating peer support and accountability features can reduce stigma, alleviate feelings of isolation, and promote sustained engagement in physical activity. From a healthcare human factors perspective, these findings can inform the creation of patient-centered digital ecosystems that bridge gaps between clinic visits, improve self-efficacy, and integrate seamlessly into the daily management of T1DM.

Impact statement

By designing exercise-focused digital health tools that integrate real-time glucose data, personalized coaching, and peer support, it is possible to improve glycemic stability, reduce diabetes-related distress, and empower adults with T1DM to safely pursue active lifestyles. This human factors-approach can enhance quality of life, support long-term health outcomes, and advance equity in diabetes care.
