Lymphedema Severity and Quality of Life as a Function of Selective Neck Dissection Technique
Authors: Skylah McLeod Van Wagoner, Colin MacKay, S Mark Taylor, Martin Corsten, Matthew Rigby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCombined lymphatic and venous insufficiency has been described as a consequence of lymph node dissections, but never in the context of selective neck dissections (SND). The objective of this retrospective cohort study was to compare two methods of SND: one (PEF), which preserves the facial vein (FV) and external jugular vein (EJV), and one (REF), which resects the FV and often the EJV.MethodsHead and neck lymphedema was assessed with a modified external lymphedema‐fibrosis scale and with the revised Patterson Scale (internal lymphedema). The FACT‐HN questionnaire was used to assess quality of life (QoL). Regression modeling was used to determine the effect of the SND technique on lymphedema, controlling for other factors.ResultsA total of 79 patients were included in the analysis, with 60 (75.9%) PEF patients and 19 (24.1%) REF. There were no statistically significant differences in demographics or adjuvant therapy between the two treatment groups. The average score for QoL out of a possible 108 was 94.1 ± 10.4 for PEF and 88.3 ± 11.6 for REF (p = 0.043); regression analysis with covariates showed no significant associations with QoL (p = 0.113). REF patients were observed to have significantly higher external lymphedema scores compared to PEF patients (OR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.00–8.02; p = 0.049). There were no significant differences in internal lymphedema scores between PEF and REF patients.ConclusionResults of this study showed resection of the FV and EJV was associated with more severe external lymphedema. A larger study that investigates differences in lymphedema between different SND techniques may therefore be warranted.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: head and neck, quality of life, radiation therapy

Introduction

Selective neck dissection (SND) is a surgical procedure primarily indicated for head and neck cancer (HNC) patients presenting with or at risk of cervical lymph node metastases. The procedure involves the resection of the fibrofatty tissues containing the cervical lymph nodes while preserving the spinal accessory nerve, internal jugular vein (IJV), and sternocleidomastoid muscle [1]. The external jugular vein (EJV) and facial vein (FV) can be resected or preserved with this method.

Head and neck lymphedema (HNL) is one of the most common adverse effects of neck dissection in HNC patients, yet it has historically been relatively understudied; HNL assessment is complicated by the lack of an established “gold standard” assessment method [2]. Assessment of HNL can pose a challenge for many reasons; most objective methods of lymphedema assessment rely on circumferential differences between limbs, or water displacement, both of which are essentially impossible in HNL. More subjective criteria exist, though they were not established in the context of HNL. Moreover, lymphedema is mostly assessed with “ad hoc clinical grading,” but this has been found to miss up to 91% of patients who would otherwise be identified to have lymphedema with more objective criteria [3]. As a result, HNL has previously been reported to occur in as few as 5% of patients [4] but has more recently been reported to occur in 62%–75% of patients [5, 6, 7] as criteria to specifically assess HNL become more established.

Quality of life (QoL) can be negatively impacted by HNL in several ways. Internal lymphedema of the neck, which can be assessed with the Patterson Scale, is correlated with dysphagia/swallowing dysfunction [8]. More generally, HNL has been associated with nutrition‐related problems, dry mouth, self‐reported speech difficulties, and poor self‐image, among other symptoms [9]. While most of these patients have both internal and external lymphedema, some patients will only experience one of these two conditions [9]. Internal and external lymphedema can have different impacts on QoL, moreover, the severity and presence of external lymphedema does not necessarily indicate equivalent severity or presence of internal lymphedema and vice versa; it is therefore important to assess both forms of this condition.

Phlebolymphedema is characterized by combined lymphatic and venous insufficiency and has been identified as an underreported condition most often described in the setting of chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), inhibiting lymphatic circulation, resulting in combined edema [10]. However, this condition has also been described as a consequence of radiation, as well as surgical operations that cause damage to the lymphatic system [11]. Nevertheless, phlebolymphedema has never been reported as an adverse effect of neck dissection specifically. Lymphedema commonly occurs when lymphatic structures are removed, and the resection of veins, such as the EJV and FV in SND, could potentially contribute to lymphedema severity by a similar or even identical mechanism as in phlebolymphedema.

Currently, it is unknown if the resection of both the EJV and FV has a significant impact on the outcomes of SND. The objective of this study was to determine whether removal of the EJV and FV contributes to the severity of lymphedema and whether this is associated with a difference in QoL.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study compared lymphedema severity between two different methods of SND differentiated by the routine preservation of the EJV and FV (PEF) versus the routine resection of the FV with or without resection of the EJV (REF). This was done at a single center, and each technique was represented by a single surgeon. Operative technique was otherwise similar between both surgeons; for example, both surgeons followed the same procedure, including routine sacrifice of the lingual tributary veins. Research ethics approval was obtained from the Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board (File #: 1029323). Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to inclusion in this study.

Participants and Sampling Procedures

Patients were eligible for the study if they were older than 18, had squamous cell carcinoma of the oral cavity or oropharynx, had an SND, and had been at least 3 months from completion of all therapy. Patients were ineligible for the study if they received a free flap tissue transfer as part of their surgical treatment, if the IJV was resected, or if they had received previous HNC treatment. Patients were grouped into two cohorts based on the method of neck dissection as outlined above (PEF and REF). Clinically relevant information, including age at treatment, extent of SND, adjuvant therapy (radiation and chemoradiation), time from treatment, cancer stage (pathological and clinical), surgeon, and site (oral cavity vs. oropharynx), was recorded as covariates to enable post hoc statistical model optimization and account for any confounding. Case inclusion by date of surgery was February 2018–March 2024; patients were recruited from August 2023 to August 2024.

Measures/Materials

External lymphedema was assessed with an external lymphedema‐fibrosis scale (Table 1). This scale was adapted from Deng et al. [7], which grades lymphedema severity from 1 to 5, where Grade 1 is described as no visible tissue edema; palpable tissue swelling, and Grade 5 is described as severe fibrosis with or without edema. The scale was modified to include a grade 0, described as no visible tissue edema; no palpable tissue swelling (i.e., no measurable lymphedema).

Internal lymphedema was determined with the Patterson Scale. This scale assesses internal lymphedema in 11 different structures, and in two different spaces, and also includes a summarized score [12]. There are four ratings of edema severity ranging from normal to severe (Grades 0–3). Flexible endoscopy was used for visualization.

The 39‐item Functional Assessment Cancer Therapy—Head and Neck (FACT‐H&N), version 4, was used to assess patients' QoL. The FACT‐H&N contains five subscales: physical, social/family, emotional, functional, and head and neck, the first four of which sum together to yield the FACT‐general (FACT‐g) score [13, 14]. The FACT‐g score was treated as a continuous variable for the purposes of data analysis.

Data Analysis

Demographic variables were compared between the two groups using chi‐squared tests. Internal consistency of FACT‐H&N scores was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. All covariates as described in Section 2.2 were used to determine any statistically significant differences between the PEF and REF groups. Patients were excluded from analysis if data were missing or if they had another medical condition that may confound accurate measurement of lymphedema (i.e., one patient was excluded as they had an infected parotid gland at the time of their appointment).

The covariates ultimately included in the multivariable regression analysis were age, clinical stage of the tumor, months from surgery, adjuvant therapy, and extent of neck dissection (unilateral vs. bilateral). External lymphedema was scored on a Likert scale, which necessitated ordinal generalized logistic regression, whereas generalized linear regression was used for both internal lymphedema and FACT‐g score. For univariate analysis, differences in external lymphedema scores were measured with ANOVA for all continuous variables and Pearson's Chi‐squared tests for discrete variables. In all cases, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. R statistical software (version 4.4.1) was used for analysis.

Results

Demographics

Seventy‐nine patients were ultimately included in the analysis with 60 patients in the PEF group and 19 patients in the REF group (Table 2). No statistically significant differences in any demographics were found. One patient was missing data for pathological cancer stage; they were not excluded as only clinical stage was included in the regression analysis. In the PEF group, 43% of patients underwent radiation and 5% underwent chemoradiation, compared to 21% for radiation and 16% for chemoradiation in the REF group; this difference was not statistically significant. To control for potential confounding of adjuvant therapy, adjuvant therapy was included as a covariate in the analysis.

In the regression analysis with external lymphedema as the outcome variable, neck dissection technique was associated with the severity of external lymphedema, with REF being associated with nearly three times the odds of increased external lymphedema severity compared to PEF patients (OR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.00–8.02; see Table 3). No other covariates were associated with external lymphedema, including adjuvant therapy. Figure 1 shows the distribution of external lymphedema severity for PEF and REF.

QoL

The average FACT‐g score for the PEF group was 94.1 ± 10.4, while the average for REF was 88.3 ± 11.6; univariate analysis showed statistical significance (p = 0.043). However, in the regression analysis, there was no statistical significance between the neck dissection methods (p = 0.113) or any other variable. Discussion

The relationship between HNL and the resection of veins in SNDs has been relatively understudied. One study found an association between EJV preservation and less severe postoperative edema [15], and other studies have found trends that did not reach statistical significance toward more cases of lymphedema in those who have undergone neck dissections compared to patients managed non‐operatively [16]. However, this is the first study to our knowledge to examine the association between posttreatment HNL and EJV and FV preservation in SNDs.

This study did show an association between neck dissection technique and external lymphedema, with a nearly threefold increased likelihood of more severe external lymphedema when the EJV and FV are resected (Table 3). There was a trend toward more severe external HNL in patients who underwent chemoradiation (OR: 5.16, 95% CI: 0.82–36.31); however, this did not reach statistical significance. The extent of neck dissection was not associated with external HNL in our study. The extent was classified as bilateral or ipsilateral dissection for modeling purposes. Neither the extent of neck dissection nor the site of HNC differed between the PEF and REF groups (Table 2).

In the external HNL assessment scale, edema and fibrosis are both included in one score, with the highest level of lymphedema graded as “severe fibrosis.” This scale is limiting, as fibrosis can often be caused by radiation alone [17], which may impact the clarity of the results. It may be worthwhile to assess lymphedema and fibrosis separately and to include a section in the scale to note extension of lymphedema to the face. It would be interesting to determine if the resection of the FV leads to facial edema, specifically. Furthermore, because fibrosis of the neck can cause facial lymphedema [18], being able to differentiate lymphedema and fibrosis severity in this patient population would offer further clarity into future analysis of this relationship between SND technique, radiation, and HNL.

Adjuvant radiation was found to be associated with an increase in internal lymphedema, while no association was found between internal lymphedema and neck dissection technique (Table 4). This is in contrast to the results of the external HNL regression analysis, and may be a function of the regional venous anatomy. While anatomical variants exist, the EJV and FV do not receive blood from internal structures of the neck; the veins of the pharynx and larynx are typically tributaries of the IJV [19], and resection of the IJV was excluded from the analysis as this is known to cause neck edema [20]. This may explain why the REF group was only associated with more severe external HNL, and not internal HNL. Conversely, radiation can disrupt more internal lymphatic structures and is more likely to result in internal lymphedema [17], which may explain the association in our study.

Univariate analysis did show an association between QoL and SND technique, with the REF group demonstrating a lower FACT‐g score. While multivariable regression analysis did not show a significant association between FACT‐g score and any covariates, REF and adjuvant radiation both trend toward a lower FACT‐g (Figure 2). Radiation therapy is known to decrease QoL [21], and HNL has previously been shown to impact QoL as well [9]. The FACT‐H&N was designed to assess QoL for patients with HNC; it does not specifically assess QoL in the context of HNL, and so this might limit the power of the analysis.

This study has several important limitations. Results were collected from two surgeons at a single center, and it is possible that results might not be generalizable. All treatment data were collected retrospectively, and as outlined in Section 2, patients were sorted into PEF and REF groups based on known surgical preference for resection versus preservation of the EJV and FV; this information was not always recorded explicitly in operative reports. Based on availability, external and internal HNL may have been assessed by residents, fellows, the staff surgeons themselves, or a medical student, and this study is not able to account for interrater variability. This study also did not account for patients who did or did not undergo lymphedema therapy (e.g., massage, occupational therapy), and this should be recorded in future analysis to account for possible confounding. In the future, a larger, potentially multicenter, prospective study would allow for clear documentation of which veins were preserved or resected, address variability between surgeons by comparing cases when FV and/or EJV could not be preserved rather than by surgeon, and would allow for masking of the neck dissection technique from those assessing lymphedema.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate the feasibility of and need for a larger‐scale study to investigate the contribution of EJV and FV resection to HNL.

Interestingly, we found that resection of the EJV and FV significantly increased the likelihood of developing more severe external lymphedema, but not internal lymphedema. As previously described in the literature, damage to the lymphatic and venous systems can decrease these systems' ability to handle interstitial fluid load [11]; SND necessarily damages the lymphatic system, and the resection of superficial veins during SND might further contribute to the accumulation of interstitial fluid. In the future, we plan to develop a study to further investigate the relationship between HNL and the SND technique.
