ABSTRACT
SummaryVasculogenic mimicry (VM) refers to the ability of non-endothelial cells to form fluid-conducting, vessel-like structures independently of endothelial cells. In cancer, VM is associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis, although similar structures may also be formed by non-cancerous cells. Here, we systematically screened more than 23 human and murine cell lines, including diverse cancer types, fibroblasts, and primary-tumor-derived cells, identifying cell types capable or incapable of VM formation. Using confocal microscopy and 3D reconstruction, we define in vitro VM as the formation of lumen-containing tubular structures by non-endothelial cells, distinguishing this phenomenon from simple cellular clustering or alignment. Importantly, cell types capable of confirmed VM in vitro corresponded closely with previous findings in mouse xenograft models. We further provide a practical protocol for screening VM capacity and demonstrate that cell density, glucose concentration, serum availability, and matrix stiffness are critical environmental determinants of VM formation.

Introduction

Tumor vascularization has long been recognized as a hallmark of cancer progression, enabling oxygen and nutrient delivery essential for tumor growth and metastasis.1 Traditionally, this vascularization has been attributed solely to angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels sprout from pre-existing endothelial-containing vasculature. In 1999, vasculogenic mimicry (VM) was first described as a process in which aggressive melanoma cells organize into the formation of lumen-containing, vascular-like structures in vitro and in vivo. These structures were devoid of endothelial markers but were glycoprotein-rich according to periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining and were capable of conducting fluid.2 These findings suggested that tumor cells could recapitulate vascular functions, thereby bypassing the need for canonical angiogenesis, challenging established paradigms, and sparking considerable debate regarding its biological relevance, mechanistic underpinnings, and clinical implications.2,3

Subsequent studies expanded the presence of VM beyond melanoma to various malignancies, including ovarian, breast, prostate, glioblastoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma.4 Importantly, VM presence has been associated with poor prognosis, high tumor grade, increased metastasis, and resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies such as bevacizumab.5,6 In a groundbreaking study, Barnett and colleagues (2016) demonstrated that macrophages form vessel-like structures independent of endothelial cells in both tumor and angiogenesis in vivo models,7 further suggesting that this ability may not be restricted to cancer cells. Human cytotrophoblasts may also form vascular structures during the process, leading to connection between embryonic and maternal circulations.8 The dynamic interplay between tumor cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) is crucial for VM formation. The ECM is recapitulated by Matrigel in virtually all VM publications. Matrigel, as opposed to plastic or glass surfaces, provides the presence of matrix proteins (e.g., collagen and laminin) and the opportunity for cells to grow in 3D. Despite these advances, the mechanisms of VM remain incompletely understood. A major challenge has been the lack of standardized experimental models capable of reliably recapitulating lumen-containing structures in vitro.4 This has led to substantial variability in experimental models and inconsistency in the criteria used to define VM. While there is quorum in the literature that the identification of VM in vivo is determined by the presence of vessel-like structures that are negative for CD31, there is still controversy over an in vitro model.4 A previous publication from our group called for a clear demarcation between true VM and artifacts of culture conditions.4 In this aforementioned paper, we emphasized the need for rigorous structural (lumen presence) and functional validation, incorporating 3D imaging (e.g., confocal z stacks and reconstructions), or demonstration of fluid transport.

Although quantitative descriptors such as tubular length, lumen size, and network area can be extracted with ImageJ, using them to define the presence or absence of VM still requires setting subjective thresholds. Moreover, their biological relevance remains uncertain, as correlations with VM architecture in patient tumors have not yet been established. Given these limitations, qualitative assessment currently offers the most straightforward and reproducible approach, focusing on the presence, absence, or general morphological characterization of VM into stages of formation.9

To help characterize the process of in vitro VM in the absence of quantification, a previous publication from our group defined four distinct phases.9 In a process that may take up to 4 days or longer, we previously demonstrated that phase 1 includes the alignment, migration, and cell clustering to delineate the area of the future tubular structure (see Figures 1A–1D). In phase 2, contraction of aligned structures occurs followed by cellular blebbing of certain cells that lose attachment. The third phase involves proliferation followed by the raising of specific areas of the cancer cell mass to form a bridge-like structure. Herein, we aim to provide guidelines on optimal culture conditions, highlight common structural artifacts that can confound VM identification, and identify the non-endothelial cell lines tested that can and cannot form lumen-containing tubular structures, thus presenting a standardized protocol for both studying VM and developing future therapeutic strategies targeting this process.

Results

The presence of a lumen is critical for VM identification

A major challenge in the study of VM has been the interpretation of network-like structures formed on Matrigel. While many cell types develop intercellular connections or align into branching morphologies within the first 12–24 h of culture in a similar way to VM, these structures do not necessarily represent VM. The current knowledge of VM formation in vitro is summarized in Figures 1A–1D, where cellular alignment, migration, contraction, proliferation, and formation of elevated tubular structures above a basal monolayer are mentioned as key stages. Figures 1E–1J shows the structures of VM after 96 h upon seeding of cells, which is shown to require intact Matrigel for their formation. For the purposes of this study, we define in vitro VM as the formation of non-endothelial, lumen-containing tubular structures. This is demonstrated by confocal microscopy and orthogonal reconstruction in Figures 1K–1N.

This ovarian cancer cell line, HEY-A8, which was previously shown to form lumen-containing tubular structures, retains this ability after transfection of green fluorescent protein (GFP) and clonal cell isolation and expansion. Upon reseeding on Matrigel, these GFP-expressing cells maintained the capacity to form VM structures, reinforcing the idea that VM competence can be retained in genetically stable clonal populations (Figures S1A and S1B). We have previously published that varying cancer cell lines form tubular structures in vitro when seeded onto the surface of Matrigel. However, to make a more faithful comparison of the tumor microenvironment, we added a second layer of Matrigel on top of the newly plated cancer cells. Figure S1 demonstrates that VM still occurs when cells are embedded in Matrigel at the time of seeding, and the resulting confocal analysis shows that these structures share the same characteristics as the previous method. As Matrigel is expensive and results are comparable, we have chosen to put forward a standard protocol and its characterization using cancer cells seeded onto the surface of Matrigel.

Cell number is a critical determinant in the formation of VM, as illustrated in Figures 1 and 2. Using the G415 gallbladder carcinoma cell line as an example of cell-density-dependent VM formation that would only form tubular structures upon seeding 300,000, we can observe how lower or higher cell numbers (150,000 or 600,000) did not reproducibly produce VM (Figures 1K–1M). Confocal microscopy confirmed that true VM structures consist of elevated tubular formations positioned above a basal monolayer, containing a clearly defined lumen (Figure 1N). Orthogonal views and cross-sectional analyses demonstrated three structural levels: a basal monolayer, a middle region containing tubular walls surrounding a hollow lumen, and an apical section showing closure of the structure. Importantly, not all structures that resemble VM by brightfield microscopy satisfy this definition. The ER+/PR+ breast cancer cell line MCF7, seeded at varying densities (15,000–100,000 cells), formed interconnected networks and compact cellular islands on Matrigel that superficially resembled vascular structures (Figure 2A). However, confocal microscopy revealed these formations to be composed entirely of densely packed cells lacking any internal lumen (Figure 2B). Cross-sectional views through basal, middle, and apical regions showed only solid cellular masses rather than tubular architecture. Video S1 further confirms this observation by z stack reconstruction through the entirety of the structure.

A similar phenomenon was observed in the DLD1 colorectal cancer cell line. Although brightfield imaging suggested the presence of vascular-like intercellular connections, confocal microscopy demonstrated that these structures lacked luminal spaces and instead consisted of compact cellular aggregates (Figures 2C and 2D). These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on brightfield morphology to define VM and reinforce the need for structural validation using confocal microscopy or equivalent three-dimensional imaging approaches.

Together, these observations establish a central premise of this manuscript: network formation alone should not be interpreted as VM unless lumen formation has been confirmed. This distinction is critical, as short-term Matrigel assays may otherwise lead to false-positive interpretations and subsequent in vivo investigations that fail to reproduce VM phenotypes.

Cell-line-dependent variability in VM formation

Patient tumor analyses indicate that VM is not uniformly present across cancer types, with reported incidences varying between approximately 15%–30% depending on tumor origin. Consistent with these observations, our in vitro analyses demonstrate that the ability to form lumen-containing tubular structures is highly cell-line-dependent and cannot be inferred solely from tissue origin or early morphological appearance on Matrigel (Figure 2E).

Cell lines incapable of forming VM

Several cancer cell lines failed to form lumen-containing tubular structures despite evaluation across a broad range of seeding densities and extended culture periods. For example, the Mel-1 melanoma and ZR75 breast cancer cell lines consistently formed only monolayers, clusters, or partial intercellular contacts after 96 h of culture on Matrigel (Figures S2A and S2B). Importantly, altering cell number between 15,000 and 300,000 cells did not induce the formation of elevated tubular structures or luminal architecture.

Similarly, TGBC2 gallbladder cancer, HeLa cervical cancer, and HS746T gastric cancer cells predominantly generated compact cellular aggregates or planar monolayers that lacked secondary elevated structures characteristic of VM (Figure 3A). Cell lines capable of forming VM

In contrast, several cancer cell lines reproducibly generated elevated lumen-containing tubular structures consistent with bona fide VM. The NOZ gallbladder cancer, T98G glioblastoma, and U251 glioblastoma cell lines all demonstrated secondary architectural organization by brightfield microscopy, which was subsequently validated by confocal microscopy as lumen-containing structures (Figure 3A).

The AGS gastric adenocarcinoma cell line similarly formed prominent tubular structures following culture on Matrigel (Figures 3B and 3C). Three-dimensional reconstruction revealed elevated tubular organization above an underlying monolayer, with structures extending approximately 90 μm above the Matrigel surface. Luminal diameters frequently exceed 50 μm and occasionally reach 200 μm. Height-based color coding further illustrated the three-dimensional organization of the VM structures and their separation from the basal monolayer.

Although the formation of lumen-containing structures was conserved among VM-competent cell lines, the morphology and kinetics of formation varied substantially. For example, HEY-A8 ovarian cancer cells entered the alignment phase of VM several hours earlier than MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, despite both ultimately forming comparable lumen-containing tubular structures at later time points. Furthermore, within individual cultures, tubular structures frequently developed asynchronously, suggesting that VM formation may reflect heterogeneous cellular states or localized microenvironmental conditions.

A comprehensive summary of all evaluated cell lines, including tissue origin, species, VM competency, and optimal plating densities, is provided in Figure 2E. Importantly, each VM-competent cell line required optimization of seeding density to reproducibly generate lumen-containing structures, emphasizing that the absence of VM under one condition should not necessarily be interpreted as an intrinsic inability to undergo the process.

Having established structural criteria for VM and identified cell-line-specific variability, we next examined the environmental conditions required to support lumen-containing tubular formation. Because VM occurs almost exclusively in the presence of ECM, we sought to determine the relative importance of culture medium composition, ECM concentration, and matrix mechanics in supporting this process.

Culture media support for VM formation

To evaluate whether VM formation is dependent on specific culture media, HEY-A8 ovarian cancer cells were cultured on Matrigel using three commonly employed cancer cell culture media, DMEM, IMDM, and RPMI, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). In contrast, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with FBS failed to sustain VM formation, although partial cellular alignment was transiently observed during the first 24 h (Figure 4A). These findings indicate that while early migratory or organizational events may initiate in response to matrix contact, a complete nutritional environment is essential for progression toward mature lumen-containing structures.

Matrigel concentration is a critical determinant of VM formation

We next examined whether ECM concentration influences VM formation. Although our previous work demonstrated that multiple commercially available Matrigel preparations support VM (Corning, Cultrex, and GelNest), it remained unclear whether matrix dilution preserves this functionality. Using Corning Matrigel at its commercial concentration (∼10 mg/mL), we evaluated serial dilutions ranging from 3:1 to 1:3 (Matrigel:Media) (Figure 4B).

VM reproducibly occurred only when Matrigel was diluted up to 1:1, whereas lower matrix concentrations failed to consistently support stable tubular formation. At dilutions beyond 1:1, healthy monolayers formed but secondary elevated structures and lumen formation were absent (Figure 4B). Furthermore, a 1:5 dilution failed to polymerize, precluding structural support altogether. These observations suggest that a minimal ECM concentration is required not only to provide structural proteins but also to establish the mechanical environment necessary for VM.

Matrix height and effective stiffness regulate VM formation

Given that cancer cells fail to form VM on plastic, hydrogel, denatured Matrigel, or excessively diluted matrix, we hypothesized that mechanical properties of the microenvironment may represent a critical determinant of VM formation. To address this possibility, we manipulated Matrigel height as a proxy for effective stiffness, exploiting the established principle that thinner gels transmit greater rigidity from the underlying glass substrate, whereas thicker gels behave as mechanically softer environments under the same polymerizing conditions (Figure 4C).

Using Matrigel heights ranging from 15 to 300 μm, we observed a biphasic response in VM formation. Reproducible lumen-containing tubular structures formed most consistently between 70 and 100 μm, while structures remained detectable, though less reproducibly, at 200 μm. At lower heights (15–30 μm), secondary elevated structures occasionally emerged, yet confocal microscopy failed to confirm lumen formation. In contrast, Matrigel heights exceeding 250 μm consistently resulted in multilayer growth of cells without evidence of VM (Figure 4C). These findings suggest that both excessively rigid and excessively compliant environments impair tubular organization.

Collectively, these observations support a model in which VM formation requires a permissive mechanical microenvironment defined by both ECM concentration and effective stiffness. The dependence of VM on intermediate matrix conditions highlights the importance of biomechanical cues in regulating non-endothelial tubular morphogenesis.

Serum, but not glucose alone, supports VM formation in vitro

As cancer cells undergoing VM generate elevated tubular structures over several days, we next sought to determine the minimal nutritional requirements necessary to sustain this process. Standard culture media contains supraphysiological glucose concentrations relative to resting blood glucose levels, raising the possibility that VM may simply reflect adaptation to nutrient-rich in vitro conditions. We therefore investigated the contributions of glucose and FBS to VM formation in the HEY-A8 ovarian cancer cell line.

When HEY-A8 cells were cultured on Matrigel in glucose-free medium lacking serum, only transient cellular alignment and intercellular connections were observed during the first hours after seeding (Figure 5A). To investigate the relative contributions of glucose and serum, glucose-free medium was supplemented with glucose concentrations ranging from 1 to 15 mM, either in the presence or in the absence of serum. In the presence of 15% FBS, VM structures formed reproducibly across all glucose concentrations tested (Figure 5B). In contrast, complete removal of serum abolished VM formation regardless of glucose supplementation, demonstrating that glucose alone is insufficient to support VM. Notably, VM was still observed in serum-containing glucose-free medium, likely due to glucose contributed by FBS itself (∼0.98 mM glucose at 15% serum).

To further define the minimal conditions required for VM formation, serum concentrations were varied from 0 to 15% FBS under both glucose-containing and glucose-free conditions (Figure 5C). No VM formation was observed in the absence of serum or at 1% FBS, irrespective of glucose availability. Interestingly, 2% serum supported VM only in glucose-containing medium, whereas ≥5% serum consistently supported VM even in glucose-free conditions, likely reflecting glucose supplied directly by FBS. Under our experimental conditions, VM formation appeared possible with approximately 0.13–0.33 mM glucose in combination with at least 2% serum, although these thresholds are likely to vary between FBS batches and cell lines.

Importantly, optimization of a single parameter alone was insufficient to guarantee VM formation. Although minimal values for serum concentration, glucose, Matrigel dilution, or matrix height could individually support VM, combinations of multiple minimal conditions frequently failed to permit tubular formation (Figure S3). These findings indicate that VM depends upon a permissive combination of nutritional and ECM cues rather than a single dominant determinant. The minimal permissive conditions identified for both HEY-A8 and MDA-MB-231 cells are summarized in Table S1.

Based on the conditions tested in this study, we recommend initially evaluating new cell lines using 100% Matrigel at a matrix height of approximately 100–110 μm in RPMI containing 15% FBS, together with a seeding density optimization ranging from 15,000 to 300,000 cells. This combination of Matrigel and serum supported VM in all cell lines examined in this study, although VM formation remained dependent on cell number. Once VM competency has been established, culture conditions may then be progressively minimized for each cell line to reduce experimental cost.

Conditioned media fail to rescue the formation of VM on denatured Matrigel

Periodic removal of medium at 1 h and then every 2 h up to 10 h (now conditioned medium) did not alter the kinetics or efficiency of tubular structure formation in cells on native Matrigel. Moreover, the addition of this conditioned medium to cells on denatured Matrigel failed to induce (rescue) VM. These findings suggest that secreted soluble factors alone are insufficient to drive tubular structure formation in the absence of an intact ECM, highlighting the critical structural role of native Matrigel in enabling VM (Figure S4).

We examined three widely used murine cancer cell lines: the ID8 ovarian cancer, 4T1 breast cancer, and B16F10 melanoma cell lines.

Trophoblast cell lines form non-endothelial lumen-containing structures

As trophoblast invasion during placental development has been proposed to involve vascular-like behavior, we next investigated whether trophoblast cell lines form lumen-containing structures under VM assay conditions. Both the SW71 and HTR8-SVneo trophoblast cell lines formed interconnected structures after 96 h on Matrigel (Figures 7A and 7B). Although confocal microscopy demonstrated that portions of these structures remained relatively solid, continuous luminal spaces were observed running along the interior of the tubular formations (Figures 7C–7G). Video S4 further confirmed these luminal structures by z stack reconstruction. Interestingly, trophoblast-derived structures morphologically resembled those formed by B16F10 melanoma cells, further suggesting that distinct forms of non-endothelial tubular formation may exist. Patient-derived primary ovarian cancer cells form lumen-containing tubular structures in vitro

To determine whether lumen-containing tubular structures observed in vitro are restricted to long-term adapted cell lines or may also occur in patient-derived material, we evaluated primary cancer cells isolated from the ascitic fluid of a patient diagnosed with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma.

Following seeding onto Matrigel under standard VM assay conditions, patient-derived cells progressively formed interconnected tubular-like structures over a 4-day culture period, as visualized by brightfield microscopy (Figure 7H). Importantly, confocal microscopy and three-dimensional reconstruction confirmed that these structures were elevated above a basal cellular monolayer and contained luminal spaces, consistent with the structural characteristics observed in VM-forming cancer cell lines throughout this study (Figures 7I–7K).

Height-based reconstruction further demonstrated the spatial organization of the structures relative to the Matrigel surface, while orthogonal imaging confirmed the presence of enclosed luminal compartments. Discussion

The process of VM, the formation of vascular-like, perfusable channels by aggressive tumor cells independent of endothelial cell involvement, has significantly expanded our understanding of tumor vascularization beyond traditional angiogenesis. Since its first description in uveal melanoma in 1999 by Maniotis and colleagues,2 VM has since been identified in various malignancies and is now regarded as a marker of tumor plasticity, invasiveness, and therapy resistance.3,4,12,13 However, as was brought to attention in a previous publication, persistent controversy around VM’s biological authenticity, coupled with methodological inconsistencies, has slowed progress in understanding its mechanistic basis and translational potential.4,6,14

While many cell types can form transient network-like structures within 12–24 h on Matrigel, these structures often lack lumens and do not reliably predict VM formation in vivo. This underscores the risk of overinterpreting short-term assays and highlights the need for more rigorous criteria. By implementing a more stringent in vitro model that requires evidence of lumen formation, we provide a framework that better aligns with in vivo VM biology. In the present study, the formation of lumen-containing tubular structures over extended culture periods correlates with reported in vivo observations. Specifically, in accordance with the lumen-containing structures observed in our in vitro assays, human MDA-MB-231,10 OVCAR3,11 U251,15,16 T98G,17 AGS,18 and ovarian cancer stem-like cells derived from SKOV319 cell lines formed VM (CD31−/PAS+) structures in mouse xenografts (in vivo). In contrast, MCF7 cells, which displayed only intercellular connections at 24 h but failed to form lumen-containing structures, did not show VM (CD31−/PAS+) structures in these mouse xenograft models.20 Importantly, this latter study exemplifies the central premise of our manuscript that the observation of intercellular connections in the absence of lumen can lead to misinterpretation and may result in researchers investing significant time and resources into in vivo studies that ultimately do not confirm VM. In murine cancer models, both the breast cancer 4T1 and melanoma B16F10 tumors exhibit clear VM features in vivo, including glycoprotein-rich, endothelial-independent lumen-containing channels, reinforcing the translational relevance of our in vitro model of VM.21,22 Importantly, we do not claim that in vitro VM formation guarantees VM occurrence in vivo, as this process is inherently influenced by the tumor microenvironment, immune interactions, coagulation pathways, and systemic factors. However, the consistent association observed across multiple models indicates that our assay enriches for biologically relevant VM phenotypes and supports greater caution when reporting VM in cell lines lacking confirmed lumen formation. We believe that the improved model presented in our manuscript will facilitate the identification of more robust and reliable biomarkers, which can then be validated more effectively in vivo. Only once such biomarkers are established can we confidently proceed to perform large screening and correlation studies of VM in tumors.

VM definition

For the purposes of this study, we have classified all non-endothelial tubular structures possessing a lumen as VM within our in vitro assays. Given that VM research remains in its early stages, it is not yet possible to determine whether all VM structures observed in vitro and in vivo are biologically equivalent. It is plausible that multiple distinct mechanisms, encoded within the human genome, can give rise to non-endothelial vascular-like networks. In this context, we believe it is appropriate to retain a broad and inclusive definition of VM, allowing for future refinement as specific biomarkers are identified and additional in vivo evidence becomes available.

One of the major contributions of this study is the clear delineation between lumen-containing structures (VM) and structurally similar but functionally distinct phenomena such as cell migration and contact, which generates cell clustering or partial matrix invasion.4,6 This is highlighted further by our results in MCF7 and DLD1 that formed interconnected networks on Matrigel yet were devoid of lumens and thus do not meet the stringent criteria for VM. We continue to advocate for an in vitro model that demonstrates the formation of lumen-containing structures as this more accurately represents VM in vivo.

Selective capability for VM among cell lines

Our analysis of over 23 cancer cell lines reveals that VM is a highly variable phenotype, exhibited only by specific tumor types. Notably, the human ovarian cancer cell line HEY-A8, gastric adenocarcinoma cell line AGS, gallbladder line G415, and murine 4T1 and human MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells robustly formed elevated, lumen-containing structures. The ability of MDA-MB-231 to undergo the initial stages of VM has been reported previously; however, we believe this is the first demonstration of a lumen-containing structure formation.23,24,25 Conversely, several commonly used breast cancer lines such as MCF7, ZR-75, and murine ovarian cancer ID8 failed to form VM despite extensive testing across multiple seeding densities and time points. These results are in line with observations from pathology; a meta-analysis of eight breast cancer studies (1,238 patients) found a VM-positive rate of only 24%.26,27

The B16F10 murine melanoma cell line produced lumen-containing structures with resemblance to structures formed by human trophoblast lines SW71 and NTR8-SVneo. This reinforces the idea that VM mechanisms are not exclusive to malignant cells but may co-opt developmental programs, particularly those involved in trophoblast invasion and vasculature formation during placentation.28,29 Furthermore, given that a circulatory system (vessels) existed prior to the evolution of endothelial cells and that, by a separate biological mechanism, macrophages can form vessel-like structures, we cannot rule out the possibility that the cancer cell has hijacked various biological processes (pre-vertebrate circulation, macrophage, or trophoblast) to acquire the ability to form vessel-like networks in tumors.7,30 This may account for the different types of VM we see among the cells lines tested; however, only further research to understand the exact mechanism in each cell type will give validity to this hypothesis.

Environmental and mechanical requirements for VM formation

The ECM is altered in the tumor microenvironment, driving cancer progression, and the involvement of rigidity in VM has been previously speculated, although not fully demonstrated in a VM model where lumen-containing structures have been confirmed.31,32 A finding from our study is the dependence of VM formation on precise microenvironmental conditions. Prior evidence indicates that changes in ECM composition can influence the transcriptional profile and phenotype of melanoma cells capable of undergoing VM.33,34,35 Our data show that the physical properties of the Matrigel, specifically changes in height over a glass coverslip as a proxy for stiffness and Matrigel dilution, are equally critical. Using a model in which Matrigel height is modulated over a glass coverslip, we demonstrate that VM formation occurs optimally when the gel height ranges from 70 to 200 μm. At extreme thinness (≈15 μm), the stiffness imposed by the underlying glass appears to impede tubule formation, whereas at heights above 250 μm the matrix becomes too soft to support structural organization. These observations were indirectly reinforced in our final experiments: although a Matrigel height of 50 μm and a 1:1 dilution each individually support VM, their combination resulted in VM failure, potentially due to altered effective stiffness.

While these findings highlight the importance of mechanical cues within the tumor microenvironment, further work is needed to quantify the stiffness (in Pascals) of the Matrigel preparations used here and to correlate these values with patient tumors that exhibit VM.36,37 Such measurements, together with analyses of different ECMs and mechano-transduction pathways (potentially YAP/TAZ and RhoA/ROCK), will help clarify the role and mechanisms determined by matrix stiffness in VM formation.38

Mediating this cellular traction may be specific laminin isoforms (particularly those present in Matrigel) that have been implicated by our group and others in VM architecture.9,39,40 We recently demonstrated that laminin-111 within Matrigel, together with integrin β1 expressed by cancer cells, is sufficient to initiate VM formation.9 Nevertheless, these initial findings do not exclude the possibility that VM can occur on alternative substrates. Controlled experiments using hydrogel enriched with collagen isoforms, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, nidogens, fibronectin, and other matrix proteins are still required to determine the minimal ECM requirements for VM. Syndecan-4 (SDC4) knockdown has been reported to inhibit VM in MDA-MB-231 cells.25 SDC4 and β1 integrin are also reported to work together in signaling and adhesion, indicating potential in examining the role of SDC4 in the laminin 111 initiation of VM.41,42

Increased ECM stiffness has been reported to alter endothelial integrity and proliferation, potentially through mechanisms involving matrix metalloproteinase upregulation and changes in vascular endothelial cadherin.43 ECM stiffening may also increase hypoxia and dysregulate pH homeostasis, elevating intracellular pH (>7.4) and reducing extracellular pH (<7.2) relative to normal epithelial cells (pH 7.0–7.3), thereby promoting adaptive cancer cell signaling.32 A recent study showed that intracellular pH decreases with increasing Matrigel stiffness and that ECM stiffening, together with low intracellular pH, promotes changes in multicellular arrangement, which was interpreted by the authors as VM.32 Future studies using our tubular-forming model should directly test the influence of extracellular pH (e.g., medium pH 6.8–7.4) and the corresponding intracellular pH dynamics during VM formation.

Nutritional and metabolic constraints

Our work also identifies critical nutritional requirements for VM. Experiments using serum- and glucose-depleted media demonstrate that both components are essential for VM structure formation in HEY-A8 cells. Interestingly, we demonstrate that both a minimal percentage of serum and a minimal concentration of glucose are required for VM in vitro. First, the presence of serum is essential for VM. Commercial fetal bovine serum includes numerous components and thus it will be interesting in the future to add growth factor back individually to define exactly which factors are required to trigger this process. We observed that 2% serum (but not 1%) could allow VM, only in the presence of supplemented glucose. However, 5% serum could bring about VM in the absence of additional glucose. Although glucose tends to vary from batch to batch of FBS, this puts the approximate range of glucose necessary at between 0.13 and 0.33 mM (≈2.34–5.95 mg/dL) for the HEYA8 cell line. In healthy individuals, resting glucose levels are usually between 70 and 95 mg/dL, with levels below 40 mg/dL often independently associated with an increased risk of mortality in critically ill patients.44 This is interesting as the intracellular concentration of glucose in both normal and cancerous cells is reported to be in the millimolar range. Intracellular glucose concentrations have been reported between MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells.45 In a recent study, MCF-7 showed a range of 0.86–7.11 mM intracellular glucose, while MDA-MB-231 cells were found to be similar to non-cancerous fibroblasts with an intracellular concentration of approximately 1.2 mM.45 It is both interesting and surprising that such a low glucose concentration in the surrounding medium (0.33mM) is sufficient to support the complex 4-day formation of 100 μm in diameter tubular structures. Although merely speculative, this ability to create vessels at low glucose may reflect the tumor environment and thus the urgent necessity to form channels to link to the blood supply and access available glucose and oxygen.

This raises further questions about the intersection of metabolic plasticity and VM: might hypoglycemia or metabolic stress in the tumor microenvironment act as a trigger for in vivo VM induction? Interestingly, the process of VM was also not deterred by the presence of 10.88 mM glucose (≈196 mg/dL), which corresponds to high-end diabetes. Future experiments looking at the role of hypoxia and autophagy might give us further insights into the mechanism used by the cancer cell to survive under such minimal nutritional concentrations. Obvious candidates include the AMP-activated protein kinase pathway, which promotes catabolic processes such as fatty acid oxidation and autophagy and induces the inhibition of anabolic processes such as protein and lipid synthesis, together with an increase in glucose uptake.46,47,48 Interestingly, and in accordance with this concept, in a previous publication we reported that very low levels of transcriptional activity were required for VM formation in vitro.9 Future studies incorporating metabolomics and inhibitors of glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation could clarify the role of metabolic adaptation in enabling VM.

Conditioned medium alone cannot induce VM

We previously demonstrated the requirement of laminin 111 in the Matrigel and integrin β1 at the cell membrane was essential to trigger the process of VM in vitro. Adding conditioned medium to cells growing on denatured Matrigel (without the natured laminin to bind to integrin) did not overcome the failure of cells to form VM on this surface. In a similar line of thinking, we hypothesized that due to the hundreds of cells necessary to form these tubular structures after 4 days, paracrine crosstalk via soluble factors (e.g., secreted cytokines and growth factors) was required. We thus assumed that the constant removal of conditioned medium from the culture dish at hourly intervals at the start of the VM process would have a detrimental effect on tubular structure formation. However, to our surprise, we observed no change in formation or in the speed of formation. Furthermore, conditioned medium from VM-forming culture cells (taken at hourly intervals) was insufficient to rescue VM in cells cultured on denatured Matrigel. This may suggest that soluble secreted factors alone are not enough to initiate VM; instead, a permissive and structurally intact ECM is required. While this experimentation does not promote a requirement for signaling from secreted proteins into the medium, this is far from confirmed. Maybe hourly intervals of media removal are too infrequent to have an effect. Future experiments depleting the secretion of extracellular vesicles in the medium may help answer this question. Interestingly, we and others have demonstrated that the inhibition of metalloproteinases prevents VM formation.9,39,49 Secreted MMPs can be released into extracellular space, where they degrade ECM components and regulate signaling. Our result may align with findings that VM-associated markers like integrin, laminin, and metalloproteinases form a cooperative scaffold localized to the cell surface and are present in conditioned media.50

There can be no absolute protocol for a model of in vitro VM, as all cancer cell lines differ in their genotype. Every investigation to determine whether a cell line or primary culture cancer undergoes VM needs to start with an analysis of cell number; given the high costs of Matrigel and FBS, we recommend the first experimentation be performed using undiluted Matrigel, 100-µm Matrigel height and 15% serum. Once confirmed, the conditions may be modified to find a more optimal (or less expensive) culture composition.

The absence of stain (CD31, CD34, or other endothelial markers) is not an optimal marker of VM presence as many pathologists may simply interpret vessel-like structures as blood vessels that did not immunoreact with the antibody. Future work should prioritize in vivo correlation by pathology-based analysis of tumor cross-sections, together with using patient-derived xenografts, organoid systems, and animal models. The use of this platform may also promote identification of core regulatory networks by transcriptomic or metabolomic profiling of VM-forming vs. VM-non-forming cells or the same cell line after genetic manipulation of identified essential proteins in this process. Functional perfusion assays using fluorescent microbeads or fluid dyes will also help understand the early stages of VM formation and the mechanobiological cues responsible.

Exploration of non-cancerous VM such as trophoblast may give the evolutionary and developmental parallels of VM and may also yield insights into physiological processes of vascular morphogenesis. With increasing recognition of VM as a contributor to therapy resistance, metastasis, and relapse, the field now demands rigorously defined, mechanistically tractable models—precisely what this study aims to deliver.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of in vitro cell culture assays is that cancer cell lines are dynamic systems that may undergo genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and phenotypic drift with increasing passage number. As a result, vasculogenic mimicry (VM) capacity is not necessarily stable over time; cell lines may lose or acquire the ability to form lumen-containing tubular structures depending on culture history and selective pressures. We therefore recommend that VM competency be routinely reassessed when establishing or reintroducing cell lines into experimental workflows, with appropriate optimization of cell density and culture conditions as needed.

In this study, we provide a physiologically grounded framework for in vitro VM analysis by integrating three-dimensional imaging, confocal validation, ECM and mechanical parameters, and nutritional constraints across human, murine, and patient-derived systems; however, the mechanisms as to why mechanical and nutritional parameters may affect the formation of VM were not assessed. We highlight that the mechanical parameters of VM formation can be further studied through different ECMs, measurement of the elastic modulus, and analyzing pathways of mechanotransduction.

Importantly, our results indicate that conclusions based solely on two-dimensional Matrigel network formation should be interpreted cautiously unless lumen formation has been confirmed. By focusing on structures that more closely resemble in vivo VM, this framework reduces false-positive interpretations, improves the likelihood of translational relevance, and facilitates more robust identification of VM-associated mechanisms and biomarkers. This standardized model may therefore serve as a useful platform for investigating VM biology and evaluating therapeutic strategies, including the distinction of VM vessels from endothelial vasculature in patient tumors.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The following commercially available cell lines were used in this study to evaluate VM in vitro. Human cancer cell lines included: ovarian carcinomas OVCAR3, SKOV3, HEY-A8, A2780 and UCI101, MCF7 and ZR-75-1; MDA-MB-231 (triple negative breast cancer); AGS, MKN45, and HS746T (gastric adenocarcinoma); U251, T98G, and U87 (glioblastoma); DLD1 (colorectal adenocarcinoma; G414, TGBC2 and NOZ (gallbladder carcinoma); HeLa (cervical carcinoma); and MEL-1 (melanoma). In addition, two human trophoblast cell lines, SW71 and HTR8-SVneo were included to explore non-endothelial lumen-containing structures in non-malignant cells. Murine cancer cell lines included: ID8 (mouse ovarian carcinoma), 4T1 (mouse breast carcinoma), and B16F10 (mouse melanoma). A2780, UCI101, U251, SKOV3, HeLa, MEL-1, MCF7, MDA-MB-231, HEY-A8, ZR-75-1, NOZ, T98G, SW71, U87, TGBC2 and HS746T cell lines were maintained in DMEM-F12 cell culture media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% streptomycin-penicillin. AGS, B16F10, DLD1, MKN-45, HTR-8-Svneo, OVCAR-3, U251, G415 and 4T1 were maintained in RPMI cell culture media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% streptomycin-penicillin. ID8 was maintained in DMEM-F12 cell culture media supplemented with 4% FBS and 1X ITS (insulin, transferrin, and sodium selenite). All cell lines were maintained in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2, with a humidified atmosphere.

Primary tumor cells

Patient-derived ovarian cancer cells were obtained from the Clinical Hospital (RedUC-Christus) of the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile with prior written patient informed consent. Procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were female subjects over 18 years of age, within no ethnic exclusion and having confirmed stage III or IV ovarian cancer. Cancer cell isolation as was previously described.51,52 The research protocol was approved for separate projects by the Ethics/Bioethics Committee of the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile, approval resolution numbers and dates: No. 012793 (19/4/22), N°13–377 (16/01/2013 & 28/05/2015), ID 170614005 (3/06/2018), N°012793 (7/04/2022), ID 170614005 (1/05/2018), ID 21062207 (23/04/2022).

Method details

Optimal vasculogenic mimicry assay protocol

Preparation (Day 1): Pre-cool pipette tips and coverslips: Place sterile pipette tips and glass coverslips (12 mm diameter) in a −20°C freezer on the evening before the experiment to ensure they are fully chilled before use. Chill pipette tips, coverslips, and pipettes ensure even Matrigel distribution and prevents premature gelation. Thaw Matrigel (complete, growth-factor-reduced): Thaw overnight at 4°C to maintain it in liquid form and prevent premature polymerization. Adjust ambient laboratory conditions: Maintain the assay room at approximately 22°C (using air-conditioning if necessary). Perform all Matrigel handling and pipetting on ice or cold blocks to prevent early gelation.

Gel Coating (Day 2, Morning): Temperature control is critical, Matrigel polymerizes rapidly at room temperature or above; therefore, always keep it on ice until plating. Pre-chill pipette tips and coverslips (using those prepared from Day 1). On ice, pipette Matrigel onto each coverslip (for a 12 mm coverslip, use 12.5 μL) and spread evenly to cover the surface. Adjust the Matrigel volume proportionally for larger coverslips or areas. Tip: Work quickly and keep all materials on ice to maintain Matrigel in liquid form until the coating step is complete. Polymerize Matrigel: Transfer the coated coverslips to a 37°C incubator for 1 h to allow complete polymerization. Avoid disturbing the coverslips during this period.

Cell Preparation (During Gel Polymerization): Harvest cells in the exponential growth phase, count them accurately, and resuspend in maintenance medium. If the cell line has not been previously characterized for VM formation, perform a seeding-density optimization curve (e.g., 15,000–300,000 cells per 12 mm coverslip) to determine the optimal density for network formation. Protocol summary and key notes for reproducible in vitro VM assay

Cell seeding number, cell suspension density and medium volume summary.

After Matrigel polymerization (1 h at 37°C), seed cells directly onto the coated coverslip. For 15,000 cells on a 12 mm coverslip, use 50 μL of cell suspension; for densities >100,000 cells, use 100 μL (refer to Table above for protocol summary). Optimize seeding density for each cell line. Adhesion period: Incubate for 1 h at 37°C without adding additional medium to allow cells to adhere and spread evenly on the Matrigel surface. Add maintenance medium: Carefully add pre-warmed RPMI medium supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution. Tips: For 6-well plates, add ∼2 mL per well, 12-well plates, add ∼1 mL per well (refer to the protocol summary above). Depending on well size, adjust medium volume to fully cover the coverslips without detaching cells. Incubate cells: Maintain under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). Monitor and analyze VM formation: At defined time points (e.g., 2, 8, 24, 48, and 96 h post-seeding), record VM progression using bright-field microscopy. When required, fix the cells for subsequent immunofluorescence staining or confocal microscopy analysis to assess different stages of VM network formation (below).

Immunofluorescence and confocal imaging analysis of VM structures

Culture medium was carefully removed from VM assays, and 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS preheated to 37°C was added to each well. Samples were incubated for 12 min (thin samples) or 20 min (thicker samples) at 37°C, then stored on glass coverslips in 2 mL of PBS at 4°C for up to two weeks. Samples to be incubated with antibodies were washed twice with 1× PBS for 3 min, followed by incubation with 50 mM NH4Cl for 10 min to quench residual aldehydes. After two additional washes with 1× PBS (3 min each), cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min and washed twice more with 1× PBS. Samples were then blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (Pan-laminin, E-cadherin and C-myc) were applied at a 1:100 dilution for 1 h at room temperature in a dark, humidified chamber. After washing three times with 1× PBS (3 min each), secondary antibodies were added at a 1:1,000 dilution, together with Rhodamine–Phalloidin or Phalloidin–Alexa Fluor 488, and incubated for 1 h under the same conditions. Following three additional washes with 1× PBS, nuclei were stained with DAPI at a 1:50,000 dilution for 30 s and were rinsed with distilled water for 5 min. Fixed samples treated with PAS were washed for 15 min with 1×PBS and incubated with 0.5% periodic acid for 10 min. They were then washed with distilled water for 10 min and treated with Schiff reagent for 25 minutes. After washing with distilled water for 15 minutes, cultures were treated with an aqueous solution of 0.05 N HCl and sodium metabisulphite 5 mg/mL for 15 minutes. Stained cultures were then washed with distilled water for 15 minutes. All samples were mounted on glass slides using 10 μL Fluoromount-G. Slides were air-dried overnight at room temperature in the dark.

Images of cancer cells formations were acquired using a Nikon Ti-2 inverted confocal microscope and a Zeiss LSM 880 system with Airyscan detection (UMA, Pontifical Catholic University of Chile); images were analyzed using NIS-Elements Viewer v5.21.00 or Zen 2.3 Black, respectively. Images of MDA-MB-231 cells were acquired with a Leica Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope and analyzed using Zen 3.8 Lite software. Three-dimensional reconstructions from confocal microscopy were generated using IMARIS 8.4 at the Center for Advanced Microscopy (CMA BIO BIO, University of Concepción, Chile), as previously described.6 Additional image analysis was performed using ImageJ 1.53q (NIH, USA), NIS-Elements Viewer 5.21.00, Zen 2012, and Infinity Analyze v6.5.2.

Minimal culture parameters for VM formation

Glucose: A standard protocol for VM was performed with HEYA8 cells on glass coverslips in 24 well plates. RPMI media with and without glucose were used, with the addition of the corresponding percentage of heat inactivated fetal bovine serum. Glucose was added to glucose-free RPMI media according to desired final concentration. Stock of D(+) glucose (Sigma G-7021) was prepared with autoclaved distilled water and filtered with 0.22 micron filters.

Conditioned Medium: A standard protocol for VM was performed with HEYA8 cells on glass coverslips in 24 well plates. Natured and denatured Matrigel controls were maintained without a change in media. Cell culture media was extracted 2 h upon seeding from natured and denatured conditions of Matrigel, replacing with fresh media or conditioned media every hour for 6 h. Images were taken every 2 h for the first 6 h and then at 24, 48, 72 and 96 h after seeding.

Matrigel Heights: For calculating an estimated volume based on a desired height of Matrigel we use the following formula to calculate the volume of a cylinder: V = πr2h (v = volume, h = height). The volume of a cylinder is the approximate shape of Matrigel seeded on a round coverslip. A 12 mm in diameter coverslip with a desired Matrigel height of 200 microns has 6 mm in radius and the volume added is 22.6 ± 0.23. The error added is 1.0% of the volume added based on pipette inaccuracy. Matrigel was applied according to the calculation for each height throughout the whole coverslip, spreading the gel with a pipette tip, within a 12 well plate, working with cooled tips and over ice. Cells were maintained with 2 mL RPMI 15% FBS, 1% streptomycin-penicillin.

Dilutions of Matrigel: Matrigel was thawed one ice as stated in the optimal VM assay protocol section above. Matrigel was mixed with ice-cold DMEM medium in ratio of Matrigel:DMEM F12 of 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3. Addition of Matrigel preparations to the coverslip and cell seedings was under the same conditions as specified in stated above in the optimal VM assay protocol section.

Cells embedded in Matrigel: Optimal VM assay was referenced for this method. The 12.5 μL of matrigel on glass covers was polymerized for 30 min in an incubator at 37°C (5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). Once 15,000 HEY-A8 cells (in 50 μL) were seeded, these were placed in an incubator for 30 min. Finally, 25 μL were extracted from the drop of media and cold 12.5 μL of matrigel was placed on top of the seeded cells and incubated for 15 min before adding 2 mL of media (RPMI, 15% FBS, 1% streptomycin-penicillin).
