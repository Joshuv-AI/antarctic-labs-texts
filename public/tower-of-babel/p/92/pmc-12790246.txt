NKX2-5 Mutation and ICD Implantation in a Pregnant AV Block Patient
Authors: Musa'ab Moh'd Alhmouz, Alaa Jamal Saeid, Mohamed AL Jaabari, Rami H Bany Ata, Mo'ath Bani Ali, Raghad Abuhalimeh, Lina Abuhalimeh, Dima Quraini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArrhythmias and conduction disorders are relatively common during pregnancy and are often amplified by the physiological changes of gestation. Although new-onset complete heart block is uncommon, it has been linked to mutations in the NKX2-5 gene, which is associated with congenital heart disorders and progressive conduction system defects.Case SummaryWe present the case of a pregnant woman with an NKX2-5 gene mutation who developed progressive atrioventricular nodal disease and chronotropic incompetence. She underwent dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement, with a well-tolerated procedure and an uncomplicated pregnancy and delivery.DiscussionThis case demonstrates the feasibility of device implantation during pregnancy and highlights the importance of genetic insight and multidisciplinary care in managing complex cardiac conditions.Take-Home MessagesLimited guidelines exist for the management of conduction mutations such as NKX2-5 during pregnancy. More research, data, and shared clinical experience are needed to better guide clinicians into multidisciplinary, timely and individualized management plans to ensure optimal maternal and fetal outcomes.Key words: arrhythmias in pregnancy, AV block, dual chamber ICD implantation

History of Presentation

A 37-year-old pregnant woman (gravida 5, para 3) at 26 weeks of gestation presented with palpitations, fatigue, dizziness, and episodes of bradycardia. These symptoms began early in her second trimester. She had been started on metoprolol tartrate 50 mg daily during that period for sinus tachycardia noted on prior evaluation. Despite beta-blocker therapy, her symptoms (including lightheadedness and bradycardia) persisted, prompting referral to cardiology for further assessment.

Medical History

The patient had a significant medical history that included congenital heart disease with surgical closure of an atrial septal defect at the age of 16 and a diagnosis of left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) cardiomyopathy with preserved ejection fraction. She also had documented genetic mutations in the NKX2-5 gene, associated with congenital heart disease and conduction system abnormalities, as well as the PKHD1 gene.

Physical Examination

On examination, the patient was alert and appeared fatigued but was not in acute distress. Vital signs were notable for bradycardia, with a heart rate in the 40s (beats/min). Blood pressure was within normal limits, and respiratory rate and oxygen saturation were normal. Cardiac examination revealed a slow but regular pulse and normal heart sounds without any new murmurs. There were no signs of heart failure such as jugular venous distension, crackles in the lungs, or peripheral edema.

Investigations

Electrocardiography showed sinus bradycardia (heart rate: ∼47 beats/min) with a 2:1 atrioventricular (AV) block (Figure 1). Ambulatory Holter monitor demonstrated intermittent episodes of 2:1 AV block and significant bradycardia, including sinus pauses up to 6.3 seconds. Exercise stress test revealed an impaired chronotropic response (inadequate heart rate increase with exercise), with evidence of high-grade AV block during exertion (Figure 2).

These findings were consistent with AV nodal conduction disease and chronotropic incompetence (inability to appropriately increase heart rate with activity).

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for this pregnant patient presenting with fatigue, dizziness, and new onset high-grade AV block included several possibilities. Medication-induced bradycardia was initially considered, as she had been on metoprolol; however, her conduction abnormalities were infranodal and persisted even after discontinuation of the medication, making this an unlikely primary cause. Intrinsic conduction system disease was strongly suspected given her clinical history and the presence of an NKX2-5 mutation, which is associated with congenital cardiac conduction defects and can be unmasked or worsened during pregnancy. Metabolic or endocrine abnormalities, such as hypothyroidism or electrolyte disturbances, were also considered but ruled out through appropriate laboratory testing.

After evaluation, the most likely diagnosis was intrinsic progressive AV nodal block (conduction system disease), likely related to the patient's congenital cardiac condition and NKX2-5 mutation, rather than an external or reversible cause.

Management

A multidisciplinary team including electrophysiology, general cardiology, cardio-obstetrics, and obstetrics specialists managed the case to ensure both maternal and fetal safety. Initial management with metoprolol for sinus tachycardia was re-evaluated once high-grade AV block was discovered, and the beta-blocker was discontinued to avoid exacerbating the bradycardia. Given the progression of AV conduction abnormalities and persistent symptoms (dizziness and fatigue), as well as concerns about safety during the remainder of pregnancy and labor, the decision was made to proceed with permanent pacemaker implantation.

The patient was planned for a dual-chamber pacemaker to treat AV block and chronotropic incompetence. Additionally, owing to her NKX2-5 mutation and potential risk of arrhythmias/sudden cardiac death, the device chosen was a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) for primary prevention. Cardiac resynchronization therapy was considered but not pursued because the patient had a normal ejection fraction (no indication for cardiac resynchronization therapy) and to avoid the longer procedure time and increased radiation exposure given her pregnancy.

Procedure

Because of the patient's gravid state, several precautions were undertaken during placement of the pacemaker and ICD. She underwent regional pectoralis nerve block with further sedation as needed by our cardiac anesthesia team. An obstetrics and prenatal team were present and provided continuous fetal monitoring throughout the procedure. The patient received dexamethasone 12 mg before the procedure to accelerate fetal lung maturation in case of early emergency delivery. She was placed in a partial left lateral position, and a thermoluminescent dosimeter badge and radiation protection drapes were placed to reduce the amount of radiation exposure. The use of fluoroscopy was minimized and used only for lead positioning. Venogram was not performed. Cephalic vein access was guided by ultrasound. Two leads were precisely positioned under fluoroscopy guidance: one in the right ventricular septum and the other (after overcoming minor dislodgement issues) in the right atrial appendage. A new generator was then connected and secured to the muscle before closure of the pocket and confirmation of proper function. Bedside echocardiogram was performed and ruled out pericardial effusion. The patient was transported to the holding area in stable condition. Repeat assessment by the obstetrics team was performed and confirmed stability of the fetus.

The patient tolerated the intervention well. Postprocedure, both maternal and fetal status remained stable. At the 1-month follow-up, she reported significant improvement in her symptoms (no further dizziness or significant palpitations). Device interrogation at that visit showed ∼99.8% ventricular pacing (indicating the pacemaker was effectively treating her AV block) and a few short runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia that did not require therapy. The patient underwent an uneventful vaginal delivery to a healthy girl at 39 weeks of gestation.

Discussion

We present a rare and complex case of a familial NKX2-5 mutation in a pregnant patient who developed AV nodal disease and chronotropic incompetence. Although the patient had previously experienced 3 pregnancies without complications or cardiac issues herself, she has a significant family history of cardiomyopathy involving her mother and siblings, and she had delivered 2 children with congenital and structural heart defects. Individuals with NKX2-5 mutations are known to require close monitoring for progressive conduction abnormalities and structural cardiac defects.1,2 For instance, arrhythmias, which often worsen during pregnancy, are exacerbated in the presence of underlying genetic mutations.1, 2, 3 The management of arrhythmias during pregnancy remains a significant clinical challenge given the lack of specific, universally accepted guidelines, necessitating a tailored approach for each case. This highlights the critical importance of a multidisciplinary team for optimal care, including cardiologists, electrophysiologists, pediatric electrophysiologists, maternal-fetal medicine specialists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists, to ensure the best outcomes for both the mother and the fetus.4

In this case, the patient had previously undergone genetic testing for arrhythmia-associated genes after the birth of 2 children with atrial septal defects and mild cardiomyopathy, which revealed the NKX2-5 mutation. This mutation impairs cardiogenesis and the development of the cardiac conduction system, leading to defective structural and electrical cardiac functions.1,5 NKX2-5 mutations have been implicated in a variety of congenital heart malformations, with atrial septal defects and ventricular septal defects being particularly common in heterozygous carriers.1 Research has demonstrated that NKX2-5 plays a critical role in atrial myocardial proliferation, and its mutation can result in arrhythmias and AV block.1

NKX2-5 is a homeobox-containing gene located on chromosome 5q35.1, which is a cardiac transcription factor essential for heart development, and mutations have been associated with congenital heart disease and progressive conduction system abnormalities. These mutations typically affect the homeodomain region, impairing normal DNA binding while preserving partial function through cofactor interactions mediated by the Y-rich domain. This dual mechanism may explain the overlapping structural and electrical cardiac manifestations observed in our patient (Figure 3).6

The NKX2-5 Protein Includes a DNA-Binding Homeodomain and a Y-Rich Domain That Interacts With Cofactors

Mutations in the homeodomain (HD) reduce binding to normal heart genes, but the Y-rich domain (YRD) allows the mutant protein to still bind other, off-target genes. This may contribute to both congenital heart defects and conduction disease.

A major concern for individuals with NKX2-5 mutations is the risk of sudden cardiac death, which is more frequent among family members of the affected individuals. Notably, sudden cardiac death has been reported even in patients with permanent pacemaker implants, underscoring the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias despite pacing.7 Additionally, NKX2-5 mutations have been associated with dilated cardiomyopathy and LVNC, as reported in several case studies.1, 2, 3,8 These findings suggest a predisposition to severe cardiomyopathy and malignant arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation, in affected individuals1, 2, 3,8 Experimental data demonstrate that Nkx2-5 loss during trabecular morphogenesis provokes severe hypertrabeculation with impaired Purkinje system development, consistent with LVNC histopathology in murine models.9

The 2023 Heart Rhythm Society expert consensus statement on the management of arrhythmias during pregnancy emphasizes that procedures such as catheter ablation and implantable device placement can be performed in pregnancy in cases of hemodynamic compromise or for the prevention of sudden cardiac death.4 Such interventions should be carried out in experienced centers with measures to minimize fetal radiation exposure, as reducing total maternal radiation is more effective than simply shielding the maternal abdomen with a lead apron. The management of arrhythmias during pregnancy is further complicated by physiological changes, concerns about fetal safety, and limited data on medication safety. Antiarrhythmic drug use during pregnancy and postpartum should generally align with nonpregnant protocols, with careful selection of drugs that have a strong safety record, the use of the lowest effective dose, and regular reassessment during the postpartum period to account for potential drug concentration in breast milk.

Although the indication for permanent pacing was clear owing to high-grade AV block and chronotropic incompetence, the decision to proceed with a dual-chamber ICD rather than a pacemaker alone was based on the patient's broader risk profile. This included the presence of a pathogenic NKX2-5 mutation, previously repaired atrial septal defect, LVNC phenotype, and a family history of cardiomyopathy and sudden cardiac death. Although NKX2-5 mutations are not yet included in standard ICD indication guidelines, emerging data highlight their association with malignant arrhythmias and sudden death, even in patients who are already paced.7 Given this information, and the unpredictability of arrhythmic events during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team opted for an ICD for primary prevention. The postimplantation detection of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, although not requiring therapy, may represent underlying ventricular irritability and supports our initial concern regarding arrhythmic risk.

Limitation

This case is limited by its single-patient nature and short-term follow-up. Long-term outcomes related to ICD performance, arrhythmia recurrence, and ventricular remodeling remain pending. Furthermore, given the variability in phenotypic expression and arrhythmic risk, the applicability of this management strategy to other NKX2-5 mutation carriers remains uncertain.

Conclusions

This case highlights the complex interplay of structural heart defects, conduction system disease, and risk of malignant arrhythmias in patients with NKX2-5 mutations, especially in the setting of pregnancy. We therefore highlight the significance of the multidisciplinary decision-making process, comprehensive long-term monitoring, and individualized treatment strategies, including careful consideration regarding device implantation during pregnancy, device-related complications such as pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, and in the broader management of these patients.
