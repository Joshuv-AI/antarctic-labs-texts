Comprehensive three-dimensional analysis of alveolar bone adaptation and root resorption following premolar extraction in Class II malocclusion: a cohort study
Authors: Hasan M Sharhan, Hanan Al-Fakeh, Huda M Sharhan, Majedh Abdo Ali Al-Somairi, Enas Senan Alyafrusee, Gaobo Tao, Nassem Ali Al-Worafi, Chao He, Najah Alhashimi, Maged S Alhammadi, Bowen Zheng, Yi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to assess the three-dimensional effects of orthodontic premolar extraction in Class II division 1 malocclusion cases treated with fixed appliances on maxillary bony changes (alveolar bone thickness and height) and maxillary dental changes (root length and tooth volume).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted with 50 adult patients (aged 18–35 years) who underwent orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances and extraction of four first premolars. Pre and post treatment cone beam computed tomography scans were analyzed to obtain quantitative measurements of alveolar bone height and thickness, as well as root length and tooth volume.ResultsThere were significant post-treatment reductions in alveolar bone height in almost all teeth, as well as the cervical alveolar bone thickness, particularly on the palatal side of the anterior teeth. Root resorption was evident across all teeth, and central incisors showed the most significant reduction in root length. Tooth volume decreased significantly across all dentitions.ConclusionsPremolar extraction in orthodontic treatment is associated with significant changes in alveolar bone height, thickness, root resorption, and tooth volume, particularly in the anterior maxillary region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-026-00616-3.

Introduction

Orthodontic treatments, including the extraction of premolars, have long been a topic of interest due to their significant impact on tooth positioning, alveolar bone structure, and overall dental health [1, 2]. Orthodontic treatment for maxillary incisor protrusion often includes the extraction of maxillary premolars and the retraction of the upper front teeth, which lead to alterations in their labiolingual inclination [3]. Excessive lingual inclination of the anterior teeth can result from overemphasis on esthetics without proper consideration of alveolar bone remodeling capacity during orthodontic movement. This excessive inclination may lead to root resorption, alveolar bone loss, and the development of fenestrations, dehiscence, and gingival recession [4–7].

Apical root resorption (ARR) is an undesirable side effect of orthodontic treatment, and its prevalence ranges from 20 to 100%. Severe cases are rare, occurring in 1–5% of patients, and can potentially involve more than 5 mm of resorption. ARR can lead to an imbalanced crown-to-root ratio or even tooth loss, which negatively affects both patient quality of life and overall treatment outcomes [8]. Various factors can contribute to root resorption, such as age, gender, the type of appliance used, the force and pattern of treatment (whether extraction or non-extraction), treatment duration, and the extent of tooth movement [9]. Fixed orthodontic appliances (FAs), particularly in premolar extraction cases, generate substantial forces on the alveolar bone, promoting bone remodeling and potentially contributing to root resorption. Evidence from Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT)-based studies indicates that external apical root resorption (EARR) is a relatively common consequence of orthodontic treatment, with a higher likelihood in extraction protocols. The biomechanical properties of FAs= such as the magnitude, direction, and duration of applied forces are key determinants of both bone adaptation and the extent of root resorption. Despite this, much of the existing literature relies on two-dimensional or suboptimal three-dimensional (3D) imaging, limiting the accuracy of resorption assessment. FAsremain the primary modality in orthodontic care, and current findings suggest that variations in appliance design and mechanics influence the incidence of ARR, although reported prevalence rates are comparable to or slightly lower than those documented in previous studies [8, 10, 11].

The assessment of tooth volume is important in orthodontics as it plays a key role in treatment planning and biomechanics [12, 13]. CBCT enables the segmentation of images to isolate the tooth from surrounding structures, which facilitates precise measurements of tooth volume, root length, and bone height and thickness [14–21]. There is limited research on the effects of orthodontic treatment on marginal bone levels around both anterior and posterior teeth, especially with regard to3D assessments following premolar extractions [7]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the alveolar bone height and thickness, root resorption, and tooth volume in patients who underwent FAs treatment involving the extraction of premolars for Class II malocclusion [22, 23].

Specific objectives or hypotheses

The specific objectives of this study were to investigate the 3D effects of orthodontic premolar extraction in Class II division 1 malocclusion cases treated with FAs on maxillary bony changes (alveolar bone thickness and height) and maxillary dental changes (root length and tooth volume). The null hypothesis is that FAs, including fixed functional appliances for Class II division 1 malocclusion, do not have an effect on alveolar bone height and thickness, root resorption, or tooth volume.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study involving adult patients who underwent orthodontic treatment with FAs and extraction of first premolars as part of their treatment plan for Class II division 1 malocclusion. All CBCT scans at T0 and T1 were obtained solely for clinical diagnostic and treatment planning purposes, and no additional radiographic exposures were performed for research purposes. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China Medical University School of Stomatology (Approval No. K-2025-020) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposures, predictors, potential confounders, and effect modifiers

Exposures in this study included orthodontic treatment involving extraction of four first premolars. Major predictors and potential confounders were considered, such as the severity of malocclusion, biomechanics, the extent of tooth movement, and the quality of extraction and treatment outcomes. Additionally, the type of orthodontic appliance (Damon Q self-ligating brackets) and treatment phases were controlled to minimize confounding. For the analysis of tooth changes, measurements from both the right and left sides were averaged to account for any asymmetries, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the entire maxillary arch.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) based on the methodology used by Al-Worafi et al. [7]. A power of 95% (1-β), a significance level of 0.05 (α), and an effect size (d) of 0.8 were used for the calculation. The required sample size was determined to be 19 participants. To enhance statistical reliability, a total of 50 participants were included in the study.

Treatment protocol

The treatment for the FA group was divided into three phases: alignment and leveling, space closure, and fine-tuning or finishing. Archwire adjustments and intermaxillary elastics were employed for space closure. The treatment was concluded once the teeth were properly aligned, and all spaces were fully closed.

Patients were treated using the Damon Q self-ligating bracket system six (Ormco, USA). Following alignment and leveling with the archwires, en-masse retraction was achieved using active tie-backs on the second molars on the working archwire, which were connected to crimpable hooks between the canines and lateral incisors. All treatments were carried out by a highly skilled orthodontist, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the procedures. The American Board of Orthodontics (ABO) Discrepancy Index was used to evaluate the case difficulty [25].

Imaging and 3D analysis

The analysis involved examining CBCT images obtained using the I-CAT Imaging System (KaVo Company, Germany). These images were acquired using established protocols and processed by a specialized technician. The imaging settings were as follows: 120 kV, 5 mA, 23 cm × 17 cm field of view, exposure time of 17.8 s, voxel size of 0.3 mm, and slice thickness of 2 mm. The images were standardized and repositioned to align with the Frankfort-horizontal (FH) plane in the sagittal view. The CBCT scans taken at T0 and T1 were converted to DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine) format and imported into Invivo 6.0 software (Anatomage, San Jose, CA, USA).

Within this software, the section view features were used for 3D visualization of the X, Y, and Z sections (corresponding to the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes). Mimics software was employed for detailed 3D analysis. The study models and data were securely stored, sealed, and coded to avoid any bias during data analysis and measurement.

The sagittal, axial, and coronal views were properly oriented according to the methodology outlined by Figueiredo et al. [26], as demonstrated in Fig. 1. Measurements were obtained for the thickness of the labial surface of anterior teeth and the buccal surface of posterior maxillary teeth. To this end, the vertical dental axis (VDA) was drawn from the tip of the incisal edge for the anterior teeth and from the tip of the posterior cusp to the apex of the root. Three weeks after the initial measurements, both the intra-observer and inter-observer reliability were assessed again by two examiners, who repeated the assessments for 20 patients selected at random.

The orthogonal planes were reoriented to be perpendicular to the long axis of each maxillary anterior incisor (CI), lateral incisor (LI) canine (C), Second premolar (2ndP), and first molar (1stM), including its mesial (1stMM), distal (1stMD), and palatal (1stMP) aspects. In the sagittal view, the FH plane was aligned parallel to the floor. For each teeth, a midsagittal plane was constructed by joining the incisal or cuspal midpoint and the root apex points.

The labial/buccal bone height for anterior and posterior teeth was measured as the line from the CEJ to the alveolar ridge crest. The root length was defined as the distance from the CEJ to the root apex (as shown in Fig. 2). Measurements of the labial and palatal ABTs were performed in the sagittal and coronal planes at three levels: 3, 6, and 9 mm from the CEJ in the apical direction, as shown in the sagittal and coronal views (Fig. 2). Sources of bias and comparability between exposure groups

Potential sources of bias in this study included patient selection bias, particularly regarding extraction criteria and treatment protocols, as well as observer bias in measuring bone height, thickness, root length, and tooth volume. To minimize these risks, examiners were blinded to baseline measurements, and intra- and inter-observer reliability was assessed for all variables. Additionally, patient selection followed strict inclusion criteria to ensure comparability between the pre- and post-treatment groups.

Statistical analysis (outcomes, subgroup analyses, confounding and missing data)

Statistical analysis was performed using appropriate software (e.g., SPSS, version 25). The analysis included:

Descriptive statistics to summarize baseline characteristics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies.

Normality of the data was assessed using both the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Since all variables satisfied the normality assumption, paired t-tests were appropriately applied for all pre- and post-treatment comparisons. Independent-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD post hoc test were conducted to evaluate differences according to tooth side, tooth type, and measurement level. Right and left measurements were compared using paired t-tests; as no significant asymmetry was found (p > 0.05), bilateral values were averaged. Multiple paired t-tests results were interpreted with caution to minimize Type I error inflation. Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for all parameters (small: d < 0.2, medium: d = 0.5, large: d ≥ 0.8) to assess clinical relevance. Additionally, simple linear regression was performed to evaluate the association between treatment duration and root length reduction. The reliability of measurements was assessed using intra- and inter-observer reliability tests, with a subset of 20 randomly selected patients re-evaluated three weeks after the initial measurements. Method error was quantified using Dahlberg’s formula, and the Smallest Detectable Difference (SDD) was derived to determine the threshold for true biological change beyond measurement noise. Missing data were handled using appropriate imputation methods, and all tests were considered significant at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

Participant flow

Through screening 400 adult patients with skeletal Class II division I malocclusion, several factors were considered to ensure accurate sample size calculation to compare treatment difficulty between groups. These factors included the severity and type of malocclusion, applied biomechanics, and the extent of tooth movement associated with first premolar extractions. Ultimately, 50 patients met the eligibility criteria. Data collection was completed during the 2018–2024 period following the completion of treatment.

A total of 1,000 teeth from 50 patients before and after treatment, who fulfilled the inclusion criteria, were examined. The mean patient age was 21.88 (5.18) years, and the mean treatment duration was 36.68 (4.27) months. Both the correlation coefficient (R) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) demonstrated excellent to very good reliability, with the ICC results ranging from 0.71 to 1.00, with a mean and standard deviation of 0.945 (0.051). Furthermore, the Smallest Detectable Difference (SDD) for all linear measurements ranged from 0.08 to 0.99 mm, with most values near or below the 0.3 mm voxel size. These results confirm that the observed dimensional changes reliably exceed methodological error and the imaging system's resolution limits.

Maxillary tooth inclination relative to the palatal plane decreased significantly after treatment, with the greatest change observed in the central incisors (10.01°), followed by smaller but significant changes in lateral incisors, canines, and first molars, while premolars showed minimal changes (Table 4; Fig. 7).

Volumetric analysis of maxillary teeth also revealed significant reductions after treatment (Table 5; Fig. 9). The first molars presented the largest volumetric reduction, decreasing by 26.95 (6.47) mm3 (P = 0.00). Differences in volume loss between anterior and posterior teeth were evident, reflecting heterogeneous patterns of resorption across tooth regions (Table 6).

Cohen's d effect sizes were large to very large for most parameters: bone thickness (0.09 to 3.27), bone height (0.98 to 4.20), root length (1.38 to 1.98), tooth volume (2.03 to 4.17), and inclination (0.35 to 3.45). Only two measurements showed small to medium effects, while the majority exceeded d = 0.8, confirming consistent and substantial biological changes across the sample.

Simple linear regression was performed to evaluate the impact of treatment duration on the observed changes. No significant association was found between treatment time and alveolar bone thickness (R2 = 0.00, p = 0.885), bone height (R2 = 0.01, p = 0.534), root length (R2 = 0.00, p = 0.927), or tooth volume (R2 = 0.00, p = 0.702). However, a statistically significant but weak association was observed for incisor inclination (R2 = 0.10, B = 0.14, p = 0.028), indicating that treatment duration accounted for only 10% of the variance in dental tipping.

Discussion

This study evaluated 3D of maxillary alveolar and dental changes associated with orthodontic premolar extraction in Class II division 1 malocclusion using CBCT, a modality that enhances diagnostic precision through accurate volumetric and anatomical assessment, [14] [28], especially when semi-automated segmentation is used in volumetric analysis [29].

It’s worth mentioning that a significant reduction in maxillary central incisor inclination (10.01°) indicates effective uprighting during retraction, which is a hallmark of FAs mechanics. However, such controlled torque movements also elevate the risk of bone and root reduction when roots approximate thin cortical boundaries [30–32].

When interpreting the magnitude of these changes, it is important to consider the measurement precision of the imaging system. While some observed changes (e.g., 0.22 mm) approach the 0.3 mm voxel resolution, they exceed our minimum calculated SD (0.08 mm) and remain statistically significant. Nevertheless, such sub-millimetric findings are interpreted cautiously as biological trends rather than major structural alterations, acknowledging the inherent resolution limits of the CBCT system.

This study revealed significant tooth-specific variations in alveolar bone height associated with premolar extraction treatment. A significant reduction in the vertical alveolar bone was detected in the anterior region, particularly on the palatal surfaces. Central incisors showed marked reductions in palatal bone height, consistent with previous CBCT studies reporting palatal dehiscence and bone thinning during incisor retraction [33–35] Lateral incisors demonstrated the greatest palatal vertical loss (2.33 mm), aligning with evidence of palatal bone narrowing during pronounced retraction mechanics. Additionally, canines exhibited distinct remodeling behavior, reaffirming their unique biomechanical journey during the distalization stage before incisors retraction [34], and further supporting findings that cervical bone regions experience the greatest resorption during retraction [5] [4].

Posterior teeth, including premolars and molars, demonstrated significant buccal bone thinning. Buccal ABT reductions of 1.20 mm in the second premolars and 0.65, 0.88 mm in the mesial and distal surfaces of the first molars, respectively reflect the tendency of posterior teeth to undergo buccal tipping or mesial migration during anterior space closure. This mesial migration of molars is a known contributor to anchorage loss and has been well documented by Teng et al., who highlighted similar movement patterns across molars influenced by patient age, crowding severity, and extraction timing [36]. Interestingly, despite the availability of extraction space, a reduction in alveolar bone was not prevented, confirming previous studies that extraction cases still experience significant vertical and buccal bone reduction, especially on lingual and palatal surfaces [16, 37, 38].

These changes might be even a normal nature of the remodeling process during FAs treatment, Al-Worafi et al. and Juliana et al., reported notable buccal ABT reductions in molars a part of the process even in non-extraction orthodontic therapy [7, 37].

From a biomechanical point of view, the contrasting patterns of decrease in thickness of the palatal bone in anterior teeth and the buccal bone in posterior teeth likely reflect differences in force vectors during orthodontic mechanics. Anterior teeth undergo palatal-directed root movement during retraction, increasing the risk of root proximity to thin cortical plates and contributing to palatal bone reduction [31, 32]. Posterior teeth, however, may experience a combination of buccal tipping forces and mesial drift, predisposing them to buccal cortical thinning. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining roots within cancellous bone boundaries and avoiding “through-the-bone” movements, a phenomenon where tooth movement exceeds the rate of bone apposition, resulting in fenestration or dehiscence [39]. Such risks are especially heightened in adult orthodontic patients, who generally exhibit reduced bone remodeling capacity.

Significant root resorption occurred across all tooth types, with anterior teeth showing the most pronounced reductions. Central incisors exhibited a mean reduction of 1.89 mm, followed by lateral incisors and canines (1.19 mm and 1.49 mm, respectively). These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that roots subjected to extensive displacement, torque expression, or contact with cortical plates exhibit increased susceptibility to inflammatory root resorption [6, 12, 15, 40, 41]. Posterior teeth showed smaller but still significant root length reductions, indicating that even mesial migration and minor tipping movements in the posterior region can produce measurable resorption. Studies have shown that prolonged treatment and initial root morphology influence resorption severity, with shorter roots and longer treatment durations associated with more extensive outcomes [12, 16].

Volumetric evaluation revealed significant reductions in tooth volume across all maxillary teeth, further confirming orthodontically induced root resorption. Anterior teeth demonstrated substantial volume loss (16–19 mm3), while posterior teeth, particularly first molars and second premolars, showed even greater reductions (23–27 mm3). These findings mirror the results of Chen et al., who found that FAs generate greater root volume loss than clear aligners, due to higher force magnitudes and more pronounced torque application [42–44].

The clinical relevance of our findings is underscored by the consistently large to very large Cohen’s d effect sizes observed across most parameters. Specifically, root reduction (d = 1.38 to 1.98) and tooth volume loss (d = 2.03 to 4.17) demonstrated robust and systematic biological changes that far exceed the threshold for a large effect (d ≥ 0.8). These high effect size values indicate that the observed dental and alveolar alterations represent consistent treatment-induced responses rather than random statistical variations. Collectively, these results emphasize the importance for clinicians to recognize the substantial magnitude of hard tissue remodeling associated with extraction mechanics, highlighting the need to balance effective tooth movement with the preservation of supporting structures.

Collectively, these findings suggest a direct correlation between tooth movement magnitude, inclination change, and the severity of both bone and root alterations, reinforcing the importance of individualized force management during orthodontic treatment.

It is noteworthy that the average treatment duration in our cohort was 36 months, substantially longer than the typical 24-month timeline often reported in private practice, even for complex Class II cases. This extended duration likely reflects the characteristics of treatment delivered in a university environment, where case complexity, training needs, and multi-step supervision may influence the overall timeline. Treatment duration is a well-recognized factor contributing to orthodontically induced root resorption, alongside force magnitude and the extent of tooth movement. Accordingly, the degree of root resorption observed in our sample may, at least in part, be related to the prolonged treatment time. In contrast, shorter and more streamlined treatment protocols commonly used in private practice may be associated with lower levels of resorption, as suggested by Yassir A. et al. These observations highlight the need for further research specifically examining the influence of treatment duration on root resorption outcomes across different clinical settings [45, 46].

The lack of a significant correlation between treatment duration and most hard‑tissue parameters may be explained by the relatively narrow range of treatment times in this sample, which limits the variability required for regression analyses to detect meaningful associations. In addition, bone remodeling and root resorption are influenced primarily by the magnitude and direction of tooth movement rather than the absolute duration of treatment. The only significant association was observed with incisor inclination, which is clinically plausible, as torque control and tipping mechanics are time‑dependent and may vary subtly across cases despite comparable overall treatment durations [47–49].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the absence of a non-extraction control group limits causal inference. Because the design evaluated pre–post changes exclusively within extraction cases, it is not possible to definitively attribute the observed dentoalveolar alterations specifically to premolar extraction rather than to general orthodontic tooth movement. In addition, the relatively prolonged mean treatment duration (approximately 36 months) may have acted as a confounding factor, as treatment time itself is known to influence alveolar bone remodeling and the severity of root resorption. Therefore, the independent contributions of extraction biomechanics and treatment duration cannot be fully separated.

Second, outcomes were assessed only at the completion of treatment, without long-term follow-up to determine the stability, recovery, or progression of the observed changes. The study population consisted exclusively of young Chinese adults with Class II malocclusion treated in a university-based setting using a single bracket system (Damon Q), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, ethnic backgrounds, malocclusion types, clinical environments, or appliance systems.

Furthermore, important biological and biomechanical variables including individual root morphology, pre-existing alveolar bone thickness, vertical facial pattern, anchorage control variability, and force magnitude were not specifically controlled and may have influenced the outcomes. Finally, while this study provides a detailed 3D clinical evaluation, it does not investigate the underlying cellular or molecular mechanisms of orthodontically induced resorption.

Generalizability

While this study provides valuable insights into the effects of orthodontic premolar extraction on maxillary bone changes, the findings may not be directly applicable to populations with different demographics (e.g., age, gender, and ethnicity). Specifically, the study included only young Chinese adults and utilized Damon Q self-ligating brackets in a university-based setting with prolonged treatment duration. These factors may limit the external validity of the findings. Further studies are needed to evaluate how these results apply to other age groups, malocclusion types, treatment modalities, and diverse patient populations. By addressing these variables, future research may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of orthodontic treatment across a broader populations.

Conclusion

Orthodontic associated with premolar extractions resulted in considerable dentoalveolar alterations, including significant decreases in alveolar bone thickness, especially on the palatal surfaces of the anterior teeth, along with observable root resorption and tooth volume reduction, particularly in the maxillary anterior region. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive pre-treatment assessments and ongoing post-treatment monitoring to mitigate potential long-term impacts on both tooth and bone health, thereby ensuring stable and successful orthodontic outcomes.
