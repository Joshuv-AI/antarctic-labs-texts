From tiers to truth – a biomarker-based framework for clinically relevant immunogenicity assessment
Authors: Lauren F Stevenson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunogenicity is a biological response to pharmacologic therapeutic intervention and therefore fits squarely within established definitions of a biomarker. Yet, unlike most biomarker assays, where analytical performance is tailored to context of use, anti-drug antibody (ADA) testing has converged on a largely uniform, three-tiered paradigm built around statistically derived cut points and titer reporting. This perspective argues that this approach arose from understandable, risk-averse responses to rare, high-impact safety events, combined with early analogies to the vaccine field, rather than from first principles thinking aligned with drug development needs.As a result, immunogenicity datasets are often reduced to binary classifications that discard biological context, inflate reported incidence, and complicate efforts to relate immune responses to clinically meaningful outcomes such as pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, efficacy, or safety. Titer-based readouts, while appropriate for large vaccine-like responses, are frequently insufficiently granular to capture the full spectrum of response magnitudes relevant to contemporary biotherapeutic modalities.Reframing immunogenicity as a context-of-use–driven biomarker measurement leverages complete, continuous response profiles (e.g. screening-tier signal-to-noise) alongside pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD), and clinical outcomes to identify clinically relevant immunogenicity thresholds. This approach preserves nuance, improves interpretability and stakeholder communication, and focuses attention on clinical impact most relevant to patients and regulators.KEYWORDS: Immunogenicity, context of use, signal-to-noise, biomarker, validation, cut point

Introduction

According to the BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource, a biomarker is a defined characteristic that is measured as an indicator of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes, or biological responses to an exposure or intervention, including therapeutic interventions [1]. Given that the development of immunogenicity to a biologic therapeutic is a biological response to exposure to that therapeutic, it undeniably follows that immunogenicity is a biomarker, although it has historically not been seen as such. Instead, immunogenicity has garnered special attention and been perceived as a distinct, separate arm of bioanalytical science, with the validation of assays to detect and characterize unwanted immunogenicity subject to very specific regulatory guidance [2–4]. In contrast, there effectively remains no formal, comprehensive, prescriptive regulatory guidance for biomarker assays.

Although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released Bioanalytical Method Validation for Biomarkers, Guidance for Industry in 2025, this brief, three-page document provides neither new nor specific information on assay validation parameters of interest and the associated approaches for evaluating them. Instead, FDA’s guidance states, “bioanalytical method validation for biomarkers should be addressed consistent with the principles of the guidance for industry M10 Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis” even while acknowledging that the M10 guidance specifically puts biomarker assays out of scope [5,6]. Perhaps unsurprisingly, given this inherent logical inconsistency, release of the guidance was rapidly met with publication of multiple perspectives and commentaries from the scientific community, all of which highlighted that biomarker assay performance requirements are dictated by the context of use (COU) for the biomarker data [7–9]. For example, assay precision requirements are very different for a biomarker assay that needs to discern a 10% change in the biomarker versus one that is required to detect large several-fold changes. Therefore, it is recognized that no singular guidance with preset specific assessments and blanket acceptance criteria can serve the needs of biomarkers. To this end, the most comprehensive industry resource to date is the Critical Path Institute’s Points to Consider Document: Scientific and Regulatory Considerations for the Analytical Validation of Assays Used in the Qualification of Biomarkers in Biological Matrices which provides a framework for COU-driven biomarker assay validation without being prescriptive [10].

One might be tempted to assume that immunogenicity assays have prescriptive guidance because these assays serve the same COU, but that would be an ill-considered conclusion. The relative risk and potential clinical impact of immunogenicity is nuanced and different for each therapeutic program. Understanding of COU is critical to ensure that the assays are designed and validated appropriately in each instance to effectively identify and characterize clinically relevant immunogenicity. In light of this understanding, it is now fair to question how a one-size-fits-all approach could ever be adequate for a complex biomarker, like immunogenicity.

The three-tiered paradigm in context

Historical perspective on the immunogenicity testing paradigm

In the early days when the development of biotherapeutics was relatively new, (1980s−1990s) routine, prospective anti-drug antibody (ADA) testing was often limited or reactive, with immunogenicity testing undertaken only if unexplained loss of efficacy or other clinical impact was observed. However, immunogenicity gained notoriety when alarming safety events occurred with EPREX (epoetin alfa) when there was an unusual spike in cases of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) caused by the development of neutralizing antibodies that eliminated both exogenous and endogenous erythropoietin (EPO) activity. Although EPO had been on the market for almost 10 years prior (1989–1998) with only a handful of PRCA cases reported worldwide (~1/10,000 patient years), in the years spanning 1998–2002, hundreds of cases arose, particularly in vulnerable patient populations. Thorough investigations were conducted, concluding that the cause of the unusual increase in immunogenicity was due to formulation and drug handling changes that had occurred in that timeframe [11–13].

This tragic series of events led to a number of changes in regulator expectations, including intensified post-marketing surveillance, tightening of manufacturing and formulation controls, and recommendations for risk-based immunogenicity strategies with the FDA and European Medicines Agency (EMA) formalizing risk-based immunogenicity frameworks in guidance documents [2,3]. However, this latter recommendation quickly devolved to expectations of systematic, prospective, three-tiered immunogenicity testing in most clinical development programs regardless of risk profile. In addition, neutralizing antibody (NAb) testing became an expectation for all biologics and risk assessments became an exercise in determining when to perform NAb testing, without considering why or if such testing would inform clinical impact.

A standardized approach, therefore, emerged: Three-tiered testing across the board with high-risk molecules performing NAb testing from first in human studies, low-risk molecules implementing NAb testing in Phase 3, and moderate/medium risk molecules falling somewhere in between. Notably, these risk categories were ill-defined, with risk assessments often equally weighting the possibility of any immunogenicity occurring with the severity of anticipated clinical impact. This led to the categorization of molecules with immunogenicity potential, but no clinical risk being classified as moderate risk. Additionally, reporting of incidence rates of immunogenicity became the norm, regardless of clinical impact. In short, true risk assessments were abandoned as regulators expected, and industry delivered, standard packages of testing, regardless of risk profile. This shift prompts an important question: Why was true risk assessment so quickly abandoned?

A slippery slope fallacy derailed assessment of risk

While scientists are rigorously trained to think critically and focus on the data, scientists are humans first, and in the aftermath of the tragic consequences of the EPREX case, the stakes were high. Although the conclusions from subsequent investigations made it clear that the true risk was narrow and well defined: a specific biologic, in a specific formulation, in a specific container, under specific conditions that led to increased risk of immunogenicity where the clinical consequences were dire, scientific thinking was derailed by a slippery slope fallacy.

A slippery slope fallacy is a logical fallacy where an argument claims that an initial event or action will trigger a series of other events and lead to an extreme or undesirable outcome without offering any evidence to substantiate the claim. Slippery slope statements are often preceded by the phrase ‘but what if’ without indication of the likelihood of occurrence. Stated otherwise, it invokes imagination without critical evaluation of true risk and consequences.

The immunogenicity slippery slope fallacy played out as follows:

Reality: Severe, life-threatening immunogenicity happened once in a specific high-risk product under specific conditions

Slippery slope thinking: What if … severe immunogenicity could happen for any/all biologics?

Slippery slope conclusion: We must test all biologics the same way to play it safe!

This faulty logical leap ignored all the specifics of the EPREX case that defined the risk. It ignored the conclusions of the investigation.

Consequently, over the next 20+ years, mandatory, blanket immunogenicity testing became the norm, regardless of true risk profile of the biologic, even for the lowest risk molecules such as fully human monoclonal antibodies to non-essential targets, enzyme replacement therapies without endogenous analogues and even non-biologics such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) with no plausible impact from ADAs. The current testing paradigm was born out of an understandable, but fear-driven reaction to safety events caused by very specific circumstances. Importantly, it was not deliberately designed from the outset to build an understanding of clinically relevant immunogenicity for all classes of biotherapeutics, which possess varied and nuanced relative risk levels based on modality, molecule design, mechanism of action, intended patient population, and more. In short, the current paradigm did not take COU into account.

The issues do not end there. Although the current paradigm was built with the intention to ‘not miss anything’ its design is flawed because it was created as a reaction, not as a foundational, first-principles framework intentionally developed to flexibly accommodate the varied and specific needs of individual drug programs. At that point in time, the underlying principle that immunogenicity responses are biological responses, and therefore immunogenicity is a biomarker, was not appreciated. Consequently, scientists did not leverage what could have been learned by looking to successfully implementec safety, pharmacodynamic, mechanistic, and diagnostic biomarkers across both clinical development and clinical practice. In the face of very limited directly relevant data sets, scientists and regulators alike turned to the >100-year-old, data-rich vaccine field, which understandably led to an early focus on titers as the appropriate means to measure immunogenicity magnitude [14,15]. This highlights the risks of reasoning by analogy in scientific endeavors.

Reasoning by analogy built the paradigm on the wrong foundation

Reasoning by analogy is a cognitive process whereby similarities are identified between different situations and inferences are made to solve problems, with the assumption that since the situations are similar in some ways, they are likely to be similar in other ways. It is a useful means to draw upon existing knowledge to understand new situations. The drawback being that while reasoning by analogy feels like appropriate leveraging of existing expertise and experience, it can lead to insufficient examination of the specifics of the new situation, and that risks getting it wrong. A simple example of reasoning by analogy going awry would be: Two houses are of the same architectural style and in the same neighborhood, therefore their market value must be the same. This hasty conclusion is clearly erroneous, but this simplistic example illustrates why although reasoning by analogy can be a useful starting place, it must be coupled with first principles questioning and careful examination of the ways in which the new situation is different from the prior situation. In scientific endeavors, the differences may be more nuanced, but deeply important, and therefore require a thorough and disciplined thought process.

The current three-tiered immunogenicity testing paradigm was born out of reasoning by analogy because it prematurely concluded that titers would be the best way to understand immunogenicity magnitude for drug development programs. When we are taught the current paradigm, we learn the following:

The screening assay identifies all putative positives.

The confirmatory assay then identifies which of those responses are specific to the drug of interest.

The titer assay provides a measure of relative magnitude of response.

This narrative gives the impression that the paradigm was intentionally built in this order – screen, confirm, titer. In reality, though, it was built with the intention of generating titer values. Unlike the vaccine field where eliciting an immune response is the therapeutic goal and all samples are expected to have large responses to measure, in drug development therapeutic molecules are deliberately engineered to minimize immunogenicity. Since titration assays are labor-intensive and low throughput, it did not make sense to titer all samples from a study where relatively few samples would be expected to have meaningful responses. Therefore, it was desirable to eliminate the expected population of ‘negative’ samples and reduce the number of samples to titer. Thus, the screening assay was born. However, implementing a screening tier required incorporation of a cut point with a statistical, false positive rate in an effort to ensure that no putative positive samples would be overlooked. Then, to eliminate the false positives that came through the screening assay, a confirmatory tier was added, with only the confirmed positive samples moving on to titration. In short, tiers of analysis were created to limit the number of samples to titer, with added complexity of cut points being introduced to try to build confidence in the culling process. Interestingly, in hindsight, even with titers being the chosen method for reporting magnitude of response, the surefire way to ensure nothing was missed would have been to accept the labor-intensive approach of titering all samples.

In retrospect, adoption of the three-tiered approach has likely represented a larger expenditure of resources in terms of time, cost, and labor, especially when considering the added efforts incurred for the cut point runs, statistical calculations, and reports as well as increased timelines associated with waiting on multiple tiers of analysis.

Reasoning by analogy led to quickly focusing on titers as the means to measure response magnitude, which ultimately resulted in an overly complex paradigm, and more concerningly, also failed to address COU for drug development. This is because titers are designed to measure large responses, and these large responses do not require fine precision of measurement. For titer schemes that employ two-fold dilutions, it is generally accepted that a titer must differ by greater than four-fold (>400%) to be considered a meaningful change [16–18]. This level of imprecision is sufficient for monitoring robust responses to vaccines. However, it is inadequate for the broader context of drug development where there is need to also reliably understand and differentiate low-level and moderate responses where discerning changes <400% is critical. More granular and precise characterization of the full landscape of response development and magnitude enables more accurate and refined correlations with clinical impact. With these goals in mind, first principles would never have led to titers as the best and sole measure of immunogenicity response magnitude. The paradigm was built from the wrong foundation.

Cut points create a false binary flaw

It is a common misperception that these three tiers of analysis with their attendant statistical cut points create scientific rigor and ensure that no positive responses are missed. However, as discussed above, cut points arose from a practical desire to limit the number of samples to titer because many, if not most, samples would be expected to be ‘negative.’ Notably, cut points were not designed to facilitate identification of clinically relevant immunogenicity, which is dictated by biological and clinical outcomes. Instead, cut points represent a statistically defined signal level based on naïve matrix background immunoreactivity that is intended to separate expected noise from potential responses. False positive rates were introduced into statistical cut point calculations to ensure false negatives were not erroneously screened out in the effort to reduce sample numbers for titration. The validity of this approach to ensure real responses are not missed has recently been challenged and sparked broader discussions on the utility of cut points [19]. Regardless of the scientific validity of the current approach to cut point determination, a critical, fundamental issue with cut points as a concept is that they create a false binary flaw.

A false binary flaw is a logical fallacy that misrepresents complex situations by limiting options to only two mutually exclusive choices. In this case, forcing a binary call of positive or negative, and subsequently discarding the ‘negative’ data and focusing only on ‘positive’ results causes biological context to be lost along with any and all insights the totality of screening data could provide. It is therefore not uncommon for the current testing paradigm to misreport normal biological variance as ADA positive, and those responses subsequently being perceived as true positives by key stakeholders and regulators alike. Ultimately, immunogenicity is reported as an incomplete data set and these cut point-censored data sets confound study interpretation. They prevent analysis of full response profiles that would allow biological variability to be reliably distinguished from low-level, clinically irrelevant responses, from meaningful ADA development (Figure 1).

Most concerningly, cut points result in data sets that are misaligned with clinical risk. It is all too common to see inflated incidence that does not correlate with clinical impact. This not only fails to deliver insightful data but also fuels unnecessary organizational turmoil. Stakeholders latch onto “percent positive” and panic before asking “Do these responses matter?” Countless FTE hours are expended managing perceptions of immunogenicity and good drug candidates may be deprioritized even in cases where no clinical impact is observed. In short, cut points compromise the ability to clearly correlate response magnitude with clinical impact. Initial analyses frequently focus on comparing ADA positive with ADA negative groups in an effort to identify statistically significant differences between groups. However, clinically relevant responses within the ADA positive group can be masked when pooled with a large number of low-level, clinically irrelevant, but cut point ‘positive’ responses. In such cases, cut points can cause real signals to be missed in the noise of irrelevance. Many organizations erroneously conclude ‘no impact’ of immunogenicity after this initial analysis and neglect to examine individual subject response profiles that could reveal an association with changes in drug exposure levels or pharmacodynamic responses. Adjudicating individual subject response profiles is not intended to provide insight into statistically significant conclusions about the population but instead provides an opportunity to gain insight into the potential relationship between magnitude of response and impact. Learnings at the individual level can then inform hypotheses and identify potential signals to monitor for in larger studies. This is a critical concept because identifying clinically relevant immunogenicity is not a statistical exercise, it is a process of discovery. Overlooking a meaningful response by drowning it in a sea of irrelevance is a failure of due diligence in the quest to discover clinical relevance.

In summary, the stark truth is that cut points cause us to discard nuance, inflate incidence, confound correlation with clinical relevance, and mislead ourselves, our stakeholders, clinicians, and regulators

In summary – a flawed paradigm

In his first inaugural address on 4 March 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt uttered the famous words “The only thing we have to fear is fear itself” [20]. Delivered during the depths of the Great Depression, his goal was to inspire courage, confidence, and decisive action against economic paralysis by asking Americans to overcome their fear and embrace bold government intervention for recovery.

The current immunogenicity testing paradigm was developed in response to catastrophic safety events, and a determination to ensure such events would not be repeated. But fear caused a failure to think like scientists. First principles thinking was neglected and human nature, full of noble intentions but susceptible to logical fallacies, built a paradigm with foundational flaws:

Developing a new paradigm

Lessons from biomarkers

When Roosevelt spoke about fear being the only thing to fear, he was indicating that the unreasoning terror of fear was preventing needed action. It is all too easy to remain paralyzed and rationalize the status quo. It takes courage to dare to rethink and do better. As scientists, we have the required skills to overcome the fear. First principles thinking, also known as ‘thinking like a scientist’ leads us to understand that immunogenicity is a biomarker. As a biological response to a therapeutic intervention, immunogenicity fits squarely within the BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource definition of a biomarker [1]. If we therefore approach the measurement of immunogenicity with a context-of-use-driven biomarker scientist mind-set, then we can build better immunogenicity assays.

Consider how we develop and validate biomarker assays and collect biomarker data. When we build biomarker assays, we do not set arbitrary thresholds to identify a sample as positive or negative. Instead, we develop and validate assays with analytical performance characteristics that reliably support the appropriate data collection to allow identification of meaningful biomarker thresholds or changes in biomarker levels that inform safety, efficacy, pharmacodynamics (PD), and more. We utilize the placebo population to understand biological context and variability that help us build an understanding of response levels that exceed expected cross-sectional and longitudinal biological variability. Similarly, when we undertake immunogenicity testing in our clinical development programs, we are essentially discovering a new biomarker, and, therefore, we should use all the data at our disposal to build an understanding of response level that has clinical relevance.

Adopting a biomarker assay mind-set

It is well understood across biomarker assay experts within the analytical community that no one-size-fits-all approach can serve the varied needs of biomarker assays. Each biomarker assay must demonstrate performance characteristics that support the specific COU for the biomarker data. Similarly, a COU-driven approach must be adopted for the development and validation of immunogenicity assays where the assay’s performance must demonstrate reliable measurement of clinically relevant responses which will differ according to the specifics of each drug program. The hypothetical examples below highlight how immunogenicity assay sensitivity requirements may differ according to COU.

COU 1: A highly potent biologic therapeutic is dosed at very low levels with anticipated Cmax in the 20 ng/mL range. Although current guidance indicates a blanket expectation for assay sensitivity of 100 ng/mL, this sensitivity level would likely be inadequate, as it suggests that the assay would only detect ADA when it was in great molar excess compared to drug [2]. This sensitivity would not ensure that all potentially clinically impactful ADA responses would be reliably detected. Thorough evaluation of potential impact of ADA on pharmacokinetics (PK), PD, safety and efficacy, and estimation of relative levels of ADA that would be required to have such impacts would need to be performed to inform appropriate assay sensitivity requirements.

COU 2: A biologic therapeutic that has an essential, non-redundant endogenous counterpart is dosed at high levels (Ctrough ~ 100 μg/mL). In this scenario, would the recommended assay sensitivity of 100 ng/mL be adequate? Perhaps, but perhaps not. The answer is unclear without additional contextual details and specific knowledge of the drug program. Blindly adopting 100 ng/mL sensitivity would not assure safety without concomitant supporting scientific rationale.

COU 3: A biologic therapeutic is dosed at high levels (Ctrough >100 μg/mL). The molecule is low-risk with anticipated impact of ADA to be on drug exposure levels that could thereby impact efficacy if the response is robust enough. In this context, is 100 ng/mL assay sensitivity appropriate? Consider the relative molar ratios of ADA:drug and likely impact on PK. When ADA:drug molar ratios are below 0.1:1, no impact on exposure is expected because ADA is in vast molar minority and drug remains in excess and functional. Therefore, in this case, where drug is present at >100 μg/mL, the assay would be expected to reliably detect clinically relevant responses with assay sensitivity ≤10 μg/mL! Of course, molar ratio considerations serve as a pragmatic guide within a biologically informed, fit-for-purpose framework, rather than a standalone determinant for the selection of appropriate assay sensitivity. One could, if desired, utilize an assay with 100 ng/mL sensitivity in this scenario, but this will dramatically increase detection of clinically irrelevant responses that do not inform interpretation of the study and will propagate issues associated with high incidence without impact. This can be managed if the resulting data are correctly interpreted and appropriately messaged to key stakeholders.

These hypothetical examples focus only on the singular parameter of assay sensitivity, where different COUs demonstrably require different sensitivity targets. Equally, COU will inform required performance characteristics for all assay parameters. Setting appropriate control levels and performance requirements for drug tolerance, precision, assay range, and more should begin with careful consideration of COU to successfully deliver truly fit-for-purpose, valid assays. Adopting a COU-driven approach therefore requires rethinking how we develop and validate immunogenicity assays.

Considerations for pre-study assay validation

The three-tiered paradigm is largely defined by the application of cut points to triage samples through to the ultimate titer tier, but the tiers themselves do not represent different assays. The screening assay is the foundational assay and as currently designed and implemented across industry, these assays have a proven track record of reliably detecting ADA. The challenges with the paradigm described herein highlight that data sets are incomplete due to cut point censoring and that titers are ill-suited to best define the landscape of response levels, particularly lower and moderate level responses. Therefore, the screening assay provides a reasonable place to begin as we adopt a biomarker assay mind-set and consider what a single-tiered immunogenicity assay, sans cut points might look like, as long as we pressure test our thinking along the way using first principles to avoid potential pitfalls of reasoning by analogy that might arise due to our prior experience and sense of familiarity with screening assays. With this caution in mind, the assay performance parameters of interest would largely align with those already found in current immunogenicity guidance, with some key alterations.

As we embark upon reimagining the paradigm with a single-tiered, biomarker assay mind-set, it is important to note that current FDA Guidance for immunogenicity assay validation overtly states, “Validation includes assessments that demonstrate that an assay used for measurement of ADA is suitable for the intended purpose” [2]. COU represents the purpose; therefore, current guidance already directs us to validate our assays to COU. The guidance also states, “The fundamental parameters for validation include (1) cut-point, (2) sensitivity and drug tolerance, (3) specificity and selectivity, (4) precision, (5) reproducibility when relevant, (6) robustness of some assay features, and (7) in-use stability of critical reagents.” However, as indicated by the inclusion of cut point as a fundamental parameter, current guidance clearly assumes that the three-tiered paradigm is fit-for-purpose, which has been demonstrated herein to not be optimal for the breadth of drug development needs. Therefore, a key difference for single-tiered assay validation is the exclusion of the cut point parameter. Additionally, in-use stability of critical reagents is often interpreted as demonstration of stability of the positive control (PC) with studies evaluating short-term stability, including freeze–thaw cycles and refrigerator- and room-temperature stability as an imperfect surrogate for sample stability. This parameter may also be a candidate for exclusion from validation under a new paradigm for the following reasons: (1) PCs are not representative of study samples, (2) Antibody stability is well-established [21–23], (3) Critical reagent stability programs for other assay types such as PK and biomarker assays are managed and tracked outside the assay validation exercise, (4) since PCs are included in every assay run their performance can be tracked and trended in real-time, providing on-going stability data. That said, if an organization deems it scientifically and operationally value-added to capture information on stability of important assay controls and other critical reagents within the validation exercise, this may be deemed desirable for inclusion.

The other assay parameters, (sensitivity, drug tolerance, specificity and selectivity, precision, reproducibility when relevant, and robustness) remain of interest in the context of a proposed new paradigm, although the approaches employed and target criteria applied to claim validation would differ according to COU. Furthermore, in a single-tiered world assay range becomes an added, critical parameter to evaluate. Unlike the current paradigm where final data are reported as titers that are derived by diluting all samples to the level of the cut point, effectively utilizing only the bottom limits of the assay range, single-tiered approach must reliably measure samples across the full signal range of the assay.

A common argument against single-tiered approaches is the concern that high level responses will reach the assay plateau and the question is posed: What if the clinically relevant response level occurs on the plateau and cannot be properly discriminated from other responses on the plateau that are not clinically relevant? There are several points to consider in response to this concern. Firstly, the inclusion of the phrase ‘what if’ in this query suggests that this may be a case of slippery slope fallacy thinking as opposed to a likely expectation. Modern day assays built on platforms such as the MesoScale Devices (MSD®) instruments routinely achieve several logs of assay range. An illustrative example from a typical assay shows no signs of reaching signal saturation at the highest positive control (PC) concentration tested (63 μg/mL) (Figure 2), indicating that broad linear S/N ranges are possible and suggesting that clinically relevant response levels would be very likely to be detected within assay range. Secondly, adopting a single-tiered approach does not negate the possibility of testing samples at dilutions beyond the method’s minimum required dilution (MRD). Under the current paradigm, we readily accept titers derived from dilutions beyond MRD. If very large responses are expected, and there is desire to further characterize those responses, then assay development and validation studies should include evaluation of dilutions beyond MRD. In-study validation

When bioanalytical scientists and regulators speak of assay validation, they are most often referring to pre-study analytical validation, but it is important to remember that in-study performance is what truly demonstrates that an assay is meeting its intended purpose. In-study validation is not a series of analytical exercises but rather is comprised of in-study performance of assay controls, quality of the generated data, ability to interpret the generated data and if necessary, investigation of anomalous data that may lend insight into limitations of the assay. For example, a prozone effect may not have been observed with control samples during pre-study validation but might be suspected if anomalous response profiles are observed in-study.

In-study validation is especially important for immunogenicity assays because unlike many biomarker assays where samples with endogenous analyte can be procured and evaluated during pre-study validation, there is no source of ‘endogenous analyte’ at the pre-study stage. In-study provides the first opportunity to see true immunogenicity responses (the endogenous biomarker), so the data must be monitored closely, sense-checked from a first principles perspective, and questioned as appropriate. As for other biomarker assays, one must also be prepared to characterize response results that may fall outside pre-study-validated range of the assay. For example, even if dilution factors beyond MRD have been ‘validated’ pre-study it will be critical to ensure that the in-study data from diluted samples make sense and if not, investigate accordingly.

This approach may feel more complex at the outset as pre-set approaches and criteria have been the historical norm for immunogenicity assays, but critical thinking and careful scientific evaluation are requisite. One size will not fit all. This has always been the case in the biomarker field. In the case of immunogenicity, there is consolation in the fact that meaningful immunogenicity responses are not difficult to identify when the entire response profile is examined. Meaningful responses notably rise above the biological noise and their trajectories can be followed over time, providing the opportunity to clearly discern low responses from high responses. Additionally, high responses that decline over time versus those that persist can be interrogated to determine whether different response profiles result in differential clinical impact (Figure 3).

Data analysis considerations – lessons from PD biomarkers

ADA response data (as measured by continuous signal-to-noise (S/N) values) can be analyzed as is done for any other pharmacodynamic (PD) biomarker. A common first step is to split the population into categories based on the distribution of responses, often by tertiles/quartiles where the data are divided into three/four equal groups (low, medium, high/very high) with clinical outcomes compared across the groups [24,25]. In this manner, it can be determined whether increasing ADA response levels are associated with clinical impact, including: (1) PK correlations by comparing average drug levels across groups, (2) Efficacy outcomes by looking at response rates, disease activity scores, or time-to-loss-of-response across groups, (3) Safety outcomes by checking whether higher ADA levels correspond with more adverse events.

These analyses can be customized as deemed most appropriate based upon overall size of the dataset and the observed distribution of responses. While there can be a tendency to want to ensure there are enough individuals in each category to allow for determination of statistically significant differences, remain aware that this is not the goal of the analysis. The goal is to identify response levels that are clinically relevant. It is important to ensure that informative individual response profiles are not inadvertently drowned out when pooled with a broader population. While observed association of ADA response level to clinical impact in only one or a few individuals would not lead to a decisive conclusion, these observations provide insight into what to monitor for moving forward. Remember, this is effectively an exercise in discovery and characterization of a new biomarker. Early signals should be noted and accumulated data will be required to ultimately identify and define a clinically relevant threshold.

Data reporting considerations

Frequent questions that arise in discussions about the prospect of immunogenicity assays without cut points are How will we know what is positive or negative? and How will we report immunogenicity incidence? To address these questions, we first need to remind ourselves that statistical cut points do not identify true positives. They identify response levels that exceed a cut point factor derived from negative samples and from which ‘outliers’ have been removed. These biological outliers later appear in clinical studies as pre-dose positives and/or placebo positives. Cut points therefore cause samples to be called positive that are not positive. Conversely, cut points can also misclassify samples as negative that show demonstrable increases in S/N after treatment when evaluated in relation to their matched baseline sample (Figure 4). It is therefore time to unlock mind-sets from the positive/negative false binary of cut points and instead focus on identifying clinically relevant response levels.

Baseline normalized response profiles missed by application of cut points.

Baseline normalized S/N was evaluated for a clinical study where the traditional paradigm reported no confirmed positives. Five subjects were identified that demonstrated ≥50% increase in S/N from baseline (designated by dashed line) at one or more time points. While these low-level responses would not be expected to have clinical impact, the identification of these profiles can assure regulators that the lack of detection of clinically relevant immunogenicity in the study was not due to inadequate assay design and performance. Interestingly, while 4/5 subjects screened (but did not confirm) positive at one or more time points according to the traditional paradigm, the subject with the greatest response magnitude post-baseline (closed triangles) screened negative at all time points due to the subject’s baseline response being notably lower than the rest of the population.

To do so, all data can be reported as S/N values and subsequent analyses performed that describe the range of S/N values observed and any associations between S/N magnitude and clinical impact. Instead of reporting incidence of ‘positivity,’ a descriptive narrative can be reported that delineates S/N ranges with attendant impact, or lack thereof. For a simple example of a low-risk drug where clinical impact would be on drug exposure levels and thereby efficacy, a narrative might look as follows:

85% of subjects had S/N values <5 with no observable impact on PK parameters.

5% of subjects with S/N values >10 had significant reductions in, or complete loss of, drug exposure and loss of efficacy according to the following measures [insert clinical measure here].

The incidence value of interest in this case is 5%, as this represents the incidence of clinically relevant immunogenicity. In the happy circumstance where no impact is observed within any range of S/N levels, then reporting would simply state that all patients had S/N values ≤ [max S/N response observed] with no impact on PK, PD, efficacy, or safety. Therefore, the incidence of clinically relevant immunogenicity in that study would be 0%. However, for clinical studies where the data set may be quite small, this limitation should be acknowledged with assurances that monitoring of immunogenicity will continue in ongoing and future studies.

A final question that arises when discussing single-tiered immunogenicity assays involves how to navigate data delivery since organizations have set up their systems to receive immunogenicity data in formats consistent with the three-tiered paradigm. As for data analysis, immunogenicity data delivered as continuous S/N response values would be delivered the same way that PD data are delivered. If your organization has a method to deliver and analyze PD data, then the necessary data delivery and handling systems are already in place.

Conclusion

The 3-tiered immunogenicity testing paradigm was developed at a critical point in time. Severe, unexpected safety events had occurred with EPREX in a broader context where the overall depth and breadth of experience with biologic therapeutics was limited. Appropriately, response to those events was approached with a sense of urgency to ensure that such events be prevented from occurring again. In the face of very limited, directly relevant data sets, scientists, and regulators turned to the >100-year-old, data-rich vaccine field, which understandably led to an early focus on titers as the appropriate means to measure immunogenicity.

These circumstances were clearly not optimal for fostering a hospitable environment for first principles thinking, which requires tackling a problem from the ground up to develop solutions. One can imagine the intensity of pressure to rapidly devise a solution would have hamstrung any efforts to take a step back and appear to be reinventing the wheel when there was an apparently obvious starting point. Consequently, circumstances and a lack of data led to foundational flaws in the paradigm. Specifically:

Titers are not fit-for-purpose for drug development because they are designed to evaluate very large responses (vaccines), not the full landscape of response development and magnitudes (all biologics)

Altogether, these foundational flaws lead to misrepresentation of immunogenicity liability (or lack thereof) for drug programs. Under the current paradigm, ADA incidence is dictated by the assay deployed and assays that report high incidence can dramatically alter perceptions of the program’s potential immunogenicity liability. While sophisticated bioanalytical scientists may understand that the lack of clinical relevance makes high incidence rates unalarming, this is not the perception that is widely experienced by cross-functional stakeholders, board members, and investors.

Furthermore, under the current paradigm, ADA incidence can be manipulated. Want to lower your ADA incidence? Redevelop the assay to be less sensitive. Want regulators and other stakeholders to believe your assay is highly sensitive? Switch in a higher affinity positive control. These manipulations do not speak to clinical relevance. Moving to a paradigm that focuses on identification of clinically relevant immunogenicity will correct this course. Any assay that is appropriately developed and validated to meet COU for detection of clinically relevant responses will deliver the same clinically relevant incidence.

Future perspective

Today, and for the future, we have the benefit of 20/20 hindsight fueled by over two decades of experience generating and interpreting immunogenicity data. And with that experience we have discovered the challenges inherent in the paradigm. In the same timeframe, we have also benefited from enormous growth in the biomarker arena, with biomarkers routinely playing a critical role in drug development, from exploratory purposes to enabling better internal decision-making and go/no-go decisions on early-phase drug programs to serving as surrogate efficacy readouts in regulatory filings. This explosion in biomarker implementation has come with commensurate breadth and depth of experience developing and validating biomarker assays to meet wide and varied COUs.

In this time, and in this present context, we are equipped to rethink [26]. We have the data sets, we have rich experience, and we have had the time to think it through. With those advantages, we now understand that immunogenicity is itself a biomarker. Clinically relevant immunogenicity for a given drug program must be considered in context (the drug modality, its mechanism of action, dose level, dose regimen, patient population, and more) and the attendant assays developed and validated to meet specific COU. To this end, appropriately designed single-tiered immunogenicity biomarker assays offer several advantages: (1) Biological context is provided by using all available data, including placebo data that directly inform biological variability in the population of interest, allowing true responses to be distinguished from biological noise. (2) Full subject response profiles clearly depict response development and magnitude. (3) Continuous response data sets enhance the ability to correlate responses with clinical relevance. (4) Complete, transparent data sets are intuitive to interpret and communicate to stakeholders and regulators, facilitating regulator review and eliminating organizational swirl that arises from high, but clinically irrelevant incidence rates.

Adopting this approach mitigates any fear that we might miss a meaningful response – because all the data are included. This is especially important for high-risk molecules where overlooking even a small signal could be catastrophic. We must unstick our minds from the false binary of positive/negative without clinical correlation and the slippery slope of fear of the unfamiliar. Instead, we must embrace first principles scientific thinking to develop and validate the best possible assays that allow us to discover and define clinically relevant immunogenicity for every drug program and thereby provide meaningful information to our stakeholders, regulators, clinicians, and patients.

Moving forward, implementing such change will require multi-stakeholder scientific alignment on numerous fronts, including, but not limited to: (1) creation of a thought framework of key considerations for crafting customized COUs that speak to the specifics of the molecule, the program phase, the patient population and anticipated clinical impacts of ADA, (2) establishment of best practices for development and validation of single-tiered immunogenicity biomarker assays, including incorporation of in-study validation monitoring, and (3) development of new approaches for reporting of immunogenicity data and defining incidence, not by a statistical cut point, but by clinical relevance.

All this will require commitment from industry scientists and regulators to collaborative, open-minded deliberation, and debate, conducted with rigor and a sense of urgency. A future is upon us where more insightful and actionable immunogenicity data can be delivered while also saving time, money, and resources. We cannot delay.
