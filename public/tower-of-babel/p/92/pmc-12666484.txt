IMPDH and GTP Metabolism in Cancer: Mechanisms, Regulation, and Translational Scope

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGuanosine triphosphate (GTP) is increasingly recognized as a critical actor in cancer cell proliferation, yet its regulatory mechanism remains incompletely defined. A key contributor to elevated GTP levels in tumors is inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2), a rate‐limiting enzyme in the de novo guanine nucleotide biosynthetic pathway. Although IMPDH inhibitors, mycophenolic acid (MPA) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), have shown potential in cancer therapies, their success has been limited due to their immunosuppressive side effects and several unresolved regulatory mechanisms, including paradoxical control of IMPDH activity by GTP. This review provides a systematic summary of the current understanding of IMPDH biology, emphasizing its complex regulation and therapeutic relevance in cancer. We will outline key unresolved questions, including isozyme‐specific roles and mechanisms for escaping regulation, and propose mechanistic and translational strategies to design IMPDH‐targeted cancer therapies.

Introduction

Cancer cells undergo extensive metabolic rewiring to support their rapid proliferation and survival. Among these metabolic changes, enhanced guanosine triphosphate (GTP) synthesis has emerged as a critical feature in multiple tumor types. The intracellular GTP level elevation is largely driven by the upregulation of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), the rate‐limiting enzyme in the de novo guanine nucleotide biosynthetic pathway, which converts inosine monophosphate (IMP) to xanthosine monophosphate (XMP) using NAD+ as a cofactor (Figure 1A) [1]. These elevated GTP levels, in turn, fuel downstream processes such as proliferation, motility, and drug resistance through GTP‐sensitive signaling pathways.

IMPDH as a rate‐limiting enzyme for GTP synthesis. (A) Role of IMPDH in GTP biogenesis. (B) The key groundbreaking findings associated with IMPDH.

IMPDH and its inhibitors have a long history of extensive investigation (Figure 1B). The human genome contains two genes encoding IMPDH isozymes, IMPDH1 and IMPDH2. Although enzymatically nearly identical in vitro [2, 3], IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 differ markedly in their tissue expression patterns. According to the original expression analysis by Natsumeda and colleagues, IMPDH2 is broadly expressed across adult and fetal tissues [4], which is confirmed later by transcriptomic studies [5] (Figure 2). Notably, IMPDH2 is frequently overexpressed across multiple tumor types [6, 7, 8, 9], and is increasingly being recognized as a relevant target for cancer therapy.

Expression of IMPDH in humans. The mRNA expression levels of (A) IMPDH1 and (B) IMPDH2 in each organ are plotted. The data were taken from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/).

Despite the importance of IMPDH2 in cancer, several critical questions remain unresolved, limiting its translational applicability as a therapeutic target. The molecular mechanisms underlying the selective overexpression of IMPDH2—but not IMPDH1—in cancer cells have not been elucidated. Although GTP strongly inhibits the enzymatic activity of IMPDH in vitro through allosteric regulation, cancer cells often maintain elevated IMPDH2 activity despite their high intracellular GTP levels. In addition, IMPDH can form large cytoplasmic filamentous structures, known as rods and rings (RRs), under certain cellular conditions; however, the physiological relevance and functional consequences of RR formation remain poorly understood. The availability of clinically approved IMPDH inhibitors, such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), a prodrug of mycophenolic acid (MPA), presents a unique opportunity for therapeutic repurposing in oncology. Translating this potential into cancer therapeutics; however, will require a deeper mechanistic understanding of IMPDH biology and its context‐dependent roles in cancer. Equally important will be the development of predictive biomarkers, optimized delivery systems, and translational frameworks that engage clinicians, academic researchers, and industry partners in accelerating the clinical application of IMPDH‐targeted therapies.

To bridge these knowledge gaps and enable clinical translation, a multidisciplinary approach—in integrating structural, biochemical, pharmaceutical, and cellular biological studies with clinical investigation—is essential. Toward this end, this review organizes current knowledge of IMPDH biology and outlines key questions that are shaping future directions in cancer research. In Section 2, we outline the structural and biochemical regulation of IMPDH2, with a particular focus on the clinically used inhibitors, MPA and MMF. Section 3 discusses unresolved mechanisms underlying IMPDH2 function in cancer. Section 4 explores the spatiotemporal dynamics of GTP and IMPDH2 in cellular processes. Finally, Section 5 evaluates the therapeutic prospects of IMPDH inhibitors, with particular emphasis on drug repurposing strategies and their translational implications.

IMPDH and Its Inhibitors

Physiological Roles of IMPDH Isozymes

IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 exhibit high structural homology and nearly identical enzymatic properties in vitro [10], yet their physiological roles diverge distinctly in vivo. IMPDH2 predominates in proliferating cells and embryonic tissues, reflecting its indispensable role in rapid cellular division and tissue formation. Genetic knockout studies reveal that Impdh2‐null mice exhibit early embryonic lethality around the blastocyst implantation stage [10]. Lineage‐specific knockout experiments show that Impdh2 is essential for developing neural crest derivatives, including the enteric nervous system, cardiac outflow tract, and craniofacial bones [11]. Although Impdh2 heterozygous mice remain viable and exhibit no overt phenotypes, their T‐lymphocyte activation is significantly impaired when combined with Hprt1 knockout, which leads to purine salvage deficiency, resulting in approximately 30% lower GTP levels and reduced proliferation upon stimulation [10].

Conversely, Impdh1 knockout mice are generally viable with normal fertility, yet subtle defects arise in the retina. The retina, characterized by remarkably high IMPDH1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels [1], displays heightened sensitivity to Impdh1 deficiency [12]. Zebrafish models complement this observation: impdh1a mutants that lack Impdh1a protein expression exhibit significantly decreased retinal guanine nucleotide pools, supporting a conserved retinal dependence on IMPDH1 across vertebrates [13].

These isozyme‐specific phenotypes and expression patterns clarify why clinical use of IMPDH inhibitors is restricted during pregnancy, underscoring the need for isozyme‐selective therapeutic strategies (further discussed in Sections 3.2 and 5).

Toward this end, the Ki values of clinically available IMPDH inhibitors measured with recombinant human IMPDH enzymes are provided in Table S1 [2, 3]. Although modest selectivity for IMPDH2 over IMPDH1 is detected, these differences are minimal and unlikely to be significant at clinically achievable plasma concentrations, reflecting the highly conserved nature of the catalytic sites between the two isozymes. Consistently, our previous study demonstrated comparable sensitivity of IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 knockout glioblastoma cells to the IMPDH inhibitor MPA [6].

Beyond its catalytic role, IMPDH also exhibits non‐canonical (“moonlighting”) activities. Intriguingly, the Drosophila ortholog functions as a DNA‐binding transcriptional repressor, revealing roles in gene regulation beyond nucleotide metabolism [14]. These emerging insights suggest that targeting the non‐enzymatic functions of IMPDH could provide novel therapeutic opportunities distinct from traditional catalytic inhibition.

Structure and Activity of IMPDH and Its Regulation by GTP

The subunits of homo‐octameric IMPDH consist of a catalytic domain and a regulatory domain, called the Bateman domain (Figure 3A). In human IMPDH2, the catalytic domain is composed of two separate regions: the N‐terminal region (residues 1–113) and the C‐terminal region (residues 238–514) (Figure 3A; blue and green). The Bateman domain (residues 114–237 in human IMPDH2) comprises a tandem of nucleotide‐binding cystathionine β‐synthase (CBS) domains (Figure 3A). Four catalytic domains assemble IMPDH into a tetramer through a fourfold axis and are located in the center of the tetrameric assembly (Figure 3B). Interactions between four pairs of Bateman domains, with each Bateman domain in each pair belonging to a distinct tetramer, link two IMPDH tetramers into an octamer, which is considered its typical functional unit (Figure 3C).

Structure of human IMPDH2. (A) Domain architecture of human IMPDH2. (B) Top and (C) side views of IMPDH2 octamer. (D) Regulation of IMPDH2 by nucleotide binding to the Bateman domain. IMPDH2 adopts extended (left) and compressed (right) forms depending on the presence of GTP.

The Bateman domains are found in various proteins, including IMPDH, CBS, AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), and chloride channels, and function as a sensory domain for intracellular nucleotides [15]. The Bateman domain of human IMPDH2 contains three nucleotide‐binding sites, designated as Sites 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 3D). Sites 1 and 2 are canonical nucleotide‐binding sites in the Bateman domain and are considered to bind to both ATP and GTP, whereas Site 3 is considered specific to GTP.

Biochemical and structural studies have demonstrated the regulatory role of the Bateman domain. When ATP binds to Sites 1 and 2, octameric IMPDH adopts an extended conformation, which is considered as an active form (Figure 3D, left). Conversely, GTP binding to Site 3 induces a conformational shift in the linker region connecting catalytic and Bateman domains to form a compressed, low‐activity conformation (Figure 3D, right) [16]. Notably, at GTP concentrations above 500 μM, IMPDH activity is significantly inhibited in vitro. This inhibition by GTP represents a typical feedback inhibition mechanism. However, in cancer cells, this feedback regulation appears to be impaired, as they continue producing GTP even under elevated intracellular GTP concentrations (see Section 3.1).

MPA: The First and Still Widely Used IMPDH Inhibitor

MPA was first isolated in 1893 from Penicillium brevicompactum as one of the earliest known antibiotics (Figure 1B), which is now known to bind competitively to the NAD+ binding site of IMPDH to block GTP synthesis. Initially classified as a mycotoxin, MPA was recognized for its ability to inhibit bacterial and fungal growth. MPA was later identified as an IMPDH inhibitor with the capacity to suppress cell proliferation [17]. The discovery led to the clinical use of MPA in immunosuppressive therapy, and its prodrug, MMF, is widely applied as an immunosuppressant after organ transplantation and for autoimmune diseases. Due to its inhibitory effects on cellular proliferation, MPA has also been explored as a potential anticancer agent. However, clinical trials have faced challenges due to the adverse on‐target effects in hematopoietic, immune, and gastrointestinal tissues, particularly in the colon, which have limited clinical translation due to dose‐dependent adverse effects [18]. In addition, patients' responses to IMPDH inhibitors vary considerably [17, 19], in part due to large interindividual variability in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics and the emergence of resistance to IMPDH inhibitors. Clarifying the mechanistic contributions of IMPDH to cancer metabolism will be critical for translating its inhibition into effective and context‐specific therapeutic strategies, as further discussed in Section 5.

Outstanding Questions in the Regulation and Function of IMPDH in Cancer

Evasion of GTP‐Mediated Feedback Inhibition in Cancer Cells

GTP, at its physiological concentrations, allosterically inhibits IMPDH activity through feedback regulation [20]. However, cancer cells maintain persistently elevated intracellular GTP levels [6], suggesting a discrepancy between in vitro regulation and in vivo metabolic behavior. This raises an unresolved question: how do cancer cells circumvent GTP‐mediated inhibition of IMPDH to sustain the GTP synthesis required for rapid proliferation? Clarifying this mechanism will advance our understanding of GTP regulation in oncogenic metabolism.

We propose three possible explanations. First, IMPDH itself becomes resistant to GTP inhibition by losing its GTP‐binding ability through quaternary structure changes, such as filament formation (Figure 4A) or mutations (Figure 4B). Recent in vitro studies demonstrated that filament formation of IMPDH2 desensitizes itself to the feedback inhibition of GTP and promotes the activity [21]. However, the in vivo significance of the IMPDH filaments remains unclear (see Section 3.3). Mutations in the IMPDH2 gene have also been documented in patients with dystonia and neurodevelopmental disorders [22]. Some of these mutations occur in the Bateman domain or the linker region between catalytic and Bateman domains (Figure 4B), potentially disrupting nucleotide binding and/or the GTP‐dependent inactivation of IMPDH. However, since the somatic mutation rate in the Bateman domain is < 1%, mutations alone would not fully explain this paradox [23]. According to the Genomic Data Commons [24], 68 of the 137 reported somatic mutations in IMPDH2 are missense type. Although these mutations are distributed across the entire protein, 32% of them (22/68) are located in the Bateman domain, which comprises only 24% of the full sequence (Figure S1A). Thus, the missense mutations are significantly concentrated in the Bateman domain. Likewise, 102 of the 170 reported somatic mutations in IMPDH1 are missense type, and these mutations are distributed across the entire protein. However, only 19% of them (19/102) are located in the Bateman domain (Figure S1B).

Second, spatial compartmentalization of GTP and IMPDH within the cell may represent an additional mechanism by which cancer cells circumvent GTP‐mediated inhibition. Subcellular GTP distribution has been shown to be heterogeneous [25]. IMPDH is not consistently co‐localized with downstream enzymes for GTP synthesis, such as GMPS, GUK1, or NDK1 [26, 27], suggesting the possibility that local GTP depletion may lift feedback inhibition, allowing IMPDH2 to remain catalytically active. The role of subcellular GTP dynamics and IMPDH localization will be discussed in Section 4, in the context of spatiotemporal regulation of GTP and its impact on cancer cell behavior.

The third hypothetical possibility is that high IMPDH activity in cancer cells results from specific kinetic properties, where IMPDH activation occurs in short bursts triggered by yet unknown interactions with cancer‐specific modulators. Such transient modulation is difficult to detect with current methods; however, if these activation events occur repetitively in a stochastic manner, subsequent conversion to GTP can be largely enhanced.

Together, these three mechanisms highlight the need for experimental strategies that can resolve spatiotemporal dynamics of intracellular GTP at high resolution. In particular, real‐time quantification of GTP concentrations in living cells would provide critical insights into the regulation of IMPDH activity in cancer‐specific contexts. However, no currently available probes or biosensors offer sufficient sensitivity or specificity to measure such dynamics. The development of reliable tools to monitor intracellular GTP metabolism remains a key technical challenge for the field.

Differential Regulation and Roles of IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 in Cancer

IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 are structurally similar and share enzymatic function, yet their expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms differ substantially, raising the possibility of distinct roles in cancer biology.

Transcriptome data (GTEx) indicate that IMPDH2 transcript levels exceed those of IMPDH1 in most tissues, with particularly high levels in the pancreas, ovary, and skeletal muscle, whereas lower levels are found in neural tissues, including the retina (Figure 2B). In contrast, IMPDH1 is highly expressed in the retina, followed by adipose tissue and spleen (Figure 2A). These differences are also reflected at the protein level [1]. Mutations in IMPDH1 are linked to retinal degeneration, whereas IMPDH2 is commonly upregulated in cancer and other hyperproliferative states. These observations point to distinct, context‐specific functions of the two isozymes in normal tissue homeostasis and tumorigenesis. Understanding their functional divergence in cancer requires further investigation. Below, we highlight three major areas where mechanistic divergence may arise.

Structural Divergence Arising From Amino Acid Sequence Variation

Although IMPDH1 and IMPDH2 share high sequence similarity, approximately 11% of their amino acid residues are not identical. These divergent residues are localized both in the catalytic domain and the Bateman domain. The biological consequences of single‐residue variation are exemplified by oncogenic KRAS and tumor suppressor p53. In KRAS, Q61 variants show distinct transforming capacities depending on the substituted residues [28]. For p53, mutations such as R248Q and R273H confer gain‐of‐function phenotypes associated with tumor aggressiveness and therapy resistance [29]. In IMPDH, the presence of isozyme‐specific residues may similarly modulate structural dynamics and context‐dependent functionality of the protein.

Compensatory Function in GTP Biosynthesis

In glioblastoma, IMPDH2 plays a dominant role in nucleotide metabolism. However, under selective in vivo conditions such as in orthotopic tumor models, IMPDH1 seems to partially compensate for IMPDH2 loss [6]. In our orthotopic glioblastoma (GBM) model, IMPDH2 knockout cells remained capable of forming tumors, in contrast to the complete tumor suppression observed in the double knockout. Notably, these IMPDH2‐deficient tumors exhibited a marked upregulation of IMPDH1 expression [6], indicating a context‐dependent compensatory response sufficient to sustain tumor growth in vivo.

Post‐Translational and Transcript‐Level Regulatory Divergence

Distinct post‐translational modifications may contribute to functional divergence between IMPDH1 and IMPDH2. For example, phosphorylations of retinal IMPDH1 at T159/S160 confer resistance to GTP inhibition [30]. However, these modifications have not been confirmed for IMPDH2, despite the partial conservation of these phosphorylation sites. Proteomic analyses have identified unique phosphorylation and ubiquitination sites for each isozyme, suggesting their context‐dependent regulation [31]. In addition, alternative splicing may further diversify IMPDH functions. IMPDH1 exists in multiple splice variants, some of which reduce sensitivity to GTP inhibition, implying that these splice variants contribute to isozyme‐specific functions [32].

Together these layers of regulation highlight the potential for isozyme‐specific control of IMPDH activity under physiological and pathological conditions.

Mechanistic and Functional Insights Into IMPDH Filamentation in Cancer

IMPDH forms supramolecular structures known as RRs, which have been observed in diverse cell types including breast cancer cells, activated T cells, and GBM tissues. These structures include rod‐like filaments that extend up to 10 μm—comparable to or exceeding nuclear diameter—and circular rings of 2–5 μm in their diameter (Figure 5) [33]. RR formation has been frequently documented in cancer cells, especially under conditions of metabolic stress such as glutamine or glucose deprivation.

In vitro studies have shown that recombinant IMPDH octamers can self‐assemble into stacked filaments (Figure 4A). These IMPDH octamers in the filaments can adopt both extended and compressed conformations and exhibit reduced sensitivity to GTP inhibition [21], suggesting a potential regulatory function. However, in vitro filaments are markedly smaller than RRs in vivo, implying that additional cellular factors may govern the RR formation.

The recurrent formation of IMPDH filaments in cancer cells suggests that filamentation may represent a metabolic adaptation to maintain nucleotide biosynthesis under nutrient‐limited conditions. Clarifying the molecular basis and regulatory triggers of filament formation will be important for delineating their functional significance. A critical part lies in distinguishing filament‐associated roles from the canonical enzymatic activity of IMPDH by rational mutation and/or the development of structure‐based probes or inhibitors that selectively destabilize filamentous assemblies without affecting their enzymatic activities.

Cellular GTP Gradients and Their Role in Regulating IMPDH Activity

GTP Sensing Proteins and Molecular Responsiveness to GTP Gradients

Despite a prevailing assumption that elevated GTP levels can increase activation of G‐proteins, the relationship in reality is more complex. In particular, small GTPases, such as RAS, bind to GTP with extremely high affinity (dissociation constants (K D) in a picomolar range) (Figure 6, blue) [34, 35, 36]. Guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) reduce the affinity of RAS to GTP; the resulting K D values remain in a nanomolar range (Figure 6, green). Because intracellular GTP concentrations typically range from 200 μM to 1 mM, far exceeding the K D values of RAS and RAS‐GEF complexes, RAS activation is not affected by physiological variation of GTP concentrations. Although reductions of RAS or G‐protein signaling have been reported in some studies following prolonged (> 24 h) treatment of IMPDH inhibitors, no direct link has been established between GTP depletion and these effects. Such observations may instead reflect broader cellular adaptations or secondary responses associated with long‐term metabolic perturbation.

Affinity to GTP and the GTP responsiveness of proteins. High GTP‐affinity proteins, such as RAS and the RAS‐GEF complex, are almost entirely in the GTP‐bound form at physiological GTP concentrations. In contrast, the GTP‐bound population of a low GTP‐affinity protein varies substantially with a physiological variation of GTP concentrations.

For proteins to express GTP‐sensing functions, their K D, Michaelis constants (K m), and/or inhibition constants (IC 50) to GTP need to be in a range of physiological variations (Figure 6, orange). A subset of enzymes has been reported to exhibit biochemical responsiveness to intracellular GTP concentrations. For example, RNA polymerase (pol) I and III show GTP‐dependent transcriptional activity in rRNA and tRNA synthesis, respectively, as originally demonstrated by our group and others [6]. Another example is PI5P4Kβ, which has an approximately 90 μM K m value for GTP. Structural, biochemical, and cell biological studies have demonstrated that its kinase activity is modulated by GTP availability, linking metabolic GTP fluctuations to phosphoinositide signaling pathways involved in tumorigenesis. This GTP‐sensing function of PI5P4Kβ was first reported by our group [37, 38, 39]. Importantly, IMPDH exhibits GTP‐dependent allosteric inhibition, with reported IC 50 values ranging from 200 to 800 μM. Taken together, these parameters place IMPDH within a dynamic window similar to RNA pol I, pol III, and PI5P4Kβ, suggesting that it may also function as a GTP‐sensing enzyme under physiological or pharmacologically altered conditions. However, whether these sensors specifically detect GTP concentrations or also respond to fluctuations in GDP or GMP remains unresolved. Given that GTP‐to‐GDP ratios vary significantly among different cell types, it will be crucial to investigate whether dedicated molecular sensors—analogous to AMPK [40]—exist for sensing GTP/GDP or GTP/GMP ratios.

Intracellular GTP Gradients and the Limits of Classical Diffusion Models

Most water‐soluble small metabolites, such as ATP, Ca2+, and cAMP, are assumed to diffuse rapidly within the cytoplasm, and their intracellular concentrations are thought to be regulated within a certain range under steady‐state conditions. In contrast, studies on phosphoinositide—particularly phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)‐trisphosphate (PIP3)—have demonstrated that steep spatial gradients in concentration can arise under specific conditions where diffusion is sufficiently limited, and metabolite production in a confined region outpaces local degradation and dispersal.

A ratiometric GTP biosensor (GEVAL) study first documented heterogeneous subcellular GTP distributions [25]. In line with these observations, we found polarized localization of GTP‑synthesizing enzymes at the leading edge in motile cancer cells [26, 27]. Despite these observations, several critical aspects of GTP gradient formation remain poorly understood. First, the enzymatic activity of polarized GTP‐synthesizing enzymes has not been directly measured; localization would not necessarily equate to their activity. Second, the mechanisms that drive and maintain the enzyme localization remain elusive. Third, the spatial distribution and regulatory dynamics of GTP‐utilizing/degrading enzymes are largely unknown. Fourth, GTP is generally present at high intracellular concentrations and is presumed to diffuse rapidly—faster than lipid signaling molecules such as PIP3. However, it remains unclear whether this rapid diffusion holds true in specific cellular subregions, such as the leading edge or within the nucleus. Under such microenvironments, local structural constraints or molecular crowding may limit GTP dispersal, allowing concentration gradients to persist.

To rigorously evaluate the existence and functional significance of GTP gradients, further investigations are needed in several areas. These include: (1) elucidation of the molecular mechanisms that regulate the localization and activation of GTP metabolic enzymes; (2) development of tools to quantify absolute GTP concentrations in living cells; (3) assessment of the intracellular GTP/GDP ratio at subcellular resolution; and (4) measurement of GTP diffusion rates in distinct cellular compartments. Together, these approaches will clarify the physical and biochemical underpinnings of GTP spatial regulation.

Strategies for Targeting IMPDH in Cancer: Inhibitors and Drug Repurposing Approaches

The development of novel anticancer drugs is a time‐consuming and costly process, involving multiple phases of clinical trials, a large number of enrolled patients, and often requiring over a decade and billions of dollars. As an alternative, drug repurposing (DR) offers a pragmatic route to accelerate therapeutic innovation by leveraging existing safety, pharmacokinetic, and manufacturing data, thus potentially allowing for bypassing initial Phase I clinical trials. MMF, a well‐characterized immunosuppressant approved for organ transplantation and autoimmune diseases, has high repositioning potential in oncology. Herein, following a discussion of translational barriers (Section 5.1), we examine MMF's repositioning potential in tumor contexts that offer distinctive therapeutic windows—such as metabolic vulnerabilities, immunoregulatory opportunities, and local pharmacokinetic targeting (Sections 5.2, 5.6) (Table 1).

Translational Barriers and Strategic Considerations for MMF Repurposing

Although MMF is a promising candidate for repurposing in cancer treatment, its clinical application faces several challenges, including its immunosuppressive effects and complex pharmacokinetics. Tumor type‐specific dosing regimens will require careful optimization, considering disease heterogeneity and interpatient variability in treatment response. In addition, the integration of MMF into standard of care therapies—including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy—requires rigorous preclinical and clinical evaluation. Furthermore, a critical disconnect exists between academic research, which prioritizes mechanistic insight and early innovation, and pharmaceutical development, which is driven by safety, commercial viability, and intellectual property concerns. Addressing these structural issues is essential to enable MMF and similar agents to transition into effective cancer therapeutics.

Synergistic Potential of IMPDH Inhibition in GBM Therapy

Gliomas are the most common primary brain tumors, characterized by high aggressiveness and limited treatment efficacy. Among them, GBM (WHO grade 4, IDH‐wildtype) remains particularly intractable, with a median survival of only 14.6 months [62, 63]. Therapeutic options are constrained by cellular heterogeneity and intrinsic resistance to standard therapies, including radiotherapy and the alkylating agent temozolomide (TMZ).

GBM exhibits metabolic reprogramming of GTP synthesis via IMPDH2 upregulation, which enhances ribosome biogenesis and tRNA synthesis [1, 6, 41]. This metabolic adaptation facilitates malignant progression, whereas normal brain cells appear less dependent on this pathway. Recent findings further demonstrate that the IMPDH2 inhibitor Gliocidin disrupts GBM cell metabolism, indicating a selective vulnerability [64]. GBM is also considered an immunologically “cold” tumor, raising the possibility that IMPDH inhibition may be applied without substantially compromising antitumor immune responses.

Beyond direct antitumor effects, IMPDH inhibitors have been shown to enhance the radiosensitivity of GBM cells by depleting GTP pools required for DNA repair. Preclinical studies combining MMF with radiotherapy have reported improved tumor control and extended survival [42, 43]. IMPDH inhibition is also linked to increased reliance on the purine salvage pathway in GBM cells [65]. When MMF is combined with TMZ, this metabolic dependency appears to amplify DNA damage and prolong survival in preclinical models. In addition, the potential use of IMPDH inhibitors in managing cerebral edema was initially proposed by our group and has since been explored in the context of GBM therapy [44, 45]. Given that MMF is approved for pediatric use from 3 months of age, it may be particularly advantageous for DR strategies targeting pediatric gliomas, such as diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), which currently lack effective standard treatments.

The preclinical data supporting MMF's activity in GBM have prompted early‐phase clinical trials aimed at evaluating its therapeutic potential (NCT04477200; NCT05236036). As Phase 0/1 and I/Ib trials, safety and dose exploration were the primary objectives alongside exploratory efficacy assessment. Although some encouraging responses have been reported, these trials have also highlighted key translational challenges—most notably, variability in patient response. Among the contributing factors, insufficient drug delivery to tumor sites remains a critical barrier, largely due to the limited permeability of MPA to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [46]. Although MPA demonstrates potent antitumor effects in vitro, its efficacy in orthotopic GBM models has been modest, likely due to limited intracerebral drug exposure resulting from poor BBB permeability [47].

Several strategies are being explored to enhance drug delivery across the BBB in GBM therapy. Focused ultrasound is a promising method to transiently open the BBB and enhance drug delivery in brain tumors. Early‐phase trials, mainly in glioma, support its feasibility, though broader clinical use awaits protocol standardization and pharmacokinetic validation [66]. Convection‐enhanced delivery enables direct drug infusion but requires precise catheter placement and prolonged infusion [67], increasing patient burden and procedural complexity. Key parameters, including catheter design, placement, and infusion protocols, remain unstandardized. Although intranasal administration of MPA offers a noninvasive route to bypass the BBB [47, 68], it remains unapproved and would require dedicated formulation development and clinical validation. Similarly, nanoparticle‐based delivery systems may improve brain penetration, but they necessitate the development of new drug constructs, thereby compromising one of the key regulatory advantages of drug repurposing. Achieving the clinical potential of MMF and related IMPDH inhibitors will require overcoming pharmacologic and regulatory hurdles—especially improving BBB penetration and delivery. Close collaboration among academia, clinics, and industry will be essential to integrate these advances within feasible repurposing frameworks.

Targeting IMPDH for the Treatment of Blood Cancers

There are three main types of blood cancer: leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma. Treating blood cancers by targeting IMPDH is a rational approach, as they grow rapidly and rely on the de novo guanine nucleotide synthesis pathway. Studies have shown that all types of blood cancer cells are susceptible to IMPDH inhibition in vitro [48, 49, 50]. Recently, IMPDH inhibitors showed a potent therapeutic effect on an aggressive blood cancer, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with MLL rearrangements, and promise in enhancing outcomes for blood cancer patients. Our recent study also showed that IMPDH inhibitors suppressed the development of AML driven by the leukemogenic fusion gene MLL‐AF9 in vivo [51]. Although preclinical studies in animal models produced encouraging results, translating these findings into successful clinical trials has proven challenging. Several clinical trials for blood cancers tested the effectiveness of IMPDH inhibitors [50, 52, 53]. However, the trials were unsuccessful due to short‐term responses and toxicity. Currently, there are no FDA‐approved IMPDH inhibitors for blood cancers.

Several hurdles must be cleared before IMPDH inhibitors can be used to treat blood cancers. First, the mechanisms driving their antileukemic effects require further elucidation. Notably, numerous studies have shown that IMPDH inhibition induces activation of the p53 pathway in cancer cells [69, 70]. However, IMPDH inhibitors are still effective against TP53‐deficient AML cells [51]. IMPDH inhibition in blood cells also induces downregulation of the MEK/ERK and mTOR pathways in IL‐3‐dependent murine blood cell lines [54], caspase‐dependent and ‐independent induction of apoptosis in human leukemia and myeloma cells [48, 55, 71], and overactivation of the Toll‐like receptor (TLR)‐TRAF6‐NF‐κB signaling pathway in AML cells [51]. Elucidating these diverse, cell‐type‐specific mechanisms of action is essential before IMPDH inhibitors can be fully harnessed for blood cancer therapy.

For instance, AML harboring rearrangements of the KMT2A (formerly MLL) gene, as well as ALL with gain‐of‐function mutations in NT5C2 (5′‐nucleotidase, cytosolic II), have shown responsiveness to IMPDH inhibitors, including MPA [51, 72]. Also, the immunological context of blood cancers may influence their responsiveness to IMPDH inhibition. In vivo studies have shown that AML cells transformed by the MLL‐AF9 fusion gene are susceptible to IMPDH inhibitors despite displaying resistance to adaptive immune responses [51, 73]. This finding suggests that tumors with low immunogenicity—so‐called “immunologically cold” tumors—may be more suitable for IMPDH‐targeted therapy. Identifying biomarkers that predict the sensitivity to IMPDH inhibition is an important future challenge in treating blood cancers.

Recent studies also suggest that optimizing treatment schedules—such as alternate‐day dosing—may enhance the therapeutic index of IMPDH inhibitors while minimizing immunosuppressive risk [51]. In parallel, rationally designed combination strategies targeting complementary pathways may provide synergistic efficacy [56, 57], underscoring the importance of integrated therapeutic approaches in the future management of hematologic malignancies.

Can Targeting IMPDH Overcome the Challenges of Ovarian Cancer?

Despite treatment with the combination of surgery and systemic therapy [74], the prognosis of advanced ovarian cancer (OC), characterized by peritoneal dissemination and malignant ascites, is extremely poor [75]. The development of molecular‐targeted drugs and the practical application of genetic testing have caused a paradigm shift in systemic therapy for OC and introduced some improvements [74]; however, the recurrence rate of advanced OC remains high, and the disease has a poor prognosis.

Research has revealed that IMPDH2 is markedly overexpressed in OC tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues, highlighting its potential role in tumor development [8, 58]. Notably, IMPDH2 levels are even higher in advanced OC, particularly those classified as FIGO stage III–IV [8]. The overexpression of IMPDH2 has been linked to a poorer prognosis, indicating IMPDH2 as a potential prognostic biomarker for OC.

OC is one of the immunologically cold cancers. Although the immune evasion makes the cancer aggressive and difficult to treat, it also limits the risk of IMPDH inhibition to impair tumor immunity. IMPDH inhibitors such as benzamide riboside, tiazofurin, and selenazofurin effectively suppress OC cell growth in vitro [59]. Thus, MMF presents a logical and potentially effective approach to therapy.

Emerging evidence indicates that MPA can suppress inflammation and angiogenic signaling, which points to a critical role for IMPDH2 in neoangiogenesis as described in our previous reviews [44, 45]. In advanced OC, ascites formation is driven by increased blood vessel permeability. Thus, IMPDH inhibitors are also expected to be beneficial in effectively managing ascites. Further investigation into IMPDH‐mediated signaling could pave the way for novel therapeutics that target GTP metabolism in OC.

Potential Advantage of MMF's Pharmacokinetics in Colorectal Cancer Treatment

In 2020, colorectal cancer (CRC) was the third most common cancer globally, with nearly 2 million new cases and approximately 1 million deaths, making it the second leading cause of cancer mortality [75]. Systemic therapy is applied to manage unresectable metastatic CRC [76].

IMPDH2 expression is markedly elevated in CRC compared to surrounding normal tissue, and its overexpression is closely associated with a worse prognosis [9]. In addition to the high IMPDH2 expression, its pharmacokinetic properties make MMF an attractive agent for CRC treatment. MMF undergoes a complex metabolic pathway post‐absorption (Figure 7). Initially, MMF is converted by carboxylesterases into its active form, MPA, which is subsequently metabolized by UDP‐glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs) into the inactive MPA‐glucuronide (MPAG) in the liver. MPAG is then excreted into the bile via multidrug resistance‐associated protein 2 (MRP2/ABCC2) and released into the intestines, where it is reconverted into MPA by the enzyme β‐glucuronidase produced by the intestinal microbiome [60, 61]. This enterohepatic recycling results in repeated gut exposure to MPA and presents a unique opportunity to exploit the high localized concentration and prolonged presence of MPA for local treatment of CRC. Although this cycle exacerbates gastrointestinal toxicity, leveraging this unique pharmacokinetic behavior could potentially improve therapeutic outcomes for CRC.

Metabolic pathway of MMF. MMF is a prodrug of the IMPDH inhibitor, MPA, with improved bioavailability. MPA is primarily metabolized by Uridine 5′‐diphospho‐glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) 1A9 and 2B7 in the liver to form inactive MPA‐glucuronide (MPAG) and be secreted into bile via efflux transporter MRP2/ABCC2. In the intestines, MPAG is converted back into MPA by β‐glucuronidase and exhibits enterohepatic recirculation.

CRC is one of the most common cancers with a well‐established understanding of its carcinogenic mechanisms. As a major obstacle to the clinical application of MMF is the unpredictability of patient response, CRC offers a unique opportunity for personalized treatment based on the proteogenomic characteristics of the CRC subtypes and the development of IMPDH‐targeted cancer therapies.

Leveraging Immunosuppression in Cancer Treatment

As antitumor immunity is a key factor in eliminating tumor cells, the immunosuppressive properties of MMF raise concerns about potential interference with antitumor immunity. However, immunologically cold tumors with low T‐cell infiltration, such as GBM, ovarian cancer, and uterine cancer, may benefit from IMPDH inhibition with minimal risk of impairing tumor immunity.

The immunosuppressive properties of MMF also present a unique opportunity for treating specific cancers associated with autoimmunity or uncontrolled immune responses. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) can effectively activate tumor‐specific T cells and deliver durable therapeutic responses [77]. However, ICIs often lead to immune‐related adverse events (irAEs) due to their disruption of self‐tolerance mechanisms, triggering autoreactive immune responses [77, 78]. Although halting ICI therapy and administering steroids is the standard treatment for patients experiencing severe irAEs, some with steroid‐refractory irAEs require alternative treatments. MMF has shown potential in addressing this unmet need by improving the symptoms in steroid‐refractory irAE patients [79, 80]. This suggests that MMF could serve as a dual‐purpose drug for managing irAEs while maintaining its anticancer effects. Although its effectiveness needs further validation, MMF may offer a safer and more effective treatment strategy for certain cancers, such as thymoma and thyroid cancer, that are closely associated with autoimmune diseases [81, 82].

Malignancies driven by viral infections, such as post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLD) and chronic active Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) disease, are characterized by chronic immune cell overactivation. A recent study demonstrated that MMF administration significantly reduced tumor formation and pathogenicity in an EBV‐infected xenograft mouse model [83]. The finding suggests that MMF could be an effective treatment option for virus‐associated malignancies by simultaneously targeting tumor growth and immune dysregulation.

Long‐term immunosuppression caused by MMF administration is associated with an increased risk of cancers, such as lymphoma and skin cancer [84, 85]. Although the incidence is relatively low, strategies aimed at minimizing extended immunosuppression should be considered to further reduce these risks during cancer therapy.

Conclusion

IMPDH functions as a central yet insufficiently characterized regulator of guanine nucleotide metabolism in cancer. This review has highlighted the mechanistic diversity and regulatory complexity of the IMPDH system, including the distinct functions of its isozymes, paradoxical activity control, and filamentous RRs. These mechanistic complexities present unique challenges, yet they also offer therapeutic entry points—both for new drug developments and for strategic repurposing of existing agents such as MMF. Bridging molecular insights with clinically actionable frameworks will be essential for translating the full potential of IMPDH‐targeted therapies into effective cancer therapeutics.
