Genomic characterization and comparative analysis of antibiotic resistance and virulence in Bangladeshi and global Klebsiella pneumoniae ST48 strains
Authors: Sudipta Chowdhury Chaity, Md Arman Hosen, Sabita Rezwana Rahman, Md Abu Sayem Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that poses a significant threat in healthcare settings worldwide. It is responsible for a wide range of infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), bloodstream infections, and wound infections, particularly affecting vulnerable populations such as the elderly, children, and immunocompromised individuals.1, 2 Globally, it contributes to approximately 10 % of nosocomial infections among hospitalized patients.3.

The clinical management of K. pneumoniae infections has become increasingly challenging due to the rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. These include isolates harboring extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases, which confer resistance to most β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems—often considered drugs of last resort.4, 5 Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) is associated with high mortality rates, reported up to 79 % in bloodstream infections, and has a global prevalence of 28.69 %, with South Asia showing the highest at 66.04 %.6, 7 In recognition of this growing threat, the World Health Organization has classified CRKP as a “critical priority” pathogen, emphasizing the urgent need for new antibiotics and alternative therapies.8.

The pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae is further compounded by an array of virulence factors, including capsular polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides, siderophores, and adhesins, which facilitate immune evasion and host colonization.9 Hypervirulent variants (HvKP) are particularly concerning due to enhanced virulence and, in some cases, co-occurring antibiotic resistance. The hypervirulence of HvKP is mainly attributed to hypercapsule, macromolecular exopolysaccharides, or excessive siderophores along with the involvement of lipopolysaccharides, fimbriae, and porin.10 Additionally, the bacterium’s ability to form biofilms on medical devices complicates treatment, leading to increased risk of persistent and difficult-to-treat nosocomial infections.11 .

Molecular typing techniques, such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST), have been instrumental in understanding the population structure and epidemiology of K. pneumoniae. Strains of K. pneumoniae from different sequence types (STs) show significant variations in virulence and antibiotic resistance profiles, with many STs being specific to certain geographical regions and some causing epidemic or endemic outbreaks.12 For instance, ST258 is prevalent in Europe and the USA, ST11 in East Asia, while New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) producers are common in South Asia.13 Among emerging clones, ST48 has gained attention for its global distribution and presence in both clinical and community settings, including food sources such as chicken meat.14, 15, 16, 17 .

In Bangladesh, ESBL-producing and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae are increasingly reported in clinical settings. A recent study found that 30 % of isolates resistant to third-generation cephalosporins in clinical samples were also carbapenem-resistant.18 A clinical observational study found that K. pneumoniae isolates carried multiple ESBL genes, with 64 % producing blaNDM-1.19 Moreover, another study identified healthcare facilities as reservoirs for MDR K. pneumoniae in Bangladesh.20 While these findings were based on conventional antibiotic susceptibility testing and PCR detection of specific genes, WGS offers a more detailed understanding of population diversity, clonal relationship and the genomic characterization of AMR and virulence in K. pneumoniae.

An increasing body of literature suggested a heterogenous distribution of K. pneumoniae populations in Bangladesh.18, 21, 22 Despite growing interest in the genomic diversity of K. pneumoniae in Bangladesh, there is a lack of focused studies on specific high-risk lineages such as ST48. This study addresses this gap by conducting a multi-scale genomic analysis of K. pneumoniae ST48, with a special emphasis on isolates from Bangladesh. Our investigation provides a detailed view of the genomic landscape of ST48 strains, an insight into the pan-genome of ST48 at both local and global levels and a phylogenetic framework for understanding the evolution and spread of K. pneumoniae ST48.

Materials and methods

Isolation, antibiotic sensitivity and biofilm formation of K. Pneumoniae S3C

K. pneumoniae S3C was isolated from the urine sample of a 5-month-old female child admitted to a tertiary care hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s parent. The urine sample was inoculated onto MacConkey agar and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies with K. pneumoniae-like characteristics were subcultured on nutrient agar to obtain a pure culture. Strain S3C was presumptively identified based on its biochemical profile, including IMViC tests, sugar fermentation, catalase, oxidase, urease production, and motility tests.

The susceptibility of S3C to a wide variety of antibiotics was determined by Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method in Mueller-Hinton agar.23 The tested antibiotics included amikacin (30 µg), gentamicin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefixime (10 µg), cefepime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), streptomycin (30 µg), piperacillin + tazobactam (100/10 μg), tobramycin (10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), sulphamethoxazole (25 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), and tigecycline (15 µg). Briefly, freshly grown culture of S3C was standardized to 0.5 McFarland standard and spread evenly on Mueller-Hinton agar. Then, antibiotic containing discs were placed on top of the agar and incubated overnight at 37℃. Following incubation, the zone of inhibition was measured and interpreted by the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline.24 .

The biofilm forming ability of S3C was quantitatively assessed using the crystal violet microtiter plate assay.25 Briefly, a freshly grown culture of S3C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth was inoculated into a microtiter plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. Non-adherent cells were then removed by washing the wells with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by staining with 200 µL of 0.1 % crystal violet for 15 min. After staining, the wells were washed again with PBS to remove excess dye. The bound crystal violet was then solubilized in 95 % ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm. Uninoculated LB broth was used as a negative control. The extent of biofilm formation by S3C was interpreted according to the criteria described by Stepanović et al.26.

DNA extraction, genome sequencing and assembly

The genomic DNA of strain S3C was extracted from cultured broth using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-genome sequencing was carried out at the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b), using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end sequencing (2 × 151 bp). FastQC (v0.12.0) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) was used to check the quality of the raw reads. Then, reads were subjected to adapter trimming using Trimmomatic (v0.39) (https://github.com/timflutre/trimmomatic).27 Finally, processed reads were assembled using SPAdes (Galaxy Version 3.15.5) integrated in Galaxy (https://usegalaxy.eu/).28 QUAST (v5.3.0)29 and BUSCO30 were used to determine the assembly features and completeness of the genome. We employed the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) (v391) (https://tygs.dsmz.de/)31 and KmerFinder v3.2 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/KmerFinder/)32 for whole genome-based identification of K. pneumoniae.

Bioinformatic analysis of draft genome

The assembled genome was annotated using PROKKA (Galaxy Version 1.14.6).33 PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/)34 was employed to predict the sequence type of the draft genome. The S3C genome was characterized for AMR genes, virulence factors, prophage sequences, plasmid replicons using CARD,35 ResFinder (v4.6.0),36 VFDB,37 PHASTER,38 and PlasmidFinder,39 respectively. PathogenFinder (v1.1)40 predicted the probability of S3C being a human pathogen. We used BacWGSTdb (https://cn/BacWGSTdb/Browse.php)41 to analyze the draft genome, generating a minimum spanning tree to visualize genetic relationships among others ST48 strains.

Comparative analysis of ST48 clonal type in Bangladesh

We retrieved the assembled fasta file and metadata of 51 (including S3C) K. pneumoniae strains that belong to ST48 clonal type from PathogenWatch (https://pathogen.watch/) (accessed on March 20, 2024). This publicly accessible database offers a free, user-friendly platform for real-time analysis of Klebsiella genomes, supporting surveillance efforts at local, national, and global scales. The MLST type of the retrieved genomes were reconfirmed using PubMLST. QUAST was used to determine the assembly characteristics including number of contigs, genome length, GC content, N50, L50 and others. All the genomes were scanned for acquired AMR genes, plasmid replicons and virulence factors against ResFinder, PlasmidFinder and VFDB database, respectively in ABRicate.42 The mobile genetic elements (MGEs) carrying AMR and virulence genes were detected using VRprofile2 (https://tool2-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/VRprofile/).43.

Pan-genome and phylogenetic analysis of K. Pneumoniae ST48

We have conducted two pan-genome analyses of local and global strains of K. pneumoniae ST48 using Roary (95 % minimum blastP identity) (https://sanger-pathogens.github.io/Roary/)44 to observe genetic diversity. The first analysis focused exclusively on 51 strains of K. pneumoniae ST48 isolated from Bangladesh, whereas, the second analysis expanded the scope to include a total of 397 genomes of K. pneumoniae ST48 (including 51 Bangladeshi strains) reported from various countries around the world. This global dataset of K. pneumoniae ST48 was also retrieved from PathogenWatch (accessed on March 20, 2024) (Supplementary file 1). The gene presence-absence data from pan-genome analysis was used to perform a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using a software package named PAST (PAleontological STatistics).

For phylogenetic analysis, the core genome alignment file of global ST48 genomes, generated by Roary, was processed with SNP-sites (Galaxy Version 2.5.1)45 to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was then constructed using RAxML (v8.0.0)46 with the GTRGAMMA substitution model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated with metadata using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/)47 was used to visualize and add metadata to the phylogenetic tree.

Results and Discussion

The strain S3C was multidrug-resistant and moderate biofilm former

The strain S3C formed pink, large, mucoid colonies on MacConkey agar (Supplementary file 2). Based on its biochemical characteristics—negative reactions for indole, methyl red, oxidase, urease, and motility tests, and positive reactions for catalase, Voges-Proskauer, and citrate utilization—S3C was presumptively identified as Klebsiella pneumoniae. In antibiotic susceptibility testing, S3C exhibited resistance to 17 out of 19 tested antibiotics, with susceptibility only to nitrofurantoin and tigecycline (Supplementary file 3). The Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index of the isolate was calculated to be 0.895, indicating a high level of resistance. These findings align with previous studies reporting multiple antibiotic resistance in clinical K. pneumoniae isolates in Bangladesh.18, 19, 48 Furthermore, based on its resistance to three or more classes of antibiotics, S3C is classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR).

K. pneumoniae is known for its ability to form biofilms on both abiotic surfaces, such as medical devices and catheters, and host tissues, including the mucosa of the respiratory, urinary, and gastrointestinal tracts.49 In this study, we assessed the adherence ability of strain S3C to abiotic surfaces using a microtiter plate biofilm formation assay. The absorbance of S3C at 492 nm (OD = 0.326) was approximately four times higher than that of the negative control (ODC = 0.085). Based on the criteria proposed by Stepanović et al., the strain was classified as a moderate biofilm former.

Whole genome sequencing revealed that S3C belongs to the ST48 clonal type

The genomic characteristics of the resistome, virulome, and mobilome of strain S3C were investigated through whole genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation. The draft genome was approximately 5.4 Mbp in length, distributed across 199 contigs (≥200 bp), with a GC content of 57.3 % (Accession no. JAYWSE000000000; BioSample: SAMN39427605). Whole genome identification via TYGS confirmed the isolate as Klebsiella pneumoniae, which was further supported by KmerFinder. Recently, the calculation of Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) by comparing two prokaryotic genomes has emerged as powerful tool for the classification and taxonomic identification of bacterial strains.50 The S3C genome sequence demonstrated a 99.4 % ANI with the reference genome (NC_009648.1), exceeding the 96 % threshold commonly used for species delineation, thereby confirming its species-level identification (Fig. 1A).

Comprehensive genomic analysis of K. pneumoniae S3C (A) circular genome map of with annotation; (B) distribution of coding sequences in different subsystems; and (C) minimum spanning tree based on whole-genome MLST.

Functional annotation of the assembled genome using PROKKA identified 5,078 coding sequences, encompassing genes involved in metabolism, protein processing, stress response, regulation, energy generation, membrane transport, and other cellular processes (Fig. 1B and 1C). In silico MLST typing predicted that S3C belong to ST48 clonal type (Pasteur Scheme) (Table 1). ST48 strains of K. pneumoniae have been reported as potential international high-risk clones due to their multidrug resistance, virulence, biofilm-forming ability, global dissemination, and association with healthcare-associated infections.16, 51 A cgMLST based minimum spanning tree showed the genetic relatedness of S3C to ST48 strains from Thailand, India, United Kingdom and other regions (Fig. 1D). S3C was linked to a strain from the United Kingdom, which formed clusters with strain from India and Thailand. The positioning of S3C on a long branch indicates considerable allelic divergence from other strains. We also observed close phylogenetic clustering of strains from Pakistan and Nigeria, as evidenced by the short allelic distances among them in the tree.

Resistome analysis revealed 13 acquired antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes conferring resistance to multiple antibiotic classes (Supplementary file 4). These included: sul1, sul2 (sulfonamide resistance), rmtC (aminoglycoside resistance), blaNDM-1, blaTEM-1, blaSHV-1 (carbapenem resistance), ble-MBL (metallo-β-lactamase resistance), oqxB10, oqxA6 (quinolone resistance), qnrS1 (fluoroquinolone resistance), aph(6)-Id, aph(3′')-Ib (aminoglycoside resistance), and fosA (fosfomycin resistance). These genes exert resistance to antibiotics by diverse mechanisms including antibiotic inactivation, target site modification, efflux mediated extrusion of antibiotics and others. In addition, several chromosomally encoded antibiotic efflux pump-associated genes were detected in the S3C genome, including kpnF, kpnE, adeF, emrR, baeR, rsmA, leuO, msbA and lptD. These genes are associated with multidrug efflux systems such as ATP-binding cassette (ABC), Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS), Resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND), that contribute to instrinsic resistance and reduced susceptibility to cephaosporins, macrolides, fluoroquinolones and others. Overall, the AMR genotypes were consistent with the phenotypic antibiotic resistance profiles, indicating strong genotype–phenotype concordance. A similar pattern was observed in the K. pneumoniae strain ARM01, which also carried 13 acquired AMR genes.52.

Screening against the VFDB database identified 76 virulence-associated genes in the S3C genome, contributing to its pathogenic potential. These included genes related to siderophore production, Type VI secretion systems, toxin-like proteins, extracellular and capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis, fimbrial synthesis, and adhesion (Supplementary file 5). PathogenFinder predicted S3C to be pathogenic to humans with an 89.2 % probability. Analysis of mobile genetic elements revealed the presence of four plasmid replicons (IncFIB(K), IncFIB(pB171), Col440I, IncFII(pKP91)) and 20 insertion sequences (IS elements). The S3C genome also harbored seven prophage sequences, none of which carried antimicrobial resistance or virulence genes (Supplementary file 6).

Bangladeshi populations of ST48 are multidrug-resistant and harbor diverse mobile genetic elements

We conducted a comparative genomic analysis of 51 Klebsiella pneumoniae ST48 strains isolated in Bangladesh to understand the genetic basis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and virulence. The average resistome size among these isolates was approximately 34.31 genes, ranging from 27 to 43. On average, each genome harbored 15.80 acquired AMR genes, with a maximum of 24 and a minimum of 9. All ST48 strains possessed the intrinsic resistance genes oqxA, oqxB, blaSHV, and fosA. The multidrug resistance genes oqxA and oqxB confer resistance to multiple antibiotics and are increasingly detected in K. pneumoniae as part of the plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) mechanisms.53 These intrinsic genes, often chromosomally encoded, contribute to resistance against β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, and fosfomycin.54.

Among extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), blaCTX-M−15 was detected in 96.08 % of the genomes, highlighting its widespread presence in Bangladeshi ST48 populations. This gene, the most prevalent CTX-M−type ESBL in Enterobacteriaceae, has also been widely reported in other K. pneumoniae isolates.55, 56, 57 Other frequently identified AMR genes included tet(A) (92.15 %), blaTEM-1B (90.19 %), sul1 (84.13 %), aac(3)-IIa (82.35 %), mph(A) (74.5 %), and aph(6)-Id (72.54 %) (Fig. 2A). Notably, three genomes carried at least one gene from three different β-lactamase classes, while 49 genomes carried genes from two different classes, further underscoring the multidrug-resistant (MDR) profile of these strains. Plasmid replicon typing revealed high frequencies of IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3 (84.31 %), IncFII_1_pKP91 (82.35 %), ColRNAI (80.39 %), and IncFII_1 (72.54 %) (Fig. 2B).

Heatmap distribution of genomic elements related to pathogenicity and resistance in Bangladeshi strains of K. pneumoniae ST48 (A) heatmap of acquired antibiotic resistance genes; (B) heatmap of plasmid replicons; and (C) heatmap of virulence genes distribution.

The detected virulence genes involved in biofilm formation, siderophore production, fimbriae biosynthesis, and capsule formation. All genomes carried genes for the Type VI secretion system (T6SS), enterobactin biosynthesis (entABCEFS, fepABCDG), type 3 fimbriae (mrkABCDFHIJ), type 1 fimbriae (fimABCEFGHIK), and biofilm-associated genes (wbbM, wbbO) (Fig. 2C). The T6SS, comprising membrane-associated components (tssL, tssM), substrate-associated proteins (tssF, tssG, tssK), and tail-associated proteins (tssA, tssB, tssC, tssD), contributes to both interbacterial competition and host pathogenesis.58 Yersiniabactin siderophore system genes (ybtAEPQ, irp1, irp2) were present in 92.15 % of genomes, consistent with its established role in respiratory tract infections through evasion of lipocalin-2.59 The rmpA2 gene, linked to hypermucoidy and increased capsular polysaccharide production, was found in 29.41 % of the genomes, suggesting the emergence of hypervirulent traits in a subset of the population.60.

Mobilome analysis using VRprofile 2 identified an average of 31.82 MGEs per genome, with a range of 20 to 41 elements (Fig. 3). PlasmidFinder revealed an average of 5.35 plasmid replicons per genome (range: 0–7), while genomic islands and prophages averaged 6.16 and 4.65 per genome, respectively. Importantly, we identified an average of 3.65 MGEs per genome carrying AMR genes (range: 1–7), whereas MGEs carrying virulence genes were less common, averaging 0.98 per genome. Notably, aac(3)-IIa was associated with ISKpn11 in 12 genomes. The blaCTX-M−15 gene was found in association with TnpA_Tn3 and ISEcp1 in 18 and 9 genomes, respectively (Fig. 4A), corroborating earlier findings.39 In three genomes, oqxA/oqxB were located within prophage regions. Other AMR genes such as blaTEM, tet(A), mph(A), and qnrS1 also co-occurred with various MGEs in both chromosomal and plasmid contexts. The presence of multiple AMR genes on single plasmids—conferring resistance to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and other antibiotics—poses a significant clinical challenge.61.

Detailed analysis of the genetic environments of MDR plasmids revealed complex arrangements and the co-localization of multiple clinically relevant AMR genes. For example, one plasmid harbored blaTEM-1, aph(6)-Id, APH(3″)-Ib, and sul2, flanked by Tn3 family transposases (Tn2, tnpR), IS110 (IS5075), and ISPsa38. Another plasmid carried ermE, blaVEB-5, sul1, aacA4, tet(C), armA, msrE, and mphE, co-occurring with TnAs2, IS26, and ISKpn21, indicating a highly recombinogenic region (Fig. 4B). Additionally, blaTEM-1, aac(3)-IId, and tet(D) were flanked by Tn2, tnpR, tmrB, and ISCR1, demonstrating the extensive integration of resistance genes via transposases and insertion sequences.

In terms of virulence genes, 22 genomes carried VF0560 (capsule-related) and 14 carried VF0561 (LPS-related) genes within genomic islands. The yersiniabactin-associated gene VF0136 was found within Integrative Conjugative Elements (ICEs) in 12 genomes. Collectively, our findings underscore the significant role of MGEs in shaping the resistome and virulome of K. pneumoniae ST48 strains circulating in Bangladesh and suggest that the presence and accumulation of certain virulence factors may contribute to the emergence of highly pathogenic variants within this lineage.

Core and accessory gene distribution indicate high genetic diversity and open pangenome in Bangladeshi K. Pneumoniae ST48 strains

Pan-genome analysis serves as a crucial tool for exploring genetic diversity within bacterial populations.62 In our study, we identified a total of 8,030 genes across all K. pneumoniae ST48 strains. Among these, 4,258 genes were classified as core genes, constituting 53.03 % of the total gene set (Fig. 5A). This substantial proportion suggests that these genes are highly conserved and likely essential for basic cellular functions and survival in Bangladeshi ST48 strains. The accessory genome consisted of 260 soft core genes, 1,379 shell genes, and 2,133 cloud genes, collectively accounting for 46.97 % of the pan-genome. This distribution reflects varying degrees of genomic conservation and variability, underscoring the genetic diversity within the ST48 clonal type.

Pan-genome analysis of the local strains of K. pneumoniae ST48 (A) pan-genome matrix; (B) conserved versus total genes curve; and (C) New versus unique genes curve.

The observed core-to-accessory gene ratio aligns with previous studies. For instance, a study on the ST15 population of K. pneumoniae in China reported that core genes made up 51 % of the pan-genome.63 Another study analyzing 315 ST147 high-risk K. pneumoniae clones found that the pan-genome comprised 10,215 genes, with 45.8 % core and 52.2 % accessory genes.64 These comparisons suggest that the proportion of core and accessory genes in K. pneumoniae pan-genomes varies depending on factors such as clonal type, geographic origin, and the number of genomes analyzed. Nevertheless, core genes typically comprise around half of the pan-genome, indicating a relatively stable distribution.

Pan-genome analysis also offers insights into genome expansion by delineating the interplay between conserved core genes, variable accessory genes, and the emergence of novel genes across diverse strains, thus highlighting the genomic dynamics and evolutionary potential of microbial populations.65 In our analysis, the increasing total gene count with the inclusion of more genomes suggested an open pan-genome, indicating that ST48 clones are still acquiring new genes. As additional genomes were analyzed, the number of core genes stabilized, representing a conserved genetic backbone across all strains (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the number of new genes (genes observed for the first time as each genome is added) declined after the initial genomes were analyzed and gradually leveled off, indicating that most of these genes were discovered early in the process. Similarly, the number of unique genes (genes found exclusively in a single genome across the dataset) per genome was relatively stable throughout, suggesting a consistent distribution of unique genes among the strains (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these findings emphasize the dynamic nature of genome evolution in ST48, with ongoing gene acquisition contributing to its adaptive potential.

Pan-genome analysis reveals high genomic plasticity in K. Pneumoniae ST48 worldwide

The pan-genome analysis of 397 K. pneumoniae ST48 strains worldwide reveals a dynamic and diverse genomic landscape. We identified a total of 16,307 genes across all strains, underscoring the extensive genetic repertoire of this sequence type. In comparison, a separate study on ST11 K. pneumoniae reported 19,732 genes across 364 genomes,66 highlighting substantial pan-genome size variation among different sequence types. This variability suggests distinct gene pools and genomic adaptations that are potentially unique to each ST type.

The core genome of the ST48 strains comprised 4,032 genes, representing approximately 24.7 % of the total gene pool, while the accessory genome—which includes soft core, shell, and cloud genes—constituted the majority (75.3 %) (Fig. 6A). The large number of cloud genes suggests extensive strain-specific adaptations and possible recent horizontal gene transfer events, underscoring the genomic plasticity of K. pneumoniae ST48. Previous studies have also emphasized the importance of the accessory genome in harboring antimicrobial resistance genes, virulence factors, plasmids, and other mobile genetic elements.21, 67, 68 .

Pan-genome analysis of the global strains of K. pneumoniae ST48 (A) distribution of the core and accessory genes; (B) conserved versus total genes curve; (C) new versus unique genes curve; and (D) Principal Coordinate Analysis plot.

Similar to the findings from the local strains, the global ST48 pan-genome displayed characteristics of an open pan-genome. The total gene accumulation curve showed a steep initial rise followed by a gradual increase, indicating that new genes continue to be discovered as more genomes are analyzed (Fig. 6B). On the other hand, the number of core genes rapidly plateaued around 4,000, supporting our core genome estimation. The analysis of new versus unique genes reinforced this pattern. Notably, the number of unique genes increased more gradually, suggesting ongoing gene acquisition—likely representing rare accessory genes or horizontally transferred elements (Fig. 6C). Overall, our findings indicate that K. pneumoniae ST48 maintains a relatively stable core genome while exhibiting extensive diversity in its accessory genome. This genomic structure supports the preservation of essential functions alongside a high degree of flexibility.

The PCoA (Principal Coordinates Analysis) plot revealed a complex population structure, with partial clustering based on the continent of isolation (Fig. 6D). A considerable overlap among continental groups indicates potential intercontinental transmission or shared ancestry. The varying degrees of clustering and distribution also reflect differences in genetic diversity across regions. The distribution pattern observed underscored the variation in accessory genome content among K. pneumoniae ST48 strains, which likely drives the observed genetic diversity. Along with pan-genome, this analysis suggested the dynamic nature of K. pneumoniae ST48 population structure globally.

Phylogenetic analysis suggests global dissemination of K. Pneumoniae ST48 strains

A core genome SNP-based phylogenetic tree was constructed to investigate the evolutionary relationships among Klebsiella pneumoniae sequence type 48 (ST48) isolates (Fig. 7). The analysis revealed a wide distribution of isolates across more than 40 countries spanning multiple continents. A large proportion of isolates grouped into major clades, indicating potential clonal expansions or region-specific dissemination. Notably, isolates from geographically distant countries—including India, Thailand, the USA, the UK, Bangladesh, and South Africa—were found within the same clades, indicating evidence of global transmission of ST48. Similar patterns have been reported for K. pneumoniae ST16 strains, where the majority (90 %) of genomes clustered into a single clade, highlighting a comparable trend of global spread and clonal expansion.69 .

Phylogenetic analysis of 397 ST48 K. pneumoniae strains worldwide.

Analysis of the capsular (K) loci distribution showed that most isolates (73.92 %) belonged to the KL62 group, spanning different clades and geographic regions. The predominance of KL62 may suggest that this capsular type confers specific fitness advantages across diverse environments. Among the 50 isolates from Bangladesh, 44 belonged to this capsular type. KL124, although less prevalent, formed distinct subclades comprising isolates from the USA, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, Singapore, Bangladesh, and other countries. The tree reveals the population structure and global distribution of ST48 isolates, with marked clustering patterns that reflect both geographical and capsular (K locus) diversity.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings revealed a high prevalence of MDR genes and a rich repertoire of virulence factors among Bangladeshi strains. Extensive genetic diversity and plasticity was also indicated in the pan-genome analysis of local and global strains. Phylogenetic analysis suggested a global dissemination of ST48 strains, highlighting their adaptability and potential for widespread transmission. Despite these significant insights, the study is limited by its geographic focus and sample size. Future research should expand to sequencing-based surveillance globally to include a larger and more geographically diverse collection of ST48 strains to better understand the global distribution and evolutionary dynamics of this clonal type. Investigating the clinical outcomes associated with different resistance and virulence profiles would provide valuable insights into their impact on public health. The findings of the study provide essential backgrounds on the genomic context of AMR in K. pneumoniae ST48 and their transmission, and thus recommend to strengthen global surveillance measures to control further dissemination of this high-risk clonal type.
