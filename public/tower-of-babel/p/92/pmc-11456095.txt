Comparative study of oxidative stress in cancer patients occupationally exposed to the mixture of pesticides
Authors: Anupama Ojha, Shashank Shekhar, Poonam Gupta, Sonali Jaiswal, Sarad Kumar Mishra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020. Reviews indicated a positive relationship between exposure to pesticides and the development of cancers. In the present study, we have estimated the level of oxidative stress markers in serum samples of pesticide exposure and unexposed cancer patients as compared to normal control. We have found a significant decrease in peroxygenase (PON) and arylesterase (ARE) activity and substantial increases in homocysteine levels in both cancer groups. The level of heme biosynthesis rate-limiting enzymes delta-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) also significantly decreased compared to control. The statistical comparison between the cancer groups does not show significant changes. We concluded the involvement of oxidative stress in carcinogenesis in both cancer group patients. However, more study is needed to put homocysteine as a novel marker for a variety of diseases on a single platform.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020 [1]. It is a complex disease whose causes are multifactorial and generate global health problems [2]. Smoking, alcohol intake, obesity, unhealthy diet, insufficient physical activity, and certain infections are possible risk factors for cancer [3]. Many published systematic reviews indicated a positive relationship between exposure to pesticides and the development of cancers [2, 4–6]. However, research articles on correlation studies between occupational/environmental pesticide exposure and cancer incidents are lacking or have not reached any descriptive conclusion. Various pesticides are categorized as carcinogenic after in vitro and in vivo studies [7–10]. It is reported that pesticide exposure induces oxidative stress and may damage lipids, proteins, and DNA responsible for their carcinogenic potential. Oxidative stress can be measured in terms of enzyme activity or biochemical products formed in exposed individuals irrespective of their health status.

The increase in oxidative stress is a common cause of carcinogenesis and pesticide toxicity. Enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant mechanisms have been functioning extracellularly and intracellularly to reduce the effect of oxidative stress. The human serum peroxygenase (PON) and arylesterase (ARE) are lipophilic antioxidant esterase enzymes [11, 12]. The paraoxonase (PON) is an endogenous free radical scavenger that acts in conjunction with ARE to function as a single enzyme [13]. PON is a calcium-dependent esterase found exclusively extracellular over the high-density lipoproteins (HDL) and contributes to the detoxification of organophosphorus compounds by hydrolyzing it. The antioxidant properties of PON are implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases including atherosclerosis, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, diabetes mellitus, and cancer [14]. Although pesticides induced oxidative stresses are believed to be an important factor in carcinogenesis, there has been little investigation on changes in PON activity in pesticide exposed and unexposed cancer patients. The present study will measure the serum PON and ARE activity and their utility as a marker in the prediction of chemotherapy requirements in cancer patients [15].

Plasma homocysteine (Hcy), is a well-known independent risk factor for coronary heart disease and cancer [16, 17]. Hcy is a sulfur-containing nonproteinogenic amino acid that is associated with the metabolism of methionine and cysteine. Methionine is converted to Hcy via S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), releasing a methyl group that is used in numerous methylation reactions like DNA methylation and creatinine synthesis. Recent literature predicts the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are possible mediators of Hcy effects and their accumulation. Elevated Hcy (HHcy) may lead to the formation of Hcy thiolactone as a result of error-prone editing by the methionyl-tRNA synthetase or reduction in the concentration of nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability add-ons generation of oxidative stress. Hcy thiolactone can be detoxified by PON through hydrolysis and converted back to Hcy [18]. Hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) has been reported in various cancer patients either through mutations in genes encoding enzymes of Hcy biosynthesis and metabolism or deficiencies of vitamin cofactors including vitamin B12 and B6 [19]. The molecular mechanisms underlying HHcy-induced cancer are complex and multifactorial and need extensive research.

Recent studies indicated that delta-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase/porphobilinogen synthase/ ALA dehydratase/aminolevulinate dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) is sensitive to situations associated with oxidative stress [20]. An increased concentration of delta-aminolevulinic acid (δ-ALA) is thought to be related to cancer also [21]. δ-ALA-D is a sulfhydryl-containing enzyme that synthesizes porphobilinogen through the asymmetric condensation of two molecules of aminolevulinic acid in the presence of zinc and is later involved in the synthesis of heme and billins. δ-ALA-D enzyme inhibition can lead to the accumulation of its substrate, 5-aminolevulinate (ALA), in the blood, which increases the intensity of oxidative stress by generating carbon-centered reactive species or release of iron from ferritin protein [22].

Each individual gets exposed to a mixture of pollutants from the environment chronically. Several environmental pollutants have been reported to have carcinogenic potential. Most of the cancer occurrences in humans reported after middle age may be due to a reduction in antioxidant status with age or due to the accumulation of carcinogenic effects induced by environmental pollutants. Human studies can’t be performed in controlled conditions, we have used questionnaires to find the cancer patients chronically exposed to pesticides. The objective of the present research article is to study the potential involvement of pesticide exposure in cancer through a selection of those cancer patients who are involved regularly in agricultural activity and directly or indirectly get exposed to a mixture of pesticides chronically. Oxidative stress status was analyzed by measuring the antioxidant level of PON, and ARE enzyme activity. Hcy has been measured as another reported potential marker of oxidative stress. Rapid cell proliferation in carcinogenesis leads to a decrease in the level of heme, whose synthesis is regulated by the δ-ALA-D enzyme. Therefore we have also measured the activity of δ-ALA-D enzyme level in pesticide exposed and unexposed cancer patients as compared to the control to check the status of heme in cancer patients. The present study will also explore the potential relationship of pesticide exposure with cancer incidence.

Material and methods

Patient’s recruitment

The present research work was a continuation of my previous work Ojha et al. [23]. Therefore, the patient recruitment detail was the same as in the previous paper. In the present study, participants were divided into 3 groups. Group I consist of 51 normal control individuals with no history of previous disease, or drug, or alcohol consumption. Group II, consists of 61 histopathologically confirmed cancer patients (who were not directly exposed to pesticides) and, group III consists of 56 histopathologically confirmed cancer patients (who were directly exposed to pesticides) involved in agricultural activities. Hanuman Prasad Poddar Cancer Hospital and Research Centre, Gorakhpur, was the center for blood collection after written consent from each patient. Sample collection was done during the period of 8 January 2021 to 14 February 2021. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital and before blood collection informed written consent was obtained from each patient. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Socio-demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, height, weight, and, diet pattern was also collected using a standard questionnaire. The extent of pesticide exposure was calculated by using the equation mentioned below No. of treatments per year X total year of pesticide application X cultivation area (ha.). Cancer patients, who have not worked within the agriculture sector as their main or secondary occupation and lived in urban and suburban regions, were classified in the without exposure group.

Exclusion criteria for the present study were as follows: taking systemic chemotherapy, surgical intervention before in any other hospital, diabetes mellitus, kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and rheumatological diseases.

Blood collection

After a 12-h overnight fasting period, venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein before initiation of chemo/radiotherapy and/or surgical resection of the tumor. The venous blood was collected through the needle and transferred into a plain yellow and EDTA-containing vial (red cap) immediately and stored on the ice at 4 °C. The plasma and serum were separated by centrifugation at 1300 × g for 20 min at room temperature and were transferred to sterile test tubes and re-centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at room temperature to remove residual cellular components. The plasma and serum were transferred to cryo-tubes and stored at − 20 °C until the analysis of paraoxonase (PON), arylesterase (ARE), homocysteine, and δ-Aminolevulinate dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) activities.

Assay of PON activity

Serum PON activity was measured by the spectrophotometric method using p-nitrophenyl acetate as a substrate, where paraoxonase catalyzes the cleavage of p-nitrophenyl acetate resulting in the formation of phenol [24]. The assay mixture contains 2.0 mM paraoxon, 2 mM CaCl2, and 20 µL of plasma in 100 mM tris-HCI buffer (pH 8.0) along with blank (without plasma) was run simultaneously to correct for spontaneous substrate breakdown. The rate of paraoxon hydrolysis was measured by the increased absorbance at 412 nm at 25 °C. The enzyme activity was calculated from E412 of p-nitrophenol (18.290 per M/cm) and was expressed as U/mL.

Assay of ARE activity

Arylesterase enzyme activity was measured at 270 nm spectrophotometry, by determining phenylacetate hydrolysis [24]. Tris–HCL tamponade (3 mL) added 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 milieu. Arylesterase concentration was calculated using 1310 M−1 cm−1 (pH: 8) molar extinction coefficient. The results were expressed as U/mL.

Assay for homocysteine (Hcy) estimation

Homocysteine estimation was done in a fasting serum sample with the help of Elbascience Biochemical estimation kit based on oxidation–reduction reaction. Oxidized homocysteine is reduced to free homocysteine by triethyl phosphine (TCEP), and the free homocysteine reacts with the substrate to generate adenosine. The generated adenosine is immediately dehydrogenated into inosine and ammonia. Ammonia further reacts with NADH under the catalysis of glutamate dehydrogenase to convert NADH to NAD + . The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm caused by the decline of NADH is proportional to the concentration of homocysteine in the sample. The value of homocysteine was expressed in µmol/L.

Assay for δ-Aminolevulinate dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) activity

δ-Aminolevulinate dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) activity was assayed in whole blood by measuring the rate of porphobilinogen (PBG) formation in 1 h at 37 °C [25]. The enzyme reaction was initiated after 10 min of pre-incubation of blood with 1 mM ZnCl2. The reaction was started by adding δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) to a final concentration of 4 mM in a phosphate-buffered solution, and incubation was carried out for 1 h at 37 °C and the reaction product was measured at 555 nm. The activity of δ-ALA-D was expressed as nmol PBG/mL/h.

Statistical analysis

Results were expressed as mean ± S.E. of all the observations. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software, Version 11.5 (SPSS inc, version 11.5, Chicago, IL, USA). All the statistical analyses were performed using a one-way analysis of variance with post hoc Bonferroni's multiple comparison test applied across the control and cancer groups. Significance was based on p-value < 0.05. The correlation between different variables was calculated using Pearson's linear correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at a p-value ≤ 0.5.

The Basal Metabolic Index of studied participants varies between 20 and 21. ARE activity

In both pesticide-exposed and an unexposed group of cancer patients, a significant decrease in the ARE activity was found as compared to the control. Cancer patients who were not exposed to pesticides had a 62.54% significant decrease in the ARE activity as compared to control while cancer patients who were exposed to pesticides showed a 69.23% significant decrease in the ARE activity as compared to control. The decrease in ARE activity was found to be significant when we compared it between cancer groups (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Level of homocysteine (Hcy)

In the present study, we have found an increase in the Hcy level in both pesticide-exposed and unexposed groups of cancer patients as compared to the control. Cancer patients who were not exposed to pesticides have found a 442.69% significant increase in the Hcy level as compared to control while cancer patients who were exposed to pesticides showed a 478.30% significant increase in the Hcy as compared to control. The increase in Hcy level was found to be not significant when we compared it between cancer groups (Table 1, Fig. 2).

δ-Aminolevulinate dehydratase (δ-ALA-D) activity

In the present study, we have found a slight decrease in the δ-ALA-D activity in both pesticide-exposed and unexposed groups of cancer patients as compared to control. Cancer patients who were not exposed to pesticides had a 9% significant decrease in the δ-ALA-D activity as compared to control while cancer patients who were exposed to pesticides showed a 7.18% significant decrease in the δ-ALA-D activity as compared to control. The decrease in δ-ALA-D activity was found to be not significant when we compared it between cancer groups (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Discussion

In the present study, most of the patients have gallbladder, breast, and uterine cancer. This can be the reason that the present study has more female patients as compared to males. It has been proposed by various studies that intake of vegetarian diets, may be related to a lower cancer risk. In a study, it has been found that low-meat eaters have a lower risk of colorectal cancer [26]. A study among Taiwanese showed that intake of a vegetarian diet or a shift from a non-vegetarian to a vegetarian diet may oppose diabetes [27]. Oxidative stress plays a vital role in the pathogenesis of several human diseases including cancer. To overcome the endogenously produced free radical/oxidative stress biological system is equipped with various endogenous antioxidant enzymes. It is not yet well established whether oxidative stress observed in tumor cells results in increased production of oxidants or failures in defense mechanisms. It is reported that tumor cells are often hypoxic, and this oxygen deprivation may alter the regulation of their enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses.

PON and ARE are multifunctional antioxidant enzymes, closely bound with high-density lipoproteins (HDL) and involved in the removal of various toxic pollutants including pesticides [28, 29]. The status of oxidative stress inside the cell influences the levels and activities of PON and ARE in different types of cancers. PONs are a significant biomarker for complications caused by different toxic pollutants in humans and also help in the prediction of the intensity of exposure.

At present, ARE activity is considered to be one of the PON functions and the most reliable indicator of antioxidant/anti-inflammatory efficacy. A decrease in PON activity leads to a decrease in its antioxidant properties and increases in oxidative stress. The decrease in PON and ARE activities was reported in cancer patients but the mechanism involved in it is still not clear. It may be possible that the involvement of PON and ARE in the removal of induced oxidative stress generated in cancerous cells leads to a decrease in their level. In the present study serum, PON ARE activities were significantly decreased in both groups of cancer patients as compared to normal. However, the level of PON and ARE non-significantly decreased in pesticide-exposed cancer group as compared to the unexposed cancer group. It can be explained through the involvement of PON and ARE in the detoxification of pesticides, which leads to more decrease in their activity. Our results match the study of Malik et al. 2014 in which they found the decreased activity of PON and ARE in serum samples of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [30]. A lower activity of serum PON has been reported in cancer patients [31]. Exposures of individuals with various environmental pollutants were found to be associated with a varied effect on PON activity irrespective of linear dose response [32]. Previous studies have shown that serum levels of PON were lower in patients with colorectal, pancreatic, or ovarian cancer than in healthy controls [33, 34].

The literature review suggests that Hcy is an important indicator of overall health status. High methionine is required in growing cancerous cells for increased processes of protein synthesis and transmethylation. Normal cells can obtain the required methionine consumption from Hcy remethylation. Tumor cells of the lung, kidney, breast, colon, and bladder cannot convert Hcy to methionine and therefore are methionine dependent. S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) and S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH), releases a methyl group and convert methionine to homocysteine. Released methyl group is used in different methylation reactions. Methionine-dependent cells have a lower SAM/SAH (S-adenosylmethionine/S-adenosine homocysteine) ratio compared with methionine-independent cells. Through remethylation Hcy can converted to methionine via 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase (MTR) or betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT). Decrease in concentration of intracellular SAM levels can change the cytosine methylation in CpG islands of DNA. It results in the suppression of tumor suppressor genes, and stimulation of protooncogenes that leads to induction of carcinogenesis. Hcy is also irreversibly metabolized by cystathion β-synthase enzyme (CBS) to cystathionine. Through the trans-sulfuration pathway cystathione successively converted to cysteine. Liver is the main site for Hcy metabolism and it removed from the body in the form of cystathionine through CBS activity.

HHcy was measured to be a self-regulating risk factor for cerebro and cardiovascular diseases [35]. Increase in level of Hcy and unaffected plasma Cys levels were reported in several studies done on patients with cancer [36, 37]. In the present study, we have found HHcy in both groups of cancer patients which may be due to a decrease in the level of PON and ARE activity that leads to hyper homocysteinemia. HHcy might lead to the buildup of SAH, which competitively inhibits the activity of methyltransferases leading to DNA hypomethylation. It results in epigenetic modification in respective cells. Involvement of HHcy has also been reported in the genesis of cancer. A fault in methionine synthesis causes accumulation of Hcy, a key for a methionine-dependent malignant phenotype [38]. A meta-analysis discovered an enlarged overall risk of cancer with the connotation of raised circulating Hcy levels [39]. In patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a higher Hcy plasma level has been reported [39, 40]. How elevated Hcy levels transforms normal cell to malignant are still an area of research by the biologist. Studies proposed lipid metabolism-associated linkage of HHcy in HCC [41]. Recent findings suggest that organochlorine pesticides may disturb the folate-dependent homocysteine metabolism pathway leading to HHcy [42].

For normal and cancerous cell survival heme is a necessary compound but its association with carcinogenicity and toxicity to cells is also well established [43]. Involvement of heme in carcinogenesis is via promotion of inflammation, modulation of immune cell function, through gut dysbiosis, promotion of cytotoxicity, hindering tumor suppressive potential of the P53 gene, and reactive oxygen species generation, and modulating the Nfr2 /HO-1 axis [43]. The role of heme in cancer has been studied focusing on the effects mediated by it. δ -ALA-D involved in the synthesis of heme, is connected with induction of oxidative stress. Inhibition of the activity of δ -ALA-D of may lead to excessive δ -ALA increase in blood, that may exaggereate the oxidative stress by producing reactive species [20, 44].

The level of δ-ALA-D is markedly reduced in patients with malignant lymphoproliferative disorders [45]. The decreased activity of δ -ALA-D has already been reported in women with cervical cancer [46]. In the present study, we have found a decrease in the level of δ -ALA-D in both groups of cancer as compared to control. Pesticide exposure is also a potent source of δ -ALA-D inhibition because of several metals present in them. Arsenic (As), cobalt (Co), chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), and lead (Pb) are the important metals used in pesticides. Available literature well reports the inhibition of δ -ALA-D activity through lead, and cadmium [47].

Conclusion

The present study supports the involvement of oxidative stress in the generation of cancer. Although we have not found significant changes in the oxidative stress marker enzymes in pesticides exposed and unexposed cancer patients, it does not deny their involvement in carcinogenesis because changes were observed. When we compared the studied parameters between the cancer group, no significant changes were observed except ARE. The unknowingness of pesticide exposed cancer patients to classify the type of pesticides they are using in agricultural activities proved a major hindrance to concluding the pathway involved in oxidative stress-mediated carcinogenesis. To date, the effects of Hcy on the growth and proliferation of tumor cells remain poorly understood. The present study shows HHcy in both groups of cancer patients as compared to control supporting their future use as marker enzymes. However, more and more study on the effects of Hcy on the growth and proliferation of cancer cells is needed to yield novel, promising strategies to curb cancer.
