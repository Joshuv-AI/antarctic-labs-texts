ECMO Support in Paraneoplastic Pemphigus With Respiratory Failure: A Case Report With Immune Cell Monitoring
Authors: Lu Li, Danxia Chen, Linzi Jin, Qinqin Shu, Juan He, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParaneoplastic pemphigus (PNP) is a life‐threatening autoimmune blistering disease with a mortality rate of 70%–90%, driven largely by respiratory complications such as bronchiolitis obliterans. Managing respiratory failure in these patients remains exceptionally difficult. We report a 33‐year‐old male with follicular dendritic cell sarcoma who developed severe PNP complicated by type II respiratory failure and obstructive bronchiolitis requiring veno‐venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support. The patient's PNP skin lesions improved during a treatment course that included ECMO support, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), immunosuppressants, hemodialysis, and plasma exchange. Serial immune cell subset monitoring showed a rising CD4/CD8 ratio (+94.8%) and Helper T cells (+33.8%), alongside declining NK cells (−48.2%), T lymphocytes (−17.7%), and Suppressor T cells (−31.6%). This case illustrates successful short‐term management of severe PNP with respiratory failure through multidisciplinary care, including ECMO. Because multiple interventions were administered concurrently, improvement cannot be attributed to any single therapy. Serial immune‐cell monitoring may help track treatment response in this setting.

Key Clinical MessageVeno‐venous ECMO can bridge life‐threatening respiratory failure in paraneoplastic pemphigus while immunomodulatory therapies take effect. Timely ECMO combined with multidisciplinary supportive care—contextualized by serial immune‐cell monitoring—may enable recovery when bronchiolitis‐driven hypercapnia is refractory to conventional ventilation.

Key Clinical Message

Introduction

Paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP), also termed paraneoplastic autoimmune multiorgan syndrome (PAMS), is an autoimmune blistering disorder first described by Anhalt and colleagues in 1990. It is set apart from other forms of pemphigus by painful mucosal erosions, polymorphic skin lesions, and a propensity for multiorgan involvement. Reported mortality ranges from 70% to 90%, with respiratory failure as the primary cause of death [1].

The immunopathology of PNP involves both humoral and cellular arms. Circulating autoantibodies target multiple plakin‐family proteins—desmoplakin, envoplakin, periplakin, BP230—as well as desmogleins 1 and 3 and plectin. This broad autoantibody repertoire distinguishes PNP from pemphigus vulgaris and accounts for the characteristic intercellular IgG and complement C3 deposition along the basement membrane zone seen on direct immunofluorescence. CD8+ T lymphocytes and natural killer cells further contribute to tissue damage through cell‐mediated cytotoxicity, producing the lichenoid interface dermatitis pattern frequently observed on skin biopsy [2].

Lymphoproliferative disorders are the most common associated malignancies. They include Castleman disease, non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, thymoma, and follicular dendritic cell sarcoma. Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, as encountered in our patient, can arise de novo or within Castleman disease and is increasingly recognized as a trigger of PNP [3, 4].

Oral lesions are often the earliest clinical manifestation, frequently preceding discovery of the underlying malignancy. A recent meta‐analysis by Messina et al. found that erosion was the predominant oral lesion type (83.3% of cases) and that overall death prevalence in paraneoplastic syndromes reached 50.9% [5]. These oral lesions are notoriously refractory to therapy, causing pain, dysphagia, and nutritional decline that compound the clinical challenge.

The most feared complication of PNP is bronchiolitis obliterans (BO)—a progressive, often irreversible small‐airway disease resulting from autoimmune destruction of the bronchiolar epithelium. BO is frequently the proximate cause of death: Lymphocytic infiltration and fibrotic obliteration of small airways produce relentless airflow obstruction that responds poorly to immunosuppression. When BO advances to end‐stage respiratory failure, bilateral lung transplantation may be the only remaining option. Conventional therapies—systemic corticosteroids, rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors, plasmapheresis—have variable efficacy, and no standardized protocol exists [6].

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is well established as rescue therapy for acute respiratory failure refractory to conventional ventilation, yet its application in autoimmune‐mediated respiratory failure remains largely unexplored [7]. In PNP complicated by BO, ECMO could serve as a bridge—sustaining gas exchange while immunomodulatory therapies take effect. Here we present a case of severe PNP associated with follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, in which veno‐venous ECMO was used alongside IVIG, cyclosporine, plasma exchange, and hemodialysis, with favorable short‐term outcomes.

Case Presentation

Clinical History and Initial Presentation

A 33‐year‐old male had been followed for four years for a mediastinal mass initially interpreted as a hemangioma. The mass remained stable on serial imaging, and no intervention was pursued. In early 2023, he began developing recurrent oral ulcers—first on the buccal mucosa, then extending to the tongue and oropharynx—that failed to respond to topical corticosteroids and antifungals. Over the following months, the ulcers deepened, causing significant odynophagia and weight loss.

Disease Progression and Surgical Intervention

By February 2024, the patient had developed widespread polymorphic skin lesions and progressive dyspnea. On Day −34 (March 2024), he required emergent intubation for worsening respiratory distress. A tracheostomy was performed on Day −31 to facilitate prolonged ventilation. At that point, the working diagnoses included pemphigus vulgaris and severe myasthenia gravis, and the clinical team proceeded with surgical resection of the mediastinal mass on Day −13, suspecting a thymoma‐related paraneoplastic syndrome.

The resected mass measured 8 × 6 × 4 cm and showed spindle‐shaped cells with mild nuclear atypia (Figure 1). Immunohistochemistry was positive for Vimentin, focally positive for CD34, and positive for BCL‐2 and CD99, with a Ki‐67 index of 10%—consistent with follicular dendritic cell sarcoma rather than thymoma. Concurrent skin biopsy demonstrated intraepidermal vesicle formation with acantholysis, and direct immunofluorescence revealed intercellular IgG and C3 in a “chicken wire” pattern, confirming the diagnosis of paraneoplastic pemphigus [8].

Post‐Operative Deterioration and ECMO Initiation

Tumor resection did not arrest the respiratory decline. By Day −5, the patient had developed rapidly worsening hypercapnia—PaCO2 exceeded 127 mmHg despite optimized ventilator settings and maximal bronchodilator therapy—raising concern for fulminant bronchiolitis obliterans. Sputum cultures grew pan‐drug‐resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae , adding hospital‐acquired pneumonia to an already critical picture. Conventional management had been exhausted. On Day 1, the patient was transferred to the Emergency Intensive Care Unit, where the decision was made to initiate veno‐venous ECMO. At transfer, the problem list included paraneoplastic pemphigus, severe myasthenia gravis, pan‐drug‐resistant hospital‐acquired pneumonia, acute type II respiratory failure, and post‐operative status following mediastinal mass resection.

ECMO Course and Treatment Response

Veno‐venous ECMO was cannulated via the femoral and jugular veins on Day 1, with heparin anticoagulation targeting an APTT of 40–60 s. Initial treatment included nebulized bronchodilators and IVIG at 5 g/day. Over Days 2–3, ECMO ran at a flow rate of 4.5 L/min with a gas flow of 7 L/min alongside continued mechanical ventilation; IVIG was escalated to 25 g/day given the severity of the autoimmune process. Laboratory results during this period were concerning: Leukocytosis (19.59 × 109/L), severe anemia (hemoglobin 56 g/L) requiring transfusion, and sharply elevated inflammatory markers—reflecting the dual burden of active autoimmune disease and superimposed infection.

Between Days 4 and 7, a pleural effusion required chest‐tube drainage, and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed multiple esophageal erosions attributable to mucosal PNP involvement. By Day 8, the clinical trajectory had shifted: Consciousness improved, oxygenation was adequate without CO2 retention, and ECMO was successfully withdrawn after seven days of support. Mucocutaneous PNP lesions also showed visible improvement during this period (Figure 2). The gains proved fragile: On Days 9–11, the patient developed recurrent bronchial obstruction, necessitating hemodialysis and therapeutic plasma exchange. Cyclosporine was added for longer‐term immunosuppression. After stabilization, the patient was transferred to a higher‐level facility with a satisfactory clinical status.

Immune Cell Subset Monitoring

Serial immune cell subsets were measured from Day 2 through Day 6. Values were normalized to the Day 2 baseline (0%) and expressed as relative percentage change (Figure 3). Over this interval, the CD4/CD8 ratio rose by 94.8%, and helper T cells increased by 33.8%, suggesting a shift toward a more regulated immune profile. NK cells fell by 48.2%, total T lymphocytes by 17.7%, and suppressor T cells (CD8+) by 31.6%. These changes tracked alongside clinical improvement, but because IVIG, cyclosporine, plasma exchange, and hemodialysis were all running concurrently, no single intervention can be credited.

Discussion

This case shows that a young patient with PNP‐associated respiratory failure—a scenario that carries near‐uniform lethality—can survive the acute crisis when aggressive, multimodal supportive care is deployed early. The patient received ECMO, IVIG, cyclosporine, plasma exchange, and hemodialysis simultaneously; short‐term improvement followed, but the contribution of each modality cannot be disentangled. This limitation is inherent to case reports of complex multisystem diseases and does not diminish the practical message: Rapid escalation of support can buy time in a condition where delay is often fatal.

The immune cell changes we observed—a rising CD4/CD8 ratio, expanding helper T cells, declining NK cells, and CD8+ suppressor T cells—are consistent with a shift from effector‐predominant autoimmunity toward a more regulated state. Each concurrent therapy could independently explain these trends. IVIG modulates T cell subsets, inhibits complement, and neutralizes pathogenic autoantibodies [9]. Cyclosporine directly suppresses calcineurin‐dependent T cell activation. Plasma exchange removes circulating autoantibodies and immune complexes [10], and hemodialysis circuits can sequester leukocytes through extracorporeal contact activation. The underlying disease course and post‐surgical recovery add further confounders. We present these immunological data as descriptive observations rather than mechanistic evidence.

The role of ECMO in autoimmune respiratory failure deserves comment. In our patient, veno‐venous ECMO maintained gas exchange during the critical window of refractory hypercapnia, allowing immunomodulatory therapies time to act. ECMO circuits adsorb drugs and interact with blood cells, which can alter pharmacokinetics and trigger inflammatory responses [11]. Whether these circuit–blood interactions confer any immunomodulatory benefit in conditions like PNP is unknown and should not be assumed.

Bronchiolitis obliterans remains the most devastating pulmonary complication of PNP and the leading cause of death. Messina et al. reinforced this association, reporting high mortality in paraneoplastic syndromes with respiratory involvement. When BO progresses to end‐stage disease despite maximal medical therapy, lung transplantation may be the only option. Cai et al. described transplantation for follicular lymphoma–associated BO [12], and Yue et al. reported successful bilateral lung transplantation in Castleman disease–associated BO [13]. In our patient, ECMO averted the immediate need for transplant evaluation, but the long‐term trajectory of his airway disease remains uncertain.

Current PNP management lacks a standardized protocol. Corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, rituximab, plasmapheresis, and IVIG are all used, with variable results. The 504‐patient systematic review by Zou et al. identified tumor type, histological pattern, and BO presence as key survival determinants [14]. Alqaidy et al. further emphasized the multiorgan scope of PAMS in their report of stroma‐rich Castleman disease [15]. Our case adds to this literature by documenting ECMO as a viable bridge in PNP‐associated respiratory failure.

Limitations

This report has several important limitations. It describes a single patient without a control group. Multiple concurrent therapies preclude the isolation of any individual treatment effect. We did not measure disease‐specific biomarkers such as anti‐envoplakin or anti‐periplakin autoantibody titers serially, which would have strengthened the correlation between immunological and clinical changes. Temporal coincidence between interventions and improvement does not prove causation. The absence of long‐term follow‐up leaves the durability of the response and the fate of the patient's bronchiolitis obliterans undetermined.

Clinical Implications

The case carries practical lessons. PNP with respiratory failure demands multidisciplinary collaboration—critical care, dermatology, oncology, pulmonology, and surgery. ECMO can sustain patients through refractory BO while immunomodulatory agents are titrated. Serial immune cell monitoring, interpreted cautiously, may help gauge treatment response. Future studies should measure disease‐specific autoantibody titers and cytokine profiles longitudinally, investigate ECMO–immune component interactions, and work toward standardized multidisciplinary protocols for PNP‐associated respiratory failure [16].

Conclusion

We report a young patient with PNP secondary to follicular dendritic cell sarcoma who survived acute type II respiratory failure from bronchiolitis obliterans through comprehensive multidisciplinary care, including veno‐venous ECMO. Mucocutaneous disease and respiratory function both improved during the treatment period. Serial immune cell monitoring documented dynamic shifts in lymphocyte subsets consistent with evolving immune modulation, though concurrent therapies preclude causal attribution. This case supports early, aggressive supportive care—including ECMO when indicated—in a condition where conventional management is often insufficient and underscores the need for controlled studies to define optimal treatment strategies.
