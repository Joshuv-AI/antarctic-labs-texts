Self‐Doped Perylene Diimide‐Based Polymers for Simultaneous Improvements in Efficiency and Thermal Stability of Organic Solar Cells
Authors: Youngwan Lee, Seul Lee, Seunggu Lee, Kihyun Bae, Taeyoon Kim, Wan Ho Cho, Minhyuk Kim, Min Thein Kyaw, Kyung‐Koo Lee, Hu Young Jeong, Bumjoon J Kim, Yongsup Park, BongSoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResearch on electron transporting layers (ETLs) for organic solar cells (OSCs) has advanced rapidly in recent years; however, most studies have focused primarily on improving device efficiency rather than stability. Here, we report new ETL polymers, PPV and PPA, incorporating PDINN moieties through vinyl and ethynyl linkers, respectively. Among these materials, PPV‐M delivers the best photovoltaic performance, achieving power conversion efficiencies of 18.45% and 16.97% in D18:Y6‐BO‐ and PM6:BTP‐eC9‐based devices, respectively, surpassing those of PDINN‐based counterparts (17.79% and 16.85%). Under thermal aging at 65°C, PPV‐M‐based devices exhibited improved operational retention compared with PDINN‐based devices, with T80 values of 637 h and 8 h, respectively. This improved efficiency and stability originate from the polymeric nature of PPV‐M, which enables smooth and uniform coverage of the photoactive layer and provides a higher glass transition temperature than the small‐molecule PDINN, thereby suppressing thermally induced molecular diffusion. These results demonstrate that polymerizing electron‐transporting units into well‐defined ETLs is an effective molecular design strategy for achieving both high efficiency and enhanced thermal stability in OSCs.Keywords: ETL polymers, electron transporting layer, organic solar cells, self‐doping polymer, thermal stability

Introduction

Organic Solar Cells (OSCs) have been attracting attention as next‐generation energy sources due to their advantages, including lightweight, flexibility, cost‐effectiveness, and solution‐processability [1]. As the chronological development of various non‐fullerene acceptors (NFA) in photoactive layers has advanced, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of OSCs has approached 20% [2, 3, 4, 5]. While the development of new photoactive layer materials has led to significant advances in OSCs, recent advances in interfacial engineering have also played a crucial role in increasing device efficiencies to state‐of‐the‐art levels. In particular, self‐assembled monolayer (SAM)‐based hole transporting layers (HTLs) have recently enabled highly efficient OSCs by reducing interfacial energy losses and facilitating efficient hole extraction [6, 7, 8]. In parallel, electron transporting layers (ETLs) remain equally important for optimizing electron extraction, suppressing interfacial recombination, and improving operational stability [9, 10].

ETLs play a key role in charge extraction by establishing efficient interfacial contact between the photoactive layer and the cathode, aligning energy levels in OSCs, and facilitating electron separation and transport by modulating the work function (WF) of the cathode [11, 12, 13, 14]. Compared to the inorganic ETLs (LiF, CsF, CsCO3, TiOx, and ZnO) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19], the organic ETLs showed broad advantages in device fabrication because of the capability of versatile modification on the molecular structure to adjust an appropriate energy level for ohmic contact and to lower the WF of the metal through the dipole moment in the molecular structure [20].

The organic ETLs can be categorized into diverse types of molecules, including fullerene‐based small molecules [21, 22], ionene and zwitterionic polymers [23, 24, 25], and n‐type conjugated small molecules developed from naphthalene diimide (NDI) [26] and perylene diimide (PDI) [27, 28]. Among the various ETLs, PDINN, based on the PDI moiety, was first reported by Yongfang Li's group in 2020 [29] and has still been applied for up‐to‐date OSCs. Furthermore, various derivatives of PDINN have been extensively investigated owing to their intrinsic self‐doping properties [30, 31]. Despite its promising charge extraction ability and capability of WF modification, PDINN derives insufficient device stability under long‐term thermal stress on the OSCs. Among several types of stabilities, thermal stability is one of the critical issues in OSCs because morphological degradation of the ETLs from temperature surge under 1‐sun irradiation is unavoidable [32], whereas the degradation caused by moisture and oxygen could be effectively mitigated through engineering approaches such as encapsulation and inverted device structure [33, 34, 35]. While polymeric components have been employed to suppress thermally induced molecular diffusion in all‐polymer OSCs [36], the molecular design of ETL polymers that combine PDINN‐like self‐doping characteristics with improved thermal robustness remains largely unexplored. This motivates the development of novel polymeric ETLs capable of maintaining efficient electron extraction and WF modification while resisting thermal morphological degradation.

In this work, we focused on the highly thermally stable ETL and two structures of polymer ETLs were designed: poly (2, 9‐bis (3‐((3‐dimethylamino) propyl) amino) propyl)‐5‐(prop‐1‐en‐1yl)anthra[2, 1, 9‐def:6, 5, 10‐d'e’f’]diisoquinoline‐1, 3, 8, 10(2H, 9H)‐tetraone) (as named PPV) and poly (2, 9‐bis(3‐((3‐dimethylamino)propyl)amino)propyl)‐5‐methyl‐12(prop‐1‐yn‐yl)anthra[2, 1, 9‐def:6, 5, 10‐d'e’f’]diisoquinoline‐1, 3, 8, 10(2H, 9H)‐tetraone) (as named PPA), which vinyl and ethynyl groups, respectively, are introduced as a linker group to promote intra chain charge transport. Within synthesized PPV, two batches of ETLs were separated by Soxhlet extraction in order of molecular weight: PPV‐M with moderate molecular weight and PPV‐H with large molecular weight. To develop novel polymers based on the PDI backbone with an alkyl amine moiety, the charge extraction from the photoactive layer by the ETLs will be ensured by moderately modifying the WF of the metal through its self‐doping ability. As illustrated in Figure 1, the introduction of polymeric ETL materials is expected to suppress molecular diffusion and promote morphological fixation against thermal stress by increasing the glass transition temperature (Tg) through chain entanglement [37, 38, 39]. The self‐doping ability of the ETLs was characterized through electron spin resonance (ESR), and the modified WFs of the electrodes were measured by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). The Tg of each material was measured by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and the UV–vis deviation metric method. Analysis of the UV–vis spectra and the Fourier transformation infrared (FT‐IR) spectra in the region of N─H and C═O vibrational peaks revealed that the ETL polymers engage cofacial packing of backbones with a higher degree of freedom of hydrogen bonding of amine groups than the H‐aggregation dominant high ordering of PDINN. In device application, the PPV‐M employed devices showed the highest PCEs of 18.45% and 16.97% in D18:Y6‐BO and PM6:BTP‐eC9, respectively, compared to PDINN based devices. The PCEs of PPV‐M based devices remain above 75% of the initial PCEs after 1000 h in a N2 atmosphere glove box at 65°C, whereas PDINN based devices only maintain 20%. Device deterioration under thermal stress was characterized by dark current measurement, photo‐charge extraction by linearly increasing voltage (photo‐CELIV), transient photocurrent (TPC), and transient photovoltage (TPV). The morphological and optical changes of ETLs under thermal stress were analyzed by grazing‐incidence wide‐angle X‐ray scattering (GIWAXS), atomic force microscopy (AFM), UV–vis spectroscopy, and Fourier transform‐infrared (FT‐IR). In the case of PDINN, significant morphological changes were observed due to thermal‐diffusion‐induced recrystallization of the molecules. Conversely, in PPV‐M, morphological distortion was successfully suppressed, thereby enhancing the thermal stability of the OSCs.

Results and Discussion

The synthetic routes for PDI‐based ETL polymers were outlined in Scheme S1. PDINN‐2Br, a monomer featuring a polar alkyl chain, was synthesized via a condensation reaction between 1,7‐dibromo‐3,4,9,10‐perylenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (PTCA‐2Br) and N,N‐dimethyl‐dipropyltriamine. To investigate the influence of linker moieties in polymers, PPV and PPA were polymerized via Stille coupling of PDINN‐2Br with 1,2‐bis(tributylstannyl)ethene or 1,2‐bis(tributylstannyl)acetylene as linkers, respectively. The synthesized PPV was purified by Soxhlet extraction; the medium molecular weight chloroform batch was named PPV‐M (number‐average molecular weight (Mn) = 12.0 kDa, weight‐average molecular weight (Mw) = 12.9 kDa), while the higher molecular weight methanol batch was named PPV‐H (Mn = 37.0 kDa, Mw = 46.5 kDa). In the case of the PPA, which has lower solubility than the PPV polymer, the chloroform batch contains oligomers of low molecular weight. Thus, only the methanol batch is referred to as PPA‐M (Mn = 11.7 kDa, Mw = 12.2 kDa). Three polymers were well dissolved in methanol, enabling orthogonal solution processing. The detailed synthetic procedures, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra, and gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis of the synthesized polymers are provided in Figures S1–S7.

The optical properties of the ETL materials were investigated by ultraviolet‐visible (UV–vis) absorption spectroscopy. UV–vis absorption spectra of PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M are shown in Figure 2a and Figure S8 for the solution and film states, respectively. In common, perylene diimide groups, which formed the backbone of the PDI‐based ETL materials, showed distinct absorption peaks of (0‐0), (0‐1), (0‐2) vibronic transitions in a range of 450–525 nm in solution state spectra, while the (0‐0), (0‐1), (0‐2) transitions were placed in the range of 450–560 nm in film state spectra [40, 41]. In solution state absorption spectra, PDINN molecules exhibited monomeric behavior without significant dimeric or higher‐order aggregation, with an absorbance ratio of (0‐0)/(0‐1) = 1.2, whereas the (0‐0)/(0‐1) ratio is below 1 in dimer‐state absorption spectra [42]. In contrast, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M showed H‐aggregation behaviors, which induce strong and broad absorption peaks at the 600–700 nm region originating from a newly formed band structure by cofacial packing of the polymer backbones [40]. The cofacial packing of PDI units along the polymer backbone increases the formation of dimeric aggregates in the ETL polymers, which reduces the peak absorption coefficients. In this aggregated state, excitonic coupling between adjacent PDI chromophores leads to splitting and redistribution of electronic transitions, resulting in broadened aggregate‐related absorption features with diminished monomer‐like vibronic bands [43]. This low absorption of the ETL polymers is slightly beneficial for electrode‐reflected light absorption of a photoactive layer with low absorption of ETL [10]. The absorption coefficient of the ETL materials is organized in Figure S9. In the Film state, PDINN exhibited strong H‐aggregation, characterized by a blueshift of the maximum absorption peak, accompanied by a decrease in the (0‐0) transition and an increase in the (0‐2) transition. Three ETL polymers exhibited a weaker tendency for H‐aggregation than PDINN. Notably, PPV‐M exhibited the least H‐aggregation, with similar intensities for the (0‐0) and (0‐1) transitions. Compared to PPV‐M, the higher molecular weight of PPV‐H and the more rigid backbone of PPA‐M may lead to stronger molecular aggregation. Because of the newly formed band structure at 600–700 nm in film UV–vis spectra, the optical bandgaps from UV–vis spectra of the ETL polymers were reduced to 1.43, 1.42, and 1.42 eV for PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively, while PDINN showed 1.93 eV.

Moreover, FT‐IR spectroscopy was employed to examine the molecular interactions of ETL materials. The simulated stretching vibrations of PDINN were further verified by DFT calculations against the experimental FT‐IR spectra. (Figure S10a,b) In Figure 2b, the ν(N─H) stretching of PDINN was observed at 3571 cm−1, whereas PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M exhibited the ν(N─H) stretching at 3380 cm−1. The ETL polymers displayed a pronounced downshift of approximately 190 cm−1 compared to PDINN, suggesting that hydrogen bonding is more favorably formed in the ETL polymers [44]. In contrast, the negligible downshift in ν(N─H) stretching between the solution state (3578 cm−1) and the film state (3571 cm−1) of PDINN indicated the presence of weak hydrogen bonding interactions in the film, with its vibrational characteristics preserved during the film formation, as shown in Figure S11a [45]. A similar trend was also observed in the C═O and C─N stretching modes, as shown in Figure S11b. To further clarify the sites responsible for hydrogen bonding in the ETL polymers, FT‐IR spectra (Figure S11c,d) were analyzed. The C═O asymmetric stretching vibrations of ETL polymers were in close agreement with those previously reported for perylene diimide derivatives (1690–1705 cm−1) [46, 47], indicating that the majority of the carbonyl groups in the imide were not involved in hydrogen bonding. In contrast, a downshift in the ν(C‐N)aromatic and ν(C‐N)3° amine peak was observed, indicative of hydrogen bonding interactions at the nitrogen of aromatic and tertiary amines [48]. Overall, hydrogen bonding in PDINN is restricted due to its H‐aggregation‐type ordered packing, which arises from the high crystallinity of PDI [49]. In contrast, the amorphous structure of the ETL polymers, characterized by mixed H–J aggregation with cofacial packing, provides increased opportunities for hydrogen bonding between aliphatic chains. Such intermolecular hydrogen bonding in the polymers promotes chain entanglement, thereby effectively suppressing thermally induced molecular diffusion [50, 51].

The thermal behavior of the ETL materials was measured by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and shown in Figure S12. The Tg and melting temperature (Tm) of the materials are summarized in Table 1. PDINN showed Tg of 61°C, similar to other reported Tg of PDI‐based molecules in the 70°C–90°C range [52, 53]. In contrast, the Tg values of the ETL polymers were measured at 150°C for PPV‐M, 153°C for PPV‐H, and 143°C for PPA‐M, which are higher than those of PDINN because of not only physical entanglement of polymers but also chemical entanglement derived from hydrogen bonding.

Furthermore, the Tg was double‐checked by UV–vis DMT methods, as reported by Lipomi group, to determine the Tg of the ETL materials in the film state, a condition similar to that of device fabrication [54]. The DMT methods were described in the Supporting Information, and the UV–vis spectra and calculated DMT plots are shown in Figure 2c and Figure S13. Similar to the trend observed by DSC, PDINN exhibited the lowest Tg of 71.6°C. However, ETL polymers showed higher Tg values: 118.3°C for PPV‐M, 126.7°C for PPV‐H, and 118.9°C for PPA‐M. These high Tg values of the ETL polymers, which are almost twice those of PDINN, highlight the thermal stability of the polymer materials. Increased Tg via polymerization could significantly enhance the thermal stability of fabricated devices by inhibiting the diffusion of ETL materials, thereby improving the interfacial stability of the devices. The Tg values of PDINN, determined by DSC measurements and DMT calculations, were the lowest among the ETL materials, whereas the ETL polymers exhibited higher Tg values, which are more desirable for the thermal stability of the fabricated devices.

To verify whether the higher Tg of the polymer ETLs contributes to the stability of the photoactive layer/ETL interface, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were conducted before and after thermal aging. Figures S14a and S15a–d showed a surface potential of −1.21 V, which shifted to −1.79, −1.37, −1.44, and −1.34 V after coating with PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively. After aging at 65°C for 100 h, the PDINN‐employed film exhibited a large surface potential shift of approximately 0.55 V, which was even greater than that of the neat photoactive layer, whereas the ETL polymer‐employed films showed negligible changes of only 0.03–0.04 V, as shown in Figures S14b and S15e–h. These KPFM results demonstrate that the ETL polymers maintain a more stable interfacial electrostatic environment under thermal stress, which is consistent with their higher Tg and supports the suppressed thermally induced molecular diffusion at the photoactive layer/ETL interface.

The energy levels of the ETL materials were determined by cyclic voltammetry (CV), and their voltammograms are presented in Figure S16. The energy levels of each material are summarized in Table 1. The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M were −5.95/−3.90 eV, −5.36/−4.07 eV, −5.33/−3.99 eV, and −5.31/−3.97 eV, respectively. According to the DFT calculation of PDINN and trimer of PPV and PPA (Table S1), the bandgap of single PDINN was 2.53 eV, whereas PPV and PPA showed decreased bandgap of 2.00 and 1.94 eV, respectively. The trend in bandgap with CV and DFT calculations also matched the calculated bandgap from UV–vis absorption spectra.

The self‐doping effect of amine pendant groups on the PDI backbone was systematically investigated using ESR, and the corresponding spectra are presented in Figure 2d. The ETL materials exhibit pronounced self‐doping behavior, with g values of 2.001 for PDINN and 2.002 for PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M. Furthermore, analysis of the double‐integrated ESR spectra indicates that the ETL polymers possess nearly 2‐fold higher self‐doping efficiency (Figure S17) [55]. This pronounced self‐doping effect is attributed to efficient electron transfer from the amine groups to adjacent PDI units, resulting in the formation of unpaired electrons on the amine moieties and PDI radical anions, thereby significantly enhancing the electrical conductivity of the ETL films.

The self‐doping effect of the ETL, in turn, results in lowering the work function of the Ag electrodes. The work functions and HOMO/LUMO energy levels of the ETL‐layered Ag electrodes were measured by UPS and low‐energy inverse photoemission spectroscopy (LEIPS). Note that in this work, when the optical bandgaps of the ETL materials are used to determine the LUMO energy levels from the UPS‐derived HOMO energy level, a contradiction arises: the estimated LUMO levels lie below the Fermi level. Therefore, LEIPS experiments were essential to determine a direct determination on the LUMO levels with respect to the Fermi level without underestimating the bandgap by an exciton binding energy [56]. A diagram of secondary electron offsets is shown in Figure 2e, and UPS‐LEIPS spectra of each ETL are shown in Figure S18. From these spectra, we mapped out the HOMO/LUMO levels of the ETL materials (Figure 2f). First, all ETL materials on Ag substrates reduced the Ag work function (4.42 eV) to 3.56, 3.86, 3.90, and 3.83 eV for PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively. Despite the work function difference of polymer ETLs being smaller than that of PDINN, it successfully reduces its work function by ∼0.56 eV, which is beneficial for extracting electrons from n‐type NFA by forming an Ohmic contact at the ETL/photoactive layer interface. Second, the HOMO energy levels of the ETLs are located below the Fermi level with offset values of 3.10, 3.11, 2.94, and 2.94 eV for PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively. Third, from the LEIPS spectra, the LUMO energy levels of the ETLs are located above the Fermi level with offsets of 0.19, 0.55, 0.50, and 0.49 eV, respectively. The determined deep‐lying HOMO energy levels and small LUMO offsets relative to the Fermi level indicated that all ETLs facilitate efficient electron collection from the photoactive layer.

Morphological and structural characterization of the ETL materials was conducted by AFM and GIWAXS. To assess the ETL‐coated surface in the device‐relevant structure, AFM measurements were additionally performed on ITO/PEDOT:PSS/BHJ/ETL films (Figure S19). The RMS roughness values of the ETL‐coated photoactive films remained comparable to those of the bare photoactive layer, indicating that the measured surface topography largely reflects the underlying photoactive morphology [57, 58]. Therefore, ETL films prepared on low‐roughness Si wafer/PMMA substrates were used to more clearly compare the intrinsic morphology and height profiles of the ETL materials themselves. In AFM images (Figure 2g–j), both PDINN and PPV‐M showed similar root mean square (RMS) roughness values of 0.53 and 0.56 nm, respectively. However, the PDINN film exhibited a fibrillar structure, due to small molecular nature with strong crystalline properties. The PPV‐H exhibited thicker, spot‐like aggregations with an RMS of 0.82 nm, which is larger than that of PPV‐M. PPA‐M exhibited severe aggregation in the film state, with an RMS of 0.86 nm. According to the GIWAXS images (Figure S20), the PDINN film showed strong crystalline features with intense peak signals, which originate from the monoclinic structure of perylene diimide backbone packing [59], with π–π stacking peaks located along the in‐plane direction and lamellar peaks located along the out‐of‐plane direction. In contrast, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M showed amorphous polymer features with isotropic scattering peaks. Especially, π–π interaction peaks of PDINN are located at q = 1.792 Å−1 with a d‐spacing of 3.51 Å, while PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M showed smaller q values of π–π interaction peaks at q = 1.723, 1.740, 1.691 Å−1, respectively, with increased d‐spacing followed by increased displacement. Detailed GIWAXS peak parameters are summarized in Table S2. Among the polymer ETLs, the PPV‐M exhibited the most preferable surface morphologies for reducing leakage current.

To evaluate the photovoltaic performance of PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M as the ETL, the OSCs were fabricated with a structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PM6:BTP‐eC9/ETL/Ag. The current density‐voltage (J–V) curves in 1 sun and external quantum efficiency (EQE) curves of the OSCs are shown in Figure 3a and Figure S21. All the ETL‐incorporated OSCs exhibited much improved photovoltaic performance, indicating the importance of incorporating appropriate ETLs in the OSCs. Among the OSCs, PPV‐M based OSCs exhibited the best PCE of 16.97% with an open circuit voltage (V OC) of 0.844 V, a short circuit current (J SC) of 26.67 mA/cm2, and a fill factor (FF) of 75.45%. The PDINN based PM6:BTP‐eC9 OSCs showed a PCE of 16.85%, while other polymer ETLs exhibited reduced PCEs of 16.48% for PPA‐M and 16.41% for PPV‐H. The photovoltaic parameters of the measured devices are summarized in Table 2. In addition, the thickness‐dependent measurements confirmed that both PDINN and PPV‐M exhibited their highest PCEs at an ETL thickness of approximately 4–5 nm (Figure S22 and Table S3), which was therefore used for the main device comparison. The presence of large aggregates and the rough surfaces of PPV‐H and PPA‐M films, as shown in the AFM images (Figure 2g–j), might have resulted in a slight decrease in solar cell parameters, including J SC and FF, compared to the corresponding parameters in the PPV‐M OSCs.

For fabricated OSCs, thermal stability was tested under exposure to thermal stress at 65°C in an N2‐filled atmosphere. As reported by the Ade group, BTP‐eC9 exhibited a Tg of 75°C [60]. Therefore, a thermal stress temperature of 65°C was chosen to minimize the degree of thermal degradation of the photoactive layer relative to the total device degradation. The trend in thermal stability of fabricated PM6:BTP‐eC9 OSCs is shown in Figure 3b, and detailed photovoltaic parameters for thermal annealing times are presented in Tables S4–S8. The T90 values (defined as the time required for the device performance to decrease to 90% of its initial value) obtained from thermal stability at 65°C for 200 h were 4, 5, 98, 16, and 44 h for the devices without an ETL, and with PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively. Compared with PDINN, the polymer ETLs generally exhibited prolonged T90 values, suggesting improved thermal stability. Notably, PPV‐M showed the longest T90 of 98 h, corresponding to nearly a 20‐fold improvement over PDINN. Furthermore, a comparison of the 1000 h thermal stability between PDINN and PPV‐M, the most thermally stable material, revealed an even greater difference. As shown in Figure S23, the T80 (defined as the time required for the device performance to decrease to 80% of its initial value) value of PPV‐M was 637 h, whereas that of PDINN was only 8 h, corresponding to nearly an 80‐fold improvement in thermal stability.

To investigate the thermal effects on the small molecule ETL and polymer ETL, PDINN and PPV‐M were selected as test groups to highlight the distinct differences. These two selected ETL materials were introduced to D18:Y6‐BO based OSCs as well. The obtained J–V curve and EQE spectra are shown in Figure 3c and Figure S24, respectively. PPV‐M based devices showed a PCE of 18.45% with a V OC of 0.870 V, a J SC of 27.12 mA/cm2, and a FF of 78.19%, while PDINN based devices showed a PCE of 17.79% with a V OC of 0.861 V, a J SC of 27.19 mA/cm2, and a FF of 75.99% (Table 3). In addition, the thermal stability of the devices under continuous thermal stress was also checked and plotted in Figure S25. PPV‐M enhances the thermal stability of the devices by retaining 89% of the initial PCE after 120 h of thermal stress. However, PDINN deteriorated the thermal stability of the devices, resulting in 55% of the initial PCE after 120 h. To explain the electron transfer between the photoactive layer materials and the ETL in Fermi level‐aligned energy levels, UPS and LEIPS measurements of the PM6:BTP‐eC9 blend on PDINN/Ag or PPV‐M/Ag were also conducted and plotted in Figure 3d,e. The HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the PM6 and BTP‐eC9 were extracted by deconvoluting the UPS/LEIPS spectra of PM6:BTP‐eC9 blends based on the PM6 and BTP‐eC9 UPS‐LEIPS spectra (Figures S26 and S27), and detailed methods are described in the Supporting Information. The corresponding Fermi level‐aligned energy level diagram is shown in Figure 3f. We found that both PM6/BTP‐eC9/PDINN/Ag and PM6/BTP‐eC9/PPV‐M/Ag exhibited cascades of LUMO energy levels between the photoactive layer and the ETL, which promotes the efficient extraction of generated electrons from the photoactive layer. With deep‐lying HOMO levels in PDINN and PPV‐M, both ETLs prevent effectively unwanted hole extraction through the cathode.

For a better understanding of the parameters of the OSCs, the electron transfer of electron‐only devices was confirmed by J‐‐V curve measurement. The electron‐only device (EOD) was fabricated with ITO/PEIE/n‐type material/ETL/Ag structure, and J–V curves are shown in Figure S28. For n‐type material, NFA BTP‐eC9 and n‐type polymer PNDI‐T2‐HD were selected. PNDI‐T2‐HD was selected as an additional n‐type semiconductor owing to its suitable energy level alignment with the ETLs and smooth film morphology, which helps suppress unwanted shunt current arising from morphology induced leakage pathways such as pinholes, incomplete coverage, or roughness induced defects [61, 62]. In the J–V curve, all 4 ETLs showed a similar charge transfer rate in the EOD, which contains the same thickness of ETL as the OSCs. Despite the bulk conductance of polymer ETLs being relatively lower than that of PDINN, as shown in Figure S29, the thin layer of ETLs showed sufficient electron transfer for all ETLs. With the results, we confirmed that PPV‐M exhibited a sufficiently high electron transfer rate compared to PDINN; that is, the J SC of the PPV‐M OSC is nearly equivalent to that of the PDINN OSC.

Contact angles (θ) of DI water and diiodomethane (DIM) droplets on the PM6:BTP‐eC9 photoactive layer and the ETL layers were measured to evaluate their surface energy and adhesion between the layers. The PM6:BTP‐eC9 showed θ DI water of 105.2° and θ DIM of 58.0° (Figure S30a,f), resulting in a total surface energy of 31.7 mN/m. For the ETLs, the water contact angles were significantly reduced to 21.9°, 10.2°, 10.1°, and 10.3° for PDINN, PPV‐M, PPV‐H, and PPA‐M, respectively (Figure S30b–e). The DIM contact angles were also reduced to 35.0°, 30.8°, 30.2°, and 27.8° in the same order (Figure S30g–j). The surface energy of each ETL layer was calculated from the contact angle and is summarized in Table S9. Compared to PDINN, polymer ETLs commonly exhibited increased wettability for both hydrophilic and hydrophobic solvents on the surface, resulting in a total surface energy of approximately 77–78 mN/m. Based on the surface energy analysis, the PDINN is slightly more favorable to the PM6:BTP‐eC9 blend than the polymer ETLs. Nevertheless, the overall photovoltaic performances of the ETL polymers‐based devices are superior to or comparable with that of the PDINN based device, suggesting that this surface energy difference is not a critical factor affecting photovoltaic performance and stability. Rather, smooth surface roughness and the sluggish movement of the polymer ETLs appear to be more critical. Considering both surface morphology and surface energy, the PPV‐M exhibited moderate smoothness with sufficient surface energy. Therefore, it leads to champion device performance with enhanced solar cell parameters, especially V OC and FF, compared to the PDINN.

For PDINN and PPV‐M based PM6:BTP‐eC9 OSCs, the electrical parameter changes accompanying thermal annealing were thoroughly characterized by comparing as ‐cast devices with devices thermally annealed for 100 h. Dark current and photocurrent of the as‐cast and thermally aged devices were measured to analyze leakage current and photocurrent generation. The equivalent J–V curves for dark current and photocurrent are shown in Figure 4a,b, respectively. In a low‐voltage regime below ∼ 0.5 V, both PDINN and PPV‐M based devices showed shunt leakage currents of 5.06 × 10−6 and 1.86 × 10−6 A/cm2 at 0.4 V, respectively [63]. After thermal annealing, PDINN exhibited a dramatic increase in leakage current to 3.19 × 10−3 A/cm2, which is 630 times larger than that of the as‐cast device. In contrast, PPV‐M exhibited a slightly decreased leakage current of 1.16 × 10−6 A/cm2, which is 0.89 times that of the as‐cast device. The increased leakage current in PDINN based devices causes recombination of photocarriers, leading to the severe deterioration of solar cell parameters, including V OC, J SC, and FF [64]. The probability of charge separation (P(E,T)) is also estimated from photocurrent‐effective voltage curves in Figure 4b [65]. Before thermal annealing, PDINN and PPV‐M based devices show the same P(E,T) of 97.9%. After thermal annealing, P(E,T) of PDINN decreased to 92.7% with photocurrent loss in all voltage sweep regions. In the mean time, P(E,T) of PPV‐M increased to 99% while maintaining a significant residual photocurrent generation. In addition to the leakage current suppression, PPV‐M maintains photocurrent even in a long‐term thermally aged device.

To identify a type of carrier recombination in as‐cast and thermally aged devices, light intensity‐dependent J–V curves were measured [66]. The relationship between V OC and light intensity (P) follows a formula of V OC ∝ nkT/qln(P). When bimolecular recombination is dominant compared to Shockley‐Read‐Hall type trap‐assisted recombination, the slope nkT/q of V OC vs. ln(P) results in kT/q; on the other hand, the slope nkT/q results in 2kT/q for dominant trap‐assisted recombination. In Figure 4c, as‐cast devices based on PDINN and PPV‐M showed slope n values of 1.14 and 1.2, respectively. However, after 100 h thermal annealing, PDINN based devices exhibited an ideality factor of 2.07, indicating that severe trap‐assisted recombination occurs, whereas PPV‐M based devices showed a value of 1.17. Also, J SC and light intensity (P) can be explained by the following equation: J SC ∝ Pα. In Figure S31, the as‐cast OSCs of PDINN and PPV‐M showed α values of 0.996 and 1.00, respectively. After thermal annealing, the α values showed negligible changes, 1.01 for PDINN and 0.998 for PPV‐M. However, thermal annealed PDINN based devices showed decreased J SC at all light intensities, when α from the J SC‐P plot showed a value of unity (= 1), which is related to the weak bimolecular recombination of the photo carriers. Both PDINN and PPV‐M based PM6BTP‐eC9 OSCs showed weak bimolecular recombination, even after thermal treatment. However, severe trap‐assisted recombination was dominant for thermally aged PDINN based OSCs, while PPV‐M suppressed trap‐assisted recombination in the OSCs even after thermal aging for 100 h.

To gain a deeper understanding of photocarrier dynamics between the as‐cast and thermally annealed devices, three types of transient measurements were conducted: photo‐CELIV, TPC, and TPV. To calculate the photocarrier mobility of the devices, the photo‐CELIV was measured [67]. The photo‐CELIV curves are plotted in Figure 4d, and the detailed methods are described in the Supporting Information. For as‐cast devices, the mobility of the photocarrier is about 2.77 × 10−4 and 2.98 × 10−4 cm2V−1s−1 for PDINN and PPV‐M based devices, respectively. After thermal annealing, the photocarrier mobility of PDINN based devices decreased to 7.97 × 10−5 cm2V−1s−1 due to the delayed extraction of trapped carriers by an applied electric field. However, the mobility of PPV‐M based devices decreased slightly to 2.47 × 10−4 cm2V−1s−1, while maintaining interface trap states even after thermal aging for 100 h.

Moreover, the TPC of the PM6:BTP‐eC9 based OSCs was measured to characterize photocarrier decay related to trap states. TPC decay is shown in Figure 4e, whole curves are shown in Figure S32, and fitted parameters are organized in Table S10. By biexponential fitting of the photocurrent‐time curves, the fast decay constant τ1 and slow decay constant τ2 can be separated, corresponding to shallow and deep traps, respectively [68]. Across the entire device, the majority of trap states are shallow traps, as inferred from the dominant fast decay constant. PDINN exhibited a delayed fast decay time constant, increasing from 0.389 to 0.868 µs after thermal annealing, accompanied by an increase in the number of extracted charge carriers. However, PPV‐M maintained its fast time decay constant from 0.413 to 0.403 µs, with a similar number of extracted charge carriers. In PDINN based PM6:BTP‐eC9 OSCs, the density of trap states increased after thermal annealing, leading to an elongated carrier extraction time and an increase in trapped photo carriers. On the other hand, PPV‐M suppressed the formation of trap states and maintained the depth and number of trap states even after thermal aging.

In addition, the charge‐carrier dynamics of the devices were investigated by TPV measurements under open‐circuit conditions. The transient photovoltage decay is shown in Figure 4f, the full decay curves are shown in Figure S33, and the fitting results are summarized in Table S11. The apparent TPV decay lifetime was obtained by monoexponential fitting of the photovoltage decay curves [69]. For the PDINN‐based device, the apparent TPV decay lifetime increased from 6.62 to 18.42 µs after thermal annealing. This longer decay should not be interpreted as an increase in the intrinsic free carrier recombination lifetime. Rather, it indicates that thermally generated trap states capture photogenerated carriers, preventing them from immediately participating in free‐carrier relaxation or recombination. As a result, part of the charge population remains temporarily immobilized or stored in trap states and is released only on a delayed time scale, leading to slower photovoltage decay and an increased apparent TPV lifetime [70, 71]. This trap‐related interpretation is further supported by the light intensity‐dependent VOC analysis, where the ideality factor of the PDINN‐based device increased from 1.14 to 2.07 after thermal aging, indicating enhanced trap‐assisted recombination [72, 73]. In contrast, the PPV‐M‐based device showed nearly unchanged apparent TPV decay lifetimes of 5.94 and 5.17 µs before and after thermal annealing, respectively, together with a nearly unchanged ideality factor from 1.21 to 1.15. These results indicate that PPV‐M effectively suppresses thermally induced trap formation, thereby maintaining VOC and charge‐carrier dynamics under thermal stress.

Furthermore, two types of half‐cell devices were fabricated to investigate the specific interfacial thermal degradation of each layer and quantify it. The first fabricated cell structure was the ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PM6:BTP‐eC9 structure, which was pre‐annealed. Then, ETL/Ag layers were deposited just before measurement to investigate the thermal decomposition of the photoactive layer. Corresponding J–V curves and photovoltaic parameters are shown in Figure S34 and Table S12. After 25 h pre‐annealing, neither PDINN nor PPV‐M affected the severe degradation of V OC and FF in devices, despite minimal morphological changes in PM6:BTP‐eC9 under 65°C thermal stress. In contrast, ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PM6:BTP‐eC9/ETL structure half cells were fabricated to determine an interfacial decomposition between the photoactive layer and ETL, as shown in Figure S35 and Table S13. PDINN based OSCs reached 50% of the initial PCE after 25 h of pre‐annealing. In contrast, PPV‐M based devices maintained their photovoltaic parameters, with only a minor decrease in V OC, and preserved J SC and FF after pre‐annealing for 25 h. After characterizing the pre‐annealed devices, additional thermal annealing was conducted and plotted as a function of the thermal annealing time for the ETL, as shown in Figure S36. From the results, most of the thermal degradation in the PDINN based devices dominantly occurred at the ETL, resulting in severe losses of J SC and FF. However, PPV‐M showed independent degradation trends with respect to the pre‐annealing time of the ETL. In both ETL based devices, additional V OC loss is observed upon comparing the as‐cast and pre‐annealed devices, with a minor loss in PPV‐M based devices compared to PDINN.

To observe a visible transformation within OSCs under thermal stress, as‐cast and thermally annealed OSC devices were investigated by cross‐sectional transmission electron microscope (TEM). For both PDINN and PPV‐M devices, there is no diffusion of Ag electrodes, which were often reported as a cause of device deterioration. To track down possible degradation, such as penetration of the Ag electrode across the device or photoactive layer diffusion resulting from thermal stress, energy‐dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was also used to analyze the cross‐sectional TEM samples. As in Figures S37–S39, there are no signs of Ag penetration or layer diffusion in both TEM and EDS images, even for 600 h thermally aged samples of both PDINN and PPV‐M based OSCs. Therefore, we find that the primary factor contributing to device deterioration is the interfaces between layers.

Along with the device characterization of both as‐cast and thermally annealed conditions, the primary degradation factor related to the ETL is the generation of trap states, especially at the interface between the photoactive layer and the ETL. The degradation of OSCs, particularly at the ETL, increases trap‐assisted recombination, resulting in a significant decrease in V OC and FF. The small molecule type PDINN exhibits thermal movement, specifically a diffusion‐limited crystallization mechanism, of the PDINN molecules. However, PPV‐M, with a high Tg and mild roughness, can suppress the thermal motion of the ETL, thereby enhancing thermal stability by preventing the formation of interfacial trap states.

To explain the transformation of PDINN and PPV‐M ETLs under thermal stress, morphological and optical studies were conducted on as‐cast and thermally annealed ETL films. The crystalline domain modification in thermally annealed films was identified using GIWAXS. GIWAXS measurements were performed on as‐cast and thermally annealed films for 100 h. In Figure 5a–d, PDINN showed an increase in crystallinity after thermal annealing, with doubled peak intensities and reduced FWHM at the (010) peak, resulting in a lengthening of the crystal coherence length and a decrease in paracrystallinity. However, PPV‐M retained the scattering images compared to as‐cast films after thermal annealing, with negligible differences in peak intensity, without generating new scattering peaks. Detailed linecut profile and parameters are organized in Figure S40 and Table S14, respectively.

Following the GIWAXS analysis, the transition in surface morphology of PDINN and PPV‐M under thermal stress was investigated using AFM to relate the optical transition of the ETL films. AFM images were achieved on the silicon wafer with a thick PDINN or PPV‐M layer (∼ 45 nm) because the roughness of the photoactive layer is too large to determine the deformation of ETL at the half‐cell structure of the OSC device sample (AFM images of the photoactive layer and ETLs on the photoactive layer are shown in Figures S41 and S42). The PDINN film showed large‐scale aggregation with a thickened fibril width after thermal annealing for 100 h (Figure 5e,f). On the other hand, the PPV‐M film showed a smoother surface with smaller aggregates after thermal annealing (Figure 5g,h).

Moreover, UV–vis absorption spectrum of PDINN and PPV‐M was measured after thermal annealing under N2 conditions. (Figure S43a,d) In the spectrum of PDINN, the intensity of (0‐1) and (0‐2) transitions decreased, and three new peaks at 733, 820, and 977 nm were generated. This spectral modification arises from self‐doping of amine groups onto the PDI backbone, leading to distinct anionic peaks in the PDI molecules [74, 75]. In the spectra of PPV‐M, however, there was no major peak transition or modification between the as‐cast film and the thermally annealed films.

After thermal annealing at 65°C for 100 h, the ν(N─H) stretching of PDINN remained at a similar wavenumber, whereas the ν(C═O)asym, ν(C═O)sym, ν(C═C), and ν(C─N)aromatic stretching exhibited upshifts from 1687 to 1691 cm− 1, 1643 to 1652 cm− 1, 1588 to 1595 cm− 1, and 1342 to 1349 cm− 1, respectively, accompanied by noticeable peak sharpening. After thermal annealing, the crystallinity of PDINN increased due to molecular diffusion, resulting in an upshift and peak narrowing in the FT‐IR spectra (Figure S43b,c) [76, 77]. In contrast, PPV‐M exhibited almost no spectral shift, suggesting that polymerization effectively suppresses molecular movement and enhances thermal stability under thermal stress (Figure S43e,f).

The increase in morphological roughness, aggregation, and crystallinity of PDINN can be attributed to the Tg of ETL materials. The annealing temperature of 65°C is sufficiently high for diffusion‐limited crystallization of PDINN, which exhibits a Tg of 61°C and results in the enlargement of crystalline domains. In contrast, PPV‐M remains frozen during thermal annealing due to its high Tg of approximately 149°C, which is above the annealing temperature [36].

Conclusions

We report thermally robust PDI‐based ETL polymers, in particular PPV‐M, that enable simultaneous improvements in photovoltaic performance and thermal stability in OSCs. By suppressing intrinsic aggregation and improving thermal stability through chemical structure design, PPV‐M mitigated interfacial degradation and inhibited trap‐state formation under thermal stress. It further preserved photocarrier dynamics and device performance even after prolonged thermal aging. These results demonstrate the advantages of polymer ETLs over small molecule counterparts and highlight their potential for achieving stable, high‐performance OSCs under operational conditions, while providing mechanistic insight into interfacial degradation and design guidelines for thermally stable interfacial materials.
