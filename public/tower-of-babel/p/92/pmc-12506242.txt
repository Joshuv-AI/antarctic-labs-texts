A 2-week old infant with Mpox in Uganda: a case report
Authors: Michael Derrick Ngobi, Lucy Amaniyo, Evelyn Kembabazi, Alice Aine, David Mukunya, Vimal Konduri, Patience Atuhaire, Sabrina Bakeera Kitaka, Victor Musiime

Background

In recent years, Mpox (formerly known as monkeypox) has emerged as a significant global health concern. Mpox is caused by the Mpox virus, which is a double-stranded DNA virus in the Orthopoxviridae family, and is divided into two clades—clade II and the more virulent clade I [1]. Both Mpox clades present with similar symptoms: a characteristic maculopapular rash that can appear anywhere on the body, fever, sore throat, headache, myalgias, arthralgias, and lymphadenopathy. Transmission primarily occurs through direct contact with infectious lesions or bodily fluids or through contact with materials such as clothing or linens that have become contaminated with infectious material from body fluids or sores [2] and can occur vertically from mother to child as well [1].

In 2022–2023, an outbreak of clade IIb Mpox spread worldwide [1]. In 2024, a clade Ib Mpox outbreak began in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and spread to several neighboring countries in East and Central Africa, including Uganda [1]. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared Mpox a public health emergency of international concern in August 2024 [3]. Between 1 January 2022 and 18 August 2024, a total of 45,652 mpox cases were reported from 12 African countries (including Uganda), resulting in 1492 deaths, a case fatality rate of 3.3% [4]. This is higher than the case fatality rate that had been reported in the earlier clade IIb outbreak.

The first case of clade Ib Mpox during the current outbreak in Uganda was recorded on 24 July 2024. As of 4 February 2025, Uganda had registered 2671 Mpox cases, of which 100 (3.7%) were recorded in children under 4 years of age [5]. In comparison, more than half of all infections globally have been in children under the age of 15 years [6].

Mpox can lead to serious complications in children, including respiratory distress or failure, sepsis, and pneumonia. Maternal complications include preterm labor, pregnancy loss, requirement for hospitalization, and maternal death. Congenital complications as result of mother-to-child Mpox transmission include prematurity, being small for gestational age, low birthweight, microcephaly, and congenital anomalies [7, 8].

Mpox spreads through direct contact with infectious sores, scabs, or body fluids. Transmission can also occur through contact with materials such as clothing or linen that have become contaminated with infectious material from body fluids or sores [2]. Mpox can also spread through other body fluids such as semen, and vaginal fluids through sexual intercourse [9]. In rare cases, it can spread through droplet aerosol transmission. While Mpox primarily affects adults and children older than 1 year [7], there have been rare case reports of neonatal Mpox [7, 9–12].

While neonatal cases, driven by vertical transmission at the time of delivery [10], have been noted in both the previous clade IIb outbreak and the present clade Ib outbreak, there have not been any published case reports of neonatal Mpox during the current ongoing Clade Ib outbreak.

Prior reports of neonatal transmission during the clade IIb outbreak were made in the United Kingdom [11], the USA (where ocular involvement was noted) [12], and Sri Lanka [13]. These cases are believed to have involved perinatal transmission; symptoms in the affected children appeared between 7 and 10 days after delivery.

As in adults and older children, the most characteristic findings in these cases have been the appearance of a maculopapular rash that begins in one area of the body and soon spreads to cover the entire body, including the palms and soles [11–13].

Complications include respiratory distress; patients in case reports from Sri Lanka and the United Kingdom required high-flow oxygen for respiratory support [11, 13].

Neonatal Mpox can easily be misdiagnosed as varicella or neonatal herpes simplex virus infection, leading to mismanagement and continued spread to other neonates and caregivers. This case highlights the need to increase the suspicion index of Mpox in neonates who present with maculopapular rash in Mpox endemic regions. This case will assist clinicians who have not interfaced with a similar case in the recognition, diagnosis, management and prognosis of neonatal Mpox. This case had no clear source of exposure and the mother did not have any known clinical features of Mpox. In addition, she had negative Mpox polymerase chain reaction (PCR) result.

Case description

We present the case of AF, a 2-week-old African female neonate who presented to the emergency unit with 1-week history of high-grade fever and a vesicular rash for 4 days. The fever was constant in nature, not associated with convulsions, and had been managed for a febrile illness with unknown oral antibiotics with no improvement at the peripheral health center from which she had been referred. The rash had started as an erythematous maculopapular rash on the forehead but later transformed into a maculopapular rash then a vesicular pattern and spread cephalocaudally to involve the palms and soles. The mother had attended antenatal care three times, tested negative for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and had no history of fevers or rashes during pregnancy. Delivery was by spontaneous vertex delivery (SVD), at term, with a birth weight of 2.8 kg. The early neonatal period was uneventful. There was no reported history of contact with anyone with a rash. The father is a commercial motorcycle driver (boda-boda rider). The child reportedly received Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine and first polio vaccine at birth as per Uganda’s immunization schedule but there was no vaccination card to confirm this. She was not yet due for the 6-week vaccinations. The child’s older sibling, who is 4 years old, is alive and healthy, and reportedly never exhibited any symptoms.

Clinical findings

On examination, we found a neonate who was irritable and febrile (temperature 38.8 °C), with diffuse maculopapular lesions on the face, trunk, extremities, palms, soles, and genital areas (Fig. 1a,b). The lesions were in various stages of evolution. She had no physical features of dysmorphism, no pallor, and no jaundice. Diagnostic assessment

Swabs of skin lesions were collected, and the samples were transported to the Central Emergency Response and Surveillance Laboratory at the Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI), a College of American Pathologists (CAP) certified laboratory (https://www.uvri.go.ug/), under appropriate cold chain protocols for testing. The DNA PCR for Mpox was done and viral DNA of Mpox was detected. This assay was performed using Creative-Biogene (https://www.creative-biogene.com) and Roche LightMix Modular (https://www.roche.com) PCR tests on the QuantStudio 7 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, https://www.thermofisher.com).

The complete blood count showed leukocytosis of 30.42 × 103 cells/μL predominantly; lymphocytes of 16.9 × 103 cells/μL; and neutrophils of 7.9 × 103 cells/μL. Hemoglobin was 14.2 g/dL and platelets were 312,000 cells/μL. Blood smear was negative for malaria parasites. Liver functional tests showed total bilirubin of 23.9 μmol/L [normal range (NR) up to 102.0 μmol/L], direct bilirubin 15.4 μmol/L (NR up to 0.6 μmol/L), aspartate transaminase (AST) 39 U/L (NR up to 110 U/L), and alanine transaminase (ALT) 7 U/L (NR up to 57 U/L). Renal functional tests showed urea 2.1 mmol/L (NR 2.7–6.4 mmol/L) and serum creatinine 15 μmol/L (NR up to 77 μmol/L). Electrolytes were normal. A lumbar puncture was not performed because skin lesions were covering the possible puncture sites.

Management

Supportive management was instituted first at the isolation unit on the pediatric infectious diseases ward in Mulago National Referral Hospital. The patient received tepid sponging for the fevers and gentle daily cleansing of lesions with saline. She continued exclusive breastfeeding. Emollients and prophylactic antibiotics were also given to prevent secondary bacterial skin infections. On confirmation of the diagnosis, the child was transferred to Entebbe Mpox isolation center, 40 km away by ambulance, on day 3 of admission. While in Entebbe isolation unit, mother tested negative for Mpox, syphilis, and HIV. No other family contact was tested.

Timeline

The neonate improved significantly while under our care, and fevers had subsided by the day 3 of admission and was breastfeeding well. Counseling was provided to the mother regarding the condition of the baby.

The child was continued on prophylactic antibiotics and improved and baby was discharged from the isolation center after 8 days of admission at the unit. Discussion

We highlight a rare case of Mpox in a 2-week-old neonate. Mpox has been reported across all ages, with the earliest age documented thus far being 4 days [14]. The neonate discussed in our case report was about 7 days of age at the time of onset of the symptoms. She presented with a 4-day history of generalized vesicular rash and fever for 1 week without history of exposure to a person with a similar rash or with previous diagnosis of Mpox. This was amid an outbreak of clade Ib Mpox in Uganda declared in July 2024 [5]. Several diagnoses, including Mpox, neonatal varicella, herpes virus infection, staphylococcal infection, and measles, were considered. However, in view of the current outbreak of Mpox in Uganda, the child’s history of a diffuse maculopapular rash involving the face, trunk, and extremities (including the palms and soles) with pustular lesions in various stages of evolution aroused greater concern for Mpox. While previous case reports of neonatal Mpox have had a clear history of exposure to a person with Mpox, usually the child’s mother [15], current findings appear to show that spread can occur from an asymptomatic person [1, 16]. The Mpox PCR result of the mother, performed at the Mpox isolation center in Entebbe, was negative, making it highly unlikely that the neonate was infected through vertical transmission of Mpox. We therefore hypothesize that the exposure to a person with Mpox in our case scenario involved an asymptomatic person. As the child’s father works as a commercial motorcycle driver, it is possible the father could have been asymptomatically infected with Mpox due to a work-related exposure from the many passengers he interacts with. Alternatively, relatives and friends who may have come to visit the baby and the mother after birth in hospital and at home may have been the source of her infection. Unfortunately, an Mpox PCR test for the father or other family contact was not done.

The symptoms in this neonate were fever and a vesicular rash that affected the entire body, including palms, soles, and genital areas. This manifestation of a vesicular rash and high-grade fevers are similar to what has been reported in other case reports of neonatal Mpox [15, 17]. Among children, rash has been reported as the most common sign. The rash in this neonate progressed from a maculopapular pattern to a vesicular and was nonsparing to the palms, soles, and the genitals. This is consistent with literature on the pattern of rash in Mpox [1, 18].

In accordance with the WHO recommendation regarding the best diagnostic sample [1], we collected a sample directly taken from skin and viral DNA PCR for mpox was performed, with a positive result. Our management was supportive and aimed at managing the symptoms of rash and fever and aimed to prevent complications, in line with current WHO guidelines [19]. Use of the antiviral tecovirimat to manage Mpox has been reported in a previous case report [15]. However, a prior trial of tecovirimat in the DRC did not show improvement in outcomes among those with clade I Mpox infections [1, 20], while efficacy data of tecovirimat for other clades of mpox have thus far been equivocal [21]. Our patient significantly improved on supportive management and was discharged in good condition, with no evidence of complications at the time of writing this case report.

Conclusion and recommendations

This case highlights the need for a high index suspicion of Mpox in neonates, even without clear history of exposure, and the need for prompt isolation and cautious handling of neonates in areas experiencing Mpox outbreak. The case also highlights the possible need for vaccines for Mpox for both children and pregnant mothers at risk.
