Clinical Impact of Early Nutritional Reversibility After Mitral Valve Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Kenichi Shibata, Masanori Yamamoto, Ai Kagase, Takahiro Tokuda, Hiroshi Tsunamoto, Atsushi Sugiura, Tetsuro Shimura, Azusa Kurita, Ryo Yamaguchi, Mike Saji, Yuki Izumi, Masahiko Asami, Yusuke Enta, Shinichi Shirai, Masaki Izumo, Shingo Mizuno, Yusuke Watanabe, Makoto Amaki, Kazuhisa Kodama, Hisao Otsuki, Toru Naganuma, Hiroki Bota, Yohei Ohno, Masahiro Yamawaki, Daisuke Hachinohe, Hiroshi Ueno, Gaku Nakazawa, Toshiaki Otsuka, Shunsuke Kubo, Kentaro Hayashida, OCEAN-Mitral Investigators

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) is an established effective treatment for patients with mitral regurgitation. The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is a well-known nutritional marker that predicts mortality risk; however, no reports clarify the clinical impact of GNRI reversibility after M-TEER.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to investigate the association between GNRI reversibility and clinical outcomes after M-TEER and identify factors predicting early GNRI improvement.MethodsData from 1,909 patients enrolled in the Japanese multicenter registry whose GNRI values were measured before and 1 month after undergoing M-TEER were retrospectively reviewed. Changes in GNRI (Δ-GNRI) were calculated and divided into two groups, namely improved GNRI (Δ-GNRI >0) and worsening GNRI (Δ-GNRI ≤0) groups. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with improvement in Δ-GNRI. Cox regression analysis was used to examine associations with long-term all-cause mortality or composite outcome.ResultsAmong the 1,909 patients, GNRI improved in 54.8% of patients. The median follow-up period was 1.3 years; 433 patients died during the follow-up period. The Δ-GNRI improvement was significantly associated with younger age, achievement of acute procedural success, lower high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and preprocedural high GNRI value (all P < 0.05), and was significantly associated with a lower risk of death and composite outcome.ConclusionsThe Δ-GNRI is a useful predictor of long-term prognosis, with reversibility observed in half of patients after M-TEER. Improvement in Δ-GNRI is associated with acute procedural success, underscoring the importance of assessing the potential for procedural success before M-TEER.Key words: early reversibility, Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, mitral regurgitation, predictor, risk stratification, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

Mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) is an established minimally invasive and effective treatment for patients with significant mitral regurgitation (MR).1,2 A Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is considered to reflect the nutritional status of the elderly population.3 Low GNRI has been reported as a poor prognostic factor in heart failure (HF) patients in meta-analyses, showing the prognostic impact of malnutrition status.4,5 Patients with a low GNRI have also worse outcomes compared to those with a high GNRI after M-TEER.6,7 However, in the low GNRI group, clinical outcomes were shown to be better in patients who underwent M-TEER than in those managed conservatively with optimal medical therapy.8 This evidence supports the effectiveness of M-TEER even in malnourished patients, emphasizing that preoperative malnutrition should not be a contraindication for treatment. Among patients with the low-GNRI group, which is generally associated with poor prognosis, M-TEER may have the potential to improve GNRI values through a therapeutic response. Capturing early reversibility in GNRI could provide new insights beyond traditional prognostic stratification studies based on preoperative GNRI levels. However, there are no reports on the trajectory of nutritional indices after M-TEER, and the impact of dynamic changes in GNRI on prognosis and predictive factors associated with changes in GNRI are unclear.

This study aimed: 1) to elucidate the association between reversibility in GNRI and clinical outcomes after M-TEER, stratified by pre-M-TEER GNRI status; and 2) to identify the predictive clinical factors associated with early reversibility in GNRI after M-TEER.

Methods

Study sample and definitions of nutritional status

All clinical information was extracted from the Japanese multicenter OCEAN (Optimized transcatheter valvular intervention)-Mitral registry. The OCEAN-Mitral registry consists of 21 Japanese institutions, and prospectively enrolling the patient data underwent M-TEER for significant MR.9,10 The University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry has registered this study with the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (UMIN000023653). All patients provided informed consent before the M-TEER procedure, and the study protocol was centrally approved by the Certified Review Board of Keio University (approval number: 20130023), and subsequently approved by the Institutional Review Board of each participating institution. The study was conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines for epidemiological studies issued by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare of Japan.

Between April 2018 and June 2023, 3,764 consecutive patients with symptomatic MR underwent M-TEER. GNRI was defined as a tool that provided a generally accepted clinical definition of malnutrition.3,8,11 Of all, 1909 patients had serum albumin, body height, and body weight measured before and 1 month after M-TEER. The method for evaluation of GNRI had already been reported (GNRI = 1.489 x albumin [g/l] + 41.7 x body weight [kg]/ideal body weight [kg]) and, as in previous study, in patients with preprocedural body weight greater than ideal body weight, we set body weight/ideal body weight ratio = 1.3 The dynamic change of the value (Δ) was calculated as follows: (value at 1-month after M-TEER–preprocedural value) x 100/preprocedural value. The cohort patients were divided into worsening nutritional status (Δ-GNRI ≤0) and improved nutritional status (Δ-GNRI >0) groups. In addition, since the rate of change is affected by the baseline value before M-TEER, patients were divided into 4 groups as subgroups by the baseline value of GNRI according to the previous formula3: GNRI <82 (n = 237, 12.4%), GNRI 82 to 92 (n = 568, 29.8%), GNRI 92 to 98 (n = 480, 25.1%), and GNRI >98 (n = 624, 32.7%).

M-TEER procedure

During the follow-up period, the MitraClip (Abbott Vascular) device is the only commercially available mitral transcatheter device in Japan. The MitraClip Generation 2 (G2) system was used before September 2020; thereafter, the latest version of the G4 system was introduced. M-TEER is indicated in patients with moderate-to-severe MR and for both degenerative MR (DMR) and functional MR (FMR) etiology. Baseline MR severity was defined based on the American Society of Echocardiography guideline,12 and after the M-TEER procedure was assessed as previously described.13 The severity of MR was classified as 0+ (none/trivial), 1+ (mild), 2+ (moderate), 3+ (moderate to severe), and 4+ (severe).

The detailed information of the M-TEER procedure has been previously described.9,10 All M-TEER procedures were performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. An acceptable MR reduction after clipping the mitral valve was defined as MR ≤2+ using peri-operative TEE or postoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) findings. The acute procedural success (APS) of M-TEER was determined as the maintenance of procedural safety without life-threatening complications and an adequate reduction of MR ≤2+ in TTE at discharge based on a previous formula and our data.9,10

Clinical outcome measures and definitions

Clinical variables with baseline patient characteristics, laboratory examinations, TTE, and TEE findings were examined at individual centers. Clinical follow-up was conducted annually after M-TEER, including baseline and 1 month. At each visit, patients were checked for the presence of HF hospitalization (HFH) after M-TEER. If patients could not visit a hospital, clinical data, including death or HFH information, were collected through telephone interviews with the patients, their family members, or their relatives. Clinical variables were compared with the improved or worsening group in GNRI. The primary endpoint of the study was the incidence of all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was the composite outcome of all-cause mortality or HFH after M-TEER.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables unless otherwise indicated. Data comparisons among the groups were made using chi-square tests for categorical covariates and Student’s t-test or using the Mann-Whitney U test depending on the variable’s distribution for continuous covariates. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and 95% CIs were reported as appropriate. To explore the factors associated with improvement Δ-GNRI, we performed multivariable logistic regression analysis using Δ-GNRI improvement as the dependent variable. The OR was calculated by model adjustment including age, sex, and clinical variables in the univariable analysis (P < 0.05).

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to generate event-free survival curves and differences in mortality and composite outcome were assessed with the log-rank test. A univariable Cox regression analysis was performed to obtain the HR for long-term all-cause mortality and composite outcome during the follow-up period. Thereafter, a multivariable analysis was performed using the baseline clinical characteristics and other variables with a univariable P < 0.05 to examine the independent association between changes in GNRI and long-term mortality and composite outcome. To evaluate the association between Δ-GNRI and mortality, a multivariable Cox regression analysis with restricted cubic splines was fitted, with knots placed at the quartiles of the Δ-GNRI distribution. The reference value was set at 0%, corresponding to a HR of 1.0. The cutoff value of the Δ-GNRI for predicting mortality was determined by a classification and regression tree survival model.14

Improvements in the predictive accuracy of changes in GNRI compared with the conventional prediction of survival using the clinical model were determined by calculating the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) based on a logit model.15 To further assess the clinical utility of each model, decision curve analysis was performed. Net benefit was estimated across a range of threshold probabilities from 0 to 1, in increments of 0.05. The resulting decision curves were compared with 2 default strategies: treating all patients and treating none. To estimate 95% CIs, bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations was conducted.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15 (Stata Corporation) and EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), which is a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).16

Results

Baseline characteristics and procedural variables

Baseline characteristics associated with changes in GNRI are shown in Table 1. GNRI improved in 54.8% of patients 1 month after M-TEER compared to before M-TEER. Several clinical characteristics differed in the group with improved Δ-GNRI, including younger age, higher prevalence of FMR and previous history of HFH, and poor nutrition at baseline. TTE parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction and left ventricular end-systolic diameter also showed significant differences (all P < 0.05). The procedural variables are presented in Table 2. The achievement rate of APS was significantly higher and the incidence of access site-related complications was significantly lower in patients with improved Δ-GNRI (all P < 0.05). Association between Δ-GNRI and clinical outcomes

The distribution of change rates of GNRI is shown in Central Illustration. Among the 1,909 patients, the median change rate of GNRI was +1.3% (IQR: −2.9% to +6.1%; minimum, −53.2%; maximum, +40.0%). The baseline GNRI value was 93.0 ± 9.2 and the proportion of GNRI ≤98 was 67.3%, while the GNRI value at 1 month after M-TEER was 94.3 ± 9.1 and the proportion of GNRI ≤98 was 62.3%. The median follow-up period was 1.3 years (IQR: 1.0-2.7 years) for the entire cohort, and 433 patients (22.7%) died during the follow-up period. Kaplan-Meier curves followed by log-rank tests showed a significant increase in all-cause mortality in the Δ-GNRI worsening group (log-rank test, P = 0.0027) (Central Illustration), and all showed the same trend when stratified by baseline GNRI values (log-rank test, all P < 0.05) (Figure 1). Patients in the Δ-GNRI improvement group were also associated with a better prognosis for the composite outcome of all-cause mortality or HFH (log-rank test, P = 0.0275), as was all-cause mortality (log-rank test, P = 0.0027). In stratification by baseline GNRI values, Δ-GNRI improvement was associated with significantly better prognosis (log-rank test, all P < 0.05), except for the group with a pre-M-TEER GNRI >98 (Figure 2). In addition, Δ-GNRI improvement showed a trend toward improved prognosis in both FMR and DMR, but no significant difference was observed in DMR (Supplemental Figure 1).

The results of the Cox regression analysis are presented in Table 4. In the univariable Cox regression, the Δ-GNRI improvement was significantly associated with a lower risk of death (HR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.62-0.9; P = 0.002) and composite outcome (HR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.72-0.98; P = 0.028) in the entire cohort. This result was not attenuated even after adjustment for multiple confounding factors for both all-cause mortality (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.38-0.63; P < 0.001) and composite outcome (HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.51-0.77; P < 0.001). Based on the findings from the Cox regression with cubic spline analysis, the nonuniform specific curve of the expected log transformed HR for all-cause mortality was drawn in the Δ-GNRI (Central Illustration). The cutoff value of Δ-GNRI for all-cause mortality in the entire cohort was −10.4% and Kaplan-Meier curves showed a significant increase in all-cause mortality with a greater reduction in GNRI between the two groups separated by cutoff values (Supplemental Figure 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the clinical impact of early GNRI reversibility for predicting the long-term prognosis of patients undergoing M-TEER. There were three important findings in this study. First, the value of GNRI showed improvement in 54.8% of patients after 1 month of M-TEER and the characteristics of patients who did not improve GNRI were older age, lower high-sensitivity CRP, and higher preprocedural GNRI values. Emphatically, Δ-GNRI improvement was significantly associated with the achievement of APS after M-TEER. Second, Δ-GNRI was significantly associated with all-cause mortality and composite endpoints in the long term after M-TEER. These results remain consistent regardless of the preprocedural GNRI values. Third, Δ-GNRI significantly improved the prognostic prediction for all-cause mortality compared to preprocedural GNRI value and its component of albumin or Δ-albumin.

M-TEER is a therapeutic approach for MR that contributes to improved cardiac function, particularly through reverse remodeling of the left ventricle and enhancement of left atrial function, as reported in previous studies.17,18 In addition to these established structural and functional improvements, our study highlights the prognostic value of nutritional status reversibility, as assessed by Δ-GNRI, providing a complementary perspective on patient outcomes after M-TEER. The significant predictive factors of Δ-GNRI improvement are younger age, lower preprocedural GNRI value, achievement of APS, and lower high-sensitivity CRP after M-TEER. It is not surprising that younger patients show greater improvements, though this appears to be an unmodifiable factor. It was also demonstrated that patients with preoperative malnutrition have more chance for GNRI improvement. This interesting result further supports the concept that preoperative malnutrition should not be a contraindication for treatment eligibility. GNRI changes early after the procedure depending on the success or failure of M-TEER, suggesting that it may be a potential therapeutic target. In a previous study, the change in grip strength after M-TEER was evaluated, and the reversibility of grip strength was more observed in patients with APS.19 The current study further supports this finding, as APS was associated with improvement in GNRI, suggesting that frailty may also improve with treatment responsiveness. A possible reason why the APS after M-TEER is associated with improvement in Δ-GNRI is the influence of changes in serum albumin, a component of GNRI. Serum albumin has traditionally been used as a nutritional indicator, but the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition has recently reported that inflammation has a significant impact on albumin levels.20,21 If GNRI, of which albumin is a component, indicates changes in inflammation related to HF before and after M-TEER, changes in GNRI and albumin may reflect the success or failure of M-TEER. In any case, the APS is the only modifiable factor that physicians may improve. It should be emphasized that in highly malnourished patients with preprocedural GNRI <82, those with no improvement in GNRI after M-TEER had a markedly poorer prognosis reaching about 60% mortality rate at 1 year. When performing M-TEER in patients with malnutrition like GNRI <82, careful preoperative screening and decision are required to determine whether APS can be achieved.

GNRI is useful for preprocedure risk stratification because it reflects systemic health status, including nutritional status and inflammation at the time of measurement. According to data from a randomized controlled trial and a clinical observational study, the prevalence of malnutrition (GNRI ≤98) was reported as 17% and 47.9%,7,8 respectively, both of which are lower than the 67.3% observed in the present study. This discrepancy may be partially attributed to the small body size of the Japanese population. Although we calculated GNRI using the commonly applied formula,3 which caps the ratio of body weight to ideal weight at 1 when body mass index (BMI) exceeds 22 kg/m2. This adjustment reduces the risk of underestimating the prevalence of malnutrition among HF patients where fluid retention can mask weight loss or exaggerate BMI values. Given such methodological variability, GNRI thresholds should be interpreted in context. Rather than focusing solely on absolute values, clinical attention should shift toward its utility in risk stratification and the potential for reversibility. However, despite several studies linking GNRI to outcomes after M-TEER, changes in GNRI over time remain largely unexplored. The association between Δ-GNRI and mortality risk after M-TEER is consistent with previous reports on pacemaker implantation22 and hemodialysis;23 however, the clinical interpretation of malnutrition may vary among diseases. Although evidence regarding the association between improved Δ-GNRI and long-term prognosis after M-TEER remains unclear, an analysis of patients in the OCEAN-Mitral registry for this purpose elucidates that improved Δ-GNRI at 1 month after M-TEER is associated with a lower risk of death and composite endpoints. These results are consistent regardless of the preprocedural GNRI value undergoing M-TEER. If the GNRI improves after M-TEER treatment, it suggests that patients with low preoperative GNRI, who are in a state of malnutrition, can receive a survival benefit. As shown in Supplemental Figure 1, an improvement in GNRI was associated with better prognosis in patients with FMR. In DMR, a similar trend was observed, although the difference did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the smaller sample size. Further studies are needed to confirm this association in DMR. Although classifying improvement and worsening using a Δ-GNRI value of zero as there is a possibility of overestimation, it is necessary to determine an appropriate cutoff value. Using the Survival classification and regression tree method, −10.4 was identified as the optimal threshold for prognostic stratification in this study. Thus, a decline in GNRI of more than 10% may be associated with worse prognosis. On the other hand, it must be taken into account that the cutoff value varies depending on the target population. It should be emphasized that the spline curve for all-cause mortality shows that prognosis progressively improves as Δ-GNRI shifts from worsening to improving. This suggests that while improving GNRI is meaningful, maintaining it without further decline may also be crucial in optimizing patient outcomes. This fact supports the importance of observing GNRI changes, in addition to pre-M-TEER risk stratification. It offers further evidence for the usefulness of this approach to assess efficacy after M-TEER.

Δ-GNRI improvement is necessary to predict prognosis after M-TEER and may serve as an indicator to determine the procedural benefit of M-TEER. Δ-GNRI also showed significant improvement in predicting long-term prognosis after M-TEER. This improvement was also observed when the preprocedural GNRI value was added to the prediction model, with greater improvement for GNRI than for albumin. This may be due to the addition of information on weight change, as GNRI is calculated from albumin, height, and weight; M-TEER may have improved nutritional status by improving MR symptoms and intestinal congestion and increasing food intake, or it may have promoted appetite recovery by improving inflammation. The results suggest that weight changes may also be important for a more accurate prognosis.

Study limitations

Several important limitations are inherent to the current registry data. First, this was a retrospective, single Japanese cohort, nonrandomized, unblinded, observational study, which might introduce a traditional bias similar to previous cohort studies. Therefore, the results and cut-off values may not be generalizable to other populations, and population-specific thresholds should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to validate the prognostic utility of Δ-GNRI across diverse cohorts. Second, a substantial proportion of patients (49%) had missing GNRI data at 30 days postprocedure. These patients were found to have more comorbidities and a worse clinical profile (Supplemental Table 1), suggesting a potential selection bias. This limitation may affect the generalizability of the findings, and future studies should aim to include such high-risk populations in follow-up assessments. Third, the multivariable analysis did not capture unpredictable factors, although considerably important clinical variables were included. Fourth, the measurement of change in nutritional status is assessed using only the GNRI and is not compared to other validated nutritional indicators. Finally, owing to the nature of the observational design, the causal relationship between Δ-GNRI and study outcomes cannot be addressed due to possible residual confounding. Nevertheless, the clinical significance of this study is that it demonstrates the value of changes in nutritional indicators in predicting long-term prognosis after discharge of patients undergoing M-TEER, using a relatively large cohort.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates the value of reversibility in GNRI as a predictor of long-term prognosis in patients undergoing M-TEER. Given that both GNRI components—albumin and BMI—are easy to measure and calculate, this single index serves as an excellent predictor of long-term outcomes. Δ-GNRI improvement is associated with APS of M-TEER, highlighting the importance of predicting the potential for procedural success before the procedure. These findings suggest that improved GNRI is essential for predicting prognosis after M-TEER and should be examined to identify the GNRI reversibility as a therapeutic target.

COMPETENCY IN PRACTICE-BASED LEARNING: M-TEER is a less invasive treatment for patients with MR. Reports on the association between change in GNRI, a nutrition-related index, and mortality after M-TEER are limited. Absolute Δ-GNRI improvement is associated with the achievement of APS in M-TEER and is associated with long-term mortality regardless of preprocedural GNRI values. Improvement in GNRI is necessary to predict prognosis after M-TEER and should be examined to identify the GNRI reversibility as a therapeutic target.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: As the multicenter registry report of M-TEER, our data indicate that post-treatment changes in GNRI values are useful markers of all-cause mortality. To further clarify the causal relationship between GNRI changes and prognosis, it is necessary to examine the prognostic impact of GNRI changes due to pre-M-TEER interventions, which could clarify treatment priorities.
