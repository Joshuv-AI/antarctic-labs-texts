Community pharmacy-led point-of-care testing (POCT): expanding roles and strengthening health systems in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)
Authors: Khaled Alshorman, Rabia Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPoint-of-care testing (POCT) offers an important opportunity to strengthen primary healthcare in low – and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to laboratory diagnostics is limited. A pharmacy-led POCT framework has been designed to guide the systematic integration of POCT into community pharmacy practice, aiming to address diagnostic gaps, enhance early detection, and improve healthcare delivery. Drawing on global and regional evidence, the article highlights the growing role of pharmacists in providing POCT for communicable and non-communicable diseases, demonstrating positive impacts on clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction, and health system efficiency. However, barriers such as regulatory constraints, limited training, supply chain challenges, and inadequate reimbursement hinder wider adoption. In this article, we discuss the opportunities and challenges of pharmacy-led POCT in LMICs using the framework and propose key policy and practice recommendations for sustainable implementation. Strengthening pharmacy-led POCT through this structured approach can advance health equity, particularly in underserved areas, and can contribute to more resilient health systems.KEYWORDS: Point-of-care testing (POCT), community pharmacy, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), non-communicable diseases (NCDs), POCT framework

Introduction

The National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry (NACB) defines Point-of-care testing (POCT) as laboratory testing conducted near the patient, often by healthcare personnel without formal laboratory training, or even by patients themselves (Nichols et al., 2020). Also known as ‘near patient testing,' ‘bedside testing,' ‘decentralised testing,' or ‘patient self-management' (Howson et al., 2017; Lingervelder et al., 2019; Luppa et al., 2021). POCT requires minimal sample preparation and can use a variety of sample types, including whole blood, urine, or swabs (nasal or vaginal), depending on the platform. It tests one sample at a time, requires no specialised technical skills, and provides rapid results (Wiencek & Nichols, 2016).

POCT is highly relevant to primary care as it supports timely clinical decision-making and enhances the overall efficiency of patient management (Lingervelder et al., 2019). In primary care settings, where early diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial, POCT allows healthcare providers to perform diagnostic tests during the patient visit, with immediate results (Lingervelder et al., 2019). This helps reduce delays associated with laboratory processing, minimises the need for additional appointments, and often eliminates unnecessary referrals to secondary or specialised care. Patient satisfaction is a simple and cost-effective way to assess the quality and effectiveness of healthcare, as it reflects the ongoing improvement of healthcare services (Hayek et al., 2021). POCT enhances patient satisfaction by providing prompt answers, enabling clearer communication, and providing immediate reassurance or treatment adjustments. As a result, POCT contributes to better health outcomes, optimised resource use, and more streamlined primary healthcare services (Florkowski et al., 2017).

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face significant challenges in delivering effective healthcare services due to issues such as limited accessibility, affordability, workforce shortages, lack of trained workforce, and inadequate infrastructure (Phelan et al., 2022). Due to an unequal distribution of healthcare resources in many LMICs, The Lancet Commission on Diagnostics and the World Health Organisation (WHO) report that 47% of the global population, approximately 2.9 billion people, lack access to essential diagnostic services (Fleming et al., 2021). In primary healthcare settings across LMICs, only 19% of the six essential tests are consistently available (Fleming et al., 2021). These tests are considered foundational for effective healthcare delivery and include: blood glucose testing, urine dipstick tests, haemoglobin measurement to screen for anaemia, malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) tests, and syphilis screening tests. These issues are further exacerbated by poor working conditions, limited training opportunities, and the ongoing migration of skilled workers to wealthier nations (Frost et al., 2021). The WHO defines a strong and effective health system as one that is built on six key components: (a) effective and timely provision of healthcare services; (b) a well-trained and competent healthcare workforce; (c) a dependable and accurate health information system; (d) reliable access to essential medicines and healthcare supplies; (e) a robust health financing system and (f) effective leadership with strong governance (Frost et al., 2021). For many LMICs, reaching these goals can appear to be an overwhelming challenge due to limited resources and underdeveloped healthcare infrastructure.

Expanding the role of community pharmacies is vital to strengthening healthcare access and outcomes, particularly in LMICs (Smith, 2009). Pharmacists can offer services such as chronic disease monitoring, medication reviews, immunisation delivery, health screenings, and patient education (Salim & Elgizoli, 2016). Integrating POCT into these services allows pharmacists to conduct rapid, on-site tests for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) like diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia and communicable diseases (CDs) like COVID-19, Influenza and sexually transmitted infections such as HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea. This enables early detection, timely intervention, and efficient referral when needed (Albasri et al., 2020). Pharmacist-led interventions, including POCT, have been shown to enhance clinical outcomes and lower healthcare costs (Kefale et al., 2024; Tonin et al., 2021; Umeh et al., 2025). In areas with limited physician coverage, pharmacists using POCT can help to improve healthcare equity. For effective role expansion, supportive regulations, professional training, integration of the healthcare team, and public engagement are necessary (Smith, 2009). This article seeks to develop a pharmacy-led framework for integrating POCT into community pharmacy practice in LMICs and to propose key policy and practice recommendations for its sustainable implementation.

Global evidence on POCT in community pharmacies

The global landscape of community pharmacy provides a comprehensive overview of how community pharmacies are evolving worldwide, transitioning from conventional roles focused on dispensing medications to more integrated providers of primary healthcare services (Hussain & Babar, 2023). In high-income countries (HICs), POCT has been widely implemented and is associated with demonstrated clinical, economic, and public health benefits. In the United States of America (USA), pharmacy-based testing for influenza and Group A Streptococcus (GAS) has improved antimicrobial stewardship and expedited treatment (Klepser et al., 2016). Community pharmacists in Australia routinely offer HbA1c, cholesterol, and blood glucose testing, especially in rural and remote areas, thus enhancing access for underserved populations (Hussain & Babar, 2023; Mitchell et al., 2011). In the United Kingdom (UK), National Health Service (NHS) backed pilots enabled pharmacies to deliver cholesterol, HbA1c, cardiovascular risk assessments, and COVID-19 antigen testing, supporting early detection and referral (Bond, 2007; Lowrie et al., 2014). The community pharmacists in Spain have started offering POCT for HIV, targeting individuals who had never previously been tested (Fernández-Balbuena et al., 2015). Similarly, France has also expanded pharmacy services to include influenza, COVID-19 antigen testing, blood glucose, and cholesterol POCT, with strong public uptake (Essilini et al., 2021).

There is an emerging trend which demonstrates strong and growing evidence that POCT in community pharmacies serves as a feasible, effective, and cost-efficient strategy for improving public health outcomes in LMICs (Dhindsa, 2025). POCT services in pharmacies address both communicable and non-communicable diseases by enabling early detection, improving referral pathways, and reducing inappropriate medication use, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

For communicable diseases (CDs), several studies from LMIC reported high acceptability and diagnostic accuracy of POCT in pharmacy settings. In South Africa (SA), community pharmacists used HIV rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) to deliver accessible, high-uptake testing with appropriate counselling and referral services. Similarly, malaria rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) are implemented in Nigerian pharmacies, helping reduce inappropriate antimalarial use and improving diagnostic clarity (Ezennia et al., 2017; Sloot et al., 2020). A multi-pathogen study in Uganda involving 450 participants across 18 pharmacies showed high feasibility and acceptance (99.8%) of POCT for HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis, especially among high-risk populations such as symptomatic individuals and those engaging in transactional sex (Kiragga et al., 2024). In the Middle East countries like Lebanon and Jordan, pharmacies increasingly provided COVID-19 rapid antigen tests during the pandemic, a sign of potential pharmacist-led POCT expansion in infectious disease screening in that region (Jarab et al., 2022; Sakr et al., 2021).

For non-communicable diseases (NCDs), POCT using glucometers and HbA1c analysers has been adopted for screening and monitoring diabetes. In Pakistan, pharmacists conducted opportunistic glucose testing, leading to the identification of undiagnosed diabetes and referral for care (Primary Care Diabetes Association Pakistan, 2025). In South Africa (SA), HbA1c POCT was employed to monitor diabetes control and screen for new cases, showing high patient acceptability and utility in primary care support (Sonday et al., 2022). A study from Haiti showed that POCT can be used to screen multiple non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in both rural and urban clinics using tests for HbA1c, creatinine, and lipids, combined with measurements of body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and blood pressure (Malcolm et al., 2019). The findings revealed that 93% of diabetes and 39% of hypertension cases were previously undiagnosed, and 26.3% had metabolic syndrome. This approach, which provided same-day results and health education, is adaptable to pharmacy settings and highlights the practicality of POCT in low-resource contexts.

Several cost analyses have demonstrated that pharmacy-based POCT can deliver comparable clinical outcomes to traditional healthcare settings while reducing overall healthcare expenditures (Buss et al., 2019). A comparative cost analysis in South Africa (SA) evaluated pharmacy-based POCT using Rapid Influenza Diagnostic Tests (RIDTs) and Rapid Antigen Detection Tests (RADTs) for streptococcal pharyngitis, and found that pharmacy services were more cost-effective than conventional clinic-based testing (Simeon et al., 2019). Studies done in the USA and Canada show that patients managed in pharmacies for conditions like influenza or streptococcal pharyngitis can avoid more expensive physician or emergency visits, with estimated savings ranging from $25 to $70 per individual case of illness or encounter managed in the pharmacy. (Friedrich et al., 2018). These services not only reduce healthcare costs but also improve access to early treatment, leading to better outcomes and a more efficient use of healthcare resources. Evidence from the UK, the USA, and Europe suggests that pharmacy-based POCT for infectious diseases is a cost-effective alternative to conventional healthcare services (Saha et al., 2023) by enabling timely diagnosis and the appropriate use of antimicrobials. POCT in pharmacies can reduce unnecessary prescriptions, avoid follow-up visits, and prevent complications, all contributing to overall cost savings (Chan et al., 2023). For instance, modelling studies suggest that implementing POCT for respiratory infections in community pharmacies can lead to significant reductions in healthcare costs while maintaining or improving health outcomes (Cooke et al., 2020; Saha et al., 2023; Kefale et al., 2024).

Collectively, the evidence highlights the potential for integrating POCT into community pharmacies as a scalable and effective public-health strategy in LMICs. Several studies indicate that such interventions can be implemented successfully, improving treatment appropriateness and alleviating pressure on public healthcare, particularly when supported by structured training, demand generation, financial incentives, and formal linkages to care systems (Chukwuzurum et al., 2021; Mbonye et al., 2015; Kuo et al., 2022; Chan et al., 2023). Pharmacists, as the most accessible healthcare providers in many communities, are well-positioned to contribute to disease prevention, early diagnosis, and appropriate referrals (George et al., 2010). However, enabling this expanded role requires policy reforms that require training, regulation, and recognition within primary healthcare systems.

Opportunities in LMICs

In the LMICs, the concept of POCT presents significant opportunities for strengthening healthcare systems, particularly in areas where access to centralised laboratory services is limited (Katoba et al., 2019). It can enhance the capacity of primary healthcare by enabling early diagnosis, timely treatment decisions, and improved disease management in remote or underserved areas (Abel, 2015). In LMICs, these tools are especially valuable for overcoming the challenges posed by limited laboratory infrastructure (Gous et al., 2014).

The WHO ASSURED (Affordable, Sensitive, Specific, User-friendly, Rapid, Equipment-free, and Deliverable to end-users) criteria (Drain et al., 2014) ensure that POCT devices are affordable, easy to use, and effective. These tools for POCT can be used by healthcare workers with basic training, offering the flexibility to be implemented across diverse healthcare settings, including mobile clinics, community pharmacies, and small peripheral laboratories (Katoba et al., 2019).

Several initiatives have been undertaken to enhance access to POCT services, one of which is the essential diagnostics list (EDL) developed by the WHO. This list outlines key indicators for the detection of common conditions and global priority diseases such as HIV, tuberculosis (TB), syphilis, and malaria (World Health Organization, 2019). Community pharmacies, due to their accessibility, trusted role, and integration within local communities, are increasingly recognised as viable sites for POCT delivery in LMICs (Jarab et al., 2024). Pharmacists can play a crucial role in expanding diagnostic services by offering timely testing, counselling, and referral, especially for conditions prioritised in the EDL. Integrating POCT into community pharmacy practice not only enhances early disease detection and management but also helps to decongest conventional healthcare settings, improve overall health system efficiency (Kelling, 2015) and offer substantial societal benefits by reducing inappropriate use of antimicrobials, limiting the spread of infections within the community, and strengthening the ability to monitor population-level exposure to infectious agents (Gubbins et al., 2014).

Rationale for POCT in community pharmacies

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the integration of POCT within community pharmacy settings. This approach is particularly relevant in LMICs, where healthcare systems often face challenges such as limited access to diagnostic services, workforce shortages, and overburdened primary care facilities (Okunlola et al., 2025). Community pharmacies are frequently the first point of contact with the health system for many individuals in LMICs due to their widespread distribution, accessibility, and relatively low cost (Gentilini et al., 2025). Introducing POCT in these settings has the potential to help address delays in diagnosis and treatment of both communicable and non-communicable diseases. which are rising rapidly across LMICs and often go undetected in the early stages (World Health Organization, 2020).

The availability of POCT in pharmacies supports earlier identification of conditions for both communicable and non-communicable diseases, particularly in underserved or rural communities (Setyawati et al., 2024). Pharmacists can offer rapid testing, provide immediate counselling, and refer patients to appropriate care when necessary (Radley et al., 2024). This could contribute to improved disease management, better patient engagement, and reduced complications over time (Gubbins et al., 2014).

Incorporating POCT into pharmacy practice in LMICs reduces some of the pressure on overstretched health systems by shifting basic diagnostic services to more accessible community-based settings (Heidt et al., 2020). Implementation should be carefully aligned with regulatory frameworks, health system capacity, and pharmacist training. Early evidence suggests this model may offer a cost-effective and scalable solution to help close gaps in service delivery (Witry, 2025).

Framework overview

The successful integration of pharmacy-led POCT in LMICs requires a structured framework that is both evidence-based and adaptable to diverse healthcare contexts. Over the past decade, several studies have consistently highlighted the growing role of POCT in community pharmacies, demonstrating its feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and impact on clinical outcomes. For instance, Albasri et al. (2020) reported that pharmacy-based POCT significantly improves treatment appropriateness and reduces unnecessary antibiotic use. Similarly, recent studies provide comprehensive evidence on POCT in private pharmacies and retail settings, confirming its utility in enhancing antimicrobial stewardship and early diagnosis, and further emphasising the role of pharmacies in expanding access to NCD diagnostics and monitoring, particularly in underserved populations (Chan et al., 2023; Gentilini et al., 2025). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that POCT contributes to earlier detection, improved patient satisfaction, and better resource utilisation across both communicable and non-communicable diseases.

Despite the positive results, several barriers constrain the implementation of POCT in community pharmacies, including inadequate pharmacist training, inconsistent regulatory frameworks, and limited financial incentives, potentially affecting service scalability and sustainability (Jarab et al., 2024; Umeh et al., 2025). Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive model that aligns with the WHO’s health system building blocks and integrates lessons from high, middle, and low-income countries. Therefore, the framework presented in this article seeks to provide a structured approach that supports policymakers, professional bodies, and practitioners in scaling up POCT services sustainably across LMICs.

The comprehensive model for POCT framework

To successfully implement POCT in community pharmacies, policymakers must adopt a comprehensive framework that aligns with national health strategies and ensures sustainability. This proposed comprehensive model reflects best practices recommended by global health authorities such as the WHO to promote scalable, safe, and patient-centred diagnostic services in both high, middle and low-resource settings (World Health Organisation, 2025). Service delivery model

The service delivery model defines how POCT is operationalised, who delivers it, where, and how it is integrated into routine care. In HICs, the POCT is often embedded in primary care, urgent care clinics, and pharmacies (Fernández-Balbuena et al., 2015). In some HIC settings, such as the USA, certain POCT platforms are authorised for self or caregiver administration without prescriptions, providing alternative delivery models that LMICs may adapt with safeguards for counselling, data reporting, and referral (Dima, 2021). The MICs may deliver POCT through task-shifting models involving trained non-physician staff in rural health centres. In LICs, community health workers are crucial, especially for maternal, child health and infectious disease control. This model must consider workforce availability, health literacy, and physical accessibility (Ezennia et al., 2017). A decentralised model improves access and coverage but requires strong coordination mechanisms. Barriers, including limited workforce capacity, resistance from other healthcare professionals, and pharmacist workload-related challenges, often hinder implementation (Jarab et al., 2024). Task-shifting, clear referral pathways, and interprofessional collaboration can help mitigate these issues.

Quality management and reporting

This component encompasses governance, accountability, audit mechanisms, and continuous improvement processes (Tawade et al., 2023). HICs implement comprehensive quality management systems with regular audits, incident reporting, and performance dashboards (Zhang et al., 2024). MICs may apply selective quality management, often dependent on donor-funded programmes. In LICs, external partners often lead quality initiatives, which may lack long-term sustainability. Embedding local leadership and capacity building is essential for continuity. Establishing clear reporting channels helps escalate issues, track indicators, and drive improvements over time (Zhang et al., 2024).

Training and competency

The POCT is only as good as the personnel performing it. HICs have formalised training programmes, certification, and ongoing competency assessments (Yenice, 2021). MICs often rely on short-term workshops and online training but may lack formal credentialing. LICs typically depend on basic on-site training due to limited human resources (HR) and funding, but task-shifting to community health workers is a key innovation. Building local training capacity and standardising competencies are critical for sustainability and scaling. Training must include not just device use, but also interpretation, biosafety, patient communication, and documentation (Shrivastava et al., 2023). While formal training programmes exist for many POCTs in HICs, not all tests require certified training, particularly those designed for home use or available over the counter. Moreover, many community pharmacists may not be fully trained in all aspects of POCT, such as the processes involved in STI testing (Bernknopf et al., 2025). Many pharmacists in LMICs lack adequate training and ongoing support to deliver POCT effectively. Structured training programmes, certification schemes, and continuous professional development (CPD) are essential to ensure competency and sustainability.

Regulatory and policy environment

A strong regulatory framework ensures the quality, safety, and legal authorisation of POCT devices and procedures. In HICs, these regulations are robust, with bodies like the food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medicines Agency (EMA) setting standards (Essilini et al., 2021). The MICs may have evolving frameworks, often influenced by the WHO guidelines and donor agencies (Kumar et al., 2025). The LICs face challenges such as fragmented regulations, limited enforcement, and dependence on external policies from non-governmental organisations (NGOs) or multilateral bodies. A supportive policy environment ensures POCT is integrated into national health strategies and reimbursed properly (Fleming et al., 2021). Weak or fragmented regulatory frameworks, unclear pharmacist roles, and a lack of reimbursement remain significant barriers in many LMICs (Chan et al., 2023). These challenges can be addressed by implementing strong, transparent regulations, introducing accreditation systems, and linking reimbursement mechanisms to POCT services.

Technology and equipment

This involves selecting appropriate technology and equipment. HICs use high-end, networked devices that link to electronic health records (EHRs) (Yadav et al., 2018). Integration of POCT results into a patient’s EHR is not universally implemented, particularly in the USA, where the feasibility of entering POCT results into the EHR depends on factors such as the availability of connectivity, the type of testing device, and existing infrastructure (Fung, 2020). Another example is COVID-19 testing in community pharmacies, which is typically tracked within the pharmacy’s own systems rather than the patient’s EHR. MICs employ a mix of these, including some high-end devices in urban hospitals and simpler ones in rural clinics. LICs often rely on portable, battery-operated POCT kits with minimal maintenance requirements due to power and infrastructure limitations. Overall, the technology must be affordable, user-friendly, and validated for local conditions, including temperature, humidity, and dust exposure (Van Stralen et al., 2009). Supply chain disruptions, expired or substandard kits, and limited infrastructure present major barriers in many LMICs. To address these, governments and stakeholders should prioritise WHO-prequalified devices, while strengthening procurement and distribution systems, and investing in local infrastructure to ensure continuity.

Quality assurance and control

Quality Assurance and Control of POCT ensures the ongoing accuracy, reliability, and safety of test procedures. HICs follow rigorous internal and external quality control (QC) protocols regulated by accreditation bodies. MICs may have partial QC systems, especially in urban centres, but face challenges in rural or remote areas. LICs often lack routine quality checks, risking false results and treatment errors (Tawade et al., 2023). External Quality Assessment (EQA) programmes and training are essential for all countries (Kostyanev et al., 2025), but especially critical for LICs and MICs to maintain trust in POCT. Without quality assurance, scaling POCT may cause more harm than benefit. The absence of consistent quality assurance, reliance on donor-funded programmes, and lack of sustainable oversight are major barriers. Introducing national quality assurance systems, standard operating procedures, and EQA programmes can ensure reliability and build trust in POCT services.

Purpose and scope

This defines the overall goals and target population of the POCT initiative (Heidt et al., 2020). In HICs, the primary focus is often on efficiency, reducing hospital burden, decreasing unnecessary emergency room (ER) and clinic visits, and enhancing chronic disease monitoring. The scope of practice varies significantly between countries due to differences in regulations, workforce training, and healthcare infrastructure (Hussain & Babar, 2023). The MICs may aim to extend diagnostic coverage to underserved urban and rural areas. In Low-Income Countries (LICs), the scope is frequently centred on accessibility for infectious diseases (e.g. malaria, HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea) due to weak central laboratory systems (Gentilini et al., 2025). Clearly defining the scope ensures the POCT service is aligned with national health priorities and tailored to the healthcare infrastructure. In many LMICs, the scope of pharmacy-led POCT is often unclear due to the absence of national guidelines or defined health priorities (Mohiuddin, 2019). To address this, governments should establish clear scope statements within national policies so that POCT services are aligned with country-specific needs and resources.

Data management and reporting

Effective POCT requires real-time data capture, integration into health records, and secure reporting mechanisms. HICs leverage advanced information technology (IT) infrastructure for automated uploads and artificial intelligence (AI) based analytics (Pillay et al., 2025). MICs may utilise digital health applications, although fragmentation and a lack of interoperability are common. LICs struggle with paper-based systems and weak digital infrastructure (Kiragga et al., 2024), which hinders data accuracy and disease surveillance. Strengthening data reporting improves accountability, supports public health responses, and allows evidence-based decision-making. Investments in mobile health (mHealth) systems are helping bridge gaps in some LIC settings (Lingervelder et al., 2019). Weak digital infrastructure and fragmented reporting systems undermine disease surveillance in many LMICs. Investment in mHealth solutions and interoperable reporting platforms adapted to pharmacy settings is needed to improve accountability and public health response.

Conclusion

Community pharmacies represent an underutilised but highly accessible platform for expanding diagnostic services, particularly in LMICs where laboratory infrastructure is limited. The integration of POCT into pharmacy practice has shown promising results in improving early detection and management of a wide range of health conditions. Despite this progress, the full potential of pharmacies in strengthening diagnostic access remains largely untapped. Therefore, a coordinated, multi-sectoral collaboration involving governments, professional pharmacy bodies, NGOs, academic institutions, and public health stakeholders is needed. Together, these participants must work to establish enabling policies, ensure quality standards, and invest in the pharmacy workforce to position community pharmacies as integral components of resilient, patient-centred primary healthcare systems in LMICs.
