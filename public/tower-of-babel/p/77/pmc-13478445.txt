Modeling treatment effects on absorbing outcomes in clinical trials: Leveraging longitudinal and ordinal data for efficiency gains
Authors: Chiara Di Gravio, Ran Tao, Yunbi Nam, Eric P Schmidt, Nathan Shapiro, Jonathan S Schildcrout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Randomized clinical trials in critically ill populations often use mortality at the end of follow-up as the primary outcome. When mortality is rare, composite outcomes that capture disease severity over time are frequently employed to improve efficiency. Motivated by the Crystalloid Liberal or Vasopressor Early Resuscitation in Sepsis (CLOVERS) trial—a randomized study comparing two fluid resuscitation strategies in hospitalized patients with sepsis—we evaluate multiple modeling approaches for estimating covariate-adjusted treatment effects on absorbing outcomes such as mortality. While analyses of binary end-of-follow-up outcomes (alive vs. dead) are natural, efficiency can be gained by incorporating low-cost longitudinal and ordinal severity data routinely collected in trials and by imposing reasonable modeling restrictions (constraints). We develop methods that leverage these data within the marginalized model framework, focusing on first-order marginalized transition models, which allows direct estimation of outcome-marginal (population-averaged) treatment effects. We highlight a key property of the marginalized transition model in the presence of absorbing states: the marginal and conditional sub-models correspond to cumulative incidence and discrete hazard models, respectively. Through simulation studies and an application to the CLOVERS data, we compare the validity and efficiency of competing approaches, including standard survival analyses.

Introduction

We seek to summarize the treatment effect on disease outcomes in critically ill participants in a randomized clinical trial. A relevant outcome for such studies is mortality at the end of a follow-up period. However, mortality is often sufficiently rare that standard analyses would require excessively large sample sizes or would otherwise lack power. To address concerns about power and efficiency, composite outcomes, such as ventilator-free or oxygen-free days, are often used. These outcomes summarize disease severity over the course of follow-up and have been implemented in multiple studies.1,2 They are often modeled with ordinal regression methods with mortality being the worst/most severe outcome category. While composite outcomes can improve power to detect associations3,4 and are pragmatic for studies of critically ill participants, they have limitations. For example, when the treatment effect on mortality is different from the treatment effect on non-fatal severity, interpreting the model’s estimates can be difficult, and power can be reduced even when compared to standard cross-sectional analyses of mortality. 5

Historically, analyses of baseline covariate-adjusted treatment effects in randomized clinical trials have been conducted with binary logistic regression or with survival analysis models. Binary logistic regression has low power if the prevalence of mortality is low, and a survival analysis can improve power in the presence of dropout. However, a covariate-adjusted hazard ratio may not be of interest because it is based on analyses of a risk set that changes over time and there is an implicit conditioning on a post-randomization, ‘intermediate’ outcome (i.e. mortality has not yet occurred).6–9 Our approach to improve efficiency over a cross-sectional, binary logistic regression analysis does so by exploiting data that are commonly collected in randomized clinical trials or that could be collected at relatively low costs. Rather than modeling a cross-sectional, end of study, binary response, we can enrich analyses by leveraging daily (longitudinal), severity (ordinal) response data.10–13 We may then impose constraints or restrictions on the regression model to directly improve efficiency. From an ordinal response model perspective, we may impose proportional odds or partial proportional odds restrictions to improve precision over a more highly parameterized, general cumulative logit regression model. From a longitudinal data perspective, we may impose restrictions on the treatment effect over time to improve estimation efficiency. Notably, like the Cox proportional hazard model, 14 longitudinal data analysis can improve efficiency and robustness of a treatment effect estimator by using the timing of events and the partial information contained in participants who drop out or are lost to follow-up.15,16 Cross-sectional, binary logistic regression and ordinal regression analyses discard participant’s data prior to dropout.

In this paper, we compare approaches for estimating baseline covariate-adjusted treatment effects on mortality at the end of follow-up. In particular, we focus on longitudinal outcome-marginal models, which estimate covariate-adjusted treatment effects without conditioning on past or future outcome values as in done in standard transition models or mixed effects models.17–20 Within the broad class of outcome-marginal models, we appeal to marginalized models. Marginalized models were developed for longitudinal binary21–23 and ordinal outcomes10,11,13 as likelihood-based alternatives to semiparametric generalized estimating equations 24 and as outcome-marginal model alternatives to generalized linear mixed models and transition models.25,26 Briefly, marginalized models consists of two components: the outcome-marginal model linking the longitudinal outcome to the exposure and covariates and the conditional response, dependence model that captures the outcome dependence or correlation over-time. We will show that when the target outcome state is mortality, these two models correspond to other, more familiar models. Specifically, the outcome-marginal model corresponds to a cumulative incidence model while the conditional, response dependence model corresponds to a discrete hazard model. Thus, unlike in standard survival analysis, marginalized transition models estimate the covariate-adjusted, treatment effect on cumulative incidence of mortality directly. Similar to standard survival models, one can then obtain an end-of-study fully marginal (with respect to both outcome and baseline covariates) treatment effect by integrating over the covariates distribution.

Organization of the paper

Motivated by the Crystalloid Liberal or Vasopressor Early Resuscitation in Sepsis (CLOVERS) randomized clinical trial, we demonstrate the potential benefit of exploiting longitudinal (daily) and ordinal (health state) data to improve estimation efficiency when interest is in an end-of-follow-up treatment effect on an absorbing outcome state (e.g. death). We extend our previous work 13 to show that marginalized transition models are well-suited for this setting. We show that the two components of a marginalized transition model naturally correspond to a cumulative incidence model and a discrete hazards model, allowing direct estimation of outcome-marginal, covariate-adjusted treatment effects. Via simulation, we also show the extent to which precision and robustness to missing data can be improved compared to common analyses (e.g. cross-sectional logistic regression) when the models are correctly specified, and the extent of bias incurred when assumption choices are overly restrictive.

In the next section, we describe the CLOVERS trial. 2 In Section 3, we (i) review the first-order marginalized transition model (MTM) for longitudinal binary response data, (ii) describe its outcome-marginal and conditional, response-dependence components, (iii) discuss restrictions imposed when one of the outcome states is absorbing (e.g. death), and (iv) explore the connection between the MTM components and the more familiar cumulative incidence and discrete hazard models. In Section 4, we show an MTM extension to longitudinal, ordinal outcomes and describe how the ordinal MTM can exploit both the longitudinal and ordinal dimensions of response data to increase efficiency of the outcome-marginal, covariate-adjusted treatment effect estimate on mortality at the end of follow-up. We then conduct a comparative simulation study to examine efficiency and robustness of the estimation strategies in Section 5. In Section 6, we turn back to the CLOVERS trial and conduct analyses using the described methods. We wrap up the paper with a discussion in Section 7.

CLOVERS study: data

The CLOVERS study was a multi-center randomized clinical trial on the efficacy of two fluid resuscitation strategies in patients with sepsis. 2 The trial randomly assigned 1,563 patients to restrictive or liberal fluid resuscitation strategies, and the primary analysis was a survival analysis on mortality before discharge within 90 days after randomization. CLOVERS also conducted numerous secondary analyses that included composite outcomes such as hospital-free days and ICU-free days through 28 days post-randomization. 2 To calculate hospital-free days and ICU-free days, the study collected daily patient state data during the 28 days post-randomization and summarized them to create the outcomes. We study the setting where interest is in a cross-sectional, outcome-marginal, covariate-adjusted treatment effect on mortality at day 28. To improve precision and power over commonly used analysis approaches, we use the available daily state data to estimate this quantity. Specifically, participants were observed daily to be in one of four ordered states from randomization to day 28: discharged/home, hospital, hospital/ICU, and death. We compare treatment effect estimation strategies using cross-sectional (at day 28) and longitudinal data analysis procedures for binary and ordinal outcomes. The binary outcome combines the states discharged/home, hospital and hospital/ICU into a single alive state while the ordinal outcome considers the four states separately.

We include 1,529 of the 1,563 participants in the CLOVERS study with available daily data. Briefly, the mean age was 59.4 years (standard deviation 15.9 years) and mean sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, indicating level of organ dysfunction, was 3.39 (standard deviation 2.67). Figure 1 shows the proportion of participants in each outcome state from day 0 (randomization) to day 28. As is common in studies of critically ill participants, the proportion of participants who die and/or are discharged/at home increases with time, while the proportions of participants in the hospital and hospital/ICU states decreases. While we are interested in the outcome-marginal, baseline covariate-adjusted, cross-sectional treatment effect on day 28 mortality (yellow vs not yellow on day 28), we want to use all intermediate follow-up days and outcome severity states to improve precision.

In studies like CLOVERS, the simplest way to estimate the treatment effect on mortality at the end of follow-up is to fit a binary logistic regression model with the outcome being mortality status (yes/no) at day 28. However, we may gain efficiency by considering alternative strategies that incorporate richer outcome information into the estimation procedure. For example, we may focus analyses exclusively on day 28 but replace the binary mortality status with a four-level ordinal severity outcome (death, hospital/ICU, hospital, discharged/home); alternatively, we may exploit the complete daily trajectory from baseline to day 28 of the binary mortality status (yes/no); finally, we may use daily, severity information to fit a longitudinal, ordinal MTM. One would expect that incorporating richer outcome information into the estimation model could result in efficiency gains. However, to realize the efficiency gains, parameter constraints are likely to be required. For instance, prior work has shown that constraining the treatment effect at baseline to be equal to zero in a randomized clinical trial can improve efficiency.27,28 This paper aims to explore the potential for efficiency gains from these alternative modeling strategies compared to binary logistic regression.

Note: In studies similar to CLOVERS where interest is in estimating the treatment effect on mortality at day 28 of follow-up, Cox proportional hazard models or other parametric survival models represent valid alternatives to those described herein. Although comparing the efficiency of MTMs with Cox proportional hazard models is not the primary focus of this paper, for completeness, we include a Cox proportional hazard model in our simulation studies and data analysis.

Marginalized transition models

Let i∈{1,…,N} denote subject, tij∈{0,…,mi−1} denote follow-up day on visit j (i.e. tij=j−1 ), YYi be a mi− vector of binary response data with Yij∈{1,2} , Xi be a binary baseline variable indicating the subject i treatment assignment (i.e. Xi=0 if subject i is assigned to the control arm or Xi=1 if subject i is assigned to the treatment arm), and ZZi be a vector of baseline covariates. An outcome-marginal probability model, is given by πij=P(Yij=1∣Xi,ZZi) . We assume this model is of logistic form so that (1) logit(πij)=[α0+αttij]⏟α(tij)+[βx+βxttij]⏟βx(tij)Xi+ZZiββz. The α(tij) function captures the tij− specific log-odds that the response is at level 1 in the control arm ( Xi=0 ) when ZZi=00 . It can also be thought of as a tij− specific intercept. The outcome-marginal probability model describes the first moment of the multivariate distribution [YYi∣Xi,ZZi] . To identify higher moments, the marginalized model class specifies a conditional, response dependence structure, and here we appeal to the first order transition model of Azzalini 21 and Heagerty, 23 (2) logit(μijc)=Δij(Xi,ZZi)+γ(Xi,ZZi)I(Yi(j−1)=1), where μijc=P(Yij=1∣Yi(j−1),Xi,ZZi) is a (conditional) Markov transition probability, and γ(Xi,ZZi) is a log-odds ratio parameter that describes the strength and nature of the relationship between Yij and Yi(j−1) . The intercept, Δij(Xi,ZZi) , is an implicit function that links the outcome-marginal probability model to the conditional, response dependence model. That is, Δij(Xi,ZZi) is the value that solves (3) πij=πi(j−1)⋅μijc,1+(1−πi(j−1))⋅μijc,2, where μijc,y=P(Yij=1∣Yi(j−1)=y,Xi,ZZi) is a transition probability. Given the vector of parameters θθ=(αα,ββ,γγ)=(α0,αt,βx,βxt,ββz,γ) , and once the values of Δij(Xi,ZZi) have been calculated for all (i,j) , subject i ’s contribution to the likelihood can be identified directly with Li(θθ;YYi,Xi,ZZi)=∏j=1miLij(θθ;Yij,Yi(j−1),Xi,ZZi)=πi1I(Yi1=1)⋅(1−πi1)I(Yi1=2)⋅∏j=2mi(μijc)I(Yij=1)⋅(1−μijc)I(Yij=2). The first term includes the outcome-marginal probability at the first observed time point πi1 because the precise value of Yi0 is either not available or is included in the linear predictor. The second term includes the conditional model probability, μijc that explicitly conditions on the lagged response value. Notably, we use Yij=2 to denote a non-event which is not typical notation for binary response models. However, the notation will be consistent with the ordinal response model notation discussed later. The model parameters are estimated using a Newton–Raphson algorithm which alternates between updating θθ and updating Δij(Xi,ZZi) so that equation (3) holds. Score and information calculations for this model are detailed in Heagerty. 23

Notes about the marginalized transition model when Yij=1 is absorbing

The marginalized transition model can be used for the analysis of longitudinal binary outcome data even when outcome state Yij=1 is absorbing, that is, one may enter but cannot exit state 1 . By examining equations (1), (2), and (3), one can see that, in the presence of an absorbing state, πij is a cumulative incidence, and μijc is a discrete hazard model. To explain, when Yij=1 is absorbing, then μijc,1≡P(Yij=1∣Xi,Zi,Yi(j−1)=1) is equal to one and πij is monotonically increasing with tij because πij=πi(j−1)⋅μijc,1+(1−πi(j−1))⋅μijc,2=πi(j−1)+(1−πi(j−1))⋅μijc,2≥πi(j−1). Further, πij=1−(1−πij)=1−P(Yij=Yi(j−1)=⋯=Yi1=2∣Xi,ZZi,Yi0=2) which is a discrete cumulative incidence function or a failure cumulative distribution function. In contrast, survival analysis methods use post-estimation calculations to obtain cumulative incidence. The cumulative incidence calculation that, in standard survival analyses is done after the hazard model is fitted, is conducted within the marginalized transition model fitting routine, at each step of the Newton–Raphson procedure.

Additionally, because when Yij=1 , μijc,1=1 , the likelihood contribution for subject i at time j , becomes degenerate after entry into the absorbing state. This implies that all contributions to the likelihood must have been preceded by a non absorbing state day [ Yi(j−1)=Yi(j−2)=⋯=Yi0=2 ] and, implicitly, μijc=P(Yij=1∣Xi,ZZi,Yi(j−1)=2,Yi(j−2)=2,…Yi0=2) . Thus, if we define W to be the entry time into the absorbing state, we can write μijc=P(W=tij∣Xi,Zi,W≥tij) which is a discrete hazard.

When severity is available: Cumulative probability models

We now incorporate severity into the analysis. That is, we expand state 2 (i.e. alive) into health states with higher values denoting better health. Our interest is still in the outcome Yij=1 versus Yij>1 , but we want to include all observed states in the model to potentially improve precision of the treatment effect estimates. We let YYi be a mi− vector of ordinal response values with Yij∈{1,…,K} . In settings with an ordinal outcome, a popular model choice is the cumulative probability model (CPM) with the proportional odds (PO) assumption.12,29 Let πij,kpo=P(Yij≤k∣Xi,ZZi) , then the CPM with the PO assumption for subject i at visit j is given by (4) logit(πij,kpo)=[αkpo+αtpotij]⏟αkpo(tij)+[βxpo+βxtpotij]⏟βxpo(tij)Xi+ZZiβzpo, where the αkpo(tij) function captures the tij− specific log-odds that Yij≤k in the control arm ( Xi=0 ) when ZZi=00 , and the βxpo(tij) function captures the tij− specific difference in the log-odds between the treatment ( Xi=1 ) and the control ( Xi=0 ). For ease of exposition, we assume the log cumulative odds association with baseline covariates, ZZi , does not change with tij . The PO assumptions in model (4) imply that (i) the association between the ordinal outcome and tij does not depend on k and is captured with αtpo , and (ii) cross-sectional differences in the log-odds between treatment and control do not depend on k . If one believes that the relationship between the log cumulative odds and tij or Xi varies according to outcome level k , the PO assumption can be relaxed and partial proportional odds (PPO) model assumptions can be made 30 for which πij,kppo=P(Yij≤k∣Xi,ZZi) can be expressed as (5) logit(πij,kppo)=[αkppo+(ααtppo)Tττtij]⏟ααkppo(tij)+[(ββxppo)Tττ+(ββxtppo)Tττtij]⏟ββxppo(tij)Xi+ZZiββzppo. Non-proportionality vector ττ can be expressed, in a K -level outcome variable, [1,I(k=2),I(k=3),…I(k=K−1)]T , so that covariate-outcome associations are allowed to differ across dichotomizations of the log cumulative odds. Model (5) is considered a PPO model because the PO assumption only holds for a subset of the relationships with the K−1 log cumulative odds (e.g. for ZZi where ττ is absent). For a four-level outcome variable, the association of Xitij with the log cumulative odds is given by (ββxtppo)Tττ=[βxt,1ppo,βxt,2ppo,βxt,3ppo]ττ=βxt,1ppo+βxt,2ppoI(k=2)+βxt,3ppoI(k=3). To write this marginal CPM out explicitly, (6) logit(πij,1ppo)=[α1ppo+αt,1ppotij]+[βx,1ppo+βxt,1ppotij]Xi+ZZiββzppo, (7) logit(πij,2ppo)=[α2ppo+(αt,1ppo+αt,2ppo)tij]+[(βx,1ppo+βx,2ppo)+(βxt,1ppo+βxt,2ppo)tij]Xi+ZZiββzppo, (8) logit(πij,3ppo)=[α3ppo+(αt,1ppo+αt,3ppo)tij]+[(βx,1ppo+βx,3ppo)+(βxt,1ppo+βxt,3ppo)tij]Xi+ZZiββzppo.

ββxtppo=[βxt,1ppo,0,0] implies PO (i.e. forcing the βxt,2ppo and βxt,3ppo to be equal to 0 induces a PO assumption). Thus, we may reduce the dimensionality of the model by constraining the treatment effect to be common across outcome levels. We note that, while the PO assumption forces there to be a common treatment effect across outcome levels, it does not restrict the possible range of the effect. For example, ββxtppo=[βxt,1ppo,βxt,2ppo,βxt,2ppo] restricts the treatment effect to be common for k=2 and k=3 but permits a different treatment effect for k=1 . While including these restrictions can improve precision, one must carefully consider whether the restrictions made are reasonable (i.e. whether they will induce appreciable bias). In the presence of one absorbing state, the relationship between Yij and tij likely induces a violation of the PO assumption, and with two absorbing states, the PO assumptions is necessarily violated. For example, in the right panel of Figure 1, the PO assumption for the tij parameter would imply that the lines separating response states are parallel with one another. This assumption is clearly violated in the CLOVERS data. As a consequence, restricting ααtppo to satisfy the PO assumption will lead to a severely misspecified model.

As in the binary outcome setting, the CPMs in equations (4) and (5) only describe a portion of the multivariate distribution [YYi|Xi,ZZi] . To conduct likelihood-based analyses, we need to specify a conditional, response dependence structure that allows us to identify the remaining [YYi|Xi,ZZi] . As before, we appeal to the first order transition model which was introduced by Lee and Daniels10,11 and discussed in Schildcrout: 13 (9) log(μij,kcμij,Kc)=Δij,k(Xi,ZZi)+∑l=1K−1γkl(Xi,ZZi)I{Yi(j−1)=l}, where μij,kc=P(Yij=k|Yi(j−1),Xi,ZZi) , γkl is the log-relative risk ratio parameter for the transition from state k to state l that could depend on Xi and ZZi , and Δij,k(Xi,ZZi) is an implicit function of the data and the parameters that links the CPMs to the response dependence model in equation (9). Similar to the binary outcome case, fitting this model requires iterating between calculating Δij,k(Xi,ZZi) and updating model parameter estimates using Newton–Raphson algorithm. Details on the calculation of the score function and Fisher information matrix are described by Lee and Daniels.10,11 As is the case with binary response data, if state k=1 is absorbing then γ11=∞ , which induces constraints on the model and fitting algorithm. 13

Simulations

Aims and data generating mechanism

Our simulation study aims to (i) characterize the validity, efficiency, type I error and power of the described models for estimating an end-of-study treatment effect when one outcome state is absorbing; (ii) highlight the potential benefit of using longitudinal data under various dropout mechanisms; (iii) study robustness of the estimation procedures to model misspecification; and (iv) compare the efficiency of marginalized transition models and Cox proportional hazard models in estimating an end-of-study treatment effect.

We begin by simulating data from a randomized clinical trial where N=1,000 participants are assigned to either treatment ( Xi=1 ) or control ( Xi=0 ) with equal probability. Let Yij∈{1,2,3,4} be a four-level outcome variable with the lowest state, k=1 , being absorbing, Zi be a binary covariate with P(Zi=1)=0.5 , and tij=log28(j+1) , j=0,…,27 . We generate data according to the CPM in equation (5) with ααkppo=(−4.75,−1.5,2) , ααtppo=(2,−1.75,−4) , ββxppo=(0,0,0) , ββxtppo=(−0.35,0,0) and ββzppo=(2,0,0) . We chose model coefficients so that approximately 15% of subjects enter the absorbing state k=1 during follow-up, and the end-of-study treatment effect log-odds ratio, when j=27 , is βx,1ppo+βxt,1ppo⋅log28(j+1)=βx,1ppo+βxt,1ppo⋅1=−0.35 . Note that with the chosen parameter values, the PO assumption for the treatment effect holds. Let ΓΓ be the 3×3 dependence model parameter matrix with elements γkl denoting the log-relative risk ratio for transitioning from state l to state k and with state 4 being the reference state. We set ΓΓ=(2521−2542−2514). The choice of (γ11,γ21,γ31)=(25,−25,−25) generates the Yij=1 absorbing state. To illustrate the potential benefit of using longitudinal modeling approaches over cross-sectional approaches, we study each estimation procedure under complete follow-up, and under two dropout at random (DAR) mechanisms of different severity: a mild DAR where each subject i has a probability of dropping out at time j equal to 0.01 if the outcome state at ti(j−1) was observed and Yi(j−1)>1 , and a severe DAR in which we increase the dropout probability to 0.04 . Approximately, 20% and 60% of subjects had missing information on the outcome state at the end of follow-up in the mild and severe DAR scenarios, respectively.

To evaluate and compare power across analysis procedures, we increase the absolute magnitude of the treatment effect from − 0.35 to − 0.50 by setting ββxppo=(0,0,0) and ββxtppo=(−0.5,0,0) in the main simulation scenario described above. The coefficients were chosen so that a CPM with a cross-sectional ordinal outcome and a single treatment effect across all outcome dichotomizations would yield approximately 80% power. Type I error rates were also evaluated by simulating data under ββxppo=ββxtppo=(0,0,0) .

To characterize robustness of the estimation procedures to model misspecification, we consider the complete follow-up setting described above and then modify coefficient values to simulate two additional scenarios: one where the PO assumption for the treatment effect does not hold, and one where the PO assumption holds for the treatment effect but not for the covariate parameter. Misspecification of the PO assumption for the treatment effect was simulated by setting ββxtppo=(−0.35,−0.20,−0.20) , while misspecification of the PO assumption for the covariate parameter was simulated by setting ββzppo=(2,0,−1) .

Finally, because the Cox proportional hazards model is a popular choice in settings like those considered here, we sought to compare marginalized transition models and Cox proportional hazards models. To do so we generated and fit data under 1) the marginalized transition model described at the beginning of the section and 2) a Cox proportional hazard model with a Weibull baseline hazard. Corresponding to 2), survival times were generated from h(t∣Xi,Zi)=h0(t)exp(βx+βzZi) with βx=−0.35 and βz=2 . The baseline hazard was specified as h0(t)=λνtν−1 , with ν=0.5 and λ=0.01 chosen so that the resulting true risk difference was close to that obtained under the marginalized transition model. The true risk difference parameter value was −0.044 when generating data under MTM PO and −0.048 under when generating data under a survival model with a Weibull hazard function. Since estimates from marginalized transition models are on an outcome-marginal, cumulative incidence scale, while the estimates from a Cox proportional hazard model are on a conditional (on not yet having experienced the event or still being the in risk set) scale, we compare parameter and uncertainty estimates for the two estimation strategies on a common, fully marginal (i.e. marginalized over Zi ) risk difference scale with standard errors calculated using the multivariate delta method. Details on the computation of the risk difference and standard errors are described in the Supplementary Material (Section S1).

First, we consider the binary outcome Yij=1 versus Yij>1 (i.e. Yij∈{2,3,4} ) and fit a cross-sectional GLM for binary data at the last day of follow-up ( j=27 or equivalently ti27=1 ), along with two longitudinal MTMs for binary outcomes that either do or do not constrain the treatment effect to be zero at baseline. Next, we consider the four outcome states separately, and we evaluate three cross-sectional CPM specifications at the end of follow-up: one assuming a common treatment effect across all the dichotomization of Yij (i.e. PO assumption on the treatment effect, CPM PO ), one allowing the treatment effect for the k=1 dichotomization of the log cumulative odds to be different from the common treatment effect for k=2 and k=3 (CPM PPO1 ), and one allowing the treatment effect to be different for each dichomtoization of Yij (CPM PPO2 ). Notice that CPM PPO1 and CPM PPO2 relax the PO restriction imposed in CPM PO . We then consider the corresponding longitudinal version of these ordinal models—MTM PO , MTM PPO1 , and MTM PPO2 —each evaluated with and without the additional constraint that the treatment effect at baseline is zero. Finally, for completeness, we include a Cox proportional hazard model in which the event of interest is defined as the entry into the absorbing state.

Figure 2 shows the treatment effect relative efficiency of each estimation procedure to the cross-sectional generalized linear model with a binary outcome (GLM). Under complete follow-up, considering a longitudinal binary outcome rather than a cross-sectional binary outcome did not result in any precision gain. However, to the extent that dropout occurs, gains were realized (e.g. 6% under mild DAR and 20% under severe DAR). Constraining the treatment effect to be zero further improved efficiency by 15% to 20% under the complete follow-up and mild DAR scenarios, but there was less of an improvement under severe DAR. We observed that the PO assumption for scalar or longitudinal models yielded enormous efficiency gains. Even though we include these results, it should be noted that the proportional odds assumption is unlikely to be acceptable (even in the absence of evidence that it is wrong) if interest is specifically in the treatment effect on the binary Yi27=1 versus Yi27>1 outcome. Pertaining to the scalar ordinal models, assuming that the treatment effect for the k=1 dichotomization of the log cumulative odds is distinct from the common treatment effect for k=2 and k=3 (MTM PPO1 ), provided an 8% to 9% efficiency gain over cross-sectional generalized linear model with a binary outcome, while assuming that the treatment effect differs for each dichotomization of Yij (MTM PPO2 ) provided no efficiency improvements. Finally, models that exploit the longitudinal and ordinal outcome data and that impose modest restrictions on the treatment effect (MTM PPO1 and MTM PPO2 ) yielded 25% to 45% percent efficiency gains over the cross-sectional generalized linear model with a binary outcome. In contrast to what we observed in the binary outcome setting, constraining the treatment effect to be zero at baseline had little to no impact on efficiency in the longitudinal ordinal models.

One interesting observation occurred under mild and severe DAR with the cross-sectional modeling procedures (CPM PO , CPM PPO1 and CPM PPO2 ). On the marginalized risk difference scale, the percent biases were substantially larger than they were on the log-odds ratio scale. For example, looking at the ordinal model where the treatment effect is assumed to be the same across all dichotomizations (CPM PO ), the treatment effect estimate was approximately unbiased on the log-odds ratio scale (Table 3) but exhibited a 30% bias on the risk difference scale. Even though the log-odds ratio for the treatment effect was not severely biased under DAR, the DAR mechanism induced biases on the intercept ( ααt ). When we combine small biases in the log-odds ratio with biases in prevalence, we observe substantial percent bias in the estimates of the risk differences.

We note that when severity is available, the treatment effect on mortality can be estimated using a discrete time multi-state survival model. 31 While we did not include the multi-state survival model in the simulations due to its heavy computational burden, we fit it to estimate the treatment effect on mortality in the CLOVERS data in the next section. A detailed summary of runtime for the currently implemented MTM models is provided in Table A4. Under complete follow-up, average runtime per replicate were 1−2 minutes for longitudinal binary outcomes and 6−8 minutes for longitudinal ordinal outcomes. These runtimes are expected to improve with updates to the software. Computation time was negligible for the GLM, CPM, and Cox proportional hazards models (<0.01 minutes on average).

CLOVERS study: Data analysis

For the CLOVERS study analyses, we implemented the estimation procedures described in Section 5 to calculate the effect of a restrictive fluid strategy on mortality at the end of follow-up. The covariate vector ZZi includes baseline information on patients’ age, sex, and SOFA score. For longitudinal models we included time using a restricted cubic spline with three degrees of freedom on the logarithm of day of follow-up, and added a time by treatment interaction. In addition to log-odds ratios, we estimated the fully marginal (with respect of the covariates) risk differences and compare the results with those from (i) a Cox proportional hazard model that includes the main effect of treatment, age, sex, and SOFA score as covariates, and (ii) a discrete time multi-state survival model with the same set of baseline covariates. In the multi-state survival model, we assumed that baseline covariates could have a different impact on each state transition; in other words, the multi-state survival model estimates a potentially different coefficient for each baseline covariate and each state transition. We estimated standard errors and 95 % confidence intervals for the multi-state survival model via bootstrap sampling with 5,000 replicates. Figure 3 shows the covariate-adjusted log-odds ratio of a restrictive versus liberal fluid resuscitation strategy on mortality at the end of follow-up and the fully marginal (with respect to baseline covariates) risk difference. Overall, there is little to no evidence to suggest that implementing a restrictive fluid strategy impacts mortality. Estimated log-odds ratio and risk differences were largely consistent across all estimation procedures.

Similar to the precision gains observed in the simulation studies for the log-odds ratio estimates, model uncertainty decreased when we used an ordinal outcome rather than a binary outcome: compared to the cross-sectional generalized linear model with a binary outcome, we observed a 3% to 20% reduction in the estimated standard error when using cross-sectional, ordinal outcome models. Not surprisingly, we observed the greatest reduction under the PO restriction for all dichotomizations of the outcome (estimated standard errors 0.164 vs 0.137). Focusing on the ordinal outcome models, estimated standard errors were lower with longitudinal models compared to cross-sectional models. For instance, under the PO restriction for all dichotomization of the outcome, the longitudinal model reduced the estimated standard error by 17% (e.g. 0.137 vs 0.116). Consistent with precision comparisons in the simulations, estimated uncertainty was similar for cross-sectional binary and unconstrained longitudinal binary outcome models (standard errors 0.164 vs 0.163). When we constrained the treatment effect to be 0 at baseline, ββx=00 , and fit the marginalized transition model, the estimated standard error was reduced to 0.154. Also, consistent with precision comparisons in the simulations, the constrained marginalized transition models for longitudinal ordinal outcomes did not reduce estimated uncertainty when compared to their unconstrained counterparts.

When estimating risk differences, the estimated standard error for the treatment effect from the Cox proportional hazard model tracked with those of the marginalized regression models for longitudinal binary outcome. Similarly, the estimated standard error for the treatment effect from the multi-state survival model tracked with the less constrained MTMs for ordinal outcomes (e.g. estimated standard error was 0.015 in both the multi-state model and the longitudinal ordinal model where the treatment effect was assumed to differ across all outcome dichotomizations).

Likelihood ratio tests can be used to compare many of the model fits. Here, we focus on understanding whether relaxing the PO assumptions and/or the assumption of a null treatment effect at baseline improved model fit. Table 7 shows the maximized log-likelihood for the MTMs with longitudinal binary outcome, the CPMs with cross-sectional ordinal outcome, and the MTMs with longitudinal ordinal outcome. For the longitudinal binary outcome models, we observed insufficient evidence to suggest that relaxing the assumption of a null treatment effect at baseline improved model fit ( P=0.138 ). This is expected since participants were randomized at baseline. Interestingly, under the longitudinal ordinal outcome models, relaxing the no baseline treatment effect assumption seemed to improve model fit ( P<0.001 across all comparisons between the constrained and the unconstrained MTM PO , MTM PPO1 , and MTM PPO2 ). This could imply some degree of model misspecification of the early treatment effect. Also interesting, relaxing the PO assumption for the treatment effect in the cross-sectional and longitudinal ordinal outcome settings did not improve model fit. That is, the treatment effect is basically null, which is consistent with the proportional odds assumption (i.e. the treatment effect is zero for each outcome dichotomization).

Discussion

We discussed and compared approaches to summarize the outcome-marginal baseline covariate-adjusted treatment effect on mortality at the end of a follow-up period in a randomized clinical trial when health state data are collected daily (i.e. longitudinally). We focused on how and when specific modeling choices, such as the use of longitudinal versus cross-sectional outcomes or of ordinal versus binary outcomes, can improve estimation efficiency compared to the commonly used cross-sectional binary logistic regression and survival analysis.

Together with the choice of outcome, we studied how estimation efficiency can be improved by imposing constraints on the form of regression model. In particular, we considered scenarios where the treatment effect at baseline was forced to be zero and, for the ordinal outcome, we examined scenarios where we imposed and/or relaxed PO assumptions. Constraining the treatment effect at baseline to zero improved estimation efficiency with longitudinal binary outcome models but not with longitudinal ordinal outcome models. This result could be explained by the number of parameters estimated in each procedure: there were 6 parameters in the MTM with a longitudinal binary outcome compared to the 18 to 20 parameters in the MTMs with a longitudinal ordinal outcomes. Hence, the removal of one parameter, known to have no effect on the outcome, may have a greater impact on the model that estimates fewer parameters overall. When we imposed the PO assumption on the treatment effect, we observed additional improvements in efficiency. However, we also note that the PO assumption could lead to invalid inference if misspecified. Thus, when deciding on the form of the regression model, one needs to strike a balance between robustness and efficiency. While we focused on treatment effect estimation in a randomized clinical trial, the proposed approach can be applied to non-randomized studies where one is interested in an end of follow-up exposure effect while adjusting for baseline covariates. However, in these settings, the inclusion of time-varying exposures and/or confounders would require careful consideration.

In the presence of at least one absorbing state, we showed how MTMs can directly model cumulative incidences without having to condition on any post-randomization outcomes. The outcome-marginal and conditional response-dependence components of an MTM correspond to cumulative incidence and discrete hazard models, respectively, and, when interest is in a fully-marginal (i.e. marginal with respect of both outcomes and covariates) treatment effect, the efficiency of MTMs tracks with Cox proportional hazard models and discrete-time multi-state survival models. In contrast to survival analysis methods, first-order MTMs provide a valid way to directly estimate covariate-adjusted treatment effects on cumulative incidence.

The design of CLOVERS did not allow us to observe transitions from the discharged/home state to either the hospital state and/or the hospital/ICU state. While, for the purpose of the analyses in this paper we assumed that none of unobserved transition occurred, this assumption might not be realistic in scenarios where the probability of remaining in the same state from day to day is lower. In future work, we will explore how marginalized transition models with longitudinal ordinal outcomes can be extended to cases where we have interval censored data, for example, when we know participants are alive, but we do not know in which state they are in. The MTMs presented in this paper can be fitted using the R package OMTM1.
