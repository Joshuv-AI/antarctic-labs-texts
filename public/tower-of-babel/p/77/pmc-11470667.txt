Development and validation of a nomogram for the estimation of the prognosis of patients presenting with a febrile seizure
Authors: Qingran Wang, Weiling Sun, Jinyan Zhao, Lili Tong, Baomin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFebrile seizures constitute a prevalent emergency in early childhood. Previous studies indicated that certain febrile seizures in children may progress to epilepsy, exerting a substantial impact on children's health and developmental trajectory. The objective of this study was to formulate a predictive nomogram to assess the likelihood of transitioning from febrile seizures to epilepsy in pediatric patients, thereby facilitating informed decisions regarding medical interventions for febrile seizures.MethodsA total of 306 patients were enrolled and categorized into training (70%) and test (30%) cohorts. Clinical characteristics were subjected to comparison utilizing chi-squared and t tests. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to identify significant factors for predicting the risk of transitioning from febrile seizures to epilepsy, leading to the development of a nomogram. The nomogram’s performance was assessed through receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration, and decision curve analysis.ResultsPredictive factors associated with the transition to epilepsy encompassed lower Na, elevated RDW, IL-6, and increased background slow rhythm and epileptiform discharges in EEG. The nomogram, incorporating five factors, exhibited commendable predictive value (AUC train = 0.812, AUC test = 0.791) for assessing the risk of transitioning from febrile seizures to epilepsy. Calibration analyses confirmed reliability, and decision curve analysis underscored its clinical utility.ConclusionsLower Na, elevated RDW, IL-6, background slow rhythm, and epileptiform discharges are associated with the risk of transitioning from febrile seizures to epilepsy. The nomogram stands as a valuable tool for predicting this risk, aiding in the strategic implementation of medical interventions to enhance outcomes for patients with febrile seizures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05132-z.Keywords: Febrile seizures, Epilepsy, Nomogram, Prognosis

Introduction

Febrile seizure (FS), defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), “Represent a seizure occurring during childhood after the first month of age, associated with a feverish illness unrelated to central nervous system infection, devoid of a history of neonatal seizures or previous unprovoked seizures, and not meeting criteria for other acute symptomatic seizures” [1]. The estimated combined occurrence rate of FS ranges from 2–5% in the United States and Western Europe [2], with the highest incidence at 18 months and prevalence peaking between 6 months and 6 years [3]. This broad range highlights the need for region-specific studies to understand the epidemiological patterns of FS and tailor intervention strategies accordingly. Beyond the age of 6, FS may manifest as febrile seizures plus (FS+), a subtype of familial genetic epilepsy syndrome, although isolated cases of febrile seizures persist [4]. Consequently,all children were followed up until after the age of 6 years .Due to the different age of onset of the disease ,the follow-up duration is not the same for each patient.

Despite the generally benign nature of FS, they carry a risk of subsequent epilepsy, impacting children's cognition and increasing socioeconomic burdens. Therefore, delineating the transition mechanisms from FS to epilepsy could pave the way for preventative strategies, potentially reducing the burden on affected families and healthcare systems. Numerous studies have shown that children diagnosed with FS face a higher likelihood of developing epilepsy compared to their healhty counterparts [5]. Clinical categorization distinguishes between simple and complex FS, with approximately 2% of children with simple FS and 4–12% of those with complex FS progressing to epilepsy [6]. Notably, 10–15% of individuals with epilepsy or unprovoked seizures have a history of FS, a rate significantly higher than the general population 2–4% [7]. Consequently, understanding the risk factors for FS progression to epilepsy is crucial for implementing early interventions to mitigate long-term complications.

Various studies have identified risk factors predicting recurrent FS, yet these factors exhibit variability in pediatric populations. Berg et al [8] highlighted seizure etiology and electroencephalogram (EEG) as influential predictors of FS recurrence.A prospective large sample study of Efterpi et al showed Main prognostic factors for the development of epilepsy after FS are: (1)complex FS that increased the risk for epilepsy 3.6 times, (2) age at onset of FS beyondthe third year of life that raised the risk 3.8 times, (3) positive family history of epilepsy7.3 times, and (4) multiple episodes of FS about 10 times. Focality at the first and the second FS recurrence increased the risk of epilepsy about 9.7 and 11.7 times [9].Other studies also identified factors such as age at onset, family history of febrile or unprovoked seizures, initial FS temperature, and history of recurrent seizure as predictors [10]. Furthermore, hyponatremia emerged as a significant risk factor for recurrent FS, with serum sodium levels below 134.72 mmol/L identified as a predictor [11]. This finding suggests that metabolic and electrolyte imbalances may be associated with the development of FS pathophysiology, suggesting that closer monitoring and management of these parameters might mitigate the risk of recurrence. Additionally, Alberto et al identified EEG characteristics with epileptiform discharges and slow activity as independent risk factors for FS recurrence [12]. Most existing studies followed children with FS up to 6 years of age, setting our study apart.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of participant characteristics and subsequently developed a nomogram to predict the risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy in pediatric patients. By extending the follow-up period beyond the typical 6 years, we aim to provide insights into long-term outcomes and refine risk prediction models, enhancing clinical decision-making and patient care.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was designed to analyze FS cases admitted to the Pediatric Wards of Qilu Hospital and Dezhou Hospital affiliated with Shandong University between January 2017 and January 2022. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) adherence to the Expert Consensus on the diagnosis and management of FS [4], (2) availability of complete clinical and EEG data, (3) and normal results from magnetic resonance imaging and cerebrospinal fluid examinations. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) instances of central nervous system infections, (2) the presence of space-occupying lesions, (3) a documented history of epilepsy, (4) and hereditary metabolic diseases. In this study, all febrile seizure cases were enrolled at the time of their first seizure occurrence and followed up until after the age of 6 years. Febrile seizures were defined in accordance with the Expert Consensus on the diagnosis and management of FS. The diagnosis of epilepsy in all the children was made by a neurologist with senior professional titles. After enrollment, cases were randomly divided into training and test cohorts using a random number table. For consistency and accuracy, venous blood was collected after the patient's first febrile convulsion to determine serum sodium and RDW levels. Annual follow-up prcedures involved monitoring seizure occurrences and changes in video electroencephalogram (VEEG).

EEG examination

The initial VEEG examination, performed 10–14 days after the onset of the first seizure, utilized the Japanese Optoelectronics 9200 EEG apparatus. Electrodes were positioned according to the international 10–20 system, recording spanned 8 to 15 hours, encompassing wakefulness, sleep, and arousal states. When feasible, hyperventilation and photic stimulation tests were performed. In accordance with the guidelines from the 2nd edition of Clinical Electroencephalography edited by Professor Liu Xiaoyan [13], a slow background rhythm was defined as a rhythm 2Hz slower than the normal background rhythm of age-matched children during quiet closed-eye states.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics were gathered, encompassing age, gender, seizure time, and various health parameters. Electrolyte levels, including sodium ion (Na+) and potassium ion (K+) were recorded, along with blood routine measures such as white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin (HB) levels, C-reaction protein (CRP) levels, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), 25-Hydroxyvitamin D (OHD25), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). RDW, denoting the index of variation in erythrocyte volume, was also included in the collected data.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as counts (percentages) for categorical variables, and group-wise comparisons were conducted using the chi-squared test. Data were described as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and compared using the t test. Predictive factors exhibiting statistically significant differences between the non-event group (without FS occurring after 6 years) and the event group (transitioning to epilepsy) were subjected to multivariate logistic regression analysis (P < 0.05). The multivariate logistic regression model, established through the back stepwise selection method, identified independent risk factors associated with the transition to epilepsy. Multivariate logistic regression calculated odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), with significance defined as P < 0.05.

For nomogram development, 306 confirmed FS cases were randomly divided into a train set (70%) and a test set (30%). Logistic regression analysis, following the back stepwise selection method mentioned above, identified independent risk factors for the transition to epilepsy in the train cohort. Subsequently, a nomogram predicting the probability of transitioning to epilepsy was constructed using the training data. The nomogram’s performance was assessed in both the training and test datasets using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC). Calibration of the nomogram was gauged through calibration curves generated with 1000 bootstrap resamples in both the training and test datasets. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the net benefit of medical intervention guided by the nomogram at various threshold probabilities in both datasets. Statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 3.6.1), with statistical significance set at a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05.

Performance of the nomogram

The nomogram performance underwebt a comprehensive evaluation in both the training and test cohorts. The AUC of the nomogram was 0.812 (95% CI 0.742–0.883) in the training cohort (Fig. 2A), and the calibration curve showed good agreement between the predicted probability and actual probability in the validation cohort (Fig. 2B). The internal validation results showed a mean concordance index of 0.810 using the bootstrap method. Good performance (Fig. 3A) and favorable calibration (Fig. 3B) of the nomogram were also verified in the validation cohort, with an AUC of 0.791 (95% CI, 0.690–0.892). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test demonstrated nonsignificant statistics with p value of 0.525 and 0.533 in the training cohort and validation cohort, respectively, indicating that there was goodness of fit of the prediction model.

Discussion

In our study, we included 306 patients, randomly divided into training and test cohorts. Baseline characteristics revealed no statistically significant differences between the two cohorts. Our study developed a nomogram encompassing the Na, RDW, IL-6, background slow rhythm, and epileptiform discharges. The nomogram’s ROC curve indicated robust discriminatory power in predicting the risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy. Calibration curve(CAL) confirmed the alignment of observed and predicted outcomes, further affirming the nomogram’s reliability. Beyond its discriminative and calibration capabilitys, the nomogram exhibited practical clinical utility, as evidenced by DCA. These findings accentuate the potential of our predictive nomogram to enhance clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Our observations unveiled an association between lower Na levels and an elevated risk of transitioning to epilepsy. Sodium's pivotal role in maintaining neuronal excitability suggests that deviations from the optimal range may heighten neuronal hyperexcitability, potentially lowering the seizure threshold. Established studies have linked abnormalities in voltage-gated sodium channels to epilepsy onset, where disruptions in their typical function can result in irregular action potential patterns, giving rise to epilepsy [14, 15]. Maintaining an appropriate sodium concentration is crucial for regulating the threshold for neuronal excitability.

Furthermore, we observed an association between elevated IL-6 levels and an increased risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy. IL-6, recognized as a proinflammatory cytokine, assumes a rucial role in epileptogenesis [16]. Research suggests that the upregulation of IL-6 is associated with decreased long-term potentiation and hippocampal neurogenesis, accompanied by an increase in gliosis, factors that may contribute to epileptogenesis [17]. IL-6 is known to disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB), facilitating the entry of IL6 from the blood circulation into the brain through a compromised BBB [18]. Several studies consistently demonstrated elevated serum and cerebrospinal fluid levels of IL-6 in various types of epilepsy during both interictal and postictal periods [16, 18]. Animal studies also underscore the association between an elevated level of IL-6 in the brain and epilepsy, as evidenced by the intranasal administration of IL-6 in rats, leading to an increased propensity for seizures [19]. Consequently, IL-6 assumes a pivotal role in predicting the possibility of transitioning from FS to epilepsy.

Moreover, we observed that an elevated RDW is related to the risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy. Previous studies have highlighted the association of RDW with inflammation. Giuseppe Lippi et al. independently associated RDW with C-reactive protein (CRP), a recognized marker of inflammation, and concluded that RDW may serve as a valuable biomarker for identifying patients with subclinical inflammation [20]. Interestingly, an elevated CRP level was also observed in the event group compared to the non-event group in our study. Therefore, the elevated RDW is related to the risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy, suggesting a potential correlation between inflammation and the risk of epilepsy development. Moreover, our findings on RDW's association with the risk of epilepsy are echoed by the research of Giuseppe Lippi et al., who identified RDW as a marker of inflammation, potentially serving as an indirect predictor of febrile seizure recurrence. The pivotal role of IL-6 in epileptogenesis, further substantiates our results, linking elevated IL-6 levels with a higher epilepsy risk, which aligns with the inflammatory hypothesis in the pathophysiology of febrile seizures.

In the 2017 edition of the "Expert Consensus on the diagnosis and management of FS" [21], it is recommended that periodic follow-up video EEG of FS, especially complex FS, should be conducted. Abnormal EEGs are recognized as an independent risk factor for a poor prognosis in FS [21]. Our study observed that the presence of a background slow rhythm in EEG recordings emerged as a significant factor associated with an increased risk of transitioning from FS to epilepsy. In line with our study, Mercier EL et al., explored the EEG characteristics in children and investigated their correlation with the subsequent development of epilepsy, demonstrating a significant association between the presence of a background slow rhythm and an increased risk of epilepsy onset [22]. Persistent slow rhythms may reflect disruptions in the balance of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal activity, predisposing individuals to epilepsy. Additionally, it may indicate ongoing structural or functional changes in the brain that predispose individuals to recurrent seizures. Moreover, our study suggested that the presence of epileptiform discharges becomes especially pertinent in predicting the risk of subsequent epilepsy development. Similarly, Kavčič Alja et al. showed that 31% of children with recurrent FS in the presence of epileptiform discharges converted to epilepsy [23]. Thus, we assert that FS with EEG characteristics of background slow rhythm and epileptiform discharges predicts a higher risk of transition to epilepsy, and both exhibit a high predictive value for prognostic conversion to epilepsy in FS. Despite the promising findings, our study's limited sample size necessitates cautious interpretation of the results. An expanded sample size could potentially enhance the robustness of the findings and provide more conclusive evidence regarding the predictive value of the nomogram. Further research with a larger cohort is essential to validate our results and explore the generalizability of the nomogram across different populations. The feasibility of conducting such a study should be assessed, considering the potential challenges and benefits of engaging a broader study population.

Although our study has yielded valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge that the expansion of our sample size was constrained by certain conditions, which may introduce potential limitations to the sampling scope. Being a single-center retrospective study may limit generalizability to broader clinical contexts. Moreover, the multifactorial influence on Na, RDW, and IL-6 levels necessitates considering the overall patient situation when utilizing the nomogram for prognosis prediction in FS.

In conclusion, our nomogram exhibits excellent predictive value for predicting the risk of transitioning FS to epilepsy, offering the potential for early interventions to prevent the unfavorable prognosis in FS.
