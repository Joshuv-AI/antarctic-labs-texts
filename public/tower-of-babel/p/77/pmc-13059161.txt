Advances in ligand-targeted nanodelivery systems for leukemia therapy: from single- to dual-ligand strategies
Authors: Mingxin Hu, Chaoxing He, Jintao Hao, Nannan Qi, Ke He, Bai Xiang, Zhiyun Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeukemia is a highly heterogeneous and aggressive hematological malignancy. Current therapeutic approaches (e.g. chemotherapy, radiotherapy, small-molecule targeted therapies, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, etc.) are widely limited in clinical practice by systemic drug toxicity, acquired drug resistance, off-target effects, and graft rejection, highlighting the urgent need for safer and more effective innovative treatment strategies.Main bodyLigand-targeted nanodelivery systems achieve precise recognition and targeted delivery to tumor cells by modifying the surface of nanocarriers with ligands (e.g. antibodies, aptamers, proteins, peptides, and small molecules, etc.). This review systematically summarizes common therapeutic targets in leukemia, existing treatment agents, and strategies for constructing ligand-functionalized nanocarriers, with a particular focus on the fundamental research and clinical progress of single-ligand and dual-ligand targeted delivery systems. Overall, most studies indicate that dual-ligand systems exhibit superior targeting efficiency and therapeutic performance compared with single-ligand systems; however, clinical translation still faces challenges, including large-scale manufacturing, immunogenicity and systemic toxicity evaluation, regulatory and intellectual property barriers, and the development of humanized models.ConclusionsLigand-targeted nanodelivery systems have been applied in multiple areas of leukemia treatment, including drug delivery, gene therapy, and diagnostic detection. Future research should prioritize the development of combination therapeutic strategies, as well as the diversified application of nanomaterials in ligand modification and immunotherapy models; meanwhile, the integration of artificial intelligence to assist ligand design and optimization may provide new directions for further improving targeted therapeutic strategies for leukemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07966-w.

Introduction

Leukemia, is a malignancy originating from aberrant hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow [1], characterized by uncontrolled and rapid proliferation of leukemic cells. Programmed cell death (PCD) is a cell death process precisely regulated by the cell itself. As a natural defense mechanism, PCD can effectively inhibit the survival and proliferation of cancer cells [2, 3]. In leukemia, cells lose this regulatory mechanism, leading to abnormal accumulation in various systems and organs. The disease primarily affects the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and spleen [4, 5], where leukemia cells proliferate abnormally and continuously accumulate, suppressing normal blood cell production and significantly impairing hematopoietic function, which poses a serious threat to human health. According to global cancer statistics published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer in 2022, leukemia ranked tenth in mortality among all cancers. Notably, leukemia exhibits the highest incidence in children and adolescents, which makes it the most significant malignancy in this age group.

Leukemia is classified into several subtypes, primarily including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) [6]. Among these, AML and CLL are the two most common types of leukemia, accounting for 33% and 35%, respectively [7]. Common therapeutic strategies for leukemia include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [8]. Chemotherapy is a commonly used approach to treat leukemia; however, its widespread application is limited owing to the lack of high selectivity for tumor versus normal cells, as well as the development of drug resistance. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is considered the most effective treatment for leukemia, as it enables patients to restore normal hematopoietic function following radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Unfortunately, the occurrence of graft-versus-host disease and rejection reactions often leads to treatment failure [9]. Targeted therapy typically involves the use of small-molecule drugs or monoclonal antibodies to recognize and attack specific cancer cells. However, resistance may develop when the target itself undergoes alterations, preventing effective interaction with the therapy. Therefore, targeted treatments are often combined with chemotherapy and radiotherapy to enhance efficacy. Immunotherapy, as an emerging treatment modality, aims to combat tumors by activating or enhancing the patient’s immune system. It typically includes immune checkpoint therapy and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy. However, the cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity associated with these therapies cannot be overlooked [10]. Furthermore, new types of treatment are being investigated in clinical trials.

Drug resistance in leukemia cells is a major cause of disease relapse and refractoriness. This resistance does not arise from a single mechanism but rather results from the combined effect of multiple factors. According to their modes of action, these mechanisms can be broadly classified into two categories: those that reduce effective drug exposure and those that enhance intrinsic cellular tolerance. The specific mechanisms for drug resistance are described as follows: (1) expression of drug efflux pumps [11]; (2) alteration of drug targets; (3) enhanced drug metabolism; (4) genetic mutations that promote cell survival by inhibiting apoptosis and enhancing DNA damage repair [12]; and (5) sequestration of anticancer drugs within or between cells in specific organelles (particularly lysosomes or vesicles), thereby distancing them from their targets and reducing effective intracellular drug concentrations, leading to drug resistance [13].Targeted nanomedicine delivery systems act as multifunctional platforms and hold promise for enhancing drug accumulation at tumor sites through multiple strategies, including active targeting, optimized delivery, and combinatorial synergy, thereby collaboratively overcoming drug resistance [14]. (1) Nanoparticles (NPs) possess flexible surface modifications to enable active targeting, which enhances specific drug accumulation in leukemia cells, thereby increasing cytotoxicity and mitigating resistance caused by drug efflux pumps [15]; (2) Compared with single-targeted NPs, dual-ligand-modified NPs can simultaneously interfere with multiple tumor progression-related targets, block alternative pathways, and reduce antigen escape, thereby mechanistically slowing the development of resistance [16]; (3) Through intelligent design of targeted NPs, such as pH-, redox-, or light-responsive release, controlled drug release at specific sites can be achieved, avoiding sequestration or inactivation in subcellular compartments caused by free drug diffusion [17]; (4) In the context of “dual-drug codelivery nanosystem” strategy, NPs can co-encapsulate two or more drugs with different properties and mechanisms of action, simultaneously restoring effective intracellular drug exposure and disrupting resistance-associated energy metabolism, thereby overcoming multidrug resistance at the systemic level [18].

Because hematological malignancies lack the localized tumor structures that support the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, active targeting ligands are essential for the specific recognition of circulating tumor cells, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and effective drug delivery. Since 1913, when Paul Ehrlich first proposed the concept of “Magic bullets” [19], the search for the “magic bullet” has become a goal pursued by scientists throughout history. From the earliest antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) to bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) and today’s CAR-T therapies, the developmental timeline of targeted biologic therapies is shown in Fig. 1a. Commonly targeted ligands for leukemia include antibodies, aptamers, proteins, peptides, and small molecules [20]. This review focuses on how different targeting ligand modifications enhance the active targeting capability of nanocarriers and highlights their great potential in leukemia treatment. Both single-target and dual-target ligand modification strategies are discussed (as shown in Fig. 1b), with particular emphasis on the ligand–nanocarrier systems designed to address distinct drug resistance mechanisms, such as cellular heterogeneity, microenvironment-mediated protection, and multidrug resistance. In addition, we discuss ligand–nanocarrier conjugation strategies and provide an in-depth analysis of the current challenges and future perspectives of ligand-targeted drug delivery systems.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search covering 2016–2025 was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and the FMRS Foreign Medical Research Information Retrieval Platform. The specific search terms are provided in the Supplementary Material [see Additional file 1]. Studies were included if they focused on ligand-modified nanocarriers for leukemia, including current therapeutic strategies, treatment drugs, relevant molecular targets, advances in single- and dual-ligand modified nanocarriers, and challenges in clinical translation. Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to leukemia or did not involve ligand-modified nanocarriers. Following screening and eligibility assessments, a total of 207 (including repeated entries) studies were included in this review.

Biomarkers for targeting leukemia

The construction of ligand-targeted nanocarriers relies on the affinity between ligands and the unique biomarkers expressed on leukemia cells. Biomarkers used for tumor targeting should be specific, exhibiting high expression in the tumor cells compared to normal cells. Numerous transmembrane proteins have been identified on leukemia cells and leukemia stem cells (LSC), as illustrated in Fig. 2. Among these, LSC-specific antigens have emerged as a major focus of current research.

Biomarkers associated with the surface of leukemia cells

CD33 is a glycosylated transmembrane protein expressed on the surface of bone marrow (BM) cells and is particularly highly expressed in AML cells (up to 90% of AML cases). MYLOTARG was the first ADC targeting CD33, to be approved by the FDA in early 2000 [21]. The BsAbs targeting both CD33 and CD123 for AML have become a hot research topic in the field.

CD44 is a receptor for hyaluronic acid (HA), playing various roles, including lymphocyte activation, recirculation, homing, and hematopoiesis. In a recent study, Li et al. leveraged the CD44-hyaluronic acid interaction to engineer a biomimetic vesicle designed to target hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells within the BM [22].

CD71, also known as transferrin receptor 1, plays a crucial role in the cellular uptake of iron [23] whereas CD70 is an immunotherapy target that regulates the immune response by binding to the receptor CD27. Studies have demonstrated that the anti-CD70 monoclonal antibody, Cusatuzumab blocks the CD70-CD27 interaction, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of LSC. It may also eliminate CD70-expressing leukemia cells through complement-dependent cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity [24].

Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) plays a fundamental role in pathogen recognition and innate immune activation. Abnormal expression and dysregulation of TLR2 can allow cancer cells to evade the immune system and promote proliferation and angiogenesis [25] and TLR2 expression is increased in the AML cell lines U937, THP-1, and HL-60 [26]. Other targets, such as CD45, CD71, and CD117, have also been extensively documented in the literature on leukemia-related target treatment [27].

Leukemia stem cell-related biomarkers

CD123, also known as IL3RA, is expressed at much higher levels in leukemic cells and LSCs compared to healthy BM cells. It promotes the survival and proliferation of AML cells by activating the JAK/STAT, MAPK, and PI3K signaling pathways [28]. AZD9829 is a CD123-targeting topoisomerase I inhibitor (TOP1I)-based antibody-drug conjugate. In preclinical studies, AZD9829 has exhibited strong antileukemic activity in CD123-expressing AML cell lines [29].

CD47 is a well-known marker of LSC. The interaction of CD47 with signal regulatory protein alpha induces macrophages to send a “don’t eat me” signal, which inhibits phagocytosis and enables evasion from immune surveillance [30]. Magrolimab, a CD47-targeting monoclonal antibody developed by Gilead, encountered challenges during a Phase III clinical trial (NCT04313881) owing to suspected unexpected serious adverse events, leading to early termination of the study. Despite these setbacks, such investigations have provided critical insights into immune evasion mechanisms and have informed the development of safer, more effective targeted strategies.

FLT3 is a type III receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a critical role in hematopoietic stem cells and precursor B cells [31]. FLT3 receptor mutations mainly include two types: internal tandem duplications and tyrosine kinase domain point mutations, with the latter being more common and associated with a poorer prognosis. Phase III clinical trial results of quizartinib indicate that its combination with chemotherapy significantly enhances survival outcomes in patients newly diagnosed with FLT3-ITD-positive AML [32, 33].

CXC chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) is a member of the G protein-coupled receptor superfamily and is expressed on AML cells and hematopoietic stem cells [34]. The ligand for CXCR4, stromal-derived factor 1, is secreted by stromal cells in the BM. The SDF-1/CXCR4 axis facilitates the homing of leukemia cells to the BM. CXCR4 has long been recognized as a potential target for leukemia treatment [35].

Human C-type lectin-like molecule (CLL-1) [36] and interleukin-1 receptor accessory protein (IL-1RAP) are two molecules that are also highly expressed in AML stem cells [37]. Specifically, CLL-1 is overexpressed in 90–95% of newly diagnosed or relapsed AML cases. A clinical trial (NCT06027853) evaluating targeted CLL1 therapy for AML using natural killer cell therapy is currently recruiting participants.

Leukemia cells intracellular biomarkers

The pathogenesis of leukemia is extremely complex, encompassing numerous biomarkers and molecular pathways. Various proteins and signaling pathways trigger distinct causes, with their expression levels varying significantly across leukemia subtypes. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) can specifically inhibit the expression of any cancer-related gene through RNA interference [38]. Several studies have been conducted in the field of leukemia, targeting genes such as RUNX1 [39] and SHARP1 [40]. However, siRNA’s clinical translation is significantly hindered by low cellular uptake efficiency, short half-life, and propensity for off-target effects significantly hinder its clinical translation.

Highly efficient encapsulation by lipid NPs (LNPs) can protect siRNA from rapid degradation [41], prolong its circulation time, enhance targeted delivery through surface modification, and enable intracellular release of nucleic acids. When designing RNA-loaded NPs (RNA-NPs) for leukemia therapy, careful consideration must be given to factors such as size, shape, surface charge, and surface engineering. Studies have shown that NPs ≤ 10 nm are prone to renal clearance [42], whereas larger NPs (> 250 nm) are rapidly eliminated by the mononuclear phagocyte system [43]. Therefore, NPs within the 50–100 nm range are generally considered optimal [44, 45], balancing systemic circulation with efficient cellular uptake. The safety of NPs is also a critical consideration. Highly cationic NPs may induce hemolysis, thrombosis, and other related toxicities, whereas the use of ionizable lipids (e.g., MC3, SM102) can effectively mitigate these issues [46]. Additionally, a balance must be struck between the extended circulation time conferred by polyethylene glycol-modified (PEGylated) lipid components and their potential immunogenicity [47].

The NPs primarily primarily enter cells via three mechanisms: phagocytosis, endocytosis, and macropinocytosis. Typically, small NPs are internalized through endocytosis, whereas larger NPs are taken up via macropinocytosis or phagocytosis. Specifically, cationic LNPs efficiently encapsulate anionic siRNA through electrostatic interactions and enter the cells via endocytosis. Within the acidic environment of endosomes and lysosomes (pH ≈ 5–6), the ionizable lipids acquire a positive charge, interact with the endosomal membrane, and facilitate endosomal escape, which safely delivers siRNA into the cytoplasm. This process enables effective silencing of target genes, thereby achieving intracellular biomarker-specific targeting [48].

Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2) is commonly mutated in adult AML, with approximately 8% of patients with AML carrying IDH1 mutations carrying IDH2 mutations. The IDH1/2-encoded enzymes participate in citrate metabolism, converting isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). Subsequently, α-KG is converted to R-2-hydroxyglutarate (R-2HG), which acts as a competitive inhibitor of α-KG-dependent enzymes. As a tumor metabolite, R-2HG leads to alterations in histone and DNA methylation, promoting tumorigenesis [49].

RUNX1, also known as AML1, is a transcription factor whose mutations are often associated with myelodysplastic syndromes and AML [50].Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is an antioxidant enzyme. In leukemia treatment, inhibiting GPX4 can induce ferroptosis, a form of non-apoptotic cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [51].

The BCL-2 protein family is a key regulatory factor in apoptosis and is highly expressed in AML progenitor cells, which is associated with poor prognosis [52]. Venetoclax (VEN) is the first BCL-2 inhibitor that effectively targets malignant tumor cells. The combination of VEN and azacitidine has a synergistic anti-leukemic stem cell effect and is one of the standard treatment regimens for older patients with AML.

The thioredoxin (Trx) system, consisting of Trx, thioredoxin reductase (TrxR), and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, functions as an antioxidant system [53]. This system is involved in tumorigenesis through its antioxidant actions. Additionally, it can stimulate cell proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and activate transcription factors.

In addition to the biomarkers discussed above, other targets have been discovered for leukemia [54, 55]. In recent years, new therapeutic targets have continuously emerged (Table 1).

Drugs commonly used in the treatment of leukemia

Conventional chemotherapeutic agents

Over the past five years, the “7 + 3” regimen, consisting of 7–10 days of cytarabine and 3 days of daunorubicin, has remained the cornerstone of AML treatment. However, owing to its limited efficacy, and poor tolerability, this regimen is unsuitable for older patients (≥ 70 years) who cannot withstand intensive chemotherapy. Studies report that the long-term overall survival rate in this population is below 10% [64]. Although new chemotherapy combinations are continually being explored, the risk of myelosuppression remains. Vyxeos (CPX-351), approved by the FDA on August 3, 2017, encapsulates daunorubicin and cytarabine within liposomes, enhancing drug concentration at tumor sites, prolonging plasma half-life, and partially reducing toxicity to normal tissues, thereby decreasing adverse effects [65]. However, a recent clinical trial on pediatric AML with Down syndrome (ML-DS 2018 trial) indicated that replacing the conventional low-intensity induction chemotherapy with the current dose of CPX-351 resulted in lower toxicity but a significantly increased risk of relapse. The investigators attributed this outcome primarily to insufficient dosing of cytarabine [66].

Small-molecule targeted drugs

In recent years, small-molecule inhibitors targeting specific molecular alterations have provided new therapeutic hope for patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia (r/r AML). Table 2 summarizes the small-molecule targeted agents approved by the U.S. FDA since 2017, many of which have reshaped the treatment landscape to varying degrees [67–69]. Although small-molecule targeted therapies have markedly transformed current treatment paradigms, they still face significant limitations, including resistance driven by target mutations, activation of compensatory signaling pathways, limited antileukemic efficacy observed with monotherapy, and inherent pharmacokinetic shortcomings. These challenges highlight the necessity of further exploring more effective therapeutic strategies [68].

FDA-approved targeted therapies for the treatment of AML

Antibody-drug conjugates

ADCs are targeted therapeutics in which highly specific monoclonal antibodies are chemically linked to potent cytotoxic payloads [70]. This design enables precise delivery of cytotoxic agents to tumor cells by leveraging the antibody’s specificity for target antigens while minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues. At present, multiple ADCs have been approved worldwide for cancer therapy, among which several are indicated for hematological malignancies, including Gemtuzumab ozogamicin, Brentuximab vedotin, Inotuzumab ozogamicin, Moxetumomab pasudotox, Polatuzumab vedotin, Belantamab mafodotin, and Loncastuximab tesirine [71]. ADCs partially overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapy related to insufficient target specificity; however, their clinical application is limited by certain safety concerns, including neutropenia, ocular toxicity, thrombocytopenia, anemia, gastrointestinal injury, and musculoskeletal adverse effects [72]. Furthermore, balancing linker stability in circulation with specific intracellular degradation in target cells remains challenging, significantly complicating ADC development and limiting the range of payloads that can be used. Finally, monotherapy with a single ADC often leads to the emergence of drug resistance [73]. In this context, a novel targeted drug delivery system—antibody–nanogel conjugates (ANCs)—has been proposed. These systems exhibit excellent stability, maintaining size consistency over five days. Interestingly, this strategy increases the drug-to-antibody ratio from single digits, as seen in conventional ADCs, to the “several hundred” range without compromising targeting specificity, thereby opening new avenues for high-payload targeted therapy [74].

Strategies for ligand-functionalized nanocarriers

In recent years, nanocarriers have become a major focus of the drug delivery system research owing to their unique size effects and surface effects. Ligand-modified nanocarrier technology facilitates the incorporation of specific biomolecules or small molecule ligands on the nanocarrier surface, enabling precise control of the drug delivery process. However, effectively modifying ligands for targeted recognition is crucial. Ideal modifications should meet the following conditions to achieve efficient, targeted delivery and avoid off-target toxicity: (1) the structure of the ligand should remain unchanged at the binding site; (2) the bonding strength and stability between the ligand and NP should be high. This article will discuss the two main methods of covalent and non-covalent modifications (Fig. 3). The modification methods should be chosen based on the characteristics of the ligands.

Functionalization strategies of nanocarriers

Covalent modification of ligands

Maleimide chemistry

Maleimide chemistry primarily relies on the Michael addition reaction, in which a thiol group undergoes conjugate addition to α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds. The mechanism involves the reaction of the free thiol group (-SH) on the ligand with the maleimide groups on the surface of the nanocarrier, resulting in the formation of a stable thioether bond [75]. This reaction is suitable for ligands or nanocarriers that are sensitive to acidic pH. A major limitation of this approach is that during the thiolation process, the ligand’s chemical structure and binding affinity may be altered. Additionally, maleimide groups are susceptible to hydrolysis, leading to the formation of non-reactive derivatives. For instance, researchers conjugated activated protamine with the -SH group on the cysteine residue of an antibody molecule to achieve antibody modification [76].

Carbodiimide chemistry

The EDC/NHS activation method facilitates the formation of stable amide bonds between the carboxyl and amine groups. EDC, or 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide, is a water-soluble carbodiimide that efficiently activates carboxyl groups, resulting in the formation of a stable activated NHS (N-hydroxysuccinimide) ester. This NHS ester can rapidly react with primary amines to form amide bonds. For optimal results, amide bond formation is typically carried out under mild alkaline conditions within a pH range of 7 to 8 [77]. This method is easy to operate; however, it is challenging to control the orientation of the binding sites and ligands.

Click chemistry

Click chemistry is an efficient coupling method, typically involving a cycloaddition reaction between azides and alkynes. Its advantages include mild reaction conditions, simple purification processes, and high product yields [20]. For example, Xia et al. developed transferrin (Tf)-guided polymeric carriers to deliver the PLK1 inhibitor volasertib, enhancing targeting of AML. The Tf modification proceeds in two steps: first, the transferrin is modified with an NHS ester containing dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) in a DMSO solution to obtain Tf-DBCO. Then, Tf-DBCO is added to a solution of azide-functionalized polymers for coupling via click chemistry, Studies have shown that a Tf surface density of 4% yields optimal targeting. In the 4% Tf-modified group, uptake by MV-4-11 cells increased approximately three-fold compared with the free drug group [78].

Non-covalent modification of ligands

Non-covalent ligand binding involves the adsorption of ligands onto the NP surfaces surfaces through weak interactions, including electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic effects, hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, etc. This method is characterized by its simplicity, rapid fabrication, and low cytotoxicity. However, the interactions between the ligands and NPs are relatively weak [79].

The biotin–avidin system is a biochemical tool based on the high-affinity interaction between biotin and avidin or streptavidin. Compared to other non-covalent methods, this approach is less sensitive to external factors such as denaturing agents, proteolytic enzymes, pH, and temperature. Chen et al. incubated biotinylated anti-human CD33 antibodies with streptavidin-coupled exosome vesicles at room temperature, which significantly enhanced the uptake efficiency of CD33-positive AML cells [80]. Recently, an Fc-specific, universal antibody capture system has been developed, achieving over an eight-fold improvement in antibody binding efficiency compared with unmodified LNPs. Collectively, these advances suggest that ligand modification is progressing toward increasingly precise, dynamic, and intelligent systems [81].

Ligand-modified nanocarriers for precise targeting of leukemia cells

For many years, chemotherapy drugs have played a key role in the treatment of hematological disorders such as leukemia. However, as clinical applications progress, challenges such as side effects, drug resistance, and limitations in biocompatibility and pharmacokinetics have become increasingly apparent. These challenges significantly limit the efficacy and range of application [82–84]. thus, the development of new treatments is urgently required. Nanocarriers, as a novel drug delivery system, offer high biocompatibility, drug-loading capacity, and targeting capabilities, bringing new hope for leukemia treatment. Here, we primarily discuss the critical issue of enhancing the targeting of chemotherapy drugs using nanocarriers. The EPR effect refers to the phenomenon in which the inter-endothelial gaps in tumor and inflamed tissues are larger than those in normal tissues owing to excessive angiogenesis. This provides a unique mechanism that allows NPs to selectively extravasate into tumors and be retained there owing to the insufficient lymphatic drainage [85]. In contrast to solid tumors, hematologic malignancies are diffusely distributed and primarily reside within the circulatory system [86], lacking a stable microenvironment for local accumulation. Therefore, active targeting is typically achieved by functionalizing the the NP surface with specific ligands to enable directed delivery [87]. However, several factors, including molecular weight, size, binding affinity, and molecular structure, must be comprehensively considered when selecting ligands (Table 3).

After years of in-depth research, the application of single ligands in leukemia has matured and been continually refined (Table 4). Furthermore, researchers have explored the use of multiple ligands by combining different functional ligands. These combinations can exhibit synergistic effects that cannot be achieved by single ligands, leading to enhanced cancer drug uptake and target specificity.

Single-ligand modified nanocarriers targeting leukemia cells

Antibody-conjugated nanocarrier

Antibody therapies have been proven effective in treating human diseases. In recent years, innovative monoclonal antibody derivatives, including ADCs, nanoantibodies, and BsAbs, have emerged [114], surpassing traditional monoclonal antibodies. The conjugation of these derivatives with NPs broadens the therapeutic scope and enhances targeting precision [115]. The Breda team developed a novel LNP carrying mRNA, conjugated with an anti-CD117 antibody. This antibody specifically targets the CD117 receptor on hematopoietic stem cells. This technology enables effective gene editing and cell function control. In vivo studies showed that animals treated with CD117/LNP-Cre exhibited a significantly enhanced editing efficiency in the peripheral blood cells and long-term hematopoietic stem cells. The editing efficiency was three times higher than that of the control group [116].

A NP structure encapsulated with a recombinant antibody fragment targeting CD19, conjugated to human serum albumin, demonstrated a significant reduction in tumor burden and improved survival rates in a humanized zebrafish model of ALL [117]. Chen et al. developed a synthetic protein nanogel formulation (CR-TNG) that effectively activates the immune system through a combination of anti-CD47 antibody (aCD47) and R848 while simultaneously targeting AML stem cells via TIM-3, in an AML CDX model, mice treated with CR-TNG exhibited a median survival of 60 days, whereas all mice in the control groups—including free aCD47 + R848, CR-ING, C-TNG, and R-TNG—succumbed within 45 days. The authors concluded that CR-TNG effectively eliminates residual leukemic stem cells in the BM, thereby preventing relapse and resistance in AML therapy [118]. Compared to conventional antibodies, nanobodies possess a smaller molecular weight, higher affinity, and improved stability. CD7 CAR-T therapy (NS7CAR-T) demonstrated remarkable efficacy and favorable safety in a Phase I clinical trial. Among 10 treated patients, seven (70%) achieved complete remission, and eight (80%) experienced only mild cytokine release syndrome (CRS), with no reports of severe (grade ≥ 3) CRS [119].

Peptide-conjugated nanocarrier

Peptide ligands offer numerous advantages, including simple structures, ease of synthesis, strong affinity for target binding, and low immunogenicity, making them ideal candidates for active targeting ligands [120]. Gu et al. reported a reduction-sensitive polymer micelle conjugated with the A6 peptide and vincristine (A6-cPS-VCR). Flow cytometry results indicated that the uptake of A6-cPS in MV4-11 cells was more than twice that of cPS, demonstrating that the targeting ability of the A6 peptide significantly enhanced the intracellular uptake of the drug [121]. In recent years, dual-function peptides have been developed, which not only possess targeting recognition capabilities but also exert therapeutic effects. For example, Xu et al. developed a nanomicelle loaded with the CD123-targeting antagonist peptide (mPO-6). This formulation not only enhanced the recognition of CD123 + AML cells but also exerted antagonistic effects, resulting in a significant reduction in leukemia cell infiltration in the bone marrow of AML mice [122]. To address immune dysfunction, Xin et al. developed an oral NP vaccine system (mPOD) that targets Peyer’s patches and activates cytotoxic immunity. By modifying MDP lipids, the system stimulates the NOD2/RIG-I pathway to elicit robust and sustained antitumor responses. In RAW264.7 cells, mPOD treatment increased IFN-β and IFN-γ levels by 2.8-fold and 1.7-fold, respectively, whereas in hMDDCs, the number of CD80⁺ cells induced by mPOD was 11.2-fold higher than in the PBS control group. In the C1498 AML animal model, mice treated with mPOD exhibited a median survival of 70 days compared with 45 days in the control group [123].

Aptamers-conjugated nanocarrier

Aptamers are oligonucleotide or peptide molecules selected for their ability to fold into specific structures and bind to target biomolecules [124] Compared to antibodies, aptamers offer numerous unique advantages, including smaller size, lower cost, ease of chemical modification, and strong tumor penetrability [125, 126]. The targeting mechanism involves the interaction between aptamers and target molecules on the cell membrane or inside the cell, facilitating internalization and tissue penetration to inhibit cell proliferation [127].

Different subtypes of leukemia exhibit variations in clinical presentation, treatment methods, and prognosis. Accurately determining the subtype is crucial for developing effective treatment plans. For example, a study demonstrated the potential of aptamers in diagnostics. Mousavi et al. conjugated T2-KK1B10 and ATP aptamers onto the surface of silica NPs. Their results showed that when the designed aptamer-based fluorescent sensors specifically bound to target cells, the fluorescence signal increased proportionally with cell number (from 2 × 10⁴ to 5 × 10⁴ cells per well), demonstrating a positive correlation between signal intensity and the number of leukemic cells [128]. Heydari et al. developed chitosan-coated mesoporous silica NPs conjugated with aptamers for the co-delivery of cytarabine and doxorubicin. The modified aptamer NPs were specifically bound to the K562 cell line and were internalized into the cells, effectively retaining the drugs and enhancing the antitumor efficacy of the chemotherapy agents [129].

Folate-conjugated nanocarrier

Folate is a naturally occurring vitamin [130], and four subtypes that are highly expressed on the surface of tumor cell membranes have been identified: FRα, FRβ, FRγ, and FRδ. Among these, the expression of FRβ in leukemia cells has been confirmed as a promising therapeutic target [131, 132]. Folate, as a targeting ligand, has numerous advantages, including small size, low cost, and low immunogenicity [133]. Wang et al. developed an amide-bond-linked folate-functionalized co-delivery NP system (As/IMA@FA NPs) for combination therapy. In folate receptor-expressing K562 tumor cells, this system induced an approximately eight-fold increase in intracellular fluorescence intensity compared to the non-functionalized As/IMA NPs. In vivo distribution studies showed that at both tumor fluorescence signals and arsenic content were significantly higher in the folate-targeted group compared with the non-targeted group at 24 h post-administration, highlighting the advantage of folate-functionalized nanocarriers in enhancing the efficacy while reducing off-target distribution [134]. Li et al. developed folate receptor-modified lipid bilayer-coated mesoporous silica NPs loaded with paclitaxel and tanshinone IIA. Experiments demonstrated that these NPs exhibited excellent drug-loading capacity and tumor-targeting capability [135].

Protein-conjugated nanocarrier

The Tf is a plasma glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in the transport of iron [136]. By binding to transferrin receptors (TfR), various substances can be delivered to target sites for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes [137, 138]. Although not expressed in normal cells, TfR is overexpressed in many malignant tumor cells [139]. This characteristic makes TfR a valuable target in leukemia treatment, and researchers have developed various therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting TfR. For example, Wu et al. employed a disassembly/reassembly approach to load Ara-C into the cavity of ferritin (Ara-C@HFn) to target AML cells. Compared with free Ara-C, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of Ara-C@HFn was reduced by 50%, exhibiting enhanced cytotoxicity. In vivo imaging showed that systemic leukemic signal intensity in the Ara-C@HFn-treated group was significantly lower than that in the free Ara-C group, approximately 15.6% of the latter, with a lower leukemic burden and no observable toxicity [140].

In addition to the commonly used ligands discussed above, additional ligands have been developed in conjunction with the identification of novel biomarkers, such as high-density lipoprotein, hyaluronic acid, and lectins. Gold NPs (AuNPs) possess biocompatibility, safety, and high tolerance [141]. Their large surface area allows most ligands to bind firmly to their surface [142]. Based on this property, Shen et al. functionalized gold NPs with high-density lipoprotein to enable binding to its receptor, SR-B1. This approach significantly enhanced the differentiation of leukemic cells in the BM, with the targeted group showing 71 ± 19% differentiation compared to 27 ± 5% in the control group and 57 ± 12% in the free BMS group [143].

HA specifically binds to CD44 [144]. In a study on AML, lipid-polymer hybrid NPs co-loaded with DOX and gallic acid exhibited excellent anti-leukemia effects both in vitro and in vivo [145]. Similarly, Xu et al. developed a nanogel (HA/Cis/Dau) composed of HA, cisplatin (Cis), and daunorubicin (Dau) through platinum coordination and electrostatic interactions, introducing a novel differentiation-based strategy for treating rrAML. In vitro, HA/Cis/Dau induced approximately a two-fold increase in nuclear size in CD44-high Kasumi-1 cells. Similarly, in primary AML patient-derived BM progenitor cells, differentiation markers CD11b and CD14 were increased 4.3-fold and 4.5-fold, respectively, compared with untreated controls. In vivo efficacy studies using the AE & CKIT⁰⁸¹⁶ⱽ rrAML mouse model demonstrated a 40-day survival rate of 75% for the HA/Cis/Dau group, compared with only 20% in the free dual-drug group. This approach avoids drug resistance caused by direct cytotoxicity of conventional chemotherapy while reducing cardiac, renal, and BM toxicity by over 60%, successfully achieving a balance between potent therapeutic effect and safety [146]. Lectins are carbohydrate-binding proteins, including glycoproteins, that can be isolated from a wide variety of plant and animal sources. Importantly, certain lectins exhibit high affinity for tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens, such as the Thomsen–Friedenreich antigen (TF-Ag). For example, Akram et al. conjugated lectin to gold NPs via PEG (Au NPs-PEG-Lectin) to target the TF-Ag protein on the surface of leukemic cells. Molecular docking revealed a binding free energy of − 6.65 kcal/mol between lectin and TF-Ag, indicating strong affinity. Compared with free lectin (IC₅₀ = 443.7 µg/mL) and Au NPs-PEG (IC₅₀ = 712.4 µg/mL), Au NPs-PEG-Lectin reduced the IC₅₀ for HL-60 cells to 132.5 µg/mL and, at a dose of 200 µg/mL, increased the apoptosis rate from 7.75% in the control group to 51.03% [147].

Dual-ligand modified nanocarriers targeting leukemia cells

The dual-targeting strategy refers to the use of two different ligands to target the same cell or two different receptors on different cells [148] (Fig. 4). Compared to single ligands, simultaneously targeting multiple pathways associated with tumor progression is more likely to reduce the risks of drug resistance and immune evasion and is less affected by the dynamic changes of cell surface receptors [149]. Next, we will focus on recent advancements in the combined modification of nanocarriers with various types of ligands for leukemia treatment.

Bispecific antibody-conjugated nanocarrier

Owing to the numerous subtypes of leukemia, monospecific antibodies can only bind to a single epitope on the cell surface [150].However, the onset of the disease often involves multiple targets and pathways [151].To overcome this limitation, BsAbs have been developed that simultaneously target two different antigens or two different epitopes of the same antigen.

The PEGylated liposomal DOX (Caelyx) represents the first liposomal drug approved by the U.S. FDA. Although it has demonstrated significant efficacy in the treatment of leukemia, further optimization is required to enhance its targeting capability toward cancer cells. Zeng et al. developed a dual-targeting nanobody T cell activator (CD33/CD123-TCE), targeting the co-expressed CD33 and CD123 antigens on leukemia cells [152]. Hokw et al. constructed a bispecific antibody by fusing an anti-CD20 full antibody with an anti-mPEG single-chain antibody, enabling recognition of methyl polyethylene glycol (mPEG) on liposomal doxorubicin. When incubated with leukemia Raji cells using different antibodies, the CD20 Ab-mPEG scFv increased internalization capability to 60% within 24 h, demonstrating specific endocytic ability [153]. In another study, a BsAb was designed with one end binding to the PEG on the surface of Caelyx NPs and the other end recognizing leukemia cell receptors (CD19, CD20, CD22, or CD38), resulting in the generation of four BsAbs. Results showed that the BsAb–Caelyx complex exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxic activity and targeting efficiency in vitro against B-ALL cell lines and primary AML samples compared with the unmodified group. The EC50 values decreased by up to 285-fold, 56-fold, and 297-fold for CD19, CD20, CD22, and CD38, respectively, whereas the EC50 was reduced by up to 20-fold [154].

A novel co-assembled formulation combining CD99 and FLT3 reduced leukemia burden and extended survival in an FLT3-ITD AML mouse model [155]. Bai et al. designed pH-responsive, dual-targeted nanocarriers using Prussian blue NPs (PBNPs) functionalized for CXCR4 and CD44, with doxorubicin as a model drug. Targeted uptake was first validated in HL-60 cells, where the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of ES/HA-PP-PBNPs was 1.54-fold higher than HA-PP-PBNPs and 1.26-fold higher than ES-PP-PBNPs. In vivo, the dual-targeted PBNPs exhibited stronger anti-AML activity, with the lowest HL-60 cell counts observed in the spleen: PBNPs group, 2.84 ± 0.77%; ES-PP-PBNPs group, 4.5 ± 0.08%; HA-PP-PBNPs group, 6.38 ± 1.01% [156]. Under dynamic blood circulation conditions, the binding ability of ligand-modified nanomedicines to their target receptors is particularly important. Song et al. constructed dual-target liposomal vesicles incorporating two integrin αvβ3 ligands, imparting “fast binding/slow dissociation” characteristics to the nanovesicles. The study demonstrated that the dual-target liposomal vesicles significantly enhanced drug uptake and antitumor efficacy under both static and dynamic conditions [157]. Zhang et al. developed a bispecific nanoplatform (CSF@E-Hn) capable of targeting the BM, bringing tumor cells and NK cells into proximity to activate NK cells for specific targeting and elimination of tumor cells within the marrow. The BM-targeting efficiency of CSF@E-Hn was evaluated in female C57BL/6J mice, with fluorescence signals peaking at 8 h—approximately 3–4 times higher than the single-ligand groups—demonstrating that hematopoietic stem cell-derived nanovesicles can achieve efficient marrow homing. Interestingly, in secondary tumor challenge experiments, CSF@E-Hn effectively controlled tumor growth, and the number of progenitor cells in the BM post-treatment was 1.8–2.5 times higher than in controls, highlighting its strong potential for long-term prevention of tumor relapse [158].

The BsAbs represent a cutting-edge approach in the antibody therapeutics and are considered a second-generation therapy for cancer treatment. However, many of them are still in the preclinical development stage (Table 5), and their industrialization faces numerous challenges, such as addressing mismatching and purification issues, improving downstream process stability, ensuring the stability of BsAbs, and balancing the expression levels of the two antibodies. More effective strategies are needed to optimize various BsAbs.

Dual-peptides-conjugated nanocarrier

Peptides possess a high degree of customizability [159]. The conjugation of dual-targeting peptides with nanocarriers enables targeted drug delivery. Chueahongthong F et al. designed FLT3-specific dual peptides, CKR1 and EVQ, targeting the FLT3 protein overexpressed in leukemia cells, and conjugated them to doxorubicin-loaded polymeric micelles [160]. Experimental results showed that the dual-peptide conjugates exhibited the highest cytotoxicity, offering valuable insights for the nanomodification of peptide pairs. Furthermore, Fan et al. developed a dual-peptide-modified drug delivery system targeting the BM niche. The DSS6 oligopeptide specifically targets the endosteal niche, whereas the F56 oligopeptide binds to the endothelial cell surface VEGFR1. This dual targeting enables effective delivery of arsenic trioxide to the vascular wall niche in the BM. The study found that 50% modification with DSS6 enhanced specific binding of NPs to the dry endosteum surface, whereas 20% coupling with F56 significantly increased NP internalization in HUVEC cells [161]. In addition to dual-peptide conjugation, peptides can also combine with various ligands to form nanodelivery systems. For example, Ren et al. designed an aptamer–peptide dual-targeted CRISPR-Cas9 nanocarrier system (P@PPM) to achieve permanent CXCR4 gene knockdown, thereby reversing leukemia relapse driven by the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis. Flow cytometry analysis showed that CXCR4 protein knockdown efficiency with P@PPM (> 60%) was significantly higher than that of P@PM (~ 40%) and P@PP (~ 20–30%). Targeting studies demonstrated that cellular uptake of P@PPM was 2.5-fold and 1.7-fold higher than P@PM and P@PP, respectively. Ex vivo editing of patient-derived cells revealed significant reductions in both CXCR4 protein and mRNA fluorescence intensity [162].

Chitosan is a natural amino polysaccharide derived from the deacetylation of chitin, known for its safety and non-toxicity [163]. Its structure contains active amine groups that facilitate the conjugation of ligands for targeted drug delivery. Researchers developed gold nanorods (GNRs) functionalized with chitosan and peptide-12, which significantly enhanced drug accumulation at tumor sites [164]. Zwitterionic betaine (ZS) is an ampholytic polymer containing equal amounts of cationic and anionic groups, known for its superior hydration properties and near-neutral surface charge. Bai et al. designed hollow mesoporous Prussian blue NPs incorporating the ZS and the CXCR4 antagonist peptide E5 ligands for targeted and synergistic chemophotothermal therapy [165].

Dual-aptamers-conjugated nanocarrier

Dual-targeting aptamers can recognize two different biomarkers on the living cells, enhancing the aptamer-receptor interactions. Taghdisi et al. designed a doxorubicin-gold NP composite modified with sgc8c and AS1411 aptamers. Treatment of Molt-4 cells with PA-Dau-AuNPs enhanced the fluorescence intensity of Dau in the target cells, which confirmed that the dual-aptamer-modified AuNPs were able to distinguish between the target and non-target cells, ensuring precise delivery [166]. In another study by Ai et al., previously selected leukemia aptamers Sgc8c and XQ-2d was utilized to develop dual-targeting circular aptamers (DTCA), replacing thymidine nucleosides in DTCA with Fu for targeted drug delivery, thereby increasing the cytotoxicity of Fu [167], The combination of this approach with nanotechnology is likely to provide a new paradigm for precise tumor diagnosis and targeted tumor therapy. Additionally, a research team recently developed a DNA-based drug delivery aptamer that can precisely recognize LSCs, achieving a “dual-hit” therapeutic effect [168].

Transferrin/alendronate-conjugated nanocarrier

Co-delivery systems incorporating alendronate (Aln) or Tf with other ligands exhibit potential for bone tissue targeting. Aln, a bisphosphonate compound, demonstrates high affinity for bone mineral matrices [169]. Abou-Elnour et al. developed lipid nanocarriers that were doubly modified with alendronate and lactoferrin to achieve the co-delivery of ivermectin and dihydrojasmonic acid. These dual-modified nanocarriers demonstrated good bone homing capability and effectiveness in leukemia treatment in both in vitro and in vivo experiments [170]. Zhu et al. developed a dual nanodrug delivery system combining aptamers and Tf modifications, with the dual-ligand modification group demonstrating the most significant therapeutic effect on AML [171]. The Wu team developed NPs that achieve precise targeting by leveraging Tf and complement C3b to specifically bind CD71 and CD11b receptors overexpressed on AML cells. In vitro, NPs achieved a DOX uptake rate of 73.9% in patient-derived leukemic cells, compared with only 6.19% for free DOX. In vivo, in AML-bearing mice, the proportion of normal mouse cells (mCD45⁺) in peripheral blood was only 21.6% in the NP-treated group, compared with 93.7% in the free DOX group, further confirming a substantial reduction in off-target effects [172]. Similarly, He et al. proposed CD44/transferrin receptor dual-targeted self-delivery micelles, which demonstrated a similar trend in the co-delivery of DOX and artemether [173].

Complexity and potential challenges of dual-ligand nanocarriers

Although dual-ligand systems may exhibit superior potential over single-ligand systems under certain conditions, their construction is generally challenging owing to complex preparation processes, compromised antibody functionality, and suboptimal nanocarrier biocompatibility. To date, antibody-conjugated NPs have not yet achieved clinical translation [174, 175]. Specifically, taking BsAbs as an example, the mode by which antibodies are conjugated to nanocarriers can significantly affect the antibody activity as well as the stability and spatial conformation of the nanocarriers, including key parameters such as particle size distribution and morphological uniformity Covalent modification methods offer higher coupling efficiency and batch-to-batch reproducibility, but they frequently generate heterogeneous products with randomly oriented ligands. Such misalignment can weaken the functional binding between the targeting antibody and cell surface receptors [176]. Recently, a research team proposed a non-covalent targeting strategy based on BsAbs and analyzed the biodistribution of luciferase mRNA-loaded LNPs in EGFR⁺ human tumor xenograft mice. In the targeted group, luciferase expression in tumor tissues was over eightfold higher than in the non-targeted group, the tumor signal decreased by only 10% after 48 h [177]. Although this strategy demonstrates improved pharmacokinetics and better maintenance of ligand affinity, the increased complexity of the two-step administration and the associated challenges with the clinical implementation must also be considered.

In fact, dual- or multi-targeting strategies do not often demonstrate superior antitumor efficacy compared with single-targeting in non-leukemia disease models. Ligand density critically influences the ultimate antitumor effect. In a previous study, researchers developed trastuzumab-functionalized gold NPs for a subcutaneous MCF-7 breast cancer mouse model. In vitro, NPs carrying two antibodies exhibited improved cellular uptake compared with single-antibody NPs whereas the opposite was observed in vivo. It was hypothesized that excessively high ligand density may have limited the accessibility of ligands to the receptors due to spatial constraints, thereby reducing the binding efficiency [178]. Current literature lacks rigorous characterization of ligand density, as most quantitative methods are indirect. There remains a need for tools capable of precisely controlling NP ligand density to systematically study ligand–receptor interactions in targeted NPs [175].

Another obstacle to the therapeutic efficacy of dual-ligand NPs is circulation stability, which can lead to premature and non-specific release of the encapsulated drugs. Although researchers have explored a range of stimulus-responsive nanomedicines such as those responsive to temperature, pH, enzymes, or reducing agents, the assessment of material biocompatibility and the complexity of material synthesis further complicate clinical translation.

From bench to bedside: translational status and challenges of ligand-modified nanocarriers

Current translational status

The clinical translation of ligand-targeted nanomedicines remains at a relatively early stage with only limited number of nanomedicines, primarily liposomal formulations, have been approved or are under clinical investigation (Table 6) At present, there is still a lack of ligand-modified nanocarriers that have been clearly approved and successfully commercialized for the treatment of hematological malignancies. CAR-T therapy has successfully implemented this concept at the cellular scale. Although there are substantial differences between the two approaches in terms of carrier scale and mode of action, they are highly aligned in the core principle of precise tumor targeting. This strategy clearly exemplifies the concept of using genetic engineering to endow effector cells with enhanced recognition capabilities to exclusively eliminate the tumor cells [179]. It provides valuable clinical proof-of-concept, guidance for target selection, and translational experience for the development of nanocarrier-based therapeutics following the same strategy [180]. To date, six CAR-T products have been approved by the FDA (Table 7) [181].

Chemistry, manufacturing, and control considerations

Although ligand-modified nanocarriers can achieve specific targeting through ligand decoration, their complexity is further increased owing to multiple functional components, such as the incorporation of targeting ligands and co-encapsulation of multiple drugs. The chemistry, manufacturing, and control aspects play a critical role in their development. Specialized equipment, production processes, and scale all impact the quality, safety, and efficacy of ligand-modified nanocarrier systems [188]. Even minor process variations, such as the mixing speed or temperature, can significantly alter key quality attributes including particle size, surface charge, antibody density, and drug loading/encapsulation efficiency, thereby affecting in vivo distribution and therapeutic performance. Therefore, large-scale, reproducible synthesis of nanomedicine products remains a major challenge in their translation from the preclinical stage to clinical applications [189].

Immunogenicity and toxicity

During clinical translation, excessively complex surface modifications on nanocarriers can readily adsorb plasma proteins, leading to the formation of a “biomolecular corona” and thereby triggering nonspecific immune responses. The use of PEG-based materials to prolong systemic circulation time may give rise to similar issues. Therefore, studies have explored several strategies to reduce immune recognition such as controlling ligand density, selecting smaller targeting ligands, and replacing conventional PEG with non-PEG hydrophilic polymers (such as ZS materials) [190].

On the other hand, the intrinsic toxicity of nanocarriers should also be taken into consideration. For example, under equivalent concentration conditions, cationic liposomes bearing permanent positive charges typically exhibit higher cytotoxicity than neutral liposomes [191]. Some inorganic NPs, such as gold NPs and carbon nanotubes, can kill leukemic cells but also exert toxicity toward normal cells, necessitating thorough in vivo safety evaluations during preclinical development [192].

Regulatory and intellectual property barriers

Nanotechnology faces significant regulatory challenges owing to its novelty and the complexity of risk assessment. Regulatory authorities typically focus on issues such as insufficient batch-to-batch uniformity, the lack of well-established characterization methods, and difficulties in adequately verifying long-term stability and safety. In addition, the commercialization of nanomedicines is hindered by substantial intellectual property challenges, particularly overlapping patent landscapes and unclear boundaries of patent rights, which may delay the product development and increase costs. Therefore, involving patent attorneys and commercialization teams at an early stage of the development of nanomedicine is essential to ensure patentability, freedom to operate, and implementation of effective intellectual property protection strategies [193].

Clinical and economic considerations

The development of nanocarriers involves substantial investment, with key challenges including: (1) high costs of instrumentation, manufacturing equipment, and other facilities; (2) technical and quality control difficulties in scaling up production, which further increase manufacturing expenses; and (3) a mismatch between the high production costs and low number of potential consumers. Furthermore, the clinical translation efficiency of nanomedicines often faces challenges, as the in vivo selectivity of targeted nanocarriers frequently does not align with promising in vitro results. These discrepancies arise from differences between the static cell culture environments and the dynamic living systems, as no in vivo model can fully recapitulate human AML [194, 195].

Finally, to enhance the clinical translation success and achieve the full potential of ligand-modified nanocarriers, it is essential to develop more predictive preclinical animal models and to continuously improve the adoption of GLP and standardized guidelines in academia.

Conclusion and prospects

In this review, we attempted to describe the roles of several classic ligands, either alone or in combination (jointly conjugated) on the surface of NP carriers, in the diagnosis and treatment of AML. Antibodies are widely recognized to offer higher specificity for their target receptors and have already been commercialized, such as the ADC conjugates. Although other ligands (aptamers, peptides, small molecules) have not yet resulted in commercial products, they have accumulated a substantial body of preclinical research. Ligand-targeted NP delivery systems have proven to be a solid resource for targeting strategies, with the following main advantages: (1) they can specifically deliver drugs to leukemia cells, thus increasing the drug efficacy; (2) they reduce dosage and minimize off-target distribution, which decrease the systemic toxicity; (3) dual-ligand targeted therapy can simultaneously block multiple signaling pathways in tumor cells, helping to reduce the possibility of tumor cells escaping drug action via a single pathway, thereby lowering the resistance of tumors to treatment.

In addition to ligand targeting in leukemia treatment, biomimetic NPs derived from cell membranes also possess active targeting functions [196]. Lian et al. proposed and validated a NP platform based on natural apolipoproteins, which enables efficient delivery of siRNA, antisense oligonucleotides, and mRNA to BM myeloid cells and their precursor cells [197]. By integrating ligand targeting with biomimetic nanotechnology, organelle-level targeting can be achieved. An engineered cell membrane vesicle NP carrier (Cas-CMV@LM) was developed, modified with CD33 and CD123 aptamers. Cas-CMV@LM can home to the bone marrow via CXCR4-CMV, and then utilize the CD33/CD123 aptamers to target leukemia cells [198]. However, challenges such as low yield of cell membranes, loss of in vitro activity, and safety concerns in human applications still need to be addressed. The SORT-LNP development team has launched a novel BM-targeting LNP. Mass spectrometry proteomics analysis of serum proteins adsorbed on the BM-homing LNPs revealed that apolipoprotein E (ApoE) facilitated the targeted delivery of LNPs to the BM [199]. CAR-T therapy has long been an effective method for treating hematologic cancers. Currently, six CAR-T cell therapies have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of various hematologic malignancies, and numerous global clinical trials are underway, primarily targeting CD123 and CD33 [200]. Currently, a disruptive technological revolution is rapidly emerging—in vivo CAR-T cell therapy! The team at Capstan Therapeutics has designed a targeted LNP that can deliver specific CAR mRNA to the CD8 + T cell subset, generating CAR-T cells in vivo, which can effectively eliminate B-cell leukemia tumors [201]. Meanwhile, Wang and colleagues have developed an innovative ‘leukemia testing chip’ system by integrating microfluidic organ-on-a-chip technology with single-cell omics analysis, creating a 3D vascularized human leukemia BM microenvironment model with immune activity, which provides an ideal platform for CAR-T cell therapy modeling and screening [202].

Combination therapies have become a major trend. The diversified application of nanomaterials in ligand modification and immunotherapy models, coupled with the assistance of artificial intelligence (AI) in the ligand design and optimization, may lead to a future revolution in AML targeted therapy. The main frontier development directions are as follows: (1) AI-driven discovery and validation of new AML therapeutic targets: Through AI platforms, true targets closely related to the onset and progression of leukemia can be identified from a large number of candidate molecules, distinguishing leukemia cells from normal cells. These targets can then be validated in preclinical models (for example, the discovery of SIK2/3 targets) [203]. (2) Real-time monitoring and therapeutic feedback driven by nanosensors: Functionalized nanomaterials, such as liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, have been employed to construct electrochemical, optical, and magnetic nanosensors capable of ultra-sensitive, real-time detection of leukemia biomarkers—including circulating tumor cells, circulating tumor dna, proteins, and micrornas [204]. These platforms enable early diagnosis and continuous monitoring of disease progression, providing a foundation for responsive and personalized therapeutic interventions. (3) AI-based innovation in ligand design and optimization: Recently, Science magazine reported the latest achievements in applying AI for the design, screening, and optimization of G protein-coupled receptors, and for the first time, it reported a series of antibodies with CXCR7 agonistic activity. AI significantly reduces research and development costs and time by accelerating the discovery of potential drug candidates. Moreover, selecting appropriate modification and conjugation strategies are crucial for preparing conjugates. Previous research reported an innovative microfluidic chip developed by Teng Lesheng’s team that integrates LNP preparation with ligand conjugation in a single-step process [205]. In another report, ApoE was fused with an antibody Fc fragment and could spontaneously adsorb onto the surface of LNPs [206]. These strategies significantly improve the uniformity and batch reproducibility of ligand modification, providing a feasible path for the large-scale production and clinical translation of targeted LNPs. With the deep integration of nanotechnology and AI, it is expected to establish an accurate medical framework for AML treatment. By collaboratively addressing the current challenges in ligand industrialization, we can advance AML treatment towards more specific, less toxic, and individualized solutions.
