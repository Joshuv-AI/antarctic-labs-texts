Network architecture optimization for ophthalmic ultrasound image detection based on modular ablation of multi-version YOLO
Authors: Zemeng Li, Xiaochun Wang, Xinqi Yu, Zhiyuan Zhao, Yan Wang, Sheng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo address the absence of a systematic evaluation method for network architecture selection in ophthalmic ultrasound image detection tasks, this study proposes a modular ablation analysis framework based on orthogonal experimental design.MethodsA clinical data set comprising 1121 ocular ultrasound images was established. YOLOv10–v12 were decoupled into backbone, neck, and head modules. A three-stage evaluation was conducted: (1) single-module benchmarking, performed via controlled variable experiments; (2) orthogonal combination experiments using an L9(34) array, analyzed through range analysis and interaction heatmaps; and (3) optimal architecture selection, implemented via Pareto front analysis. The best model was applied to ocular tissue localization, and a segmented sound velocity matching algorithm was used to automatically measure biometric parameters, including anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and axial length.ResultsThe backbone improved both accuracy and efficiency, while the neck and head exhibited a speed–accuracy trade-off. The neck most significantly influenced detection accuracy, and the head dominated computational efficiency. The optimal combination (Bv11–Nv11–Hv10) achieved 64.0% mAP at 26 FPS, while the mobile-optimized variant (Bv10–Nv10–Hv11) attained 63.5% mAP with only 8.6 MB parameters. Automatic and manual measurements showed strong agreement (mean absolute error ≤ 0.133 mm, ICC ≥ 0.839).ConclusionsThis study validates the feasibility of cross-version module combination. The proposed framework offers a systematic, quantitative decision-making basis for network design in ophthalmic ultrasound, balancing accuracy, speed, and deployment feasibility. Clinical results confirm high consistency between automatic and manual measurements, supporting automated and precise ocular biometry.Keywords: Ophthalmic ultrasound images, Target detection, Yolo, Modular ablation analysis, Architecture optimization

Introduction

With the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic techniques in ophthalmology, the diagnosis and treatment of ophthalmic diseases tend to be standardized, individualized, and precise [1]. Ocular tissue biometry refers to the application of examination methods to measure structural parameters of ocular biological tissues to provide a reference basis for the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases. With the development of cataract extraction combined with intraocular lens implantation surgery and refractive corneal surgery, the technique of ocular axis biometry has been increasingly emphasized by clinicians [2–4]. How to obtain accurate parameters of each component of the ocular axis is a matter of great concern, since any small error can make the surgery less than ideal [5].

Early studies have shown that 17–36% of postoperative refractive errors are caused by inaccurate measurements of ocular axial length [6]. Ultrasound biometry is a common clinical method for measuring the ocular axial length. A study [7] has shown the correlation between OCT-based IOLMaster measurements and ultrasound-based segmental acoustic velocity measurements, both of which can measure ocular axis parameters and can be used interchangeably. The advantage of ultrasound technology over optical biometry is that it is not dependent on the transparency of the ocular tissues (cornea, aqueous humor, lens, and vitreous). For example, in the case of vitreous hemorrhage, optical biometry may not be able to measure axial length, whereas ultrasound systems can. Among various types of ultrasound biometry, B-scan ultrasound-guided segmental acoustic velocity measurement is a true visual acoustic measurement method that can intuitively differentiate between the interfaces of tissues, which is especially useful for identifying the emergence of abnormal intraocular lesions, such as vitreous hemorrhage or retinal detachment, and accurately labeling the interface position. The operation method is to set the B + A scanning mode, anesthetize the surface of the eye, insert a suitable eye cup into the conjunctival sac, and inject normal saline. After the probe is started, the probe is immersed in the goggle solution medium with the probe marker line facing the nasal or temporal side of the affected eye (horizontal axial scan). Gain is adjusted so that there is no obvious clutter in the vitreous cavity and the structure between the tissues is clear. The sampling line should coincide with the optic axis and pass as parallel as possible to the center of each refractive interstitial interface to the center of the macula. Finally, the operator manually identifies five corresponding A-scan ultrasound signals from the B-scan image to calculate the axial length [8].

However, the effectiveness of traditional manual measurement is affected by the operator’s ultrasound experience, working conditions, and other factors, resulting in high labor costs and low detection efficiency. With the rapid development of machine vision detection technology and the frequent occurrence of ocular diseases, more researchers are focusing on ocular biometry. Jeong et al. [9] designed a deep-learning method based on fundus images to predict axial eye length and visualization techniques to localize specific regions in the input image used for prediction. Wang et al. [10] predicted ocular axis length from ultra-wide field images in patients with moderate to severe myopia based on the InceptionResNetV2 network architecture. Wang et al. [11–13] proposed a method that enables automatic localization of the scleral spur and angle recess as well as quantitative assessment of ocular tissues. Through the intelligent recognition of the anterior chamber angle and trabecular–iris angle, preventive treatment of early glaucoma is possible. Li et al. [14] developed a software package that can automatically measure the iris trabecular angle with good agreement between automatic and manual measurements. Minhaz et al. [15] developed a fully automated ciliary segmentation and quantitative volume measurement method for three-dimensional ultrasound biological microscope images. Cao et al. [16] proposed a deep-learning-based method to utilize two-dimensional digital photographs to automatically and objectively assess morphological eyelid features. Medeiros et al. [17] and Christopher et al. [18] proposed a new method using optical coherence tomography data to train a deep-learning model that enables automatic measurement of the thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer and, thus, assessment of glaucomatous structural loss. Chu et al. [19] proposed a CBC–YOLOv5s segmentation method for optic nerve sheath ultrasound images that combines local and global features, enabling the model to efficiently and accurately identify the boundary of the optic nerve sheath. Although recent studies have proposed multiple methods for ocular biometry, most focus on single-version optimization, lacking systematic evaluation of cross-version module combinations, and rarely assess the computational requirements of models for hardware platforms or subsequent deployment challenges.

To address the limitations of existing research, this study determines an optimal network architecture for detecting ophthalmic ultrasound images through modular ablation analysis of multi-version YOLO. The proposed architecture can automatically locate the anterior corneal surfaces, anterior/posterior lens capsules, and fovea of the retina, and calculate the anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous chamber length, and axial length—thereby automating manual operations. This approach enhances the automation of clinical examinations, streamlines workflow, effectively reduces the impact of human intervention, and improves the accuracy and consistency of axial length biometry.

Results of the single-module performance tests

In the comparison experiment of backbone networks, Bv12 demonstrated the best precision–efficiency trade-off characteristics, with an mAP of 62.5%. A vertical comparative analysis showed that during the iteration of the backbone network from Bv10 to Bv12, the model achieved a 9.4% reduction in Parameters (10.6→9.6) and a 25.3% decrease in FLOPs (7.9→5.9), while FPS increased by 8.3% (24→26). This performance improvement was mainly attributed to the collaborative optimization strategy of the Area Attention Mechanism and Remaining Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (R-ELAN) introduced in YOLOv12, which enhances the multi-scale feature representation ability while reducing redundant computations.

The test results of the neck networks showed differential characteristics. Nv11 achieved a new high in mAP at 62.7% (Δ + 0.3%), with a 5.8% reduction in parameters compared to Nv10 (10.4→9.8), and an 8.7% increase in FPS (23→25). Notably, while maintaining a comparable mAP to Nv11 (62.6%), the Nv12 version further compressed parameters to 9.6 MB (Δ − 2.0%), which verifies the effectiveness of the Area Attention Mechanism in optimizing computational efficiency.

The performance evolution trend of the head network was significantly correlated with that of the neck network. The change curve of FLOPs for each version of the head network closely matched that of the neck network. This reveals that the YOLO series adopts a symmetric computational optimization architecture in the design of head-layer networks, achieving efficient allocation of computational resources by sharing convolution kernel parameters.

A horizontal comparison of module performance revealed the following patterns: first, the optimization of the backbone network showed a positive development, where both precision and efficiency are improved simultaneously, while the neck and head networks exhibited a trade-off characteristic, where an improvement in precision is accompanied by fluctuations in speed. Second, the monotonic decreasing trend of parameters and computational complexity indicates the structural optimization characteristics during the model iteration process. This structure-driven improvement in parameter efficiency confirms that the goal of model lightweighting could also be achieved through modular design optimization. Third, the significant jump in precision from Bv11 to Bv12 with the number of parameters remaining unchanged verifies that the attention mechanism can effectively improve the utilization rate of parameters.

The above results provide the theoretical foundation for the subsequent orthogonal experimental design. A mAP-oriented combination strategy was adopted, using the optimal backbone network version (Bv12) as the foundation, and, respectively, coupling the high-precision neck network version (Nv11) and the optimized head network version (Hv11) to investigate the nonlinear synergistic effects between modules. This phased optimization approach based on the performance characteristics of single modules provides an interpretable technical path for constructing the Pareto front solution set of detection accuracy and inference efficiency.

To analyze the inter-module synergies in the object detection architecture, interaction heatmaps were constructed (Fig. 1) covering three dimensions: backbone–neck, neck–head, and backbone–head. The experimental results showed that at the feature interaction level of backbone–neck, the Bv11–Nv11 combination exhibited the highest feature transfer efficiency, indicating an ideal match between the feature pyramid structure and the backbone network. Interaction heatmaps

Visual analysis of the heatmaps indicated that the Bv11–Nv11–Hv10 combination achieved synergistic optimization in feature extraction, multi-scale fusion, and prediction head optimization, providing a clear direction for cross-version module combination optimization. Notably, this optimal combination differed from the theoretical stack of independent sub-module optima (Bv12, Nv11, Hv11) from single-module tests. This finding empirically validated the importance of nonlinear inter-module interactions: simply stacking optimal modules cannot achieve overall optimality, and systematic synergistic analysis is necessary to exploit architectural potential.

Results of Pareto multi-objective optimization analysis‌

This study explored the equilibrium mechanism of object detection models across multiple performance dimensions by constructing the three-dimensional Pareto front analysis diagram (Fig. 2) for mAP, FPS, and Parameters. The experimental results showed that the Bv11–Nv11–Hv10 combination was located the vertex position of the Pareto front with an mAP of 64.0% and an inference speed of 26 FPS, verifying the synergistic advantages of deep feature extraction and multi-scale fusion. This combination is suitable for deployment scenarios in clinical diagnostic equipment with sufficient computing power. The Bv10–Nv10–Hv11 combination demonstrated significant advantages in the number of parameters while maintaining an mAP of 63.5%. This solution is located at the inflection point of the front curve, achieving an optimal balance between detection accuracy and operational efficiency for mobile devices. Although the Bv12–Nv12–Hv12 combination had a lower comprehensive performance score, with its balanced characteristics between high-performance and lightweight architectures, it provides a flexible trade-off for edge computing devices in scenarios with fluctuating computing power. The Pareto multi-objective optimization analysis framework quantified the performance boundaries of models under different hardware constraints, providing a scientific decision-making basis for architecture selection in practical engineering applications. Results of consistency testing

After obtaining the coordinate intersections of the vector sampling lines detected by the optimal cross-version model Bv11–Nv11–Hv10 with the anterior/posterior corneal surfaces, anterior/posterior lens capsules, and fovea of the retina, quantitative calculations of anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous chamber length, and axial length were achieved using the segmented sound velocity matching algorithm. The consistency test results (Table 5) between manual and automatic measurement parameters showed that the mean absolute error between A-scan manual measurements and model automatic measurements was ≤ 0.133 mm, and the standard deviation of errors was ≤ 0.400 mm. This indicated that the automatic measurement results not only had a low deviation from the true values but also exhibited good stability. Statistical analysis showed that all parameters have P values greater than 0.140 (P > 0.05), and the ICC values were all higher than 0.839 (ICC > 0.75), verifying the high consistency between the model’s automatic results and manually annotated results.

In addition, the consistency test indicators for lens thickness were relatively poor, which was presumably due to insufficient detection accuracy of the anterior and posterior lens capsules. This phenomenon reflects the core impact of the object detection model’s performance on intelligent biometric measurement—precise anatomical structure localization is the prerequisite for automated measurement of ocular biometric parameters, further highlighting the critical role of improving model detection performance in intelligent ophthalmic diagnosis. It should be noted that clinical manual annotation may contain inherent subjective errors, and this systematic human bias may indirectly amplify the deviation between automatic measurement parameters and true values. The performance of the proposed model may degrade significantly or fail when processing low-quality images, those with significant artifacts, or cases of rare complex ophthalmic diseases that severely distort normal anatomy. Consequently, at this stage, its localization outputs should be utilized primarily as supplementary aids for clinicians. In clinical applications, multi-source factors influencing human–machine measurement differences must be comprehensively considered.

Discussion

Summary of main results

Aiming at the systematic evaluation of network architecture selection in ophthalmic ultrasound image detection, this study constructed a modular ablation analysis framework based on orthogonal experimental design. Through the three-stage progressive research paradigm, the quantitative evaluation and combination optimization of cross-version modules of YOLO-series models were realized for the first time, which provides a reproducible methodological framework for the architecture selection of ophthalmic ultrasound image detection models.

In the single-module performance evaluation, three key rules were identified: first, the continuous optimization of the backbone network has entered a stage of “Pareto improvement” (Bv10→12: mAP up by 0.9%, FPS up by 8.3%), confirming that model lightweighting can be achieved through modular design optimization. This provides a new technical pathway for the embedded deployment of medical imaging devices. Second, the neck/head network exhibits a significant "accuracy–efficiency trade-off", which may reflect the inherent limitations of current convolutional neural network architecture paradigms in feature fusion and localization branch design. Third, Bv12 achieves accuracy improvement without increasing the number of parameters, demonstrating that the attention mechanism enhances parameter utilization efficiency by boosting feature selectivity—a principle analogous to the feature gating mechanism in transformers.

The multidimensional interaction effects revealed by orthogonal combinatorial experiments possess important engineering value. First, as the information hub of the feature pyramid, the neck module’s version selection has a dominant impact on detection accuracy [R(mAP) = 1.233], which aligns with the strong correlation characteristics of multi-scale features in medical images. Second, the head module demonstrates a dual leading role in the dimension of computational efficiency [R(Parameters) = 2.033, R(FPS) = 1.667], which may be inherently linked to the architecture design of classification–localization branches. Third, the Bv11–Nv11–Hv10 combination, which performs optimally in the cross-layer architecture of backbone–neck–head, verifies the synergistic enhancement mechanism between deep feature extraction and shallow semantic fusion.

Pareto front analysis further expands the engineering application value of this framework. The Bv11–Nv11–Hv10 combination achieves the optimal balance between detection accuracy and speed in standard computing power scenarios, while the lightweight combination Bv10–Nv10–Hv11 provides an efficient solution for mobile devices. This multi-objective optimization strategy breaks the limitations of traditional single-version model selection and offers a systematic solution for the differentiated requirements of medical image detection tasks.

In the clinical application validation, comparison of errors between automatic and manual measurements (mean absolute error ≤ 0.133 mm, ICC ≥ 0.839) indicates that the model constructed in this study not only achieves high-accuracy localization of ocular tissues but also provides a reliable automated tool for ophthalmic clinical parameter measurement through a segmented sound velocity matching algorithm. This achievement is expected to reduce subjective errors in manual measurement and improve the standardization level of ophthalmic ultrasound examinations, especially in scenarios requiring frequent axial length parameter measurements, such as pediatric ophthalmic screening and myopia control, which possesses substantial clinical application potential.

Generalization for architecture design

We abstracted and distilled the specific findings of this study to formulate general principles that can serve as a reference for researchers in the field, as detailed below:

First, Compatibility over Isolated Performance. A key insight from this study is that the compatibility and synergistic effects between network modules are more critical than the isolated performance of individual modules in benchmark tests. This implies that when selecting and combining network modules, researchers should not focus solely on the performance of a single module, but must prioritize the compatibility and coordination across different modules. Only when modules interact efficiently can the full potential of the overall network be realized.

Second, task alignment: the optimal combination of network modules depends strongly on the requirements of the downstream task, rather than being fixed. In the context of ophthalmic ultrasound imaging examined here, the neck network has the greatest impact on accuracy, due to its role in integrating features from numerous fine-grained ocular structures and boundaries. For other applications—such as large-object detection or processing simple textures—the most critical module may differ entirely. Therefore, the primary contribution of this work is not a universal architecture, but a systematic framework for evaluating the importance of modules across diverse tasks.

Finally, the decoupling paradigm: this study introduces a generalizable methodology that involves decomposing complex networks into core modules and then rigorously evaluating different module combinations through experimental design. This approach is applicable not only to the models and ophthalmic ultrasound modality examined in this work, but can also be extended to other architectures (e.g., transformers or CNN variants) and medical imaging modalities (e.g., CT and MRI). The proposed framework can be adopted by any modularly designed network to facilitate optimization and enhance performance.

Limitations and future work

This study had some limitations. First, since the gold standard in this paper was manual labeling by clinicians, the gold standard inevitably introduced human selection bias. These errors in the gold standard interfered with the feature learning process of the model, affecting the final detection effect. Second, because the data sets used in this paper were all from the same hospital and the same device, without validation using external standard data sets, the model’s generalizability to other hospitals or devices remains to be verified.

Future research can further refine the analysis at the functional unit level (such as attention modules and activation functions) and explore the potential of cross-modal data (e.g., optical coherence tomography images) to enhance model performance. Furthermore, by combining transfer learning and federated learning techniques to construct personalized detection models adapted to different device terminals, this framework represents an important development direction for clinical implementation.

Conclusion

The modular ablation analysis framework based on orthogonal experiments proposed in this paper systematically addresses the problem of network architecture selection in ophthalmic ultrasound image detection. By decoupling the core modules of YOLO-series models and conducting cross-version combinatorial optimization, it identifies the independent contributions and interaction mechanisms of different modules, ultimately screening out the optimal architecture combination that balances detection accuracy, inference speed, and deployment efficiency. Clinical validation further demonstrates that the automatic measurement method based on the optimal model exhibits high consistency with manual annotation, providing reliable technical support for the automation and precision of ophthalmic ultrasound examinations.

Furthermore, this study not only aims to identify high-performance network architectures for ophthalmic ultrasound image detection tasks but also proposes a replicable and generalizable methodology for architectural decision-making in deep learning. The core principles of this methodology can be adapted to various visual tasks, providing researchers in the field with valuable quantitative support and actionable guidance for designing efficient and specialized models.

Methods

Image preprocessing

The source of image data used in this study was the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (MD2400S, Tianjin Maida Medical Technology Co., Ltd.), and all images were desensitized to protect the privacy of patients and the security of health information. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association, approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (KY2024006), and was a retrospective study informed consent was waived.

When performing ocular axis segmental sound velocity measurements in the clinic, standard practice is a prerequisite for obtaining accurate values, and rigorous screening and high-quality annotation of image samples are critical for the model to achieve tissue localization and ocular axis measurements. The inclusion criteria were that the image is clear and the central vector sampling line passes through the corneal apex, the central lens, and the vitreous body and finally reached the fovea of the retina.

All images in the data set were labeled using Labelme software [20], including the anterior corneal surfaces, anterior/posterior lens capsules, and fovea of the retina. The schematic diagram of B-scan ultrasound-guided segmental measurements is shown in Fig. 3, with distance 1 being the anterior chamber depth, distance 2 being the lens thickness, distance 3 being the vitreous chamber length, and distance 4 being the ocular axis length. The sum of anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and vitreous chamber length is the ocular axis length. The ultrasound-guided segmented sound velocity matching method is fundamentally grounded in the property that ultrasound propagates at different speeds through various ocular tissues. The core step of this method is the precise localization of key anatomical structures—such as the anterior corneal surface, anterior and posterior lens capsules, and fovea centralis of the retina—followed by the calculation of pixel distances between these identified landmarks. Based on these pixel distance measurements, the ultrasound propagation time within different ocular tissue segments is derived. By incorporating tissue-specific sound velocities, the method ultimately enables the quantitative measurement of critical ocular parameters, including anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous cavity length, and ocular axis length.

Schematic diagram of B-scan ultrasound-guided segmental measurements of the speed of sound

The modular ablation process for multi-version YOLO is shown in Fig. 4. First, image preprocessing is performed. All images were labeled by two ophthalmologists with more than 10 years of experience. When the labeling results of the two experts agreed, they were directly taken as the final results. When there was disagreement, the final result was arbitrated by another ophthalmologist with more than 15 years of experience. A total of 1121 BMP images from different patients were included in the data set, with each patient contributing only a single image. All image had a resolution of 640 × 512 pixels (width × height). The five-point coordinate information was converted into rectangular bounding boxes centered at each coordinate with a size of 5 × 5 pixels. After labeling and preprocessing, the data set was then randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio for subsequent training and testing of the localization network. Next, staged ablation experiments were conducted in a GPU (Tesla P100-PCIE-16GB) computing environment. The AdamW optimizer was used to configure experimental parameters, with a batch size set to 32 and an initial learning rate of 0.001. The process sequentially included single-module performance evaluation, orthogonal combination experiments, and Pareto multi-objective optimization analysis. Finally, a consistency test was performed between the automatic measurement results of the optimal model and clinical manual annotations to achieve quantitative evaluation of the model's performance.

Staged ablation experiments

Single-module performance evaluation

This study employed the controlled variable method to establish a benchmark testing framework for single-module performance. Specifically, coupling effects were eliminated by systematically freezing non-target modules, ensuring that fluctuations in performance metrics exclusively reflected the impact of changes to the target module. The experimental design aimed to quantitatively analyze the weight of influence of each functional module on overall performance without interfering with other system components.

Orthogonal combination experiment

From a theoretical perspective, the most comprehensive experimental approach is the full-factorial design, which would require 27 model configurations in this study. However, the computational and time costs of training and evaluating all these models are prohibitively high—making this approach often impractical in real-world research. The key advantage of the orthogonal design lies in its ability to efficiently estimate the main effects and interaction effects of various factors using a minimal number of experimental runs.

In this study, adopting an orthogonal experimental design represents a necessary trade-off between efficiency and result reliability. The L9(34) orthogonal table was used to construct the experimental matrix with three factors (backbone, neck, head) and three levels (v10, v11, v12). This design significantly reduces the number of experiments, thereby lowering research and time costs, although the resulting “optimal combination” remains an efficient approximation within the full-factorial parameter space. If future studies have more abundant computational resources, further validation could be conducted via full-factorial experiments. Nonetheless, the results of this study demonstrate that the orthogonal design can systematically rank modules by importance and effectively identify high-performance architectures—fulfilling the core objectives of this research.

The range method [25] was selected as the analysis method for the main effects. Range analysis is the core algorithm for evaluating the main effects in orthogonal experimental design. Mathematically, it constructs range statistics from the mean values of level responses. In this study, a standardized three-stage range analysis process is adopted:

To enhance the rigor of statistical inference, this study builds upon range analysis by further employing analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine the statistical significance (α = 0.05) of the main effects of the backbone, neck, and head modules. In addition, partial η2 (ηp2) is calculated to quantify the practical strength of each module’s effect on performance metrics. This integrated approach controls for random error while objectively evaluating the specific contribution of each module to performance variation, thereby providing statistical validation for the order of module importance derived from the range analysis.

Interaction effects between modules are visualized via heatmaps for intuitive interpretation. Warm color tones (red/orange) represent positive synergistic effects, while cool color tones (blue/purple) represent negative antagonistic effects.

Pareto multi-objective optimization

The Pareto optimal solution [26] is a key concept in multi-objective optimization theory, used to compare the superiority and inferiority relationships between different solutions. In optimization problems involving multiple conflicting objective functions (such as the detection accuracy, inference speed, and deployment efficiency in this study), a trade-off model for objective functions under multidimensional constraints is established. By solving this vector optimization problem, a Pareto optimal solution set that satisfies strict multi-objective collaborative constraint conditions is obtained. This solution set must simultaneously satisfy:

Evaluation indicators

Real-time performance

To evaluate the real-time performance of the network model, model parameters, frames per second (FPS), and floating-point operations (FLOPs) were used as evaluation metrics [27]. The number of model parameters obtained during model training determined the size of the model file and the memory resources consumed by the model, which represented the model's demand for the computational power of the hardware platform. The number of images that could be detected by the model per second is defined as FPS, which is used to evaluate the model's real-time performance. FLOPs represent the amount of computation and can be used to measure model complexity.

Detection performance

To evaluate the detection performance of the network model, multi-category average precision (mAP), recall, and precision were used as evaluation metrics. Average precision (AP) is the detection accuracy of a single category, numerically equal to the area under the recall and precision curves. The mAP is the average of the AP values of all categories, which reflects the comprehensive detection performance of the model. Precision indicates the proportion of true positive cases in a predicted positive sample. Recall indicates the proportion of positive examples in the sample that were predicted correctly. Consistency test

To evaluate the reliability of the network model's measurement results for anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous chamber length, and axial length, this study constructed a multidimensional evaluation index system, specifically including the mean and standard deviation of the error, significance test (P value), and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between predicted and true values. The significance test uses the independent samples t test method [29], which is commonly used to compare the significance of mean differences between two independent samples. The P value is used to determine whether there is a statistically significant deviation between the automatic measurement results and manually annotated values. As a measure of reliability, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [30] reflects the correlation and consistency between two sets of data, and can assess the reliability of quantitative measurements from multiple measurement methods or raters. In general, an ICC below 0.4 indicates poor reliability, 0.4–0.75 moderate reliability, and above 0.75 good reliability.
