Genotype frequency of Stargardt disease in Labrador Retrievers in Japan
Authors: Masamine TAKANOSU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Canine Stargardt disease causes progressive retinal dysfunction leading to late-onset vision impairment and is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. The causative mutation, c.4176insC in the ABCA4 gene, has been identified in Labrador Retrievers. This study investigated the genotype frequency of this mutation in Labrador Retrievers in Japan, providing the first estimate for this breed population. Genomic DNA from 120 dogs was analyzed by high-resolution melting with real-time PCR. Of these, 65 (54.2%) were homozygous wild types, 51 (42.5%) were heterozygotes, and 4 (3.3%) were homozygous mutants, with a mutant allele frequency of 0.246. The high carrier rate indicates that the mutation is present at a relatively high frequency in the breed population. Genotyping may help identify individual genotypes and reduce the risk of propagation through breeding.

Human Stargardt disease, known as juvenile macular degeneration, is characterized by progressive central vision loss in young patients [9]. It is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, and numerous mutations have been identified in the ABCA4 gene [3]. Although other causative genes, ELOVL4 (STGD3) and PROM1 (STGD4), have been associated with autosomal dominant forms of Stargardt disease, mutations in ABCA4 remain the major cause [5]. Mutations in the ABCA4 lead to dysfunction of photoreceptor-specific transporters, resulting in impaired clearance of all-trans-retinaldehyde. The accumulation of this compound promotes the formation of cytotoxic secondary metabolites, which contribute to progressive retinal degeneration. [10].

Recently, canine Stargardt disease has been reported in Labrador Retrievers without rod-cone degeneration [4]. Affected dogs may appear to have normal vision on ophthalmologic examinations, such as cotton ball test during youth; however, visual impairment often develops in middle to old age, although some dogs retain vision throughout life. Clinical signs are typically moderate, and ophthalmic abnormalities in the fundus become apparent only in older dogs [2].

The causative mutation of canine Stargardt disease has been identified as a cytosine insertion in exon 28 of ABCA4 (c.4176insC) [4]. Although the wild-type allele contains seven consecutive cytosines and the mutant allele contains eight, these are hereafter referred to as C7 and C8, respectively. This insertion occurs within a mononucleotide repeat, resulting in a frameshift mutation when eight cytosines are present. The frameshift introduces a premature stop codon, likely resulting in loss of functional ABCA4 protein. As in humans, the condition is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

Although genetic characterization and clinical descriptions of canine Stargardt disease have been reported [2, 4], the genotype frequency of this mutation has not been determined in Labrador Retrievers in Japan. While rod-cone degeneration, known as progressive retinal atrophy (PRA), is well documented in many dog breeds including the Labrador Retriever [8], the prevalence of Stargardt disease in Japan remains unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the genotype frequency of canine Stargardt disease in Labrador Retrievers in Japan.

A total of 120 client-owned Labrador Retrievers and 123 dogs of non-Labrador Retriever breeds were included in this study. Among Labrador Retrievers, genomic DNA was obtained from whole blood (n=96), cytology samples (n=19), and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues (n=5). All non-Labrador Retriever samples were derived from whole blood (n=123). Whole blood samples were obtained from dogs that visited several animal clinics for physical examinations or other tests, whereas cytology and FFPE samples were collected from materials originally submitted for diagnostic purposes.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and adjusted to 10 ng/µL. For FFPE-derived DNA samples, integrity was assessed by PCR amplification of a 164 bp fragment of the MDR1 gene using the following primers: forward primer: 5′-TCATGATGCTGGTTTTTGGA-3′; reverse primer: 5′-TGAGGGCTAAACATCCTTCTAA-3′. Only samples yielding a clear amplification product were included in subsequent analyses.

As a control for the mutant allele (C8/C8), a 500-bp double-stranded DNA fragment corresponding to the genomic region encompassing c.4176insC was synthesized (gBlocks, Integrated DNA Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) and resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Preliminary genotyping was conducted using high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis with genomic DNA representing the wild-type allele (C7/C7) and synthesized C8/C8 DNA as a mutant control. To generate a heterozygous control (C7/C8), wild-type genomic DNA and the synthesized C8 fragment were mixed. Primers for HRM were: forward 5′-GGCTCTGATGCTTTCCATCAT-3′ and reverse 5′-TCAAAGCAGGATATTCACCAAA-3′, yielding a 54-bp amplicon. The HRM amplicon was designed to be short (54 bp) to ensure reliable amplification even from partially fragmented DNA, including FFPE-derived samples. HRM reactions used 10 ng DNA in a 10 μL volume with 5 μL Precision Melt Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and 1 μL each primer (2 μM) on a MyGo-Mini real-time PCR system (IT-IS International Ltd., Middlesbrough, UK). Cycling: 95°C for 2 min; 50 cycles of 95°C for 10 sec and 60°C for 15 sec. Melting analysis: 95°C for 30 sec, 60°C for 1 min, then melt from 60 to 95°C in 0.1°C increments. Genotype frequencies were assessed by χ2 test for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (χ2 test) [6].

To validate the HRM-based genotyping results, sequence analysis was performed using three samples from each genotype. HRM analysis produced clearly distinguishable genotype clusters; therefore, a representative subset of samples from each genotype was selected for sequencing to confirm concordance between HRM classification and sequence data. Primers spanning the mutation (forward 5′-CCACCTACCAACACCCACAT-3′ and reverse 5′-AGAAAGTGTACTGCTGCCCA-3′) yielded a 163-bp product. PCR used 10 ng template in 10 μL (components: 10× buffer, 2 mM dNTPs, primers 2 μM each, 0.5 units Blend Taq Plus; Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Cycling: 95°C for 120 sec, then 35 cycles of 94°C for 15 sec and 68°C for 30 sec. Amplicons were analyzed by Qsep100 capillary electrophoresis (BiOptic Inc., New Taipei City, Taiwan), treated with Exo-SAP IT Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and sequenced as described previously [7].

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Tochigi Veterinary Medical Association (approval no. 132), to which the author belongs.

The present study provides an initial estimate of the genotype frequency of the c.4176insC mutation in Labrador Retrievers in Japan. Genotyping provides practical benefits for veterinary practitioners, particularly for selecting dogs for breeding and for diagnosing Stargardt disease. Previous studies have shown that dogs under 2 years of age with Stargardt disease exhibit no obvious visual impairment [2]. At this stage, only small (<1 mm) focal hyporeflective retinal lesion may be present. In middle-aged dogs, visual impairment is generally not observed, although mild abnormalities such as hyporeflection of the retina and visual streaks have been reported [2]. Dogs over 10 years of age may show mild pupillary dilation and abnormalities in the cotton ball test. Thus, young affected dogs used for breeding may not display any symptoms other than a very small fundus lesion. Heterozygous carriers or homozygous mutants may have been unwittingly used for breeding, thereby increasing the risk of producing affected offspring. This may partly explain the observed presence of the mutation in Labrador Retrievers in Japan. Because heterozygous carriers and affected dogs cannot be reliably detected through routine ophthalmological examinations, genotyping may be a useful tool for screening dogs without the mutation for breeding purposes.

In the present study, I demonstrated that the ABCA4 mutation associated with Stargardt disease is present at a relatively high frequency in the Labrador Retriever population. One likely reason for the spread is that heterozygous carriers, which remain asymptomatic throughout life, as well as affected dogs, which show no clinical signs at a young age, may have been used for breeding. To prevent the disease from propagating further, it may be beneficial to select dogs without the mutation for breeding. Selection of breeding dogs based on genotype may contribute to reducing allele frequency and disease prevalence over time.

In this study, genotyping of a small population of non-Labrador Retriever breeds revealed no heterozygous carriers or homozygous mutants of the c.4176insC mutation. Although rod-cone degeneration is common in many dog breeds [1, 2], Stargardt disease has not, to our knowledge, been reported in breeds other than the Labrador Retriever. This finding may be consistent with the possibility that the c.4176insC mutation is primarily present in the Labrador Retriever population. However, because the number of non-Labrador Retriever samples examined in this study was limited, the presence of the mutation in other breeds cannot be excluded. Therefore, further large-scale studies, particularly including breeds related to the Labrador Retriever, are warranted.

A limitation of this study is that the phenotypic data were not evaluated because the samples were collected from client-owned dogs presented to veterinary clinics. As a result, the study population may not fully represent the broader Labrador Retriever population in Japan, and potential sampling bias cannot be excluded. Although FFPE-derived DNA may be subjected to fragmentation, the use of a short amplicon and validation by Sanger sequencing support the reliability of the genotyping results. Consequently, it is unknown whether the homozygous mutant individuals identified in this study have developed clinical signs of Stargardt disease. Further studies are needed to confirm the correspondence between genotype and phenotype.
