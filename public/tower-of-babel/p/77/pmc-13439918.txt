Gut microbiota dysbiosis and host metabolite–immune crosstalk drives the pathogenesis of neonatal lupus erythematosus: a multi-omics analysis
Authors: Wenqiang Sun, Xinyun Jin, Yihui Li, Minqian Zhou, Xue Liu, Huawei Wang, Haifeng Geng, Lili Li, Jinhui Hu, Xihui Zhou, Yufeng Liu, Xueping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal lupus erythematosus (NLE) is a rare autoimmune condition triggered by the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies. Despite its recognized clinical manifestations, the underlying pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. This study seeks to explore the disruption of the gut microbiota-host metabolism-immune axis in anti-Ro/La-positive neonates, and to assess its potential role in the development of NLE.MethodsThis multicenter, cross-sectional study included 90 neonates, divided into three groups: 30 with neonatal lupus erythematosus (NLE), 30 with positive antibodies but without clinical manifestations (No-NLE), and 30 healthy controls. We performed 16 S rRNA sequencing to analyze gut microbiota composition, untargeted plasma metabolomic profiling, and proteomic analysis to identify alterations associated with the pathogenesis of NLE.ResultsWe identified significant alterations in the gut microbiota, plasma metabolome, and proteome profiles of anti-Ro/La-positive neonates. NLE infants exhibited marked enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae and depletion of Bifidobacterium and Clostridium butyricum. Metabolomic analysis revealed hyperactivation of β-alanine and purine metabolism, along with impaired α-linolenic acid metabolism and endocannabinoid signaling. Proteomic profiling indicated aberrant protein expression that modulated IFN signaling, particularly within the C-type lectin receptor pathway. Dysregulation of the spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) and high-affinity immunoglobulin epsilon receptor subunit gamma (FCER1G) decoupling was observed, correlating with elevated IFN-α and NF-κB p65 levels. Integrated correlation analysis revealed significant associations among differential microbial taxa, plasma metabolites, and proteins. Notably, E. coli-associated metabolites and proteins displayed inverse relationships with those associated with C. butyricum.ConclusionsThese findings represent comprehensive evidence of dysregulation along the “gut microbiota-host metabolism-immune” axis in neonatal lupus erythematosus (NLE), providing novel insights into the disease’s underlying heterogeneity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04963-0.

Background

Neonatal lupus erythematosus (NLE) is an acquired autoimmune disorder resulting from the transplacental transfer of maternal autoantibodies, characterized by distinctive clinical manifestations such as cutaneous erythema, hematologic abnormalities, and congenital heart block, etc. [1, 2]. Epidemiologic studies indicate that while 15–20% of pregnant women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) carry anti-Ro/Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigen A (SSA) and anti-La/Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigen B (SSB) antibodies, only 1–2% of antibody-positive mothers give birth to infants with NLE [1, 3]. This marked discrepancy in disease incidence suggests that, in addition to maternal autoantibodies, other critical factors likely contribute to the pathogenesis of NLE.

The current understanding of NLE pathogenesis primarily centers on the pathogenic role of maternal autoantibodies, which can cross the placental barrier and bind to fetal tissue antigens, triggering inflammatory responses and tissue damage [4]. However, the marked clinical variability among neonates with similar antibody exposure remains unexplained. This clinical heterogeneity likely arises from complex interactions between fetal genetic background, environmental factors, and epigenetic regulation [5, 6].

Recent advances have underscored the critical regulatory mechanisms governing early-life immune development in autoimmune disease research. Substantial evidence indicates that neonatal immune maturation is finely regulated by a variety of intrinsic and extrinsic factors [7]. In particular, microbiota-immune interactions play pivotal roles in establishing immune tolerance. The early postnatal period represents a critical window for both gut microbiota colonization and immune system development, during which microbial dysbiosis may predispose individuals to various immune-related disorders [8]. In autoimmune research, growing evidence suggests that host-microbiota interactions may influence disease susceptibility through multiple mechanisms, including immunomodulation by microbial metabolites, molecular mimicry between microbial and host antigens, and direct regulation of immune cell differentiation and function [9–11]. However, the specific contributions of these mechanisms to NLE pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidated.

Building on current evidence, we hypothesize that early-life microbiota-immune interactions may play a critical role in NLE pathogenesis. To test this hypothesis, we employed an integrated approach that combines 16 S rRNA sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, proteomics, and clinical cohort validation to systematically investigate the tripartite relationship between microbial communities, metabolic signatures, and immune regulatory networks in anti-Ro/La-positive neonates. This study provides novel mechanistic insights into NLE development and lays the groundwork for future preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This multicenter observational study was conducted from January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2025, at the neonatal units of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University, the Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, Huai’an Maternal and Child Health Care Center, and the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. This study involves human participants and was approved by the institutional review boards of all hospitals (No. 2025CS253). Written informed consent has been obtained from the patient’s family, granting permission for the publication of their child’s clinical data.

Neonates who met the diagnostic criteria for NLE were enrolled in the NLE group. Diagnosis was based on serum positivity for anti-SSA/Ro-SSB/La ribonucleoprotein complexes (including 52 kD SSA/Ro, 60 kD SSA/Ro, or 48 kD SSB/La), along with the presence of NLE-associated clinical manifestations [12]. Using a gestational age and birth weight matched design, we randomly selected anti-SSA/Ro-SSB/La-positive neonates without clinical manifestations as the No-NLE group at a 1:1 ratio, and 30 healthy neonates from routine health screenings served as controls. Clinical assessments were conducted independently by both neonatologists and rheumatologists. Any diagnostic discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third specialist, and cases with an uncertain diagnosis were excluded from the analysis.

Considering the colonization dynamics of neonatal gut microbiota [13], both fecal and plasma samples were collected between postnatal days 7 and 10 for subsequent analyses. To minimize antibiotic interference with microbial communities, strict exclusion criteria were applied: all included neonates were antibiotic-naïve at the time of sample collection.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were collected through a comprehensive review of electronic medical records, supplemented by structured in-person interviews and standardized online surveys. The collected data encompassed three key aspects: (1) demographic characteristics, including maternal age and neonatal parameters (gestational age, birth weight, and sex); (2) maternal medication history, focusing on autoimmune disease treatments such as glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, low molecular weight heparin, aspirin, and immunosuppressants; and (3) detailed documentation of organ involvement in NLE cases, specifically cutaneous, hematological, congenital heart block, and gastrointestinal manifestations [1]. All data collection procedures adhered to standardized protocols to ensure consistency across study sites.

Omics research

Detailed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, metabolomics, proteomic sequencing methods, and data quality control methods are presented in Additional file 1 [14].

ELISA kit and biomarker assay

The following commercial assay kits were used for protein quantification: SYK ELISA Kit (Jianglai Biotechnology, JL12417), Human IFN-α ELISA Kit (PBL Assay Science, 41110), biotin-conjugated high affinity immunoglobulin epsilon receptor subunit gamma (FCER1G) antibody (Cusabio, CSB-PA008533LD01HU), and Nuclear Factor Kappa-B p65 (NF-κB p65) ELISA Kit (Abcam, ab176663). All ELISA procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.5.1) and GraphPad Prism 9. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene’s test. Normally distributed quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using t-test. The comparison between the three groups was ANOVA test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as median with interquartile range (P25, P75) and analyzed using non-parametric tests. The comparison between the three groups was Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. For high-dimensional omics data, differential features were initially identified based on predefined nominal thresholds as described in each omics section. To control for multiple testing, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to estimate the false discovery rate (FDR). Unless otherwise specified, FDR-adjusted q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman correlation coefficient (r). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

The spectrum of organ involvement in NLE neonates revealed the following pattern: cutaneous manifestations (25/30, 83.3%), hematological abnormalities (23/30, 76.7%), congenital heart block (CHB) (8/30, 26.7%), and gastrointestinal involvement (14/30, 46.7%). Gut microbiota dysbiosis progresses with disease status in Anti-Ro/La-positive neonates

Fecal samples collected between postnatal days 7 and 10 underwent 16 S rRNA sequencing for gut microbiota analysis. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) revealed significant compositional differences between anti-Ro/La-positive neonates and controls (Fig. 1A). Compared to controls, the anti-Ro/La-positive group exhibited significantly reduced α-diversity metrics, including the Shannon index, Simpson index, ACE index, Chao1 index, and observed Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) (Fig. 1B-F). At the phylum level, decreased relative abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria was observed alongside an increased abundance of Proteobacteria. At the genus level, unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcus were significantly enriched, whereas Bifidobacterium were significantly reduced (Fig. 1G-H). LEfSe analysis further identified taxonomic differences at finer resolution, showing significant enrichment of g_unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae and g_Corynebacterium, accompanied by depletion of g_Bifidobacterium in anti-Ro/La-positive neonates (Fig. 1I).

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) revealed distinct microbial community structures between the control, No-NLE, and NLE groups (Fig. 2A), although no significant differences were observed based on weighted UniFrac distances (Fig. 2B). Notably, while the Shannon and Simpson diversity indices were similar between the control and No-NLE groups, both indices were significantly reduced in the NLE group (Fig. 2C-D). Further analysis demonstrated substantial microbial dysbiosis across taxonomic levels. At both the phylum and genus levels, we observed significant alterations in bacterial composition among the three groups. At the phylum level, Firmicutes exhibited a progressive decrease, whereas Proteobacteria showed a corresponding increase across the three groups. At the genus level, unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae and Staphylococcus demonstrated incremental increases, while Bifidobacterium showed a stepwise reduction. These compositional shifts were most pronounced in the NLE group (Fig. 2E-F). LEfSe analysis identified the control group exhibited significantly higher abundance of g_Bifidobacterium, whereas the NLE group showed marked enrichment of g__unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae and g_Corynebacterium (Fig. 2G). The No-NLE group demonstrated significantly elevated Streptococcus abundance compared to controls (Fig. 2H). More strikingly, compared with the No-NLE group, neonates with NLE exhibited significant compositional shifts at the genus level. LEfSe analysis identified increased relative abundance of g__unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae and g__Corynebacterium, along with decreased abundance of g__Rothia, g__Bifidobacterium, and s__Clostridium_butyricum in the NLE group (Fig. 2I). To account for the compositional and sparse nature of microbiome data, differential abundance was further evaluated using metagenomeSeq with FDR correction. This analysis confirmed significant enrichment of g__Bacillus and g__Microbacterium in the NLE group (FDR-adjusted q = 0.022).

Metabolic dysregulation and microbial-metabolite interactions

Maternal autoimmune diseases are well recognized for inducing metabolic abnormalities in offspring that further exacerbate disease progression and immune dysre gulation. To investigate this phenomenon, we conducted plasma untargeted metabolomic analysis on gestational age- and birth weight-matched neonates from the control, No-NLE, and NLE groups (n = 12 per group), with detailed demographic characteristics provided in Additional file 2: Table S1.

Metabolomic profiling revealed significant disturbances in anti-Ro/La-positive neonates compared to controls. Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) demonstrated clear separation between groups (Fig. 3A), with volcano plot analysis identifying 557 differentially abundant metabolites (346 upregulated, 211 downregulated) (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway analysis showed these metabolites were primarily enriched in purine metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and endocrine resistance pathways, etc. (Fig. 3C). The most affected metabolite classes included amino acid derivatives, benzene/substituted derivatives, and aldehydes, etc. (Fig. 3D). Key metabolite alterations in anti-Ro/La-positive neonates included elevated levels of 4-ethylbenzoic acid, sinapyl alcohol, and velnacrine, along with reduced levels of His-Thr, erythrose, and 1-myristoyl-2-stearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine(Fig. 3E). Correlation analysis between differentially abundant gut microbial taxa and the top 20 plasma metabolites revealed statistically significant associations when comparing anti-Ro/La-positive neonates with controls. Most beneficial microbial groups, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, clustered together, whereas harmful microbial groups, including Enterobacter and Escherichia coli, predominantly formed separate clusters. These findings indicate distinct metabolic and microbial interactions associated with the presence of anti-Ro/La antibodies in neonates (Fig. 3F).

Significant plasma metabolome differences were observed among the control, No-NLE, and NLE groups, as shown by both OPLS-DA and heatmap visualization (Fig. 4A-B). The No-NLE group exhibited distinct metabolic separation from controls in OPLS-DA (Fig. 4C), with 442 differentially abundant metabolites (270 upregulated, 172 downregulated) identified by volcano plot analysis (Fig. 4D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these metabolites were predominantly enriched in nucleotide metabolism, purine metabolism, and sulfur metabolism pathways, etc. (Fig. 4E). No-NLE-specific upregulation affected bile secretion, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and drug metabolism via cytochrome P450, etc., while downregulated metabolites clustered in metabolic pathways, including glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism and D-amino acid metabolism, etc. (Additional file 2: Fig. S1A-B). Differentially abundant metabolites were primarily distributed among amino acid derivatives, benzene/substituted derivatives, and aldehydes, etc. (Fig. 4F). Lollipop plot analysis revealed significant metabolic perturbations in No-NLE neonates, with pronounced elevation of dihydroroseoside, fludrocortisone acetate, and Ile-Met. Among the top 20 most significantly altered metabolites, three showed marked downregulation: uracil, reboxetine, and biochanin A (Fig. 4G). There is a significant correlation between differential microbes and some differential metabolites between the two groups. There is a significant correlation between differentially abundant gut microbial taxa and several differential metabolites in No-NLE neonates compared with controls (Fig. 4H). Specifically, Streptococcus were notably correlated with metabolites such as 3-Hydroxyvalproic acid, Fludrocortisone acetate, and Cycloate.

Significant metabolic separation between NLE and No-NLE groups was observed by OPLS-DA (Fig. 4I). Volcano plot analysis identified 573 differentially abundant metabolites in the NLE versus No-NLE comparison, with 181 upregulated and 392 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 4J). KEGG analysis demonstrated predominant enrichment in nucleotide metabolism, purine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism pathways, etc. (Fig. 4K). Further analysis revealed NLE-specific metabolic alterations, with upregulated metabolites primarily enriched in β-alanine metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and ubiquinone biosynthesis pathways, etc. (Additional file 2: Fig. S1C), while downregulated metabolites were mainly associated with α-linolenic acid metabolism, retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, and arachidonic acid metabolism pathways, etc. (Additional file 2: Fig. S1D). Comparative metabolomic analysis identified benzene/substituted derivatives, amino acid metabolites, and heterocyclic compounds, etc. as the predominant classes of differentially abundant metabolites between groups (Fig. 4L). Lollipop plot analysis revealed significant elevation of felbamate, glycyl-L-tyrosine, and 4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazine ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), etc. in NLE patients, while four metabolites among the top 20 most differentially abundant species showed marked reduction: 2-octanamidoacetic acid, dimethindene, uncarine A, and furfural diacetal(Fig. 4M). Integration of microbial and metabolic data demonstrated significant correlations between specific bacterial taxa and metabolite levels in NLE versus No-NLE comparisons (Fig. 4N). Corynebacterium and unidentified_Enterobacteriaceae were strongly correlated with metabolites such as Tetrahydroharman-3-carboxylic acid and Cycloate.

After Benjamini-Hochberg correction, 251 upregulated and 124 downregulated metabolites remained significant in the Anti-Ro/La group compared with controls (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). KEGG enrichment analysis based on these FDR-significant metabolites yielded pathway distributions similar to those obtained using nominally significant metabolites (P < 0.05), again highlighting purine metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and nucleotide metabolism (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B). Similarly, 164 upregulated and 88 downregulated metabolites remained significant in the No-NLE group versus controls after FDR correction, with enrichment in pathways akin to those observed using nominally significant metabolites (P < 0.05) (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C-D). In the NLE versus No-NLE comparison, 86 metabolites were upregulated and 264 downregulated following FDR adjustment, with enrichment primarily in purine, nucleotide, and pyrimidine metabolism pathways (Additional file 2: Fig. S2E-F). Overall, pathway enrichment patterns were largely consistent between nominal and FDR-restricted analyses across all comparisons, indicating that the major metabolic signatures were robust rather than driven by isolated statistical findings.

Significant dysregulation of serum proteins in Anti-Ro/La-positive neonates

To identify potential biomarkers associated with NLE development in anti-Ro/La-positive offspring, we performed Astral-DIA proteomic analysis on plasma samples from gestational age- and birth weight-matched neonates in the control, No-NLE, and NLE groups (n = 8/per group), demographic characteristics provided in Additional file 2: Table S2. Principal component analysis (PCA) of plasma proteins demonstrated significant separation between anti-Ro/La-positive offspring and controls (Fig. 5A). Volcano plot analysis identified 561 differentially expressed proteins (335 upregulated and 226 downregulated) when comparing two groups (Fig. 5B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the identified proteins in collagen-containing extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and extracellular matrix, etc. (Fig. 5C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated predominant involvement in Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase (PI3K) -Akt signaling pathway, morphine addiction, and lysosome, etc. (Fig. 5D). Heatmap analysis between the two groups revealed that Immunoglobulin lambda variable 1–36 (IGLV1-36), calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CAMK2G), eart development protein with EGF like domains 1 (HEG1) were significantly up-regulated in cord plasma of zygotes from anti-Ro/La antibody-positive pregnant mothers, whereas proteins such as Arrestin-C, Exoribonuclease 1, and T-cell surface glycoprotein CD3 gamma chain (CD3G) were significantly down-regulated ( Fig. 5E). There was a significant correlation between top 20 differentially abundant plasma metabolites and top 50 differentially expressed proteins between the two groups (Fig. 5F).

Critical roles of IFN-α and C-type lectin pathway proteins in NLE pathogenesis

Extensive research has established the central role of type I interferon (IFN) signaling in NLE pathogenesis [16, 1]. Persistent IFN activation amplifies inflammatory cascades and mediates autoimmune injury, particularly affecting the cardiac conduction system (resulting in congenital heart block) and the skins [1]. GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins between controls and anti-Ro/La-positive offspring identified several immune regulatory pathways with direct or indirect connections to IFN signaling, including T cell activation, negative regulation of interleukin-2 production, and regulation of protein tyrosine kinase activity, etc. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins revealed significant involvement of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, hematopoietic cell lineage regulation, and Lysosome, etc., all pathways known to interact directly or indirectly with IFN signaling networks. Expanded sample (n = 30/per group) measurements revealed no significant changes in plasma IFNα and NF-κB p65 levels in children with No-NLE compared to controls, whereas both IFNα and NF-κB p65 were significantly elevated in the NLE group (Table 3).

Discussion

This study integrates multi-omics analyses to characterize dysregulation of the gut microbiota–host metabolism–immune axis in anti-Ro/La–positive neonates. Key findings are as follows: 16 S rRNA sequencing identified distinct gut microbial alterations in infants with NLE; untargeted metabolomics demonstrated enhanced β-alanine and purine metabolism, alongside reduced α-linolenic acid metabolism and endocannabinoid signaling; proteomic profiling further revealed a previously unrecognized decoupling of SYK and FCER1G expression, correlated with IFN-α and NF-κB p65 levels. Together, these findings corroborate the central role of IFN-α signaling in NLE and support a cascade model linking microbial dysbiosis to metabolic disturbance and immune dysregulation.

Mechanisms linking dysbiosis of intestinal flora to immune abnormalities

This study demonstrates a distinct pattern of gut microbial alteration in infants with NLE, characterized by reduced microbial α-diversity, increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, and decreased levels of the immunomodulatory bacterium C. butyricum. Because LEfSe relies on LDA and does not explicitly account for multiple testing, it may increase the risk of false-positive signals in microbiome studies. Therefore, LEfSe results in the present study were used primarily as an exploratory approach to identify candidate discriminatory taxa, while complementary differential abundance analysis using metagenomeSeq with FDR correction was performed to improve statistical robustness, which further supported the enrichment of Bacillus and Microbacterium in the NLE group and suggested expansion of low-abundance taxa under microbial imbalance. Such changes are consistent with inflammation-associated dysbiosis described in other immune-mediated conditions [19]. Although partially overlapping with alterations reported in adult SLE [20, 21], the microbial signature observed in NLE appears more specifically reflect a shift toward an inflammatory-associated configuration. Mechanistically, this dysbiosis may may contribute to immune activation through amyloid-driven autoimmunity, as curli fibrils from Enterobacteriaceae biofilms induce molecular mimicry and activate Toll-like receptor signaling, thereby enhancing pathogenic autoantibody production and disease progression [22, 23]. Furthermore, depletion of C. butyricum and the resulting reduction in short-chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, may disrupt immune homeostasis through altered epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming [24, 25]. Consistently, exogenous sodium butyrate supplementation alleviates gut dysbiosis and inflammatory tissue injury in SLE mouse models [26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that altered microbial composition may be linked to immune disturbances in NLE.

Pathophysiologic significance of metabolic disorders

Metabolomic profiling identified disturbances in purine and steroid hormone metabolism in anti-Ro/La antibody–positive neonates, with more pronounced alterations in the NLE group. Elevated purine metabolites have been associated with immune activation and enhanced inflammatory signaling [27, 28], while steroid hormone imbalance may impair immune development by affecting immune cell differentiation and function [29]. Notably, metabolic dysregulation was more pronounced in NLE neonates than in the No-NLE group. Among the altered pathways, β-alanine metabolism was markedly upregulated in NLE, a pathway closely linked to cellular energy balance, oxidative stress, and inflammatory injury [30]. In parallel, downregulated pathways included α-linolenic acid metabolism and retrograde endocannabinoid signaling. Impaired α-linolenic acid metabolism may weaken anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacity [31, 32], whereas defective endocannabinoid signaling could limit cannabinoid receptor 2–dependent M2 macrophage polarization, thereby favoring pro-inflammatory responses [33]. Although FDR correction reduced the number of statistically significant metabolites, the overall pathway enrichment patterns remained broadly similar, suggesting that multiple-testing adjustment did not substantially change the main metabolic signals. Considered together with the microbial alterations described above, these findings point to concurrent changes in metabolic and immune-related pathways in NLE. As this study is cross-sectional, causal relationships cannot be established; however, the pathways identified here may inform future mechanistic studies.

New findings of abnormalities in the CLR signaling pathway

Proteomic analysis revealed dysregulation of the CLR signaling pathway in NLE patients. While SYK and FCER1G expression showed the expected positive correlation in both the control and No-NLE groups, this relationship was reversed in NLE neonates, indicating disrupted coordination between these functionally linked molecules [34]. Several mechanisms may underlie this decoupling. Transplacental maternal anti-SSA/Ro antibodies could influence FCER1G-associated receptor signaling, potentially affecting receptor turnover or immune cell composition, including alterations in mast cells and B-cell activation states [35]. Such changes may indirectly disturb the balance between SYK and FCER1G expression. Functionally, enhanced SYK activity has been associated with increased IRF5 phosphorylation and downstream IFN-α production, whereas reduced FCER1G expression may impair immune complex handling [36]. This combination is consistent with a pro-inflammatory milieu characterized by amplified signaling and diminished regulatory capacity. We further speculate that inflammatory pathway activation, including NF-κB signaling, may participate in feedback regulation of FCER1G expression. Overall, these findings suggest that altered SYK-FCER1G coordination may represent a feature of immune signaling imbalance in NLE, pending further functional investigation.

Reconceptualization of the IFN-α signaling pathway

This study further supports the central role of IFN-α signaling in NLE pathogenesis and suggests additional regulatory layers influencing this pathway. In addition to the direct effects of maternal autoantibodies, IFN-α overactivation may be associated with gut microbial dysbiosis and metabolic disturbances [37, 38]. The observed SYK–FCER1G decoupling may represent another modulatory mechanism contributing to enhanced IFN-α-related signaling [39–41]. Taken together, these findings provide an integrated perspective on IFN-α–mediated immune dysregulation in NLE. They also identify several potential regulatory nodes, including microbiota-related TLR signaling, metabolic pathways, and SYK activity, which merit further investigation in future mechanistic and translational studies.

Clinical translational value and future directions

This study suggests a potential biomarker framework for risk stratification in NLE by integrating gut microbial features, including Enterobacteriaceae enrichment and C. butyricum depletion, with the SYK/FCER1G expression ratio. These findings provide a basis for further evaluation of combined microbial and immune indicators in disease assessment. From a therapeutic perspective, SYK inhibition may warrant exploration, given the established role of SYK in neutrophil activation, NET formation, and regulation of IFN-α production through the Syk–IRF5 axis [42, 43]. In parallel, targeted probiotic supplementation such as C. butyricum could be investigated for its potential to restore microbial balance and promote short-chain fatty acid–mediated regulatory T cell responses [44]. However, these implications remain exploratory, as no in vivo or clinical validation has yet been conducted in NLE. Future studies, including prospective cohort validation and mechanistic investigations in relevant experimental models, are needed to clarify the predictive value and therapeutic relevance of these findings.

Study limitations

Although this study applied a multi-omics strategy to investigate the gut microbiota–metabolism–immune axis in NLE, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the overall sample size was modest, which may have reduced statistical power for detecting subtle molecular differences, particularly in subgroup analyses. Notably, the proteomic profiling was performed in a relatively small subset of samples. Given the large number of proteins tested simultaneously, this limited sample size may have reduced the ability to identify proteins that remain statistically significant after multiple-testing correction. Therefore, the proteomic findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded primarily as exploratory signals that may help generate hypotheses and guide future validation studies. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships among microbial dysbiosis, metabolic alterations, and immune signaling. The observed associations therefore cannot establish temporal or mechanistic links among these biological layers. Third, the biological functions of several detected metabolites remain incompletely characterized. As a result, the functional interpretation of certain metabolic signatures remains limited and warrants further biochemical and experimental investigation. In addition, due to the rarity of NLE and the limited availability of clinical samples, in vitro functional validation of the SYK-FCER1G decoupling was not performed in the present study. Consequently, this observation should be interpreted as associative rather than mechanistic evidence.

Future studies incorporating larger and longitudinal cohorts, together with experimental validation in cellular or humanized models, will be important to establish causality and further clarify the biological relevance of the CLR–SYK–FCER1G imbalance. In parallel, improved annotation and functional characterization of currently unannotated metabolites may further advance understanding of the microbiota–metabolism–immune interplay in NLE.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides integrative evidence supporting a multifactorial model of NLE pathogenesis that extends beyond a solely antibody-centered framework. Multi-omics analyses suggest that IFN-α pathway activation in NLE may be associated not only with maternal autoantibodies but also with alterations in gut microbiota, host metabolism, and SYK–FCER1G signaling balance.These findings broaden the current understanding of NLE pathophysiology and identify several pathways that may warrant further investigation in future translational studies, including interferon signaling, microbial homeostasis, and SYK-related immune regulation. Continued research will be required to determine the clinical relevance of these observations and their potential implications for risk assessment and therapeutic development.
