Arrest discretion and training needs for interacting with people with substance use disorders: a qualitative secondary analysis among police recruits in Washington State
Authors: Samuel Janson, Leif Layman, Melody McKee, Christine Rickert, Brandon Rogel, Becky Starnes, Mandy D Owens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrequent interactions between law enforcement and individuals with substance use disorders carry significant public health implications. Pre-arrest diversion programs rely on officer discretion to redirect individuals toward a range of medical and social services rather than make an arrest. To inform more effective evidence-based training, this study examines how police recruits anticipate exercising this discretion and identifies their self-reported training needs for navigating encounters with this population.ResultsA qualitative thematic analysis of open-ended survey responses from 115 recruits at the Washington State Basic Law Enforcement Academy was guided by the Focal Concerns Theory and Theory of Planned Behavior. Four themes emerged: (1) divergent views on addiction as a matter of personal choice versus disease, influencing perceptions of blameworthiness; (2) a primary concern for community protection, shaped by distinctions between violent and non-violent offenses; (3) a belief that incarceration can be a practical pathway to rehabilitation; and (4) a tension between adhering to departmental policy and being guided by personal and professional experiences. For future training, recruits requested more practical knowledge on drugs and de-escalation and strongly recommended experiential learning, including interactions with people with lived experience.ConclusionsRecruits’ anticipated use of discretion is shaped by core beliefs, practical considerations, and personal history. Training must move beyond knowledge transfer to actively address underlying attitudes and heuristics influencing arrest decisions. Effective implementation will also require aligning this training with supportive departmental policies and leadership.Keywords: Policing, Discretion, People who use drugs, Diversion

Background

Interactions between people with substance use disorders (SUD) and law enforcement officers (LEOs) are common (Friedman et al., 2021; Olgin et al., 2020; Richardson et al., 2023), and are increasingly complicated by behavioral health issues and the social determinants of health (Butler et al., 2022; Dailey & Dubrow, 2024; Laniyonu & Goff, 2021). These interactions, particularly those that result in incarceration, can have significant negative effects on the physical and psychological health of the individuals arrested (Doherty et al., 2022; Lo et al., 2018; Rima et al., 2023). In recent years, LEOs have played a more active role in helping facilitate entry into substance use treatment including through arrest alternatives such as law enforcement-assisted diversion (LEAD) (Collins et al., 2017; Gilbert et al., 2025; Joudrey et al., 2021; Perrone et al., 2022; Schaible et al., 2021).

Seattle launched the LEAD model in 2011, and jurisdictions nationwide have since replicated it (Clifasefi et al., 2017; Collins et al., 2017). LEAD connects individuals who would otherwise face arrest for controlled substance offenses with medical and social services, and the charge is cleared if the individual engages with services within a designated timeframe. Evaluations show that LEAD effectively facilitates entry into substance use treatment, decreases recidivism, and reduces costs related to arrest, incarceration, and hospitalization (Bernard et al., 2020; Collins et al., 2017, 2019). These positive outcomes are largely attributed to the combination of medical services (e.g. medications for SUD) and social support (e.g. employment, housing) provided through a diverse range of service providers (Clifasefi et al., 2017; Collins et al., 2017, 2019; Gilbert et al., 2025).

Prior research has investigated the beliefs of LEOs regarding LEAD and other diversion programs, people with SUD, and rehabilitation to explore how these views inform field discretion. Studies show that officers’ views vary; specifically, veteran officers express greater skepticism toward these programs than their less-experienced counterparts (Donnelly et al., 2024; Rouhani et al., 2019; Schaible et al., 2021; Worden & McLean, 2018). The attitudes of LEOs towards diversion programs are also influenced by departmental leadership. LEOs more readily support diversion if their leaders do, while those who hold more punitive views on policing are less likely to endorse or implement alternatives (Donnelly et al., 2024; Joudrey et al., 2021). Additional barriers to implementing diversion programs among LEOs include stigma towards people who use drugs, the procedural complexity of making diversion referrals, and a lack of guidance on the diversion process (Joudrey et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

While external factors like leadership and policy shape diversion outcomes, the ultimate decision to arrest or divert occurs within an officer’s individual cognitive process. Researchers have used multiple theoretical to understand the diversion decisions of LEOs and how training can help better inform discretionary decisions. One such framework is the Focal Concerns Theory (FCT), which was first used to examine sentencing disparities among criminal defendants (Steffensmeier & Demuth, 2001, 2006). FCT posits that judges base sentencing decisions on three main considerations: the defendant’s blameworthiness or culpability, the need to protect public safety, and the practical constraints or consequences of the sentence (Steffensmeier et al., 1998). Because judges often operate under time pressure and with incomplete information, their decisions are frequently influenced by personal experiences and implicit biases, factors that can contribute to unequal sentencing outcomes (Steffensmeier et al., 1998). More recently, researchers have applied FCT to understand how LEOs make arrest decisions in the field (Campbell & Fehler-Cabral, 2018; Higgins et al., 2012; Ishoy & Dabney, 2018; Vito et al., 2021), where similar time constraints and information limitations exist. Ishoy and Dabney (2017) found that officers assessed blameworthiness primarily through perceptions of personal responsibility and the seriousness of the offense. In evaluating the need to protect the community, officers often considered whether the suspect was a repeat offender, their attitude and demeanor, and the potential to prevent immediate or related crimes (Ishoy & Dabney, 2018). Practical considerations were largely defined by adherence to legal requirements, departmental policies, and the need to preserve “beat integrity”, ensuring their assigned area remains covered while transporting a suspect to jail (Ishoy & Dabney, 2018).

A core tenet of FCT is that the decisions of criminal justice actors are not purely objective, but are filtered through their personal experiences, beliefs, and values as they assess blameworthiness, community protection, and practical constraints (Steffensmeier et al., 1998). This subjectivity is critical in interactions with people with SUD, who are often viewed through a moralized lens emphasizing personal responsibility, which can inflate perceptions of blameworthiness (Lloyd, 2013; Volkow, 2020) .Furthermore, stereotypes conflating substance use with criminality or unpredictability can heighten perceived threats to public safety (Del Pozo et al., 2021; Greer et al., 2022). Consequently, using FCT’s domains as a decision-making heuristic can channel stigma into discretionary outcomes, potentially exacerbating systemic inequities (Harris, 2009).

Another useful theory for examining LEOs’ discretion decisions is the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which posits that an individual’s intention to engage in a behavior is influenced by three factors: their attitude toward the behavior, perceived social norms and pressure, and perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1991). Researchers have applied TPB to better understand police discretion in interactions with people who use drugs (PWUD) (Del Pozo et al., 2021). Del Pozo et al. (2021) found that LEOs perceive a high degree of control over their decision to arrest for misdemeanor drug-related offenses (Del Pozo et al., 2021). Officers who believed their supervisors and peers supported treatment referrals, and who believed diversion could prevent future arrests, were more likely to refer individuals to treatment (Del Pozo et al., 2021). However, the study also found that higher levels of stigma among officers were negatively associated with treatment referrals (Del Pozo et al., 2021). Additionally, many officers held the belief that PWUD are dishonest or untrustworthy in their interactions with police (Del Pozo et al., 2021).

Taken together, TPB and FCT offer two complementary frameworks for understanding LEOs’ use of discretion in interactions with people with SUD. TPB explains officers’ intentions when deciding whether to make an arrest, as these intentions are shaped by attitudes toward drug use, perceived social norms, and perceived behavioral control. FCT extends this by specifying how those intentions are translated into action during encounters characterized by time, resource, and information constraints. For example, an officer who believes they have a high degree of control over their arrest decisions may rely on the focal concerns of blameworthiness, protection of the community, and practical constraints to determine the most appropriate response. In this context, officers’ beliefs about a person with a SUD, including perceptions of responsibility, risk of reoffending, and trustworthiness, may shape how these focal concerns are assessed, ultimately influencing whether an arrest or diversion is pursued.

In 2021 the Washington State legislature passed Senate Bill 5476 which mandated all new LEOs receive training on interacting with people with SUD (Owens et al., 2024). To satisfy this training, the Washington State Criminal Justice Training Commission partnered with the Addictions, Drug, and Alcohol Institute at the University of Washington to develop and begin providing this SUD training at the Basic Law Enforcement Academy (BLEA). This collaboration also initiated a preliminary evaluation of the SUD training, including pre-post surveys among recruits completing the Academy with and without the SUD training (Owens et al., 2024). Existing research on police attitudes toward diversion has largely focused on experienced, sworn officers and departmental leadership (Bailey et al., 2025). Other studies have documented the attitudes and barriers among veteran officers, such as the influence of departmental culture, stigma, and practical concerns (Del Pozo et al., 2024; Joudrey et al., 2021; Regalado et al., 2025). However, there is a critical gap in understanding the perspectives of law enforcement trainees who receive training on SUD and who will soon be responsible for making arrest decisions. Understanding how recruits anticipate using discretion, what beliefs they hold about addiction, and what training they feel they need is essential for designing effective training programs and supporting the long-term success of pre-arrest diversion programs.

The present study seeks to fill this gap in the literature through a secondary analysis examining BLEA trainees’ open-ended survey responses to examine two questions. One question focused on how the recruit anticipates their life experience will influence their decision to arrest a person with a substance use disorder, and the other focused on additional training needs. A deeper understanding of how recruits’ personal experiences shape arrest decisions can shed light on when and why diversion occurs. Paired with insights on future training needs, these findings can inform strategies to strengthen LEAD programs and other diversion initiatives nationwide.

Methods

Data Collection

Surveys were conducted with LEO recruits within two weeks of starting BLEA (“pre”) and again within two weeks of graduation (“post”). This pre-post study design allowed for a comparison of changes in knowledge and perspectives among recruits completing the BLEA with and without the new SUD training. BLEA trains recruits from across WA State, is 720 hours and approximately 19 weeks, and is completed entirely in person. Briefly, the new SUD training was four hours and included lecture-based content, small-group discussions, and videos of people in recovery. Topics focused on SUD as a medical condition and signs of substance use, risk factors of SUD, pathways to recovery, and strategies to improve interactions with PWUD (Owens et al., 2023). For the purposes of this study, only post surveys were examined among recruits completing the BLEA with the new SUD training. Surveys were anonymously completed via paper, and included quantitative questions based on two subscales of the Drug Problem Perceptions Questionnaire (Mahmoud et al., 2023) and additional questions added by Washington State Criminal Justice Training Commission partners (Owens et al., 2024). Following the quantitative questions, respondents were asked two open-ended, free response questions: (1) How will your life experience influence your decision to arrest someone with a substance use disorder?; and (2) What should we consider about future training for interacting with people with a substance use disorder?

The training was offered to 9 cohorts of trainees, each averaging around 30 people (approximately 270 total before attrition during training). At the conclusion of BLEA, a total of 211 recruits completed the post-survey. The analysis in this study focuses on responses to the two open-ended items. Of the 211 post-survey completers, 98 recruits (46.4%) provided at least one substantive, codable open-ended response. Specifically, 87 recruits responded to Question 1 (life experiences), 86 recruits responded to Question 2 (training needs), and 75 recruits (35.5% of the post-survey sample) provided codable answers to both questions. The textual responses were concise and direct. For the 87 substantive responses to Question 1, the length ranged from 1 to 58 words, with a mean of 11.5 words. For the 86 substantive responses to Question 2, length ranged from 1 to 49 words, with a mean of 10.8 words. The majority of responses for each question fell between 3 and 20 words. This brevity indicates that participants provided focused, declarative statements rather than extended narratives.

Data analysis

Handwritten responses were transcribed into a spreadsheet and verified for accuracy by two study team members. The qualitative analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis process (Braun & Clarke, 2006), guided by the theoretical frameworks of FCT and the TPB. Two coders (SJ, LL) developed a codebook based on key FCT constructs (blameworthiness, community protection, practical constraints) and TPB constructs (attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control). After independent coding of all responses to Question 1, we held a consensus meeting to reconcile differences, and a third study team member broke any ties. Through an iterative process of grouping and refining codes, coders identified four primary themes: (1) Perceptions of Addiction: Personal Responsibility vs. Disease; (2) Community Protection; (3) Rehabilitation through Incarceration; and (4) Weighing Policy and Personal Experience. These researcher-constructed themes were then mapped onto the relevant FCT and TPB constructs (see Table 1). In line with qualitative exploratory methods, we did not quantify theme frequencies, instead determining salience through the recurrence of ideas and their conceptual relevance to the frameworks.

Results

Theme: perceptions of addiction: personal choice vs. disease

We identified a key theme regarding recruits’ perceptions of addiction, centering on the degree to which they viewed it as a matter of personal responsibility versus a disease. This theme was defined by the intersection of respondent perspectives on the blameworthiness of people with a SUD and the subjective norms they hold about addiction and treatment. Respondents expressed different views on the role of individual choice. Some strongly emphasized personal responsibility and fault, such as the recruit who stated, “We all have a choice to be clean or not,” and another who said, “I personally do not have much sympathy for drug users as in the end, it is a choice.” Others presented a more nuanced or sympathetic perspective, acknowledging the complex etiology of addiction. One recruit noted, “Life is all about choices. I understand that people don’t always choose to become addicted to drugs. I am sympathetic to the people who are addicted due to being prescribed opioids and getting hooked because of it. I am far less sympathetic though when it comes to people who began with poor choices and continue to make poor choices.”

In contrast, many recruits viewed substance use as a symptom of underlying issues or a disease process, suggesting these factors should inform officer arrest discretion. Responses included, “Substance use stems from something deeper such as emotional or psychological abuse, childhood causes, etc., so discretion of arrest is key,” and “I understand that addiction is powerful and typically isn’t as simple as choosing to stop. My job will be to enforce laws but also provide community caretaking for members of the community.” Another recruit stated, “I will be careful to take my past EMS experience into account when interacting with drug users. I will treat them with dignity and recognize their disease and not treat them primarily as criminals.”

A recurring related belief was that an individual’s voluntarily seeking treatment is a prerequisite for effective treatment. This belief increased the perceived appropriateness of arrest when readiness was doubted. For example, one respondent said, “Habitual users that have no interest in quitting drugs or alcohol will more than likely have very little interest in anything I will say in terms of rehabilitation options.” Another expressed skepticism about mandated treatment, stating, “ A lot of officers think it’s silly to send people to treatment and resources if it’s someone who doesn’t want to stop using.”

Theme: community protection

The focal concern of community protection was a common driver in recruits’ anticipated arrest determination. A recurring theme was distinguishing between violent and non-violent offenses as a basis for their arrest decision. One recruit stated, “If they are violent aggressive criminals who commit violent crime it is a very different response than, say, a nonviolent person who is deep in addiction.” Others indicated they would be less lenient with individuals who had repeatedly been offered services. As one participant explained, “Based on the scene, I will determine whether to provide resources or not. If the person has received resources in the past three times and is putting other lives in danger, I will make an arrest.” Some recruits framed public safety and resource provision not as opposing goals, but as compatible responsibilities of law enforcement. As one noted, “They need assistance, however criminal acts are not excusable because of SUD. Even if arrested, [law enforcement officers should] provide resources.” For some recruits, concerns for community safety escalated into critiques of state policy, which they perceived as failing to address a public health crisis. One recruit argued, “Washington State has a drug problem because of the current state government/legislation. It’s time to fix it. Put people on drugs back in jail. Fentanyl is killing people. It’s scary that our state doesn’t do a lot about it.”

Theme: rehabilitation through incarceration

Responses revealed conflicting perspectives regarding the role of incarceration, aligning with the FCT construct of practical constraints. While some respondents viewed jail as a necessary, and sometimes the only, pathway to treatment, others explicitly rejected this carceral approach. Those who saw incarceration as a practical pathway to treatment made statements such as, “Giving people help is the key to helping substance use. Sometimes it’s connecting [with resources], other times it’s arresting,” and “Many need the help but do not know where to go. Jail may be a method for the help to be given.” Others refuted this logic, advocating for community-based resources instead. One respondent argued, “I don’t agree with Illegal use of any substance. However, with the law and the state of our jails—resources for them (people with a SUD) are the best option.” Practical considerations, such as the availability of long-term support, also factored into the anticipated decision-making process, as one recruit noted: “Discretion in arresting individuals depends on available long-term resources and the severity of the crime.” A unique concern was the fear that incarceration could cause harm, with one participant expressing a specific subjective norm about medication for opioid use disorder: “For offences where arrests are not mandatory, I feel taking them to jail can be detrimental sometimes. If an arrest could lead to developing a methadone addiction, sometimes it’s best for the person to not be booked.”

Theme: weighing police department policy and personal experiences/relationships

Participants expressed divergent views on how personal experiences and relationships would shape their interactions with PWUD. Some recruits reported that they would not rely on personal or professional experience at all. As one respondent stated, “I don’t imagine my life experiences influencing my decision to arrest someone.” Another recruit maintained a personal and professional separation, saying, “I don’t believe they will influence my decision… I’ve interacted with people who abuse substances before and have been able to show empathy.” In contrast, many recruits with deeper personal or professional ties anticipated that their history would actively guide their discretion. For some, this exposure fostered a preference for diversion, as illustrated by responses like, “Seeing one of my siblings affected by a substance use disorder will help me consider connecting them with resources rather than making an arrest,” and, “From knowing someone who overdosed, I know drug use is usually not criminal and people need resources and help from family and the community more than to go to jail.” However, a smaller number drew different practical conclusions from similar exposure. For instance, one recruit with corrections experience noted, “Having experience working in jails, I’ve seen that jails often have more resources and access to those resources than patrol officers on the street,” framing arrest as a potential pathway to services. Others acknowledged that a lack of familiarity would also shape their approach, with one participant explaining, “More life experience can influence a person negatively or positively. I do not have much life experience with (substance use) disorders so my decision may be different.” Overall, whether citing the presence or absence of experience, respondents expressed a high degree of perceived control in these decisions, frequently emphasizing that, given the complex roots of substance use, “…discretion of arrest is key.”

Question two

Theme: topic areas for future training

In response to question two, participants suggested a range of topics for future training. Most requested more training on specific substances and their subjective effects. One participant wrote, “You should consider having more class time regarding recognizing drugs and the effects specific drugs have on people.” Related to this, trainees requested the opportunity to see different types of substances in-person, saying “[The trainers should] display and let people see physical examples of drugs.” A few respondents expressed a desire to receive more training regarding the process for helping PWUD enter SUD treatment or access other services, saying, “We should focus a lot on proper procedures on how to help get people into rehabs and what resources we can offer.” Relatedly, a participant mentioned training regarding “How to be more involved with those people dealing with their life changing challenges. To include giving resources to help promote wellness and employment to those suffering with their challenges.”

Respondents also emphasized a need for more training on how to interact with people while under the influence of drugs, saying, “I do not think [the training] spends enough time teaching about substance abuse. I do not think we are shown how to communicate and understand people on substances and how to de-escalate the situation.” Related to this point, several other trainees expressed a desire for more examples of how best to communicate with someone while under the influence of drugs, and one participant specifically mentioned training meant to lower stigma towards PWUD.

Theme: training modalities

Respondents suggested a range of methods that could be used to enhance future training. Participants emphasized the need for experiential learning more than strictly classroom-based sessions, saying “100% classroom-based learning is ineffective.” Respondents suggested using training time for more interactive sessions with PWUD. One respondent suggested “Success stories or meeting those individuals to ask questions and connect to understand their perspective and listen to their side of the story. How do they perceive Law Enforcement and how can we better help them?” Several participants suggested mock scenes might help trainees practice de-escalation skills. One participant suggested that small group discussion may be beneficial opportunity for trainees to discuss their own perspectives and experiences with SUD, saying “Allow recruits to have one-on-one or small group conversations about their opinions on this topic. I have heard a lot of negative feedback that was not expressed in class on this topic.” Overall, recruits expressed a desire to receive training that is better aligned with real-world scenarios and police practices.

Discussion

This study generated new insights into how law enforcement trainees anticipate their life experiences may influence their arrest and diversion decisions for substance-related offenses, as well as their recommendations for improving training. Key findings revealed that recruits hold competing views on the nature of addiction, as a matter of personal choice versus a disease, which may shape perceptions of blameworthiness. Furthermore, a significant tension exists regarding the role of incarceration, with many recruits viewing jail as a necessary pathway to SUD treatment. Regarding training, recruits expressed a desire for more hands-on training, a greater exploration of real-world scenarios, and suggested the inclusion of people with lived experience of drug use in the training curriculum.

Participants expressed competing views on personal responsibility versus the disease model of addiction, reflecting a broader tension in how they perceive the blameworthiness of individuals. Prior research shows that LEOs who view addiction primarily as a moral failing, rather than as a medical condition, are less likely to support diversion and more likely to favor punitive drug policies (Del Pozo et al., 2024; Murphy & Russell, 2021). The link between attitudes and behavioral intention is a key component of TPB. Therefore, future training with LEO recruits could seek to change attitudes toward illicit drug use by emphasizing the biopsychosocial model, highlighting the roles of personal history, environmental factors, and biology in understanding addiction (Gonzalez & Skewes, 2013). Several recruits in the current study who reported no prior personal or professional experience with substance use endorsed moralistic or punitive views of illicit substance use. While this observation aligns with recent evidence suggesting that LEOs with personal connections to substance use disorders view diversion programs more favorably (Regalado et al., 2025), it is important to note that our study did not explicitly test for a causal or statistical correlation between these variables. Consequently, a broader quantitative analysis would be required to determine if this association holds true within our specific sample population.

Many recruits expressed concern for community safety, often describing arrest decisions in terms of how violent or responsible they perceived an individual to be. A recurring theme was the distinction between violent and non-violent offenses when determining whether arrest was warranted. While such assessments reflect an emphasis on discretion and situational judgment, prior research suggests that perceptions of threat and violence may be susceptible to bias. Experimental and observational studies indicate that law enforcement officers may overestimate the threat posed by individuals from minority racial backgrounds (Najdowski et al., 2015), and people with a SUD from these same backgrounds report heightened experiences of racialized stigma in police encounters (Trombley et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings suggest that for people of color with SUD, subjective evaluations of violence and responsibility during police encounters may operate within broader systems of intersectional stigma, contributing to racialized disparities in arrest decisions and downstream outcomes (Pamplin et al., 2023; Pinedo, 2019).

Recruits varied in how they perceived incarceration as a pathway to substance use treatment. Many viewed jail as a necessary pathway to treatment, despite evidence that many carceral settings inconsistently offer evidence-based treatments for SUD (Owens et al., 2023; Pourtaher et al., 2024); Berk et al., 2025; Hoover et al., 2023). A national survey of jails found that while two-thirds provide buprenorphine, less than half provide methadone, with availability constrained by financial and policy barriers, especially in rural areas (Flanagan Balawajder et al., 2024). Future training should address these misconceptions by explaining the limited availability of comprehensive treatment in jail and highlighting why community-based approaches may offer better long-term outcomes (Belenko et al., 2013; Fardone et al., 2023).

Relatedly, some respondents expressed skepticism that individuals would meaningfully engage in treatment when it is not mandated by the criminal legal system. This skepticism may reflect a broader misconception that treatment is generally ineffective or unwanted. LEO recruits may not be aware that many people with a SUD wish to reduce or stop their use but face access barriers (Farhoudian et al., 2022; Kingston et al., 2024). Educating LEOs on the diversity of available SUD treatments and the iterative nature of recovery could help them see the benefits of pre-incarceration diversion as connecting willing individuals to a variety of voluntary services, not mandating SUD treatment.

Overall, responses highlight multiple areas to improve future training efforts on SUD for LEOs. While respondents expressed a high degree of perceived control over arrest decisions, a smaller subgroup stated they would defer strictly to departmental policy. This highlights a need for training that actively bridges abstract policy and the on-the-ground application of discretion. Recruits overwhelmingly critiqued passive, classroom-based instruction as insufficient, instead calling for practical, hands-on modalities such as role-playing, analysis of body camera footage, and small-group discussions to prepare for complex field interactions. This type of experiential training could more effectively demonstrate the reality of police discretion, moving beyond the idea of simply deferring to departmental policy. Furthermore, many trainees requested the inclusion of people with lived experience in training, an evidence-based strategy for stigma reduction (Bielenberg et al., 2021). These training enhancements could serve as a critical intervention to mitigate the impact of negative field experiences and stigma, and to promote more uniform, equitable discretionary outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although respondents were drawn from across Washington State, including both rural and urban areas, they may not be representative of LEOs in other states with different legal contexts. The handwritten nature of the survey may have led some responses to be more concise than they might have been in an oral interview. However, the confidential format likely encouraged honest reporting. Additionally, because these participants were recruits who had not yet begun active duty, their responses may not generalize to more experienced officers. The survey captures what recruits believe will influence their arrest decisions, which may not reflect actual future behavior. Moreover, officers may not necessarily know whether someone with a controlled substance offense has a diagnosable substance use disorder, limiting the applicability of their perceptions to real-world cases. Future research should consider longitudinal designs that follow officers over time to assess the lasting impact of training and related interventions. Combining quantitative and qualitative approaches could also strengthen the understanding of relationships between training, perceptions, and behavior.

Conclusions

By analyzing open-ended survey responses through the lenses of FCT and TPB, our findings illustrate how law enforcement recruits anticipated that a combination of personal experiences and relationships, beliefs about addiction, perceptions of community safety, and departmental policy may influence their use of arrest discretion in interactions with people with a SUD. Many recruits acknowledged that making an arrest or diversion into services would require their independent judgment, while some stated that they would defer only to objective criteria in their departments’ policies. The effective implementation of pre-arrest diversion programs could be supported by training recruits to feel equipped to utilize pre-arrest diversion. This may be achieved by using real-world examples from body-camera footage, LEOs’ accounts, or role-playing. Further, addressing recruits’ misconceptions about the nature of addiction and SUD treatment availability in correctional facilities may make LEOs more likely to use pre-arrest diversion alternatives. The inclusion of people with lived experience of drug use in training may help reduce stigma among recruits and could be paired with experiential learning opportunities to complement classroom instruction. Future research should follow officers longitudinally to assess how these attitudes translate into real-world behaviors and to evaluate the long-term impact of enhanced training on the use of pre-arrest diversion.
