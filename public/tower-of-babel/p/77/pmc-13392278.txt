Anthropogenic climate change accelerates the onset of global flood timing
Authors: Wei Qi, Yanli Liu, Xin Jiang, Junguo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Floods are among Earth’s most devastating natural disasters, with cascading societal and ecological impacts. Flood timing shifts amplify risks by disrupting preparedness, yet their global patterns remain largely unquantified. Using multi-model ensembles, we provide a global-scale assessment of flood timing changes under incremental warming (1.5 °C–4.0 °C). Here we show that anthropogenic forcing advances global flood timing by 0.43 ± 0.25 days per 0.5 °C of warming, with regional divergence: early-flood regions shift even earlier, while late-flood regions experience further delay. At 1.5 °C, 50.73 ± 4.23% of global land area faces flood timing shifts greater than 7 days, escalating to 52.85 ± 3.20% at 2.0 °C. Countries including China, India, and the United States are projected to experience greater population exposure. These findings highlight the need to change how flood risk is conceptualized and managed. The traditional focus on flood magnitude and frequency must expand to incorporate timing as a fundamental variable in climate adaptation planning.

Introduction

Floods constitute among the most devastating natural disasters worldwide, causing substantial economic losses, threatening communities, and displacing populations1–3. Previous research has focused predominantly on variations in flood frequency and magnitude, their drivers and societal consequences4–10. The timing of flood events, however, is equally critical for risk mitigation, water resource management, and agricultural planning; even minor shifts could result in catastrophic impacts. While regional analyses11–16 and studies focused on historical periods17–19 of flood timing exist, a holistic global assessment—especially under projected climate change conditions—remains lacking. Anthropogenic climate forcing has profoundly altered the global water cycle5,20–22. Although efforts have been made to quantify the specific contributions of anthropogenic drivers to flood frequency and magnitude changes4,5,10,23,24, understanding of anthropogenic impacts on flood timing remains limited. These gaps persist despite being explicitly noted in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report25.

Under climate change, temperature and precipitation patterns exhibit marked spatiotemporal heterogeneity26–29, driving shifts in wet-season timing30–32. Concurrently, warming increases the proportion of rainfall, altering snowfall-to-rainfall ratios33,34. In rainfall-dominated basins, flood timing is primarily driven by precipitation characteristics35,36; short-duration events typically control rapid-onset floods, whereas long-duration rainfall dominates in large river basins characterized by slow-response floods16,36. Meanwhile, in snow-influenced regions, flood characteristics are modulated by snowmelt dynamics, often governed by temperature indices such as the onset of above-freezing periods16,37. These changes are likely to drive spatially divergent shifts in flood regimes. However, the global-scale impacts of varying warming levels on flood timing remain poorly understood.

This study provides a global assessment of flood timing changes and associated risks under incremental warming levels. We used an ensemble of global hydrological and General Circulation Models (GCMs; see Methods), analyzing historical, pre-industrial, and future projections under Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 2.6, 6.0, and 8.5 (1976–2099). Using the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (ISIMIP) hydrological model outputs coupled with the CaMa-Flood hydrodynamic model, we simulated global river discharge, enabling comprehensive analysis of spatiotemporal variations across warming scenarios. Flood timing was considered as the day of the year on which the annual peak flood discharge occurs. We also assessed population exposure to flood timing shifts at 1.5 °C, 2.0 °C, and beyond. Our results reveal robust (see Methods for evaluations), spatially heterogeneous changes of flood timing under global warming, with early-flood regions accelerating further and late-flood zones experiencing delays (see Methods for definitions). Overall, anthropogenic forcing advances global flood timing by 0.43 ± 0.25 days/0.5 °C. We project over five billion people exposed to ≥7 days flood timing shifts by 1.5 °C warming. This work provides critical insights into global flood timing variability and its implications for vulnerable populations worldwide, demonstrating that these shifts constitute a substantial, frequently overlooked dimension of flood risk.

Global average changes

Globally, flood timing is projected to advance significantly under climate warming (Fig. 2c). For each 0.5 °C warming increment between 1.5 °C and 4.0 °C, the mean global flood timing advances by 0.43 ± 0.25 days. By contrast, under pre-industrial control (picontrol) conditions, the trend is statistically indistinguishable from zero (p > 0.05). The divergence in flood timing between picontrol and anthropogenic warming scenarios widens with increasing temperatures (Fig. 2d), with the picontrol trend measuring only half of the anthropogenic signal. These results establish anthropogenic forcing as the dominant driver of accelerated global flood timing, causing a systematic shift of peak flood events to earlier dates.

In regions with delayed flood timing, precipitation timing shifts constitute the principal driver (Fig. S5a, b), whereas the annual onset of the first 7-day period with temperatures exceeding 0 °C (T7) advances with warming (Fig. S5c). Conversely, in regions exhibiting advanced flood timing, both precipitation and temperature mechanisms contribute, as the timing of annual maximum daily precipitation (P), 7-day accumulated precipitation (P7), and T7 all advance (Fig. S5d–f). The temporal shifts in P and P7 exhibit similar trends, indicating that short- and long-duration precipitation exert comparable effects on flood timing shifts16. This alignment allows for a unified interpretation of precipitation-driven changes. Notably, T7’s influence is primarily restricted to the Northern Hemisphere, where T7 timing exhibits minimal advancement in the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. S6c). For example, in eastern Brazil, southern Africa, and eastern Australia, advances in P and P7 (fig. S6a, b) drive earlier flood timing without corresponding T7 shifts (Fig. S6c). In contrast, across snow-dominated Northern Hemisphere basins—which contain the majority of global seasonal snowpack—earlier T7 timing promotes accelerated snowmelt, directly advancing flood timing in high-latitude regions such as northern Canada and western Siberia.

Potential risks from timing alterations

Under global warming, both the magnitude and spatial extent of flood timing shifts (ΔT) increase remarkably (Fig. 3). At 1.5 °C warming, moderate shifts (7 ≤ ΔT < 14 days) are concentrated in the Southern Hemisphere, Europe, and the United States, expanding under incremental warming to include Canada, Siberia, and South/Southeast Asia (Fig. 3a–f). The global land area experiencing severe (14 ≤ ΔT < 21 days) shifts increases from 11.33 ± 1.10% (1.5 °C) to 13.85 ± 1.37% (4.0 °C; Fig. 3h), while exceptional (ΔT ≥ 21 days) impacts grow from 20.49 ± 3.92% to 27.95 ± 2.82%. Total land area affected by ΔT ≥ 7 days rises from 50.73 ± 4.23% (1.5 °C) to 52.85 ± 3.20% (2.0 °C) and 60.03 ± 2.07% (4.0 °C). Population exposure follows similar remarkable escalation. Moderate shift exposure rises from 1.99 ± 0.25 billion (1.5 °C) to 2.06 ± 0.44 billion (4.0 °C), while severe and exceptional exposures surge from 1.18 ± 0.18/1.98 ± 0.30 billion to 1.33 ± 0.27/2.62 ± 0.30 billion (Fig. 3g). Cumulative exposure to shifts ≥7 days reaches 5.15 ± 0.18 billion (1.5 °C), 5.41 ± 0.22 billion (2.0°C), and to 6.01 ± 0.15 billion at 4.0°C. Each 0.5 °C warming adds approximately 13.55 ± 11.14 million (moderate), 29.68 ± 14.97 million (severe), and 128.80 ± 24.83 million (exceptional) exposed individuals.

Geographically, several countries, such as China (148.61 ± 32.80 million at 1.5 °C), India (112.80 ± 72.70 million at 1.5 °C), the United States (106.55 ± 14.33 million at 1.5 °C), Russia, Brazil, and Indonesia, face disproportionately high exposure to moderate shifts across all warming levels (Fig. S7). For severe and exceptional categories, these countries experience substantially greater impacts as well (Figs. S8–S9), joined by countries in the middle part of Africa as additional critical hotspots for exceptional shifts. This spatial progression underscores the compound risks facing multiple populated regions and highlights the urgency of integrating flood timing into climate adaptation planning.

Discussion

This study establishes that anthropogenic climate change is driving spatially heterogeneous shifts in global flood timing, representing a dimension of hydrological risk largely overlooked in previous research. While prior work has focused on historical periods16,32,37,39–41 or single-country analyses11,42, our global assessment across incremental warming levels reveals systematic advances in flood timing that intensify with rising temperatures. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report25, acknowledges regional trends but lacks conclusive evidence on planetary-scale shifts—a critical gap this research fills. Our analysis demonstrates that these changes pose substantial threats to vulnerable populations, demanding integration into climate adaptation frameworks.

Comparisons with previous regional studies validate both our findings and methodology. While the study by Han et al.37 represents a significant contribution to the field of streamflow seasonality, divergences from our findings likely stem from key methodological distinctions. The study by Han et al.37 focused on the historical period (1950–2020), and our study examines future climates under intense global warming scenarios. Additionally, Han et al.37 analyzed seasonality at a monthly scale, whereas our results are based on daily data; consequently, shifts in daily timing that remain within the same month may not be apparent at the monthly scale. Despite these distinctions, our results share similarities with previous studies. Historically, high-flow months in northern North America and Europe were concentrated between March and July37, aligning with our flood-based results (Fig. S11). Earlier flood timing has been reported across many regions of North America and Europe19, including northern North America, northern Europe, and southern Europe, a trend generally mirrored in our results (Fig. 1). It has been demonstrated that annual maximum precipitation, annual maximum 7-day precipitation, and the timing of the first emergence of 7-day periods with air temperatures exceeding 0°C reasonably explain flood timing shifts in Europe during the historical period16, and our analysis confirms that these factors similarly account for variations in global flood timing (Fig. S5).

The implications for flood management infrastructure are particularly pressing. Reservoir operations, governed by rules assuming stable seasonal cycles43,44, face increasing obsolescence as flood timing shifts exceed three weeks in some regions (e.g., eastern South America, southern Africa, southeastern China, and eastern North America at 4.0 °C warming). While medium-to-long-term hydrological forecasts offer potential for adaptive operations—such as adjusting water release timing—their implementation faces institutional barriers due to legally rigid regulations. Policymakers must therefore prioritize developing flexible regulatory frameworks that accommodate evolving hydrological patterns, enabling proactive rather than reactive flood management.

Agricultural systems face parallel challenges from temporal mismatches45–47. For example, China’s winter wheat harvest (May–June)48 traditionally precedes peak flood seasons (July–August)49. However, our projections indicate earlier flood timing in central and northern China under global warming, increasing the likelihood of overlap with critical harvest windows. During May–June 2023, unseasonal heavy rainfall disrupted harvests in central China, prompting over $10 million in government aid to expedite wheat drying and recovery. Despite interventions, national wheat yields declined by 0.9% in 2023, underscoring climate-driven threats to food security50. Similarly, in Brazil, climatological flood periods (April) risk encroaching on the January–March planting window for corn and sorghum as warming climate leads to earlier flood timing51. Our results suggest advancing flood onsets could destabilize planting cycles in these regions—a systemic risk underprioritized in global food security discourse. Adaptive agricultural scheduling52,53 may mitigate such disruptions but requires recalibrating international crop trade networks, potentially exacerbating price volatility54.

Temporal shifts in flood regimes threaten aquatic and riparian ecosystem stability. Floods facilitate the transport of sediment and organic matter—critical nutrients sustaining riverine food webs—while synchronizing with key ecological processes such as fish spawning. Floodplains serve as essential breeding grounds for fish, providing habitat for egg deposition55 and hydraulic connectivity for migration to spawning sites56. Similarly, floods recharge interconnected lakes and wetlands, sustaining biodiverse aquatic habitats57. Altered flood timing disrupts these cycles, decoupling nutrient fluxes, habitat availability, and reproductive cues—impairing fish spawning, juvenile development, and population resilience58. Avian species reliant on floodplain inundation for nesting and chick rearing face analogous risks, as mistimed floods may inundate vulnerable fledglings or desiccate critical breeding habitats59.

Both climate change mitigation and adaptation offer pathways to reduce future population exposure25. Global efforts are currently underway to limit greenhouse gas emissions through clean energy adoption, with many regions committed to restricting global warming to 2.0 °C, and preferably 1.5 °C60–62. Under a 1.5 °C warming scenario, the population exposed to moderate, severe, and exceptional timing shifts is projected to reach 1.99 ± 0.25 billion, 1.18 ± 0.18 billion, and 1.98 ± 0.30 billion, respectively. At 2.0 °C warming, these figures rise to 2.06 ± 0.31 billion, 1.23 ± 0.22 billion, and 2.12 ± 0.38 billion. Crucially, limiting warming to 1.5 °C rather than 2.0 °C would prevent a global mean flood timing advance of 1.27 ± 0.61 days, corresponding to a reduction in total exposed population of ~0.26 billion people. This underscores the critical role of mitigation in reducing exposure. Beyond mitigation, land-use strategies such as reforestation and wetland restoration, which are commonly used to reduce flood risk63–65, could modify runoff responses and attenuate shifts in flood timing. Proactive adaptation, such as shifting agricultural calendars66 and optimizing land-use management67, could further ameliorate societal exposure. Future work integrating high-resolution land-use projections with the hydrological modeling framework used here would be valuable for quantifying these effects.

Fundamentally, flood timing represents a critical third dimension of flood risk alongside traditional metrics of frequency and magnitude. Shifts in flood timing amplify global flood hazards, with earlier onsets posing greater risks than delayed events due to reduced anticipatory capacity. When floods occur within anticipated intervals, preparedness enhances societal resilience, thereby mitigating losses68. Conversely, earlier flood timing—occurring outside expected exposure windows—exposes populations and infrastructure without adequate preparedness, exacerbating socioeconomic damages. Thus, flood timing constitutes a critical yet underappreciated dimension of flood risk, complementing traditional metrics like frequency and magnitude69. Existing research has predominantly focused on the latter, neglecting the systemic implications of temporal shifts. Our study provides a comprehensive global analysis of flood risk attributable to timing variability, revealing potential exposure for over five billion individuals. These findings necessitate integrating temporal risk frameworks into flood hazard assessments to address this emergent climate adaptation challenge.

Collectively, these findings highlight the need to change how flood risk is conceptualized and managed. The traditional focus on flood magnitude and frequency must expand to incorporate timing as a fundamental variable in climate adaptation planning. Future research should prioritize developing dynamic forecasting systems, adaptive governance models, and ecosystem-based approaches that address the multifaceted challenges revealed by our global analysis.

Methods

Datasets

An ensemble of six global hydrological models, including WEB-DHM-SG70, WaterGAP2-2c71, H0872, LPJml73, CWatM74 and MATSIRO75, was employed to assess climate-driven flood timing shifts. These models were forced with climate projections from four General Circulation Models (GCMs: GFDL-ESM2M, HadGEM2-ES, IPSL-CM5A-LR, MIROC5), generating 24 ensemble members. These hydrological models are commonly applied in global hydrology research5,76,77. Global hydrological simulations were obtained from the ISIMIP2b. Daily river discharge was simulated using the CaMa-Flood hydrodynamic model at 0.5° spatial resolution78. Desert regions were excluded from the analysis. Future projections covered 2006–2099 under RCP2.6, RCP6.0, and RCP8.5 scenarios, while historical (1976–2005) and pre-industrial control (1976–2099) simulations provided baseline and counterfactual conditions. Anthropogenic forcing, including greenhouse gas emissions and land-use changes, was explicitly represented in the global climate and hydrological models used for this study.

Time periods corresponding to specific warming thresholds (0.5 °C steps between 1.5 °C and 4.0 °C) were identified using 30-year running means of global mean temperature from the GCMs in ISIMIP2b, following IPCC methodological standards79. These derived periods were applied consistently across both future projections and pre-industrial control scenarios. Future population projections under Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) were employed to estimate population exposure80,81. The SSP-RCP combinations include SSP1-RCP2.6, SSP3-RCP6.0, and SSP5-RCP8.5, aligning with conventions in flood risk studies82,83.

We consider the influence of precipitation duration on flood timing. Annual maximum daily precipitation (P) is a well-established indicator of rapid-onset flood timing16,35, whereas annual maximum 7‑day accumulated precipitation (P7) corresponds more closely to the timing of slow-response floods16,36,70,84. This dual-metric approach captures the influence of both short- and long-duration precipitation events, which are known to govern rapid and slow flood response mechanisms, respectively16,35,36. Our results demonstrate that P and P7 exhibit similar trends in timing shifts under warming. This consistency indicates that short- and long-duration precipitation exert comparable directional influences on flood timing changes at the global scale—a finding that aligns with and extends earlier regional analyses16. Consequently, while both metrics are diagnostically useful, their convergent trends allow us to interpret precipitation-driven shifts through a unified conceptual framework. Additionally, as demonstrated in previous studies33,34,85,86, snowfall and rainfall fractions are intrinsically governed by temperature33. Consequently, flood timing variations driven by altered snow dynamics can be effectively captured through temperature indices. The onset of the first 7‑day period with air temperatures above 0 °C (T7) is an established proxy for flood timing changes induced by snowmelt and the snowfall‑to‑rainfall transition16. Accordingly, the T7 indicator was adopted in this study to represent these shifts. Building on these foundations, we adopt P, P7, and T7 to collectively explain regional divergence in peak flood timing changes, as confirmed in our results.

Obtaining reliable daily-scale projections of future snowpack volume and catchment wetness from global hydrological models currently remains a significant challenge. This is a recognized, community-wide limitation; for instance, the state-of-the-art global hydrological models within the ISIMIP framework provide these specific state variables primarily at a monthly resolution. Because our projected flood timing shifts range from −30 to 28 days across various global warming levels, relying on monthly-scale state data is fundamentally too coarse to effectively resolve or attribute these specific daily-scale timing changes. Nevertheless, relying on precipitation and temperature indices to explain long-term shifts in flood timing is both physically sound and methodologically justified. As demonstrated by Zaffaroni et al.40, global flood timing changes are primarily climate-controlled and can be explained by shifts in the timing of precipitation and the onset of above-zero temperatures. Furthermore, a recent study by Jorgenson et al.87 also indicates that climate is the dominant driver of high-flow seasonality in northern North America. Time lags between these meteorological triggers and the resulting flood peaks are primarily influenced by the time required for the concentration and accumulation of precipitation or above-zero temperature effects40. Catchment states such as snowpack volume and soil wetness act as passive modulators that influence the duration of this lag. Precipitation and above-zero temperatures, in contrast, serve as the active triggers that dictate the occurrence of the flood peak itself. Because our study investigates the decadal trends of flood timing rather than event-specific lag dynamics, attributing these shifts directly to the timing of atmospheric triggers is appropriate40. A detailed, quantitative attribution of the lag dynamics themselves remains an important avenue for future research, which can be pursued once global hydrological models are capable of providing reliable, daily-scale simulations of catchment wetness or snowpack volume.

The GCM data underwent bias correction using multiple reference datasets, including WFDEI-GPCC (combining WFDEI data with GPCCv5 and v6 precipitation totals for adjustment), CRU TS3.1 (for wet day frequency), and ERA-Interim (for rainfall/precipitation ratios). Details of these reference datasets and the specific correction methods are provided in the foundational ISIMIP studies88–90 and the technical documentation available via the ISIMIP project portal (https://www.isimip.org/gettingstarted/isimip2b-bias-correction/). These datasets and associated high-flow simulations have been widely utilized in recent literature5,32,76,91,92. Specifically, global hydrological model simulations using these bias-corrected data have underpinned research across diverse domains, including drought, flood, heat waves, agriculture, heat-related mortality, water resources, and hydropower93–98. Collectively, these precedents validate the suitability of the datasets employed.

The GCM simulations of the timing and seasonality of P and P7 are evaluated against the GSWP3-W5E5 global dataset99 (Fig. S10). GSWP3-W5E5 serves as the standard baseline for the open global glacier model and impact assessments in phase 3a of ISIMIP100,101. In general, the spatial distributions of timing and seasonality simulated by GCMs align closely with the dataset. Mean simulated timing for P and P7 is 189.40 ± 1.84 days and 187.34 ± 2.09 days, respectively, closely matching the GSWP3-W5E5 values of 187.53 and 184.58 days (Fig. S10a–d). Similarly, mean simulated seasonality is 6.69 ± 0.06 months for P and 6.63 ± 0.07 months for P7, consistent with the GSWP3-W5E5 estimates of 6.63 and 6.53 months (Fig. S10e–h). This agreement in timing and seasonality strengthens confidence in the GCM-based simulations.

Our study employs a global spatial resolution of 0.5 degrees. While small river basins may not be fully represented at this grid scale, the use of 0.5-degree resolution is common practice in global studies5,32,91,102,103. Consequently, the spatial resolution used herein is appropriate for global-scale analysis, although future research focusing on regional small rivers remains necessary.

Timing calculation

Flood timing was quantified as the day of the year (1–366) of annual maximum discharge, enabling direct global comparison across diverse geographical basins and climatic regimes. Flood timing was extracted by first locating the annual maximum discharge104–106, and then determining the corresponding day of the year on which that maximum occurred. For each grid cell, timing values were averaged over 30-year windows for historical (1976–2005) and future warming scenarios. The 30-year mean flood timing (Fig. S1) ranges from day 41 to 289 across all scenarios. Flood timing shifts relative to the mean range from −30 to 28 days across global warming levels. Historically flood timing extremes, when adjusted for these projected shifts, remain within the 1 to 366-day range; consequently, flood timing remains confined to a single calendar year (as shown in Fig. S1).

Timing anomalies (ΔT) were calculated as the difference between scenario-specific and historical baseline averages, where negative values (ΔT < 0) indicate flood timing advances and positive values (ΔT > 0) denote delays. Temporal trends in flood timing across warming levels (1.5 °C to 4.0 °C at 0.5 °C increments) were assessed via linear regression based on least squares regression, with slopes representing the rate of change per 0.5 °C warming. The statistical significance of trends was evaluated using the non-parametric Mann–Kendall test at p < 0.05.

Evaluations

The discharge seasonality of ISIMIP2 models has been evaluated across 840 catchments globally107, showing that interannual variability is well represented. Additionally, seasonality has been evaluated in six large Pan-Arctic watersheds and 57 large river basins globally, revealing strong agreement with observed seasonal dynamics108,109. In Europe, evaluations across 426 catchments demonstrate effective capture of the interannual variability of low, mean, and high flows110. Models also reasonably reproduce the seasonal dynamics of high flows in 12 large-scale river basins worldwide111. Furthermore, water storage serves as a robust indicator of flood dynamics10,112; comparisons of ISIMIP2 water storage simulations against Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) observations show generally consistent seasonal patterns97. Building on previous studies102,113, we included 377 gauges with at least 20 years of daily data spanning 1976–2005 to evaluate model performance (Fig. S11). Mean observed flood timing was 155.23 ± 40.58 days, compared to the simulated 153.61 ± 36.96 days; similarly, the mean observed flood month was 5.57 ± 1.35, versus the simulated 5.53 ± 1.24 months. The mean observed flood timing trend was −0.20 ± 1.08 days/year, aligning with the simulated trend of −0.24 ± 0.58 days/year. These results demonstrate the models’ robust capability to reproduce observed patterns.

Robust performance of hydrological models during the historical period enhances confidence in projected future climate impacts and helps constrain projection uncertainty. This approach aligns with methodological conventions in flood risk studies91,114–116. Therefore, the methodology used in this study is acceptable.

Definitions

In this study, early-flood regions and late-flood regions denote regions exhibiting great deviations in flood timing relative to the global mean (Fig. 1b). For example, the most evident early-flood regions correspond to latitudinal bands between 4.25°S and 24.25°S, where peak discharge occurs approximately two months before the global average (green dashed boxes, Fig. 1b); the most evident late-flood regions occur between 10.75°N and 25.25°N, with peak discharge timing delayed by roughly two months relative to the global mean (purple dashed boxes, Fig. 1b).

Severity of flood timing changes was categorized as follows: Moderate (7 ≤ ΔT < 14 days), Severe (14 ≤ ΔT < 21 days), and Exceptional (ΔT ≥ 21 days). Current societal vulnerability to flood hazards typically relies on flood return periods derived from flood frequency and magnitude96,117; however, vulnerability to flood timing shifts remains poorly understood. Given this limitation, we employed a 7-day threshold (a common temporal unit) to classify severity, noting that the developed methodology is adaptable to alternative thresholds.
