ADMET, QSAR and Docking studies to predict the activity of tyrosinase-derived medications inhibitors based on computational techniques
Authors: Adele Sadat Haghighat Hoseini, Abed Mohebshahedin, Shamim Ghiabi, Mahdi KhalatbariLimaki, Mahshid Arastonejad, Sorour Hassani, Mahsa Alem, Esmaeil Roohparvar Basmenj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tyrosinase enzyme plays a pivotal role in melanin pigment production; however, heightened tyrosinase activity can lead to undesired pigmentation. Consequently, inhibiting this enzyme's function stands as a critical research avenue for devising effective strategies to mitigate pigmentation issues. This study aimed to forecast the biological activity of chemical compounds capable of inhibiting tyrosinase and elucidate pivotal elements influencing this enzyme's activity. To achieve this goal, we employed computational techniques to construct a model predicting the biological activity of these compounds. Initially, we identified 27 tyrosinase inhibitors from previous studies. Subsequently, after performing ADMET studies, we extracted and pre-processed the significant features of each compound to develop a Stepwise-MLR model. Moreover, with the help of this model, we were able to identify the most influential and novel structural features that directly affect enzyme activity and determine the importance factor of each feature. Furthermore, all derived inhibitors with evaluated inhibition constants were docked to the active site of target tyrosinase to investigate the binding mode of the compounds. Docking analysis indicated T1 as the most stable compound with a binding energy of −8.00 kcal/mol. T1 as the most active compound identified through these computational studies can be applied as a prospective tyrosinase inhibitor. The implications of our findings extend to the development of new therapies for pigmentation disorders, notably within the cosmetic and dermatological sectors.Keywords: ADMET, Qsar, Molecular docking, Computational drug design, tyrosinase

Introduction

Tyrosinase, also known as polyphenol oxidase (EC 1.14.18.1), is an enzyme containing copper that is found in various organisms, including plants, animals, and humans (Chase et al., 2000) The enzyme's essential function is in the production of skin melanin (Solano et al., 2000). The accumulation of skin melanin beyond the normal level can lead to the development of melanoma, a type of skin cancer that poses significant cosmetic and health concerns for humans (Chompoo et al., 2012). Tyrosinase may play a role in the development of melanoma (Pan et al., 2011), as well as in the onset of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's disease (Zhu et al., 2011). Tyrosinase in humans is a glycoprotein located on the cell membrane and comprises approximately 13 % carbohydrates. The enzyme's active site contains two copper atoms that interact with dioxygen to create a reactive intermediate, which oxidizes the substrate (Durai et al., 2021). Inhibiting the biological activities of tyrosinase is a viable approach to reducing skin melanin formation, thereby preventing the onset of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's disease (Durai et al., 2021). Given the growing concern for health issues, the discovery of effective, stable, and novel tyrosinase inhibitors with lower side effects is imperative.

Computational approaches have become increasingly important in designing inhibitors and developing drugs, offering a promising solution to this challenge. Among these methods, ADMET, quantitative structure activity relationship (QSAR) pharmacophore modeling and molecular docking are widely employed in drug design due to their effectiveness (Tandon et al., 2019). Bioinformatics techniques have been employed in studies centering on the tyrosinase enzyme. For instance, in Hongwei Gao's study (Gao, 2018), tyrosinase inhibition was predicted using 3D QSAR pharmacophore models, and potential tyrosinase inhibitors were designed from a Traditional Chinese Medicine database. However, Hongwei Gao's focus in that study was solely on the Traditional Chinese Medicine database. Similarly, Xiaofang Li and colleagues (Li et al., 2022) constructed a QSAR model based on cysteine-containing dipeptides and screened natural tyrosinase inhibitors; again, in Xiaofang Li's study, the focus was exclusively on cysteine-containing dipeptides.

In addition to previous studies, our research aims to examine the pharmacokinetic profiles of available ligands and identify key factors influencing the biological activity of tyrosinase inhibitors. We also intend to develop a predictive model for this activity. Furthermore, by employing molecular docking, we seek to gain structural insights into compounds that can inhibit tyrosinase, ultimately aiming to design new chemical inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Data set

In our study, we obtained a set of 27 chemical compounds that effectively inhibit tyrosinase from the work of Gao et al. (Gao, 2018). The inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of the compounds were subsequently converted to corresponding pIC50 values, which are represented as the negative logarithm of the IC50 values. Initially, the 27 medicinal compounds mentioned in Gao et al.'s article were available in a 2D format, to improve the accuracy of the representation, we employed Hypercam software 8.0.3 (Laxmi and Priyadarshy, 2002) to produce the compounds in a 3D format. The optimization of the geometry of these compounds aimed to identify their most stable and energetically favorable configuration, which could have an impact on their biological activity and pharmacological properties. This optimization process was carried out with the goal of enhancing the potency, selectivity, and efficacy of drug compounds in drug design and development. All 27 compounds were subject to geometry optimization using the Polak-Ribiere algorithm (conjugate gradient) with an RMS gradient of 0.1 Kca.(A mol) or 255 maximum cycles, and a screen refresh period of 1 cycle. The optimized structures of these compounds can be found in Table S1 of the supplementary file.

ADMET studies

Pharmacology is closely related to Lipinski's rules, which help us determine which compounds are suitable for advancing the project. By comparing the pharmacological profiles of two ligands, we can gain valuable structural information about them and understand how structural changes in existing inhibitors may affect their pharmacological properties. In this phase, we conducted these studies on the obtained and available ligands using the SwissADME server (Daina et al., 2017). First, with the help of OpenBabel software (O'Boyle et al., 2011), the HIN format of the compounds was converted to SMILES, and the server was run with default settings on all available ligands.

Molecular descriptors

Several software tools have been developed for calculating molecular descriptors, with Dragon (MauriA et al., 2006) being one of the most effective programs in this field. In this step, by inputting the structures of all chemical compounds into Dragon 5.5 with default parameters, we obtained valuable features that aid in data exploration and analysis.

Data preprocessing

Missing information

That missing data refers to any absent, incomplete, or unrecorded values in a dataset, which can distort analyses and lead to unreliable or biased results. missing data can lead to unsatisfactory outcomes, so it is important to address such issues in a timely and efficient manner. In this section, we aimed to identify any incompatible data and resolve these issues as effectively as possible to achieve the desired results in our data exploration. Dragon software, with default parameters, was utilized to preprocess our data. We used Dragon's built-in feature to disregard missing data, removing all instances of missing data during our research.

Correlation coefficient

The correlation coefficient is a frequently used metric for evaluating the correlation between two variables, as well as pairwise correlations between chemical compound properties. In our data matrix, we identified columns with a correlation coefficient greater than 90 % and removed one of them to avoid multicollinearity issues. This step was taken to ensure the credibility and trustworthiness of subsequent analyses and interpretations.

Feature selection and model build

In our research, we utilized the Stepwise-MLR model. This method initially incorporates all independent variables into the model and subsequently eliminates features with minimal impact on the dependent variable. Furthermore, multiple linear regression (MLR), a commonly used statistical technique, employs multiple explanatory variables to predict the outcome of a response variable. The primary objective of MLR is to establish a model that captures the linear relationship between independent and dependent variables.

Leave one out cross validation

At this stage, we evaluated the algorithm's ability to respond to different inputs. This evaluation aims to assess how generalizable and independent the results and the algorithm are from the training data. For this purpose, we selected the first ligand as test data, then used the remaining samples (n-1 ligands) as training data. We trained the model using the training data and then evaluated the RMSE (Root Mean Square Error) between the actual activity and the predicted activity of the test ligand. We repeated these steps for each ligand, leaving out one ligand as test data each time and training the model with the remaining ligands. After each step, we assessed the model's ability to predict the activity of the left-out ligand. Finally, we calculated the average RMSE by summing the RMSE values from all steps and dividing by the number of ligands.

Molecular docking

Protein and ligand preparation

The tyrosinase from Agaricus bisporus was selected as a target protein for investigation of docking study. The 3D structure of tyrosinase in complex with tropolone (PDB ID: 2Y9X) with 2.78 A0 resolution was retrieved from the protein databank (Fig. 1) (Ismaya et al., 2011). All required input files for the docking process were prepared with MGLTools 1.5.7 software (Morris GM et al., 2009). The crystallized water molecules and tropolone were removed from the PDB file. After this modification step, the Ramachandran plot of the protein structure for the validity of its structure was checked through the SAVESv6.0 (PROCHECK) server (Laskowski et al., 1993).

Ribbon representations of Mushroom tyrosinase (2Y9X) with bound tropolone. The inhibitor binds in an extended active site and is displayed in magenta and atom-type colors.

Docking study

To find the optimal conformation and orientation of ligands when they interact with the receptor, the molecular docking simulation was applied. We utilized Autodock Vina for docking (Trott and Olson, 2010). In this method, the ligands were set flexible during the docking stage whereas the protein receptor remained rigid. The modified protein and the optimized ligand structures were loaded to MGLTools, polar hydrogen was added to the receptor, and the Kollman charges and the Gasteiger one were assigned to the receptor and ligands, respectively. Vina runs were done based on the atomic coordination of tropolone in the crystal structure of 2Y9X with the center coordinate of (X = −0.47, Y = 26.42, Z = −44.98 A°), the volume of 43.008 A°3 as well as the box dimensions (A) X = 40, Y = 40, Z = 40 A°). Furthermore, the docking results were evaluated and visualized by Discovery Studio Visualizer was applied (Biovia et al., 2019).

Criteria for selecting each feature in the model

In total, 1591 descriptors were generated for each chemical compound using the Dragon software, resulting in a 27∗1591 matrix. Each row of the matrix represented a chemical compound, and each column represented a specific descriptor (independent variable or X matrix of the problem). Furthermore, the concentration of each chemical compound required to inhibit 50 % of the tyrosinase enzyme's activity was known beforehand and recorded in a Y column (dependent variable). The decision to include each feature in the model is based on various statistical criteria, with our study prioritizing RMSE and R-Squared. Additional statistical terms for model validation

R-Squared

This is a statistical metric in a regression model that measures the goodness of fit of the data to the regression model. In our investigation, we assessed the activity of the chemical compound and found that the value for our model is 0.92, which is considered a satisfactory evaluation for the desired model.

F-value

In a regression model, the F statistic is the ratio of the variance explained by the model to the variance that is not explained by the model (residual variance). The F-test assesses the overall significance of the model and determines whether the model with independent variables provides a better fit than the model without them. In our study, we found that the F statistic for our model was 27.04 after evaluating the activity of the chemical compound.

P-value

The p-values in a regression analysis help to determine whether the observed relationships between variables in a sample also apply to the larger population. Specifically, the p-value for each independent variable in a linear regression model tests the null hypothesis that the variable is not correlated with the dependent variable. A commonly used threshold for statistical significance is p < 0.05, meaning that the observed data would occur less than 5 % of the time under the null hypothesis. If the p-value is below the chosen alpha level, the result is considered statistically significant. Upon evaluating the activity of the chemical compound, we found that our model's p-value was 1.64953e-08, indicating statistical significance.

RMSE

The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is a crucial performance indicator for a regression model, measuring the average deviation between predicted values and actual values. It provides an estimate of the accuracy of the model in predicting the target value. RMSE values between 0.2 and 0.5 are generally considered acceptable, indicating that the model can relatively predict the data with accuracy. After evaluating the activity of the chemical compound, our analysis revealed that our model's RMSE is 0.179.

Discussion

Our goal in this research was to conduct computational studies on tyrosinase enzyme inhibitors to uncover new insights about these compounds. This information is crucial for designing novel inhibitors against tyrosinase and clarifies the path for further research. To achieve this, we first identified and evaluated 27 potential inhibitors of the enzyme. The pharmacokinetic profiles of these compounds were analyzed based on Lipinski's rules (Benet et al., 2016) (Lipinski et al., 1997), which guide us in determining viable drug candidates. In order to this rules, the compounds identified as potential main inhibitors should possess the following characteristics:

The molecular weight (MW) of the compounds should be less than 500 Da to enhance their drug-likeness. Additionally, the log of the octanol/water partition coefficient (Log P) should be less than 5 to prevent the drug from preferentially dissolving in fat, thereby facilitating absorption through cell membranes. The number of hydrogen bond donors (nHBD) must be fewer than 5, while the number of hydrogen bond acceptors (nHBA) should be less than 10. A total polar surface area (TPSA) of generally less than 140 Å2 is preferred, as larger values may decrease the likelihood of cell membrane penetration. The molar refractivity (MR) typically falls within the range of 40–130, reflecting the size and solubility capacity of the drug molecule. For skin permeability, a log Kp value ideally below −2.5 indicates better drug penetration through the skin. The bioavailability score (BS) is expressed as a percentage, with an optimal value above 0.5, ensuring good bioavailability in the body. It is also preferable for the number of rotatable bonds (nRotB) to be less than 10, as an increase in these bonds enhances molecular flexibility, which may affect the ability to penetrate cell membranes. Lastly, synthetic accessibility (SA) is typically rated on a scale of 1–10, where lower scores indicate easier synthesis, and a score below 5 suggests good synthetic accessibility.

Through our pharmacokinetic profile analysis, we found that most compounds fell within acceptable ranges. Notably, compounds that did not meet certain criteria may still provide valuable insights for subsequent stages of our research. By examining the structures of these existing compounds, we can uncover interesting computational facts. If we encounter various compounds under different conditions, we can more easily identify these insights.

In the next step, to investigate the key factors important in determining the activity of available chemical compounds that can inhibit the tyrosinase enzyme, we used QSAR techniques. After preparing the data, we conducted a Stepwise-MLR model and found the main factors and descriptors that are crucial to the activity of the compounds. Our findings indicate that the absolute values of the coefficients for the features 'Mor11m′, 'RDF110v′, and 'E1m′ are notably higher compared to those of other features. This suggests that these features play a significant role in determining the activity of tyrosinase inhibitors. In practical terms, variations in these features are likely to induce more substantial changes in the activity of tyrosinase inhibitors compared to variations in other features. Conversely, the absolute value of the coefficient for the feature 'H0m′ is lower than that of other features, indicating its relatively lesser importance in predicting the activity of tyrosinase inhibitors. This implies that changes in 'H0m′ will result in smaller variations in the activity of the inhibitors compared to changes in other features. Additionally, our results reveal that the features 'Mor11m′, 'RDF110v′, 'Mor11u′, and 'H0m′ have negative coefficients, whereas the features 'Mor24m′, 'E1m′, 'DISPe', and 'RDF105m′ exhibit positive coefficients. This indicates that an increase in features with positive coefficients leads to an increase in the activity of the target inhibitor, while an increase in features with negative coefficients results in a decrease in the activity of the target inhibitor. Furthermore, we observed that the coefficients for 'Mor11m′ and 'E1m′ are similar but opposite in sign, suggesting an inverse relationship between these features. For example, a one-unit increase in 'Mor11m′ results in a decrease in IC50 by approximately 1.066, while a one-unit increase in 'E1m′ corresponds to an increase in IC50 by approximately 1.042. This implies that an increase in 'Mor11m′ by one unit is associated with a decrease in 'E1m′ by one unit, reflecting their inverse relationship regarding their impact on the activity of the chemical compounds.

In the next step, to gain structural insights into the ligand-receptor complex, we conducted docking analysis. Tyrosinase has an H2L2 tetramer structure, with the active site containing binuclear copper located at the bottom of an expansive cavity on the surface of the H subunit. The studies show that this cavity is positioned somewhere that it is easily accessible by the solvent, and not blocked by the L subunit or by loops of side chains of the H subunit (Ismaya et al., 2011). Furthermore, the L chain of tyrosinase is a non-immunogenic protein with structural homology close to the lectin family with no specific biological activity so far revealed for this unit. Unit H demonstrates its full activation in the absence of unit L (Ismaya et al., 2017) (Matoba et al., 2006). For validation, a group of researchers computationally examined this issue and the results of a blind docking on the entire surface of the LH dimmer showed that the substrate occupied a position similar to what was witnessed in the absence of an L unit (Hassani et al., 2018). The binding energy value, the distances of the T1 functional groups to the copper ions, and the lengths of the hydrogen bonds indicate a strong interaction of T1 with the active site pocket, as compared with other inhibitors. This can explain why T1 obtained a much lower value of IC50 (1.67 μM) than that of others. As seen in Fig. 5A, T1 is located in the active site where the hydroxyl functional group is toward CUA in a short distance of about 3.2A°. This ligand orientation justifies the strong binding of T1 to 2Y9X and consistently low IC50. Based on Fig. 5A and 5B, we can conclude that the monophenol rings play no roles in the H-bonds of this ligand vis-versa of the resorcinol phenyl ring. Remarkably, the spatial orientation of the phenolic moiety of T1 is almost in the same direction as the aromatic ring of L-tyrosine experienced in the active site (Hassani et al., 2018). The crystal structure by Ismaya, W.T. revealed that His244, Glu256, and Ala286 are all conserved in PPO1, PPO2, PPO3, and PPO4 (Ismaya et al., 2011) interestingly T1 witnessed interactions with the mentioned residues for location in the active site according to our docking results. Indeed, it turned out from accurate docking experiments for 27 compounds shown in Table S3 that functional groups of most inhibitors orientated toward the CU ions to facilitate the enzyme reaction mechanism.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small dataset of 27 compounds, which was restricted to inhibitors of the same structural class; future studies should expand this dataset to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the predictive models. This study is computational in nature and was designed to provide preliminary insights and predictive models that may guide future experimental work. While the results offer mechanistic hypotheses and useful prioritization of potential inhibitors, they require confirmation through experimental validation. In vitro assays (e.g., enzyme inhibition and cellular activity) and in vivo studies will be crucial to verify the accuracy and biological relevance of our predictions. We therefore highlight that such experimental validation represents an important future direction to strengthen and extend the findings of this work.

Conclusion

In this study, our goal was to utilize computational techniques to uncover new information about the relationship between the activity and structure of chemical compounds that can inhibit the tyrosinase enzyme. We identified 27 tyrosinase inhibitors and optimized their three-dimensional structures. After checking their pharmacokinetic profiles, we extracted effective features for each compound and pre-processed the data. By using the Stepwise-MLR method, we developed a model that could predict the biological activity of chemical compounds acting as tyrosinase enzyme inhibitors. We assessed the model's performance and found it to have high quality in silico. This research presents a possible strategy for slowing down pigmentation and discovering new tyrosinase inhibitors. Additionally, utilizing computational approaches in inhibitor design and drug development is a promising area for future investigation. In silico molecular docking of tyrosinase inhibitors through AutoDock Vina, one of the most accurate docking software reveals that the orientation of phenolic moiety of T1 seen in our model is almost the same as those of co-crystallized phenolic ligands in other tyrosinases such as tyrosine and p-tyrosol in Bacillus tyrosinase (PDB entries 4p6r, 4p6t (Goldfeder et al., 2014)). Furthermore, the distance of 3.2 Å and 3.5 Å between the hydroxyl group of T1 and the copper ions in 2Y9X enhancements the possibility of the presence of metal coordination and salt bridges between the CUs and the hydroxyl group.
