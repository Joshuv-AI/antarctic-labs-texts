Circulating Cardiovascular Biomarkers in Cancer Therapeutics‐Related Cardiotoxicity: Review of Critical Challenges, Solutions, and Future Directions
Authors: Gillian Murtagh, James L Januzzi, Marielle Scherrer‐Crosbie, Tomas G Neilan, Susan Dent, Jennifer E Ho, Vinesh Appadurai, Ray McDermott, Nausheen Akhter
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiotoxicity is a growing concern in the oncology population. Transthoracic echocardiography and multigated acquisition scans have been used for surveillance but are relatively insensitive and resource intensive. Innovative imaging techniques are constrained by cost and availability. More sensitive, cost‐effective cardiotoxicity surveillance strategies are needed. Circulating cardiovascular biomarkers could provide a sensitive, low‐cost solution. Biomarkers such as troponins, natriuretic peptides (NPs), novel upstream signals of oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis as well as panomic technologies have shown substantial promise, and guidelines recommend baseline measurement of troponins and NPs in all patients receiving potential cardiotoxins. Nonetheless, supporting evidence has been hampered by several limitations. Previous reviews have provided valuable perspectives on biomarkers in cancer populations, but important analytic aspects remain to be examined in depth. This review provides comprehensive assessment of critical challenges and solutions in this field, with focus on analytical issues relating to biomarker measurement and interpretation. Examination of evidence pertaining to common and serious forms of cardiotoxicity reveals that improved study designs incorporating larger, more diverse populations, registry‐based approaches, and refinement of current definitions are key. Further efforts to harmonize biomarker methodologies including centralized biobanking and analyses, novel decision limits, and head‐to‐head comparisons are needed. Multimarker algorithms incorporating machine learning may allow rapid, personalized risk assessment. These improvements will not only augment the predictive value of circulating biomarkers in cardiotoxicity but may elucidate both direct and indirect relationships between cardiovascular disease and cancer, allowing biomarkers a greater role in the development and success of novel anticancer therapies.Keywords: biomarkers, cardiotoxicitySubject Categories: Cardio-Oncology, Cardiotoxicity, Cardiomyopathy, Heart Failure, Biomarkers

Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in survivors of cancer. 1 , 2 Individuals undergoing treatment for cancer may experience any of several cancer therapeutics‐related cardiotoxic effects, including (but not limited to) heart failure, myocarditis, myocardial ischemia and infarction, valvular heart disease, arrhythmia, hypertension, and thromboembolism. 1 The most commonly reported is left ventricular systolic dysfunction (cancer therapeutics‐related cardiac dysfunction [CTRCD]), principally related to anthracyclines or trastuzumab. Up to 40% of US patients with breast cancer experience reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) related to their treatment, but symptomatic heart failure (HF) is rare. 1 , 3 Other forms of toxicity such as myocarditis related to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are uncommonly reported (0.1%–1.4%) but are associated with high rates of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality (25%–50%). 4 Over a third of US patients with cancer could soon be eligible for ICIs, 5 including those with triple negative breast cancer. 6 Preventative and therapeutic strategies to avoid withholding of cancer therapy are available including treatment of underlying risk factors, dose reduction, modification of delivery (eg, liposomal doxorubicin) and initiation of cardioprotective agents (eg, beta blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors). 7

Accurate and cost‐effective cardiotoxicity surveillance strategies are therefore needed. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and multigated acquisition scans are traditionally used for this purpose, but by the time functional abnormalities are noted using these modalities, substantial myocardial damage may have occurred. Furthermore, neither TTE nor multigated acquisition scans have been shown to improve outcomes or be cost effective in low‐risk populations. 8 , 9 Novel imaging techniques such as left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS), cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and 3‐dimensional TTE provide sensitive, accurate functional assessment and pathological insights. A comprehensive baseline TTE with 3‐dimensional LVEF and GLS, where available, is recommended by most guidelines. 7 , 10 , 11 Monitoring strategies vary depending on the risk for cardiotoxicity, cancer therapy type, and dose. Higher risk patients require closer surveillance during and after treatment. Monitoring strategies may also differ in resource‐poor regions, where the availability of more complex investigations is limited. Globally, novel imaging techniques are frequently limited by cost and availability, requiring specialized equipment and training. Another challenge is that definitions for many types of toxicity, such as CTRCD and myocarditis, are not universally accepted (Tables S1 and S2) and formal criteria for adjudication in clinical trials are lacking. 8 Controversy persists over the degree of LVEF impairment that qualifies as CTRCD. A decline of ≥10% to ≤50%–55% is frequently included 1 , 9 , 12 (conventional definitions) but individual criteria vary. The impact of these issues on the interpretation of pivotal studies is discussed later.

Circulating biomarkers have the potential to provide more sensitive, personalized, and cost‐effective surveillance in those undergoing antineoplastic therapy. Tns (troponins) and natriuretic peptides (NPs) are reflective of myocardial injury and stress. Low cost and widely available, Tns and NPs have demonstrated predictive capability for multiple forms of cardiotoxicity including CTRCD and myocarditis. 13 Novel upstream signals of oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis as well as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics may provide complementary mechanistic insights and prognostic capability. The field of cardio‐oncology has experienced rapid growth, 14 and presently, over 200 publications involving cardiotoxicity and biomarkers are published per year. 15 Based on available evidence, several recommendations have been published, and recent guidelines recommend Tns and NPs at baseline in all patients treated with potentially cardiotoxic agents 7 (Table S3).

Nevertheless, the routine use of biomarkers for this purpose is not clearly supported by current evidence, 16 and it has been stated that the guidelines have overreached. 14 , 17 This raises questions as to the limitations of prior studies. Previous reviews and recommendations have provided valuable perspectives and highlighted opportunities to optimize certain aspects of study design. 16 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Importantly, though challenges and solutions pertaining to use of circulating biomarkers in cardio‐oncology were mentioned in these publications, analytic aspects were not the primary focus and remain to be evaluated in depth. Addressing these concerns would aid development of a rubric for future circulating biomarker studies. Accordingly, in this review we present an in‐depth assessment of critical challenges, present solutions, and provide future directions for circulating biomarkers in cardiotoxicity.

Circulating Cardiovascular Biomarkers: Critical Challenges

First, clinical and analytical features of each biomarker class and their utility in pivotal publications will be examined in relation to common and serious forms of cardiotoxicity (anthracycline or trastuzumab‐related CTRCD and ICI‐related myocarditis). Then critical challenges of each biomarker class will be summarized. For the purposes of this review, the term biomarker is used to refer to circulating biomarker, and the terms cut point, cutoff, and threshold are used interchangeably.

Methods

Publications pertaining to circulating cardiovascular biomarkers and cancer therapeutics‐related cardiotoxicity that were cited in recent guidelines, consensus, position statements from professional associations, and scoping reviews were assessed. 7 , 11 , 19 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Twenty‐six studies were selected by the authors for in‐depth review, representing a range of study designs and settings. Studies involving larger populations with multiple commercially available biomarker subtype analyses were evaluated to ensure representation of multiple analyte subtypes (eg, Tn, NP subtypes) where applicable. Publications involving smaller populations and fewer biomarker classes in varying settings and geographic regions were then added to ensure a broad representation of potential challenges was evaluated. Finally, 1 more recent pivotal study was assessed. 30 Of note, this review comprises a focused evaluation of analytical methods employed in selected biomarker studies within the cancer population, allowing in‐depth appraisal of recurrent issues.

Troponins

Troponin is a protein central to the myofibrillar apparatus with 3 isoforms (I, T, C). TnI and TnT are cardiac specific. Elevations of Tn exceeding the 99th percentile of a healthy population define myocardial injury. 17 High‐sensitivity (hs)‐Tn assays demonstrate a coefficient of variation at the 99th percentile <10% and measurable concentrations in >50% of healthy populations. 20 These assays are 7‐ to 10‐fold more sensitive than previous generations, which may allow earlier detection of myocardial injury. Owing to greater sensitivity at low concentrations, some hsTnI assays may be more strongly associated with cardiovascular outcomes than hsTnT 21 but robust comparative analyses between hsTn assays in cardiotoxicity are lacking.

However, hsTn assays are not standardized, varying by capture and detection epitopes, that is, the part of the troponin molecule to which the antibody attaches. 20 Therefore, interassay results are not comparable, nor can the 99th percentile act as a consistent harmonization factor as populations and methods used to derive the 99th percentiles differ. This has not been widely acknowledged. 7 , 16 , 21 This heterogeneity of assay and reference range study design results in considerable variability in reference ranges across hsTn assays, 31 which can affect patient classification. For example, when 3 hsTn assays (hsTnT and 2 hsTnI assays with overall 99th percentiles of 19, 28, and 59 ng/L respectively) were recently assessed in suspected myocardial infarction, only 37.4% of samples were classified into the same analytic benchmark category. 32 In the absence of a true reliable standard for comparison of categorization between hsTn assays, prognostic data act as a frame of reference. Biological variation is another important consideration. Troponins have a low index of individuality when measured using hs methods (≤0.3), reflective of analytical, within‐subject, and between‐subject coefficients of variation. 62 , 63 , 64 This indicates good correlation with physiological characteristics such as age, sex, and body mass, such that changes over time that are biologically significant for a patient may not exceed reference thresholds for a population. Additionally, studies designed to evaluate reference change values for both hsTnI and hsTnT assays have demonstrated an reference change value of approximately 32% (95% probability, minimum 26%, maximum 45%, range 5–40 ng/L) and overall similar imprecision. 64 Within‐subject interval changes (delta values) exceeding this range may thus be preferred to absolute cutoffs, emphasizing the importance of procurement of baseline/pretreatment biomarker values as well as analyses incorporating novel decision limits (eg, different high‐ and low‐risk thresholds, delta values, and values derived using machine learning techniques).

With regard to published literature, early studies demonstrated that TnI predicted CTRCD after high‐dose chemotherapy (including anthracyclines) for aggressive malignancies using high Tn thresholds (eg, 500 ng/L) and older assay methodologies. 34 , 35 Studies involving women treated with trastuzumab showed that TnI >80 ng/L conferred prognostic value, 36 but the majority of patients in this study had metastatic disease. Elevations of this magnitude are less frequent in early‐stage cancer, when cardiotoxicity leading to treatment interruptions may be of greater relative concern. Notably, the race or ethnicity of the population involved was not reported, an issue that frequently affects biomarker studies in this field (Table S4). In the absence of detailed information, it may be assumed that the lack of diversity noted in clinical trials also affects these predominantly European and US studies. 65

More recently, research involving hsTn assays has reawakened interest in circulating biomarkers for prediction of cardiotoxicity. 64 , 66 A panel of biomarkers including hsTnI was assessed in 78 patients with early‐stage HER2+ breast cancer in 1 study; changes in TnI and myeloperoxidase (MPO) predicted CTRCD (Cardiac Review and Evaluation Committee definition; 26 cases). 41 When delta values of both MPO and TnI were elevated (>90th percentile) by 15 months, the probability of cardiotoxicity approached ~50%. MPO was robust to alternative CTRCD definitions, but no biomarker predicted a decline of ≥10% in LVEF to ≤50% LVEF. A precommercial hsTnI assay was used, and MPO assays currently require that samples are placed on ice immediately following phlebotomy. 67 Though the sample selected was small, this study involved conventional definitions and exploration of novel decision limits.

Pivotal publications have demonstrated that different high and low thresholds are more promising than a single cut point. This was illustrated in another study of 323 patients with breast cancer, wherein 100% negative predictive value to rule out CTRCD was achieved using TnT at baseline and at completion of anthracycline with a very low concentration (hsTnT <5 ng/L, approaching the limit of detection or lowest concentration of a substance that can be reliably detected for that assay). 47 A second, substantially higher rule‐in 99th percentile threshold (14 ng/L) conferred 2‐fold increased risk for CTRCD post‐anthracycline in this cohort. Conversely, in a substudy of the HERA (Herceptin Adjuvant) trial involving 452 patients (46 of whom developed significant LVEF reduction), 2 Tn assays (non‐hs TnI and hsTnT) were evaluated based only on their respective 99th percentile values (40 and 14 ng/L respectively). 43 Troponin elevations after completion of anthracyclines were associated with a 2.4‐ to 4.5‐fold risk for subsequent LVEF reduction. The authors noted that all conclusions regarding predictive value were constrained by low numbers of events, but generalizability was limited in any case by use of 2 different Tn assays and focus on the aforementioned single‐threshold approach. Other evaluations incorporated hsTn but reported subclinical LVEF changes with few CTRCD events by conventional definitions, limiting generalizable conclusions. 44 , 48

Though TTE and multigated acquisition scans are traditionally used to monitor for cardiotoxicity, novel imaging techniques have also been investigated in combination with circulating biomarkers, particularly Tns. A landmark study showed that TnI >15 ng/L and >10% reduction in GLS predicted CTRCD in 43 patients with breast cancer, 39 but <10 patients developed CTRCD. TnI measured using a research‐phase hs assay was then assessed in 81 similar patients, of whom CTRCD occurred in 26. 40 TnI was predictive using the 95th percentile; negative predictive value of 91% was reported combining GLS <19% or TnI >30 ng/L, but thresholds were derived and tested in the same cohort. The perceived performance of hsTnI may be underestimated when mild LVEF decreases are accepted as CTRCD, and 99th percentiles alone are used to define abnormality. 49 However, novel imaging techniques and hsTn assays may work synergistically. When hsTnI was used with GLS in a study of 52 anthracycline‐treated patients (5 cases of CTRCD), an area under the receiver operating characteristics curve of 0.94 was reported for baseline hsTnI >99th percentile. 46 Evaluation of novel decision limits in addition to the 99th percentile may allow improved risk stratification beyond baseline and increase transferability of these promising early findings. Regarding TnT, another notable publication reported the capability of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, strain and non‐hs TnT to predict subsequent CTRCD in 42 patients with breast cancer treated with trastuzumab. 37 In this study, CTRCD developed in 10 patients who demonstrated lateral late gadolinium enhancement and abnormal lateral systolic velocity, whereas biomarkers did not demonstrate predictive capability. These findings have not been replicated to date.

The value of Tns in ICI‐related myocarditis is an area in need of clarity. Tn is one of the main diagnostic criteria for this condition. 53 Myositis is generally thought to occur in 20% to 25% of patients with myocarditis. 13 Given that TnT cross‐reacts with skeletal muscle 61 the cardiac specificity of TnI may be preferred, if available, for prediction and diagnosis of ICI‐related myocarditis. 53 Heterogeneity of assays and thresholds and underreporting of assay specifications are frequent limitations in this field. Pharmacovigilance studies have reported almost universally elevated Tn in cases of ICI‐related myocarditis, but without assay and threshold details. 68 Troponin was elevated in 98/101 patients with ICI‐related myocarditis and correlated with lower GLS but not LVEF in 1 multicenter registry, 69 but Tn parameters were not provided. Surveillance of 214 ICI‐treated patients using hsTnI has been attempted but using the 99th percentile as the primary threshold; 3/24 with TnI >55 ng/L were diagnosed with myocarditis (positive predictive value 12.5%) whereas at 1000 and 2000 ng/L, the positive predictive value was 75% and 100% respectively. 52 The negative predictive value of low concentrations was not provided. With regard to TnT, TnT>upper reference limit was reported in >90% of patients receiving ICIs in 1 multicenter series in which 86% of sample testing involved non‐hs TnT, and values ≥1500 ng/L were associated with a 4‐fold elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events but prediction of myocarditis was not the focus. 4 Similarly, hsTnT ≥14 ng/L at baseline provided 100% sensitivity and 73% specificity for major adverse cardiovascular events in a study of 30 ICI‐treated patients, but myocarditis was not specifically reported. 50

A recent study further explored the prognostic capability of Tns in known ICI‐related myocarditis, 30 demonstrating that among 60 patients with ICI‐related myocarditis, TnT:upper reference limit (99th percentile) ≥32 was associated with 11 times the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events compared with values below that level. TnI was measured in one‐third of patients using 4 different assays. TnI and TnT assays were run on samples taken within 6 hours of each other, rather than consistently run on the same samples. External validation occurred using 87 cases, for which variable local upper reference limits were used, and assay details were not available. Nonetheless, this study raises important questions as to the role of Tns in systemic myotoxicity and associated adverse events.

In summary, TnI and TnT are cardiac‐specific biomarkers, the 99th percentile of which defines myocardial injury. hsTn assays may allow earlier detection of myocardial injury, but they are not standardized, and biological variability is an issue. Initial studies employed single high thresholds, but more recent publications have identified low potential thresholds (eg, values at or approaching the limit of detection) that may exclude CTRCD with high negative predictive value, as well as suggesting higher thresholds (eg, delta values, 99th percentile) that more effectively identify increased risk for cardiac dysfunction. Higher values in the thousands carry high positive predictive value for ICI‐related myocarditis. However, many studies have not investigated novel cut points, relying on single thresholds adopted from other settings, which may limit performance. Combining Tn with changes in GLS is particularly promising. Overall, Tns have demonstrated predictive and prognostic utility for common and serious forms of cardiotoxicity. Nonetheless, large sample sizes, diverse populations, clinically relevant definitions, highly sensitive assays, and novel decision limits are inconsistently used.

Natriuretic Peptides

BNP (B‐type NP), a bioactive cardiac neurohormone, and NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal proBNP), its inactive fragment, are central to the diagnosis of HF and improve outcomes when used for screening. 70 This makes them attractive for the prediction and diagnosis of cardiotoxicity. NPs vary significantly depending on factors such as atrial fibrillation, neprilysin inhibition, and obesity. Biological variation is again a notable consideration. In the case of NPs, there is high intra‐ and interindividual biological variability, with an index of individuality exceeding 0.6. This means that absolute reference values may be expected to play a more prominent role. 62 , 63 Additionally, when interpreting NPs, it is important to consider renal function, as concentrations of both peptides may increase in the presence of renal dysfunction. 71 BNP and NT‐proBNP results cannot be used interchangeably; as with Tns, no analytic frame of reference exists, rather diagnostic and prognostic data act as a frame of reference. Furthermore, as with Tn assays, BNP assays are not standardized. Overlap in studies involving NPs and Tn occurs as illustrated in Table, but head‐to‐head comparisons using novel decision limits are lacking. BNP and NT‐proBNP have demonstrated capability in risk stratification for several forms of cardiotoxicity, but evidence is conflicting, as demonstrated by the studies reviewed next.

Several studies have demonstrated increases in BNP among anthracycline‐treated patients 72 and persistently elevated NT‐proBNP after breast cancer treatment 38 , 42 but without correlation to cardiotoxicity end points. More promising signals are evident from studies evaluating the relationship between NPs and LVEF, 54 but statistical power is often limited by paucity of events related to the low doses of anthracyclines used to treat breast cancer as opposed to higher doses used for hematological malignancies. When clinical end points were studied in 333 patients receiving anthracyclines for hematological and other cancers over longer follow‐up periods, high BNP>100 pg/mL was 92% specific for prediction of HF hospitalization (21 patients). 55 Low BNP of 30 pg/mL was 81% sensitive for prediction of this outcome. Importantly, this study employed relatively large number of higher‐risk patients, clinically relevant end points, and novel decision limits. Ideally, external validation of the NP cut points selected would have been provided. 23 However, other studies have not demonstrated a consistent relationship between NPs and CTRCD. For example, the CARDIOTOX (Cardiovascular Toxicity Induced by Antitumoral Drugs: Risk Assessment and Early Diagnosis) registry enrolled 865 patients at moderate/high risk for cardiotoxicity, in 27 of whom the LVEF dropped to less than 40% or symptomatic HF developed. 51 NT‐proBNP at baseline was not predictive of this degree of cardiac dysfunction. The timing of sample procurement may have influenced this finding. Samples were taken before cancer therapy, 3 weeks, and 12 weeks after initiation of cancer therapy, but interval changes of predictive significance between 3 and 12 weeks may have occurred. Within the HERA biomarker substudy, 43 higher NT‐proBNP increases from baseline were noted in those patients whose LVEF significantly dropped, but the ability to predict these drops in LVEF was compromised by the lack of a well‐established elevation threshold, further illustrating the need for evaluation of novel decision limits.

NP elevations are frequent in ICI‐related myocarditis, reportedly occurring in all 14 patients with NPs in one series 69 and over 66% in another, 5 but the predictive capability of NPs for this form of cardiotoxicity has not yet been fully elucidated.

To summarize, BNP and NT‐proBNP are biomarkers central to HF with similar clinical performance but differences in biological variability and behavior in the presence of comorbidities such as renal dysfunction. They are attractive candidates for prediction and diagnosis of cardiotoxicity and frequently demonstrate elevations at baseline and during treatment in patients who develop CTRCD. Predictive capability varies, particularly when single threshold approaches are employed. However, different lower and higher thresholds (eg, 30 and 100 pg/mL for BNP) appear to provide improved discrimination of lower and higher cardiotoxicity risk, respectively. As in the case of Tns, studies to date have been limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneity of assays and thresholds, lack of diversity, head‐to‐head comparisons, and evaluation for novel decision limits.

Novel Circulating Biomarkers,

Though myocardial injury and stress likely occur in cardiotoxicity, upstream biomarkers are also of great interest. Novel circulating signals of oxidative stress (eg, MPO), inflammation (eg, CRP [C‐reactive protein], soluble suppressor of tumorigenicity 2, interleukin‐6), and fibrosis/cellular proliferation (eg, galectin‐3 [Gal‐3]) might provide mechanistic information and may predict cardiotoxicity alone or in conjunction with Tn and NPs. Personalized solutions are being evaluated using techniques such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. 16 , 73 These panomic technologies may explain why patients with similar cardiovascular risk differ in vulnerability to cardiotoxicity 73 but many candidate markers are highly ubiquitous and variable. This may lead to elevations due to comorbid conditions and difficulty in establishing generalizable thresholds and delta values. Biomarkers must also be targeted by cancer type (eg, high levels of inflammation in acute leukemia may result in spurious associations between cytokines or other participant biomarkers and outcomes), to avoid false positives owing to cancer itself rather than cardiotoxicity. 16 Though the development of biomarkers unique to cardio‐oncology is an attractive concept, the complex pathophysiological pathways involved in the development of cardiotoxicity may overlap with other conditions. Head‐to‐head comparison with conventional biomarkers is also important because novel biomarkers present issues of cost, availability, and scalability. Notably, detailed description of analytic issues that may affect each potential novel biomarker in this field is beyond the scope of this article. For example, a recent scoping review identified 272 biomarkers that met criteria for inclusion, of which only 13 were described in more than 1 study. 24 A summary is thus provided, with focus on those markers for which either ≥1 publication or a high‐throughput assay exists.

Soluble suppressor of tumorigenicity‐2, GDF‐15 (growth differentiation factor‐15), PlGF (placental growth factor), sFlt‐1 (soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase receptor‐1), and interleukin‐6 among others have failed to conclusively demonstrate capability to predict cardiotoxicity more accurately than Tn and NPs and are not yet recommended for routine clinical use. 7 As described previously, comprehensive evaluations of multiple biomarkers run head‐to‐head (including TnI, CRP, NT‐proBNP, GDF‐15, and Gal‐3) in cohorts for which cardiotoxicity rates by conventional definitions were reported represent some of the strongest evidence in the field. 41 Based on this evidence, Tns and MPO appeared to have the most robust relationship with CTRCD. However, the preanalytic requirements of MPO assays (described in the Troponins section) limit scalability at the present time. Single biomarker evaluations do not carry this level of impact because no comparative data are provided. Recurrent limitations, particularly in terms of the number of cardiotoxicity cases using conventional definitions, are also evident. For example, soluble suppressor of tumorigenicity‐2 appeared to correlate with changes in LVEF in 126 Chinese patients with breast cancer in 1 study, but the rate of cardiotoxicity by conventional definitions was not reported. 74 Increases in Gal‐3 and CRP following anthracycline administration were noted in the PRADA (Prevention of Cardiac Dysfunction During Adjuvant Breast Cancer Therapy) trial. 48 Though these increases were not found to be related to LV function, only 1 patient met conventional cardiotoxicity criteria.

In a study involving proteomic analysis of 3 CTRCD cases and 4 age‐ and cancer‐matched controls, immunoglobulins E and G1 as well as their ratio emerged as a potential marker of CTRCD in patients with breast cancer. 58 Interestingly, higher baseline immunoglobulin E levels were significantly associated with lower risk for CTRCD in a validation cohort of 35 patients, 18 of whom were being treated for symptomatic CTRCD. This may indicate that immunoglobulin E mediates a protective immunological response following a cardiotoxic stressor, but larger validation studies are needed. Nonetheless, the elegant design of this study sets a precedent. Other novel immune response protein markers have been identified as potential markers of cardiotoxicity. In 1 study of 27 patients with breast cancer, of whom 5 had a reduction in LVEF of >10%, multiplex immunoassay analyses were used to evaluate 82 chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and other cardiovascular biomarkers. 57 Though an expansive array of biomarkers was studied, a conventional (non‐hs) Tn assay was used. Changes in several biomarkers were noted in this pilot study, for example, CCL23 (chemokine ligand 23) was higher at baseline and after the second cycle of anthracyclines among those with reduced LVEF. These findings have not yet been studied prospectively in terms of their relation to LVEF in larger populations. With regard to other technologies, microRNAs (miRNAs) have garnered interest as potential markers of cardiotoxicity. MiRNAs are short noncoding RNAs involved in regulation of gene expression, involved in regulation of cell differentiation, apoptosis, and death. MiRNA‐1 has shown more promise than other miRNAs, appearing statistically superior to TnI in correlation with LVEF in 1 study, 56 but an older Tn assay was used and there were no cases of CTRCD by conventional definitions. MiRNA‐30c has also been associated with bevacizumab‐related reductions in cardiac function in a study of 80 patients from China with lung cancer, of whom 30 met criteria for CTRCD as defined for this study. 75 However, comparison with conventional markers was not performed.

Beyond CTRCD, candidate microRNAs were recently identified from 4 myocarditis cohorts and further validation is awaited in those treated with ICIs. 76 T‐cell receptor sequencing of myocardium from 2 cases of fatal ICI‐related myocarditis 59 demonstrated clonal T‐cell populations identical to those in tumor, indicating potential value as immunolomic markers. Furthermore, cytotoxic CD8+ T effector cells reexpressing CD45RA (Temra CD8+ cells) were identified in 8 cases with ICI myocarditis compared with controls. 60

In summary, these novel circulating biomarkers offer valuable mechanistic insights, but robust studies incorporating comparisons with conventional biomarkers are needed before clinical use.

Critical Challenges: Summary

These examples illustrate that the limitations of pivotal circulating biomarker studies in patients with cancer relate to 3 main categories: (1) study populations, (2) cardiotoxicity end points and definitions, and (3) biomarker methodologies, as illustrated by the 26 studies listed in the Table and Table S4.

First, sample size constraints often result in low numbers of cardiotoxicity cases upon which to base conclusions. Of the 26 studies listed, 16 (62%) involved <100 patients or <10 patients with cardiotoxicity as defined by conventional definitions. Study populations frequently demonstrate heterogeneity in terms of cancer type and treatment, although lacking in racial and ethnic diversity. Only 5/26 (19%) of studies reported the race or ethnicity of the populations involved, and within those the majority of participants were White. Across all publications, few appear to have involved non‐US/European Union populations. Second, consensus is lacking in view of the multiple definitions and recommendations for cardiotoxicity that have been developed, reducing the generalizability of biomarker findings. In almost a quarter (6/26=23%) of pivotal studies, no clear definition for the form of cardiotoxicity in question was provided. Of those remaining, the Cardiac Review and Evaluation Committee definition of CTRCD was the most frequently evaluated for that end point (7 studies) but others varied. Third, there is substantial heterogeneity in analytic parameters. Elevations or changes in biomarker levels are often reported in relation to fixed thresholds adapted from populations without cancer. A single threshold was used but not reported for ≥1 assay in 6 studies (23%), and a single threshold approach with no/minimal evaluation for novel decision limits was used in 16/26 (62%) publications. Biomarkers are frequently measured using variable assay formats. Of the 26 pivotal studies, 1 study involved Bayer assays, 4 Dade Behring, 5 Abbott, 8 Roche, and 9 Siemens for Tns/NPs. Beyond Tns and NPs, for which multiple high throughput assays exist that could be used in this setting without changes to analytic parameters, novel markers are constrained by cost and scalability. As a result, reliable conclusions regarding baseline and serial biomarker utility cannot yet be reached, and elevated biomarker levels alone would not be sufficient to fully inform decisions regarding treatment or additional testing. Indeed, over 75% of the 272 recommendations (including those pertaining to biomarkers) made in recent guidelines were supported by level C evidence. Few health care providers are thought to practice in alignment with these recommendations, and widespread adoption could take several years. 17 These caveats aside, the predictive ability of hsTn, NPs, and novel biomarkers for the most common and serious forms of cardiotoxicity appears promising, emphasizing the importance of developing effective solutions to these challenges.

Study Populations

Larger sample sizes are required and could be facilitated by involving patient and oncologist stakeholders in direct‐to‐patient study designs 77 and using social media for awareness and buy‐in. This would help address challenges relating to the interdisciplinary nature of cardio‐oncology studies. Oncology providers may then become more active advocates for potential cardiovascular biomarker research opportunities, increasing sample sizes and identification of study candidates at baseline. 47 Global cardio‐oncology registries are increasingly recognized as providing an ideal framework for the evaluation of longitudinal patient samples, allowing validation of biomarker findings from pilot studies and assessment of diverse, older populations as well as those with comorbidities that are often excluded from oncology clinical trials. 16 , 22 , 78 Notably, harmonized circulating biomarker methodologies are a crucial component of such registry‐based approaches.

Cardiotoxicity End Points and Definitions

Clinically relevant and widely acceptable definitions of cardiotoxicity defined by oncologists, cardiologists, and laboratory medicine experts are recognized as a critical need in this space, 22 assuming even greater importance when their impact on the generalizability of circulating biomarker studies is considered. For example, in the case of CTRCD, symptoms or signs caused by a structural or functional cardiac abnormality, corroborated by elevated biomarker levels or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion 81 such that an oncologist in discussion with a cardiologist would consider initiating cardioprotective therapies to optimize delivery of their cancer therapy. Use of such a clinically relevant definition may inform end points for evaluation of circulating biomarkers in this setting. Elevated biomarker levels in the absence of other clinical signs or symptoms should be approached with caution, and not necessarily overinterpreted as evidence for CTRCD.

Biomarker Methodologies

Harmonized data collection has been identified as an area requiring further attention in cardio‐oncology. 22 This is of paramount importance for circulating biomarker data, particularly when multicenter designs are being considered. Uniform, centralized biobanking and analysis would increase efficiency and facilitate comparison of results from different sites and settings. Care should be taken to avoid comparison of results obtained from assays with different capture and detection epitopes, an issue that can be avoided when centralized biobanks are used; in this case, one assay (or several) may be run on all samples. Where centralized biobanking is not possible, biomarker measurements should be conducted using the same assay method (and the same laboratory, where possible) throughout treatment. 7 , 66 Older assay formats should be avoided as recommended in other settings. 33 Preanalytic and analytic factors should be addressed: care should be taken to specify blood sample type (eg, plasma versus serum), volumes, units, and compliance with sample handling instructions. If samples are obtained in the primary care or outpatient setting, preanalytic sample handling instructions assume greater importance. Results pertaining to less stable analytes (eg, BNP, MPO) may not be reliable if sample handling requirements (eg, spinning down and freezing within 4 hours, putting samples on ice) cannot be consistently followed.

Novel decision limits should be considered. Several studies have shown that different high and low thresholds, as opposed to a single cut point, as well as analyses involving changes over time (delta values) deliver superior discriminative performance for prediction of clinical end points (Figure S1). 41 , 47 , 67 This indicates that baseline (pretreatment) and sampling at several subsequent time points during and at completion of treatment should be obtained, allowing comprehensive assessment of biomarker performance. Algorithms and risk scores incorporating machine learning may further improve risk stratification, as demonstrated in acute coronary syndrome. 82 These would allow complex analyses on high‐dimensional data sets incorporating clinical, demographic, and biomarker parameters to be integrated to provide more personalized risk assessment. Thresholds for low, intermediate, and high risk may be presented to improve clinician interpretability. External validation of all decision limits and machine learning models remains imperative. If effective, a circulating‐biomarker guided strategy incorporating these novel decision limits could also be evaluated.

Circulating Cardiovascular Biomarkers: Future Directions

The solutions outlined here also have relevance when circulating biomarkers are considered for expanded utilities in the cardio‐oncology space.

Utility of Biomarkers at the Intersection of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease

Beyond CTRCD and myocarditis, circulating biomarkers may also help elucidate direct and indirect relationships between cardiovascular disease and cancer 29 , 83 , 84 (Figure 2). Cardiovascular disease may result from cancer and its treatment, and vice versa. Common to both are risk factors, pathological processes (genetic aberrations, cellular immortality, angiogenesis, neurohormonal stress and inflammation) and prognostic capability for circulating markers (hs‐CRP, soluble suppressor of tumorigenicity‐2, interleukin‐6, Gal‐3, TnT, and others). 85 Canakinumab 86 is associated with both improved cardiovascular outcomes and reductions in cancer when hs‐CRP is ≥2 mg/L, underlining the role of inflammation in both pathologies. Future work should further elucidate this complex interplay through biomarker discovery (high‐throughput and panomic technologies), biomarker‐guided strategies and therapeutic agents.

Role of Biomarkers in Antineoplastic Drug Development and Clinical Trials

Efforts to standardize sample procurement and centralize biobanking in clinical trials are needed. Though Tn and NPs are frequently used as inclusion/exclusion criteria, surrogate end points, signals of efficacy, toxicity, and safety in clinical trials, many similar inconsistencies are evident. 87 Cardiovascular biomarkers should be measured routinely in oncology trials (Figure 3). Selectivity and lack of cardiovascular biomarker data in oncology trials can mask cardiovascular toxicities later evident in community populations. 88 It is important that biomarker studies are designed early in the development of cancer therapeutics, with a goal to define patterns of biomarker release and mechanistic associations from preclinical stages. Beyond preclinical stages, biomarkers may enrich the study population and inform end points. Therefore, it is important that consistent, harmonized biomarker methodologies are employed. Cardiovascular biomarker standards developed for cardiovascular trials should be employed in oncology trials involving agents suspected of cardiovascular toxicity based on mechanism of action or preclinical data. Increased collaboration between trialists from both specialties would facilitate improved study designs and development of algorithms aimed at balancing efficacy of the antineoplastic agent with cardiovascular toxicity, accelerating regulatory approval for agents without cardiotoxicity signals.

Biomarkers may play a prominent role in all phases of anticancer drug development and clinical trials. It is important that consistent, harmonized biomarker methodologies are employed and that biomarker standards developed for cardiovascular trials are used in oncology trials involving agents suspected of cardiovascular toxicity.

Conclusions

Circulating biomarkers may provide sensitive, cost‐efficient risk stratification for cardiotoxicity related to cancer therapies, including the most commonly reported and serious forms. Tns measured using high‐sensitivity assays and NPs have shown most promise in the prediction of CTRCD, and Tn and NP elevations are frequent in ICI‐related myocarditis. Panomic technologies have identified early candidate markers for both conditions. However, pivotal supportive evidence has been hampered by small study populations, lack of diversity, variable cardiotoxicity criteria, and inconsistent biomarker methodology. Improved study designs are needed, incorporating larger, more diverse study populations, and registry‐based approaches. Harmonized, clinically relevant definitions and standardized circulating biomarker methodologies are required. Increased collaboration is also needed between trialists and researchers from cardiology and oncology. This improved environment will not only augment confidence in the predictive value of circulating biomarkers in cardiotoxicity but also elucidate both direct and indirect relationships between cardiovascular disease and cancer, allowing a greater role in the development and success of novel anticancer therapies.
