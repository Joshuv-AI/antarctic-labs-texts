MOS is a key regulator of meiotic midbody assembly and prevents abnormal divisions in mouse eggs and their polar bodies
Authors: Gisela Cairo, Muhammad A Haseeb, Zachary Boyce Joy, Karen Schindler, Soni Lacefield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMammalian female meiosis is uniquely regulated to produce a developmentally competent egg capable of supporting embryogenesis. During meiosis I, homologous chromosomes segregate, with half extruded into the first polar body. The egg then arrests at metaphase II and only resumes meiosis and extrudes the second polar body following fertilization. The MOS/MAPK signaling pathway is important for maintaining the metaphase II arrest; in Mos−/− mutants, a subset of eggs undergo spontaneous parthenogenetic activation and exhibit additional abnormal cell divisions. To further understand the cell cycle mis-regulation in activated Mos−/− eggs, we used time-lapse microscopy to monitor the abnormal divisions. We discovered that, following parthenogenetic activation, the first polar body can assemble a spindle, segregate chromosomes and divide with timings similar to anaphase II onset in the egg. This behavior contrasts with wild-type polar bodies, which do not divide and typically degenerate. We demonstrate that Mos−/− oocytes and polar bodies can exchange cytoplasm for a longer duration due to an extension of telophase I, likely allowing the transfer of cell cycle regulators between the two compartments. Further inspection revealed that Mos−/− oocytes have defective meiotic midbody assembly with most oocytes lacking a cap structure, which is needed to separate the oocyte and the polar body prior to abscission. We report that the polar bodies of Mos−/− eggs can re-enter the cell cycle and undergo additional aberrant divisions. These findings identify MOS as a crucial regulator of meiotic midbody formation and uncover a novel consequence of disrupted MOS/MAPK signaling – the potential for polar bodies to become mitotically active.Keywords: Meiosis, Oogenesis, MOS, Polar body, Midbody, Cytokinesis, Abscission, Oocyte, Egg

INTRODUCTION

Meiotic chromosome segregation is tightly regulated during mammalian oogenesis to ultimately produce an egg capable of supporting early embryonic development (Mihajlović and FitzHarris, 2018). Meiosis is initiated in the fetal ovary with DNA replication, programmed double strand break formation and repair, followed by a long prophase I arrest. With each menstrual cycle, a subset of oocytes resumes meiosis and enters the first meiotic division. A bipolar spindle assembles and localizes to the cortex, resulting in an asymmetric division in which half the homologous chromosomes are retained in the oocyte and half are extruded into a smaller polar body (Dunkley et al., 2022). In telophase I, the actomyosin ring contracts and a specialized structure known as the meiotic midbody assembles, recruiting proteins needed for abscission during cytokinesis (Jung et al., 2023; Uraji et al., 2018). The oocyte then assembles a bipolar spindle and arrests at metaphase II, awaiting fertilization (Mihajlović and FitzHarris, 2018). The polar body eventually degenerates (Charlton, 1917; Takase et al., 1995; Zamboni et al., 1972).

The meiotic divisions are orchestrated by the dynamic interplay between cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) and the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), an E3 ubiquitin ligase (Kim et al., 2023; Kotani et al., 1998; Kubiak et al., 1992; Ledan et al., 2001; Nabti et al., 2014; Pines, 2011). CDK1, in complex with a B-type cyclin, phosphorylates substrates essential for meiotic progression, including those involved in spindle assembly and chromosome alignment at the metaphase plate (Bouftas and Wassmann, 2019; Pines, 2011). CDK1 activity also promotes activation of the APC/C, which in turn ubiquitylates cyclin B and other regulatory proteins, targeting them for subsequent proteasomal degradation, triggering anaphase I onset (Lahav-Baratz et al., 1995; Pines, 2011; Rudner and Murray, 2000). Unlike mitosis, oocyte meiosis has a long period of chromosome alignment in prometaphase I and metaphase I (Brunet et al., 1999; Davydenko et al., 2013; Holubcová et al., 2015; Kitajima et al., 2011; Lane et al., 2012). In mouse oocytes, cyclin B1 destruction initiates during prometaphase I, prior to chromosome alignment on the spindle (Davydenko et al., 2013; Herbert et al., 2003; Homer et al., 2005; Kitajima et al., 2011; Lane et al., 2012; Levasseur et al., 2019; McGuinness et al., 2009).

To enter meiosis II, APC/C activity must be inhibited, allowing cyclin B1 levels to reaccumulate and sustain high CDK1 activity (Hörmanseder et al., 2013; Kubiak et al., 1993; Ledan et al., 2001; Yamamoto et al., 2005). Eggs then arrest at metaphase II, which requires high CDK1 activity and APC/C inhibition (Hörmanseder et al., 2013). Because CDK1 activates APC/C, an additional inhibitory mechanism is required to establish and maintain metaphase II arrest. In female mammals, establishment of APC/C inhibition requires EMI2 (also known as FBXO43), whereas maintenance of the metaphase II arrest requires both EMI2 and Moloney sarcoma oncogene (MOS) (Araki et al., 1996; Colledge et al., 1994; Dupré et al., 2011; Hashimoto et al., 1994; Hirao and Eppig, 1997; Hörmanseder et al., 2013; Madgwick et al., 2006; Ohe et al., 2010; Suzuki et al., 2010; Tang et al., 2010). EMI2 functions as a direct inhibitor of APC/C, whereas MOS, a serine/threonine kinase, is thought to act indirectly through activation of downstream kinases (Dupré et al., 2011; Ohe et al., 2010; Tang et al., 2010; Verlhac et al., 1996, 2000b). Although originally identified as the transforming gene of Moloney murine sarcoma virus (v-mos), the MOS activity is largely restricted to oocytes (Dupré et al., 2011). MOS is an upstream activator of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, which is also essential for sustaining metaphase II arrest (Choi et al., 1996; Verlhac et al., 1996, 2000b). The loss of MOS results in lower levels of cyclin B1 in meiosis II likely through two mechanisms – a failure to maintain APC/C inhibition and lower translation of cyclin B1 (Cao et al., 2020; Dupré et al., 2011; Kalous et al., 2018; Madgwick and Jones, 2007; O'Keefe et al., 1991; Sha et al., 2017).

Notably, a subset of eggs from Mos−/− mice fail to maintain metaphase II arrest, and instead undergo spontaneous parthenogenetic activation and premature anaphase II (Cairo et al., 2025; Colledge et al., 1994; Hashimoto et al., 1994; Hirao and Eppig, 1997; O'Keefe et al., 1991; Verlhac et al., 2000a). Some of these activated eggs proceed through additional abnormal cell divisions. Approximately one-third of female Mos−/− mice develop ovarian germ cell tumors, suggesting that the activated eggs can proliferate aberrantly in the ovary (Colledge et al., 1994; Furuta et al., 1995; Hashimoto et al., 1994; Hirao and Eppig, 1997).

We sought to determine the molecular and cellular causes of the abnormal cell divisions observed in mouse Mos−/− eggs. Using time-lapse imaging, we found that in parthenogenetically activated Mos−/− eggs, the first polar body can assemble a spindle, segregate chromosomes, and undergo cell division with timing similar to the inappropriate onset of anaphase II in the egg. These findings were unexpected, as polar bodies in wild-type eggs are typically destined for degeneration. Additionally, we observed that the cytoplasm of the Mos−/− egg and polar body remains connected for an extended period, allowing cytoplasmic components to pass between the two compartments. Further investigation revealed that this prolonged connection is likely due to an increased duration of telophase I and defective formation of the meiotic midbody, leaving an open intracellular bridge between the egg and the polar body. Notably, polar bodies can re-enter the cell cycle, implicating them as potential contributors to tumor formation. Together, these findings identify MOS as a key regulator of meiotic midbody assembly and demonstrate its role in preventing abnormal divisions in both the egg and polar bodies.

RESULTS

Polar bodies undergo chromosome segregation and cell division in Mos−/− eggs

Previous reports have shown that Mos−/− mutant eggs undergo a variety of phenotypes – some extrude one polar body, others extrude two polar bodies and some undergo additional aberrant divisions after anaphase II (Araki et al., 1996; Cairo et al., 2025; Colledge et al., 1994; Furuta et al., 1995; Hashimoto et al., 1994; Hirao and Eppig, 1997; Verlhac et al., 2000a). In addition, a previous study has reported that polar bodies of Mos−/− eggs undergo abnormal cleavage events, unlike wild-type eggs, whose polar bodies degenerated (Choi et al., 1996). To gain further insight into these aberrant cleavage events and their potential contribution to abnormal cell divisions, we performed time-lapse imaging of wild-type and Mos−/− eggs. We collected oocytes at prophase I arrest (germinal vesicle), released them from the arrest, and then monitored the spindle and chromatin during the resumption of meiosis. We initiated the imaging at between 13 and 15 h after prophase I release and then imaged for 24 h so that we could monitor meiosis II and beyond (Fig. 1A). We added SiR-DNA and SPY55–tubulin to observe the chromatin and spindle, respectively. Wild-type eggs typically remained arrested at metaphase II and the polar body eventually degenerated (Fig. 1B; Movie 1). To our surprise, 53% of Mos−/− eggs formed bipolar spindles in the first polar body (PB1), which elongated and segregated chromosomes (Fig. 1C–E; Movie 2). We also observed bipolar spindle formation in Mos−/− PB1s in immunofluorescence images of fixed Mos−/− eggs, suggesting that the phenotypes observed in live eggs were not due to phototoxicity or the use of cell permeable dyes (Fig. S1A).

We further categorized Mos−/− eggs with polar body divisions by their phenotypic outcomes – whether they underwent a second polar body extrusion and additional divisions beyond anaphase II. The largest fraction observed was Mos−/− eggs with polar body divisions undergoing anaphase II, extruding a second polar body, and then continuing to undergo additional divisions (45% of total Mos−/− eggs) (Fig. 1E). A smaller fraction of Mos−/− eggs underwent anaphase II to make a second polar body but did not proceed to undergo additional divisions (8% of total Mos−/− eggs). Only 5% of Mos−/− eggs extruded two polar bodies and underwent additional divisions but did not divide the first polar body (Fig. 1E). Therefore, most Mos−/− eggs that parthenogenetically activated and underwent additional divisions also had polar bodies that assembled bipolar spindles and divided.

The polar bodies of wild-type eggs do occasionally have abnormal membrane invaginations that are different from the polar body divisions observed in Mos−/− eggs. Approximately 20% of polar bodies of wild-type eggs underwent abnormal membrane cleavage, but most did not form bipolar spindles, nor did they segregate chromosomes in the polar body (Fig. S2A–C). Instead, the abnormal membrane cleavage occurred without other noticeable cell cycle events (Fig. S2B). All Mos−/− eggs that divided their polar bodies assembled a bipolar spindle and segregated chromosomes in the polar body (Fig. S2C; Fig. 1C,D).

We next asked whether bipolar spindle assembly in the polar body was a common feature of parthenogenetic activation. To address this question, we used strontium chloride (SrCl2) to chemically activate wild-type eggs to undergo parthenogenesis (Fig. 1A) (Jellerette et al., 2000). Chemical activation triggers similar processes to those that occur during fertilization, such as maternal mRNA processing, increased protein synthesis, mitochondrial activity for ATP production and membrane dynamics processes including cortical granule exocytosis (Bellido-Quispe et al., 2024; Horner and Wolfner, 2008; Jellerette et al., 2000; Krauchunas and Wolfner, 2013). Upon chemical activation, eggs undergo anaphase II, extrude a second polar body and initiate embryo-like divisions. We found that polar bodies in chemically activated eggs did not assemble bipolar spindles; nor did they segregate chromosomes (Fig. 1E; Fig. S2C). Therefore, the feature of polar body chromosome segregation and cell division is specific to Mos−/− eggs, and not to eggs that undergo parthenogenetic activation.

Polar body divisions occur with similar timings to that seen for anaphase II onset in Mos−/− eggs

In our time-lapse imaging, we noticed that Mos−/− eggs often underwent polar body divisions with similar timings to that seen for anaphase II onset and the second polar body extrusion. To quantify this observation, we monitored the time between anaphase II onset and polar body division (Fig. 1F). 55% of Mos−/− eggs underwent both events simultaneously (Fig. 1D,F). In the remaining Mos−/− eggs with polar body divisions, we found 24% of eggs took 20–40 min and 21% of eggs took 40 min or more between egg and polar body divisions (Figs 1C,F, 2A). In some Mos−/− eggs, anaphase II onset occurred prior to the polar body division (Fig. 2A) and in others, the polar body division occurred prior to anaphase II onset (Fig. 1C).

The observation that most Mos−/− eggs divide their polar body with timing similar to anaphase II onset was unexpected, as we did not anticipate that the two compartments would maintain cell cycle coordination. We propose two hypotheses that explain how the two compartments could maintain coordination that we test in the following sections: (1) large polar bodies in Mos−/− eggs could contain more cell cycle regulators that allow cell division; or (2) the egg and the polar body maintain a cytoplasmic connection and share cell cycle regulators.

Polar body size does not correlate with polar body divisions

Previous studies have reported that polar bodies are larger in Mos−/− eggs compared to what is seen in wild-type eggs. This increase in size is due to a failure to position the spindle close to the cortex prior to division (Chaigne et al., 2013; Choi et al., 1996; Verlhac et al., 1996, 2000a). We also observed larger polar bodies in some of the Mos−/− eggs in our time-lapse imaging (Fig. 2A). We hypothesized that a larger polar body could allow for more cytoplasm and cell cycle regulators to enter the polar body, allowing a coordinated cell division. We therefore asked whether the larger polar bodies were more likely to assemble a spindle and divide than the normal-sized polar bodies. We measured the size of the first extruded polar body relative to the egg by calculating the polar body area as a percentage of the egg area in wild-type and Mos−/− eggs (Fig. 2B). We found that the polar bodies were, on average, larger in Mos−/− eggs compared to what is seen in wild type, as shown in previous studies (Fig. 2C) (Choi et al., 1996; Verlhac et al., 1996, 2000a). However, we found that both large and normal-sized polar bodies in Mos−/− eggs assembled bipolar spindles and divided (Fig. 2C). Similarly, there were both large and normal-sized polar bodies that did not assemble spindles and divide. Using Pearson correlation, we found no significant correlation between the size of the polar body and whether they divided (correlation coefficient r=0.06; Fig. 2D). Our data shows that there are large polar bodies that do not form bipolar spindles and divide and some large polar bodies that do. Similarly, there are some normal sized polar bodies the form bipolar spindles and divide and some that do not. However, we did not perform volume measurements so we cannot fully exclude some size differences in volume. In conclusion, our findings do not support the hypothesis that larger polar bodies are more likely to undergo division as compared to normal-sized polar bodies, but we cannot fully exclude that polar body size is a contributing factor.

Cytoplasmic content is inappropriately exchanged between Mos−/− eggs and polar bodies

To test our hypothesis that Mos−/− eggs maintain a connection between the egg and the polar body, we asked whether components within the egg cytoplasm could pass into the polar body. To this end, we injected wild-type and Mos−/− eggs with lysine-fixable dextran conjugated to a fluorescent dye and then monitored whether the dextran passed into the polar body in living oocytes and eggs. We first injected the eggs with dextran at 13.5 h after prophase I release (Fig. S3A). We chose this timepoint because polar body divisions in Mos−/− eggs occur 13–15 h after prophase I release. In wild-type eggs, we observed that the fluorescent dextran did not pass into the polar body, suggesting that the egg and polar body cytoplasms were not connected (Fig. S3B). In contrast, 50% of Mos−/− oocytes and eggs had fluorescent dextran that passed into PB1, suggesting that cytoplasmic content continued to pass from the egg into PB1 (Fig. S3C,D).

We then asked whether the cytoplasm of the Mos−/− egg and the polar body remained connected or had a delayed separation. We repeated the dextran experiment in eggs at 18 h after prophase I release. As expected, we did not observe fluorescent dextran in the polar body of wild-type eggs (Fig. S3D). The percentage of Mos−/− eggs with fluorescent dextran passing from egg to polar body reduced to ∼10%, indicating that by 18 h Mos−/− eggs and polar bodies eventually separated into two compartments. These results suggest that there is a delayed abscission between Mos−/− eggs and PB1s.

To further parse out the meiotic stages at which cytoplasmic content is exchanged between the oocyte and polar body, we injected dextran in wild-type and Mos−/− oocytes at different timepoints between 10 and 14 h after prophase I release. Cells were fixed immediately after the injections. We immunostained for α-tubulin to observe the spindle, allowing the determination of the meiotic stages based on spindle morphology. Based on previous standards, we scored anaphase I (full spindle width), early telophase I (spindle with some restriction in the middle), mid telophase I (spindle with thinning at the middle), late telophase I (spindle with threadlike attachment at the middle) and metaphase II (new spindle formation in the egg) (Fig. 3A) (Jung et al., 2023). Oocytes without leakage were devoid of any dextran signal in the polar body, whereas oocytes with leakage had variable amounts of dextran within the polar body. We found that leakage of dextran from the oocyte into the polar body occurred in both wild-type and Mos−/− oocytes injected at anaphase I, early telophase I and mid telophase I (Fig. 3A,B). However, at late telophase I, dextran did not pass into the polar body of most wild-type oocytes, whereas dextran did pass into the polar body in most Mos−/− oocytes (Fig. 3A,B). By metaphase II, dextran did not leak from egg to polar body in either wild-type or Mos−/− eggs. These results suggest that the oocyte and polar body no longer exchange cytoplasmic content in late telophase I in wild type but remain connected in Mos−/− oocytes.

Remarkably, during our analysis, we noticed that many of the Mos−/− oocytes with mid and late telophase I spindles had decondensed DNA either near or separate from the spindle (Fig. 4A,B; Fig. S4A,B). All the injected oocytes with decondensed chromatin and a telophase I spindle had dextran leak into the polar body. Through time-lapse imaging, we found that wild-type oocytes did not enter an interphase-like state from telophase I, but ∼30% of Mos−/− oocytes decondensed their DNA in telophase I (Fig. 5A–C). Some of those eggs stayed in an interphase-like state for the duration of the movie and some eventually progressed and assembled a metaphase II-like spindle (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that there is a loss of coordination in Mos−/− oocytes between the telophase I spindle and the cytoplasmic cell cycle stage.

Telophase I is strongly delayed in Mos−/− oocytes

From our dextran immunofluorescence experiments, we noticed that most wild-type oocytes were at metaphase II at earlier timepoints after prophase I release compared to what was seen for Mos−/− oocytes. To quantify this finding, we used time-lapse imaging to monitor the duration of anaphase I onset to entry into metaphase II, which includes all of telophase I (Fig. 5A–C). We excluded the Mos−/− oocytes that entered an interphase-like state because many of them did not form a metaphase II spindle by the end of the movie. We found that Mos−/− oocytes were strongly delayed by ∼90 min in comparison to wild-type oocytes (Fig. 5D). These findings are consistent with the activation of the abscission checkpoint. To our knowledge, the abscission checkpoint has not been demonstrated in oocytes; however, this checkpoint is known to delay telophase and abscission in mitotic cells when induced by certain triggers (Gibieža and Petrikaitė, 2024). We propose a model in which delayed telophase I in Mos−/− oocytes prolongs the cytoplasmic connection between the oocyte and polar body, permitting extended exchange of cytoplasmic content.

The meiotic midbody is defective in Mos−/− eggs

Given the movement of dextran from the polar body to the egg, we next asked whether cytokinesis defects contribute to the delayed separation of egg and polar body cytoplasm in Mos−/− mutants. From our time-lapse imaging, it can be seen that the membrane ingresses between the egg and polar body, suggesting actomyosin ring assembly and contraction. We therefore assessed the assembly of the meiotic midbody (mMB), a transient organelle that forms during early to late telophase I as the oocyte and polar body separate. At the mMB, the microtubules have a unique orientation and a mMB substructure made up of centralspindlin complex assemblies (Hu et al., 2012; Jung et al., 2023; White and Glotzer, 2012). In somatic cells, the centralspindlin component mitotic kinesin-like protein 1 (MKLP1; also known as KIF23) forms a ring at the midbody, whereas oocytes form both a ring and a cap, with the cap pointing into the polar body (Hu et al., 2012; Jung et al., 2023; Kuriyama et al., 1984; Mullins and Biesele, 1977; White and Glotzer, 2012). The mMB cap creates a barrier to prevent nascent proteins from entering the polar body, ensuring their retention within the egg (Jung et al., 2023).

We assessed assembly of the mMB structure in Mos−/− oocytes. We used immunofluorescence and super-resolution microscopy to observe MKLP1 localization in fixed telophase I-staged oocytes. The majority of wild-type oocytes had the ring and cap substructure, as previously observed (Fig. 6A) (Jung et al., 2023). In contrast, ∼90% of Mos−/− oocytes had a defective substructure (Fig. 6B–E). Most of the Mos−/− oocytes had an MKLP1 ring, but the cap was absent (Fig. 6B). We also observed substructures with a ring and a faint cap that pointed into the oocyte instead of the polar body, or an entirely abnormal structure, appearing fluffy instead of being well defined (Fig. 6C,D). These results indicate that Mos−/− oocytes have a defective mMB substructure.

We next examined the localization of a component of the core mMB, protein regulator of cytokinesis 1 (PRC1) (Hu et al., 2012; Jung et al., 2023). In most wild-type eggs, PRC1 localized as two disk-like structures at telophase I with a dome-like disk pointing into the polar body (Fig. S5A) (Jung et al., 2023). In 65% of Mos−/− oocytes, we observed that PRC1 localization was defective, showing either absence of one disk or a defective structure (Fig. S5B–E). Overall, our results demonstrate that Mos−/− oocytes have defective mMB structures.

A previous study has shown that loss of the mMB cap allowed nascent proteins that are usually retained in the egg to pass into the polar body (Jung et al., 2023). Furthermore, defects in midbody assembly can lead to the activation of the abscission checkpoint in mitotic cells (Strohacker et al., 2021). Therefore, we propose that midbody defects combined with an extended telophase I allow a prolonged cytoplasmic connection between the egg and the polar body. This connection allows the passage of components that drive spindle assembly and chromosome segregation, allowing PB1 to undergo chromosome segregation with similar timing to anaphase II onset in parthenogenetically activated Mos−/− eggs.

Polar bodies in Mos−/− eggs enter a new cell cycle and undergo DNA replication

Altogether, our results suggest that the prolonged connection between the Mos−/− oocyte and the polar body allows the polar body to undergo an additional round of chromosome segregation. Our next question was whether the polar body could initiate a new cell cycle and replicate its chromosomes, possibly contributing to the cell cycle divisions that could lead to germ cell tumor formation. To address this question, we used a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) clickIT assay, which allows the detection of newly synthesized DNA through incorporation of EdU, a thymidine analog (Salic and Mitchison, 2008). We matured wild-type and Mos−/− eggs in EdU-containing medium and fixed them 38–40 h after prophase I release (Fig. 7A). We then incubated with the Click-IT reaction mix and stained the DNA with DAPI. As expected, for wild-type eggs that naturally arrested at metaphase II, we detected no EdU signal (Fig. 7B). In contrast, we observed EdU staining in both Mos−/− eggs and polar bodies with decondensed chromatin, indicating that DNA replication can occur in both the egg and the polar body (Fig. 7C–E). It is important to note that this experiment is not a time lapse, but instead a timepoint image showing the eggs and polar bodies that were actively undergoing DNA replication. Therefore, our numbers reported for Mos−/− eggs and polar bodies with EdU staining in Fig. 7E are likely an underestimation. Our results show that in Mos−/− mutants, both eggs and polar bodies can initiate a new cell cycle and undergo DNA replication. These data suggest that polar bodies contribute to abnormal mitotic divisions initiated in Mos−/− eggs that escaped meiosis.

Polar bodies in Mos−/− eggs can survive and segregate chromosomes after separation from the egg

We then asked whether PB1s from Mos−/− eggs could undergo abnormal divisions when separated from the egg and zona pellucida. We matured wild-type and Mos−/− oocytes and dissolved their zona pellucida and then allowed the PB1 to abscise and separate from the egg. We then collected these PB1s and either monitored morphological changes every hour or used time-lapse imaging to monitor the chromatin and spindle with Sir-DNA and SPY555–tubulin, respectively (Fig. 8A–D). 85% of isolated wild-type polar bodies typically survived for less than 3 h and did not divide (Fig. 8A,D). The remaining 15% survived between 3 and 8 h and did not divide. In contrast, Mos−/− polar bodies typically survived longer – ∼50% of Mos−/− polar bodies underwent chromosome segregation and most of those survived for more than 8 h (Fig. 8B–D). Interestingly, the isolated Mos−/− polar bodies also underwent rapid morphological changes with incomplete cytokinesis (Fig. 8B,C; Fig. S6). These changes were not typical of the polar bodies that remained connected to the egg and with the zona pellucida intact. Therefore, the egg and zona pellucida might provide connections that stabilize the morphology of the polar body in Mos−/− eggs that undergo additional divisions. In conclusion, these experiments show that the polar bodies isolated from Mos−/− eggs can survive and undergo chromosome segregation, and attempt cell division, supporting the model that they contribute to germ cell tumor formation in vivo.

DISCUSSION

MOS/MAPK signaling plays crucial roles in several aspects of mouse oocyte meiosis, including cell cycle progression, normal spindle assembly, filamentous-actin-mediated cortical remodeling and RNA regulation (Cao et al., 2020; Chaigne et al., 2013, 2015; Choi et al., 1996; Deng et al., 2005; Kalous et al., 2018; Lefebvre et al., 2002; Sha et al., 2017; Terret et al., 2003; Verlhac et al., 1996, 2000b). Among its important functions, MOS is pivotal for maintaining metaphase II arrest (Dupré et al., 2011; Hörmanseder et al., 2013). In the absence of MOS, a subset of eggs exits metaphase II and proceeds through aberrant, inappropriate divisions (Cairo et al., 2025; Colledge et al., 1994; Furuta et al., 1995; Hashimoto et al., 1994; Hirao and Eppig, 1997). Notably, approximately one-third of Mos−/− female mice develop germ cell tumors, suggesting that some of these aberrantly dividing eggs undergo uncontrolled divisions within the ovary (Colledge et al., 1994; Furuta et al., 1995; Hashimoto et al., 1994).

We used time-lapse imaging to gain further insight into the additional divisions in Mos−/− eggs. We found that the first polar body assembles bipolar spindles and segregates chromosomes with similar timing to anaphase II onset in the Mos−/− eggs that undergo parthenogenetic activation (Fig. 1). The Mos−/− eggs are known to have larger polar bodies (Choi et al., 1996; Verlhac et al., 2000a), but both larger and normal-sized polar bodies assembled spindles and divided their chromosomes (Fig. 2). Some larger Mos−/− polar bodies did not form spindles. Our results showed that the major predictor of spindle assembly in the Mos−/− polar body was not polar body size, but instead whether the egg underwent anaphase II. Most Mos−/− eggs that parthenogenetically activated displayed bipolar spindles in the polar body (Fig. 1E).

The finding that spindle assembly and chromosome segregation take place in the first polar bodies of Mos−/− mutants was surprising because wild-type polar bodies are typically non-functional cells that undergo an apoptotic-like degeneration (Choi et al., 1996; Miao et al., 2004; Wakayama and Yanagimachi, 1998). However, in Mos−/− eggs, 53% of polar bodies formed bipolar spindles and divided (Fig. 1; Fig. S1) (Choi et al., 1996). Although the polar body divisions mostly occurred in Mos−/− eggs that underwent parthenogenesis, the phenotype is not a feature of parthenogenesis; wild-type eggs that were chemically activated to undergo parthenogenesis did not have polar body divisions (Fig. 1E; Fig. S2C).

We do not fully understand the variability in phenotypes in Mos−/− oocytes and why only a subset undergoes parthenogenesis and have polar bodies that divide. Parthenogenesis and additional egg and polar body divisions require the oscillatory activity of CDK1. The loss of MOS/MAPK signaling results in lower levels of cyclin B1 due to a failure to maintain the inhibition of APC/C (Hörmanseder et al., 2013; Ledan et al., 2001; O'Keefe et al., 1991; Pasternak et al., 2016). We speculate that minor differences in levels of cyclin B1 and CDK1 activity could lead to variability in entering a specific cell cycle state.

Many of the polar bodies underwent division with similar timing to the egg, which led to the question of whether the cytoplasmic connection between Mos−/− eggs and polar bodies is maintained for a longer duration than in wild type. In wild-type oocytes injected with fluorescent dextran, the dextran could pass into the polar body during anaphase I and early and mid-telophase I. In Mos−/− injected oocytes, the dextran could pass in anaphase I, and early, mid and late telophase I (Fig. 3). Furthermore, Mos−/− oocytes had a prolonged telophase I, delayed ∼90 mins in comparison to wild-type oocytes (Fig. 5D). During this time the cytoplasmic content between the egg and the polar body remains connected. At a later timepoint, fluorescent dextran was unable to pass into the polar body, suggesting that abscission does eventually occur in Mos−/− eggs. Overall, our results argue that in Mos−/− oocytes that ultimately parthenogenetically activate, a prolonged connection permits continued transfer of cell cycle regulators between the oocyte and PB1, enabling PB1 to enter a new cell cycle and undergo additional cell divisions, just like the egg.

Furthermore, a large subset of Mos−/− oocytes decondense their chromatin, entering an interphase I-like state directly from telophase I and are still able to exchange cytoplasmic content during this period (Figs 4, 5C; Fig. S4). Previous work has shown that oocytes can enter an interphase-like state before metaphase II (Pasternak et al., 2016), and now we have further pinpointed the stage as mid and late telophase I (Fig. 4). Therefore, there are multiple points that Mos−/− oocytes enter an interphase I state – during telophase I, from metaphase II and after anaphase II (Araki et al., 1996; Cairo et al., 2025; Pasternak et al., 2016; Verlhac et al., 1996). In other organisms like starfish and Xenopus, MOS prevents DNA replication after meiosis I (Sagata et al., 1988; Tachibana et al., 2000). An interesting future direction will be to examine whether, in mice, Mos−/− oocytes undergo DNA replication when they enter an interphase-like state from telophase I.

We also found that ∼90% of Mos−/− oocytes had a disrupted mMB structure. In somatic cells, the midbody component MKLP1 forms a ring structure (Hu et al., 2012; White and Glotzer, 2012). In oocytes, MKLP1 forms a ring and a cap, with the cap pointing towards the polar body (Jung et al., 2023). Our data revealed that most Mos−/− mMBs had an MKLP1 ring but lacked the cap (Fig. 6). The midbody is an active site for new translation in both somatic cells and oocytes, and the cap prevents nascent polypeptides from entering the polar body (Jung et al., 2023). Experiments disrupting the cap showed leakage of the nascent polypeptides into the polar body. The lack of the cap in Mos−/− mMBs could explain why dextran could pass from the oocyte into the polar bodies in late telophase I, a timepoint in which dextran did not pass from most wild-type oocytes into polar bodies (Fig. 3).

How the mMB assembles into a unique structure with a cap during meiosis is currently unknown, but our results demonstrate that MOS/MAPK signaling is essential. Previous studies on mitotic midbodies have shown that ERK1 and ERK2 (ERK1/2; also known as MAPK3 and MAPK1, respectively) localizes to midbodies (Kasahara et al., 2007; Shapiro et al., 1998; Zecevic et al., 1998). Whether it also localizes to meiotic midbodies is not known. However, if ERK1/2 did localize at the midbody, it could then phosphorylate other midbody components to mediate proper assembly and maintenance of the midbody. Because the cap is unique to meiotic midbodies, cap assembly might either require additional proteins or differences in regulation of assembly when compared to midbody assembly in mitosis. Additionally, MOS/MAPK signaling might indirectly affect midbody assembly. MOS has a known role in microtubule organization through the phosphorylation of microtubule-associated proteins (Verlhac et al., 1996). A possible disruption of microtubule organization at the central spindle could affect cap assembly, as complete loss of microtubules at the central spindle causes defects in cap assembly (Jung et al., 2023).

The long duration of telophase I in Mos−/− oocytes is likely due to the activation of the abscission checkpoint. There are multiple triggers of the abscission checkpoint, including lagging chromosomes, ultra-fine bridges and differences in cortical tension (Gibieža and Petrikaitė, 2024). In mitosis, the loss of the integrity of the midbody, by disrupting the nuclear pore complex, has also been shown to activate the abscission checkpoint through the delay in recruiting the proteins involved in scission (Strohacker et al., 2021). We hypothesize that the abnormal midbody structure in Mos−/− oocytes triggers the abscission checkpoint and delays telophase I.

Overall, our results support a model whereby a delayed telophase I and a defective meiotic midbody cap prevent timely abscission and permit prolonged cytoplasmic exchange between the egg and the polar body. We propose that two key factors contribute to the aberrant polar body divisions observed in Mos−/− eggs: (1) parthenogenetic activation of the egg, and (2) a prolonged intracellular bridge between the egg and the polar body that enables leakage of cell cycle regulators from the egg into the polar body. Another key finding from this work is that, following the first meiotic division, polar bodies can enter a new cell cycle, as evidenced by the initiation of DNA replication (Fig. 7). These polar bodies, even when isolated, can survive in culture, segregate chromosomes and undergo morphological changes (Fig. 8). These results indicate that polar bodies can undergo abnormal mitotic divisions, which might ultimately contribute to ovarian germ cell tumor formation.

Our results also have implications for understanding human infertility. Variants of the MOS gene have been identified in women undergoing fertility treatment (Jiao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021, 2022). In vitro fertilization of the eggs from these individuals often results in abnormal early embryonic divisions, resulting in developmental arrest and fragmentation. Embryos derived from these eggs have varying cell sizes. In the future, it would be interesting to determine whether polar bodies themselves contribute to these abnormal divisions in human eggs and embryos.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mouse strains and genotyping

All animal procedures were conducted at the Geisel School of Medicine at Dartmouth College in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Dartmouth in accordance with NIH guidelines (IACUC Protocol# 00002303). Sexually mature wild-type and Mos-knockout (Mos−/−) mice, aged 8–12 weeks, were used for all experiments. The originating Mos+/− heterozygous female and male mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratories (cat. #002723). The knockout mutation was made by introducing a neomycin resistant cassette upstream of the Mos kinase domain in a C57BL/6J background. Experimental animals were generated by mating heterozygous females to either homozygous or heterozygous males. Genotyping was performed using a common forward primer (5'-TCAGCTGCAGAGAACAACTGA-3′) and two allele-specific reverse primers to either detect the wild-type Mos (5'-GTGTACGTGCCCCCTATGTG-3′), or the neomycin resistance cassette in the Mos gene (5'-GCCAGAGGCCACTTGTGTAG-3′). Mice were housed under standard conditions, including a 12-h-light–12-h-dark cycle, ambient temperature [72±3°F (∼22°C)], 30–70% humidity, and access to food and water ad libitum.

Mouse oocyte isolation and maturation

Prior to each experiment, female mice were injected intraperitoneally with 5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; BioVendor R&D RP1782725000) 48 h before oocyte collection. Prophase I-arrested oocytes were collected from ovaries and placed in minimal essential medium (MEM) prepared with Earle's salts (Sigma M0268), 0.1 g pyruvate (Sigma P4562), 1 ml gentamicin (Sigma G1272), 25 ml 1 M HEPES pH 7.3 (Sigma H3784) and 3 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) pH 7.3 (Sigma P2307). To prevent spontaneous meiotic resumption, 2.5 μM milrinone (Sigma-Aldrich, M4659) was added. Oocytes were then cultured in Chatot, Ziomek and Bavister (CZB) medium containing 81.6 mM NaCl (Sigma S5886), 4.8 mM KCl (Sigma P5405), 1.2 mM KH2PO4 (Sigma P5655), 1.2 mM MgSO4 7H2O (Sigma M7774), 0.27 mM Pyruvic acid (Sigma P4562), 1.7 mM CaCl2 2H2O (Sigma C7902), 30.8 mM DL-lactic acid (Sigma L7900), 7 mM taurine (Sigma T0625), 0.1 mM EDTA (Sigma E5134), 25 mM NaHCO3 (Sigma S5761), 0.1% gentamicin, (Sigma G1272) and 0.3% BSA (Sigma A4503). Oocytes were cultured in CZB medium without milrinone to allow meiotic progression. All cultured oocytes were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Each experiment was independently performed at least twice, with multiple control and experimental animals included in each replicate.

To induce parthenogenetic activation, wild-type eggs were matured in CZB medium for 15–16 h following prophase I release. Mature eggs were then washed and incubated in Ca2+-free CZB medium with 10 mM strontium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich 25521) for 2 h. Successful activation was assessed by the appearance of a pronucleus and/or extrusion of a second polar body. Activated eggs were subsequently washed and maintained in CZB for imaging.

Live imaging of mouse oocytes and eggs

For live imaging, Sir-DNA and SPY555–tubulin (Spirochrome) were reconstituted in DMSO and added to CZB medium at final concentrations of 100–250 nM, 45 min to 1 h prior to imaging. Live imaging was performed using a CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope, equipped with a humidified chamber set to 5% CO2 and 37°C. Oocytes and eggs were imaged in 250 μl of CZB medium placed in chambered cover glass dishes (Cellvis C18-1.5H) using a 40× Plan Apochromat λS 1.15 numerical aperture water-immersion objective. All experimental repetitions were imaged on the same microscope under identical imaging settings. Tubulin and DNA signals were captured using exposure times of 200 ms and 30 ms, respectively, with neutral-density filters transmitting 25% (tubulin) and 5% (DNA) of the excitation light. The researcher undertaking these experiments was aware of experimental conditions. For time-lapse imaging, Z-stacks of 13 optical sections at 4 μm intervals were acquired every 20 min for 24 h, allowing for high-resolution monitoring of meiotic progression.

Polar body and egg areas were measured from DIC images of time-lapse images using Fiji/ImageJ software. Both the polar body and the egg were manually outlined using the polygon selection tool, enabling boundary tracing regardless of shape. The researcher undertaking these experiments was aware of experimental conditions. For each egg, PB area was normalized to the corresponding egg area and expressed as a percentage; these values were plotted.

EdU Click-IT assay

To assess DNA replication, incorporated EdU was detected using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 647 Imaging Kit (Invitrogen C10340) following the manufacturer's instructions. Wild-type and Mos−/− eggs were first cultured in CZB medium for 20 h after prophase I release. Eggs were then washed and incubated in CZB supplemented with 10 μM EdU for 12-14 h, followed by fixation in 3.7% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, eggs were washed twice in 3% BSA in PBS, then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. Following two additional washes with 3% BSA in PBS, eggs were incubated with the Click-iT reaction cocktail for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. Eggs were then washed once with 3% BSA in PBS and then mounted on a slide containing 5 μl of Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000) containing DAPI (Life Technologies D1306) at a 1:170 dilution. Imaging was performed using a CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a 60× Plan Apochromat λD 1.42 numerical aperture oil-immersion objective. EdU and DNA were visualized using exposure times of 100 ms and 700 ms, respectively, with neutral-density filters that transmitted 25% of the excitation light for both channels. Z-stacks were acquired at 0.4μm intervals.

Dextran microinjections

To assess separation of the oocyte or egg and the first polar body (PB1), wild-type and Mos−/− eggs (and not PB1) were injected with 10 mg/ml lysine-fixable Texas Red™-labeled fluorescent dextran (Invitrogen D3328) at 13.5 and 18 h following prophase I release. Dextran was reconstituted in cell culture-grade water (Corning 25-055-CM) and stored at −20°C in the dark until use. Injected eggs were imaged 10 min post-injection using a CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a humidified incubation chamber set to 5% CO2 and 37°C. Imaging was performed in 250 μl of CZB medium placed in chambered cover glass dishes (Cellvis C18-1.5H) using a 40× Plan Apochromat λS 1.15 numerical aperture water-immersion objective. Dextran fluorescence marking the cytoplasm was visualized using an exposure time of 100 ms with neutral-density filters transmitting 20% of the excitation light. Using settings for wild-type and Mos−/− eggs, a single-plane image was acquired on the plane of focus containing the polar body.

To characterize the exchange of cytoplasm between the oocyte and PB1 at different stages of meiosis, from anaphase I through metaphase II when abscission is completed, dextran was injected in batches of wild-type and Mos−/− oocytes cultured for 10–14 h following prophase I release. Following microinjections, oocytes were immediately fixed. Fixation occurred for 10 min at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The oocytes were then processed using the immunofluorescence protocol described below to stain for tubulin and DNA. The visualization of tubulin allowed precise staging of anaphase I, early telophase I, mid telophase I, late telophase I and metaphase II. Images were acquired using a 60× Plan Apochromat λD 1.42 numerical aperture oil-immersion objective on the CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope. For both wild-type and Mos−/− eggs, identical imaging settings were used. Z-series were acquired with a 1 µm step size over a total depth of 40 µm.

Immunofluorescence

For immunostaining of DNA and tubulin, wild-type eggs and Mos−/− eggs were fixed 24 h after prophase I release in 2% paraformaldehyde prepared in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. Following fixation, eggs were transferred to blocking solution consisting of 0.3% BSA, 0.01% Tween-20, 0.02% NaN3 in PBS and stored at 4°C for no longer than 48 h. Eggs were then permeabilized in 0.3% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.02% NaN3 in PBS for 20 min and washed three times in blocking solution, each for 10 min. For tubulin detection, eggs were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in blocking solution containing Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-α-tubulin rabbit monoclonal antibody (11H10; Cell Signaling Technology 5063S; dilution of 1:100) in a humidified chamber. Following incubation, eggs were washed three times for 10 mins each in blocking solution. Eggs were then mounted in 5 μl of Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories H-1000) containing DAPI (Life Technologies D1306; 1:170) to detect DNA. Images were acquired with a CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope with a 60× Plan Apochromat λD 1.42 numerical aperture oil-immersion objective. Tubulin and DNA were imaged with exposure times of 700–900 ms, using neutral-density filters transmitting 20–25% of the excitation light. Z-stacks were collected at 0.3 μm intervals.

To visualize midbody components MKLP1 and PRC1, along with tubulin and DNA, wild-type and Mos−/− eggs were fixed using 2% paraformaldehyde at 9–11.5 h post prophase I release. Fixed eggs were permeabilized for 20 min at room temperature, followed by three 10 min washes in blocking solution. Eggs were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a humidified chamber with primary antibody against either MKLP1 or PRC1, diluted in blocking solution. The primary antibodies used were: rabbit anti-MKLP1 polyclonal antibody (Novus NBP2-56923; 1:100) and rabbit anti-PRC1 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech 15617-1-AP; 1:100). After primary incubation, eggs were washed three times, 10 min each, in blocking solution. Next, eggs were incubated in blocking solution with Alexa Fluor488-conjugated mouse anti-alpha-tubulin monoclonal antibody (DM1A; Cell Signaling Technology 8058S; 1:100) and the appropriate secondary antibody to detect either MKLP1 or PRC1: Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H+L; Life Technologies A10042; 1:200) or Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit-IgG (H+L; Invitrogen, 1:200), respectively. Following incubation, the eggs were then washed three times with blocking solution for 10 min each and mounted in 5 μl of Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories H-1000) containing DAPI (Life Technologies D1306; 1:170) to detect DNA.

Oocyte images were acquired with a CSU-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a 60× Plan Apochromat λD 1.42 numerical aperture oil-immersion objective. MKLP1 or PRC1, tubulin, and DNA were visualized with exposure times of 800 (MKLP1 and PRC1), 800 (tubulin) and 600 ms (DAPI), with neutral-density filters transmitting 25%, 25% and 20% of excitation light, respectively. Z-stacks were acquired at 0.4 μm intervals, covering a total depth of 30–35 μm. High-resolution images of MKLP1 or PRC1, tubulin and DNA were captured using the super-resolution mode of the CSU-SoRa system with 2× SoRa magnification and the same objective. For these images, MKLP1 or PRC1, tubulin, and DNA were visualized with exposure times of 400 ms, 900 ms and 600 ms, respectively, with all channels using 20% neutral-density filters. Z-stacks were collected at 0.2 μm intervals across a total depth of 30–35 μm.

PB1 cultures

Oocytes were harvested and released from prophase I in CZB medium at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 3 h, oocytes that had undergone germinal vesicle breakdown (GVBD), identified by the absence of a pronucleus, were isolated and then denuded, as previously described (Fan et al., 2021). Briefly, oocytes were bathed through droplets of acidic Tyrode's solution (Sigma Aldrich MR-004-D) for 40 s, followed by 20 s in CZB medium containing 0.05% Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EO0491). Denuded oocytes were washed in fresh CZB droplets and then incubated in CZB at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 10 h, cultures were monitored for abscised first polar bodies (PB1s), which appeared as free-floating cells in the medium. Naturally abscised PB1s were transferred to fresh CZB culture medium and maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. Separately cultured PB1s were examined hourly under a stereoscope to assess viability and cell division-like morphology.

To further analyze additional-division-like phenotype observed for Mos−/− PB1s WT and Mos−/− PB1s were subjected to live-cell imaging (described above) in the presence of 250 nM SiR-DNA and SPY555–tubulin (Spirochrome). Images were acquired every 5 min for 12 h. For Z-series acquisition, a 4 µm step size was used to cover a total depth of 60 µm.
