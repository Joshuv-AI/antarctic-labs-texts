An enteric neuron ionotropic receptor regulates salt stress resistance
Authors: Jihye Yeon, Jinmahn Kim, Koji Sato, Stephen Nurrish, Laurie Chen, Nikhila Krishnan, Sam Bates, Sayoko Ihara, Sina Rasouli, Charmi Porwal, Vivek Venkatachalam, Kazushige Touhara, Piali Sengupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe detection of internal chemicals by interoceptive chemosensory pathways is critical for regulating metabolism and physiology1. The molecular identities of interoceptors, and the functional consequences of chemosensation by specific interoceptive neurons, remain to be fully described. The pharyngeal neuronal network of Caenorhabditis elegans is anatomically and functionally analogous to the mammalian enteric nervous system2,3. Here we show that the I3 pharyngeal enteric neuron responds to cations via an I3-specific ionotropic receptor to regulate salt stress tolerance. The GLR-9 ionotropic receptor and the GLR-7 IR25a co-receptor orthologue localize to the gut lumen-exposed sensory ending of I3, and are necessary and sufficient for salt sensation. Salt detection by I3 protects specifically against high-salt stress, as glr-9 mutants show reduced tolerance of hypertonic salt but not of sugar solutions, with or without prior acclimatization. Whereas cholinergic signalling from I3 promotes tolerance of acute high-salt stress, peptidergic signalling from I3 during acclimatization is essential for resistance to a subsequent high-salt challenge. Transcriptomic and reporter gene analyses show that I3 modulates salt tolerance in part by regulating the expression of salt stress response genes in distal tissues. Correspondingly, mutations in a subset of salt- and GLR-9-regulated genes reduce salt stress resistance. Our results describe the mechanisms by which chemosensation mediated by a defined enteric neuron regulates physiological homeostasis in response to a specific abiotic stress.Subject terms: Sensory processing, Ion channels in the nervous system, Stress and resilience

Main

Animals have evolved multiple mechanisms to detect and cope with toxic chemicals that adversely affect health and longevity. A first line of defence involves behavioural avoidance mediated by exteroceptive olfactory and gustatory neurons4,5. Ingested chemicals are detected directly or indirectly by non-neuronal cells, gut-intrinsic enteric neurons and extrinsic sensory afferents to regulate metabolic homeostasis6–9. Unlike exteroceptive chemotransduction, the molecular mechanisms and functional roles of interoceptive chemosensation remain to be fully described.

Inorganic cations are essential for multiple physiological processes and are typically attractive at low concentrations but harmful at higher concentrations10,11. High salt is detected by gustatory neurons to drive avoidance, and by interoceptive neurons in organs such as the gut and brain to modulate salt and fluid intake11,12. Although the toxic effects of high salt are generally attributed to osmotic stress, differential cellular and physiological responses to equiosmolar salt and sugar solutions suggest that individual hypertonic solutes can be discriminated12–14.

Salt sensation is mediated by diverse molecules across species, including amiloride-sensitive Na+ channels in mammals and an expanded family of ionotropic receptors related to ionotropic glutamate receptors in Drosophila11,12,15. In Drosophila, distinct ionotropic receptor protein complexes in different external and internal taste sensilla detect a range of salt concentrations11,16–18. Although ionotropic receptor proteins are encoded by all protostome genomes19, their chemosensory roles beyond insects remain to be definitively established.

The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans is attracted to low salt but avoids high salt using ciliated chemosensory neurons in head and tail sense organs5. High NaCl rapidly paralyses and eventually kills worms, whereas prior exposure to sub-lethal NaCl concentrations increases tolerance (acclimatization)14. Although high sucrose also rapidly paralyses worms14, differential survival on equiosmolar NaCl and sugar suggests that as in mammals, unidentified salt-specific sensors and pathways may regulate salt stress14.

Food ingestion in C. elegans is driven by the foregut or pharynx2,20, a neuromuscular organ that is innervated by a densely interconnected, autonomously functioning network of 20 pharyngeal neurons that exhibit developmental, anatomical and functional analogy to the mammalian enteric nervous system2,3,21. Fifteen of these pharyngeal enteric neurons (PENs) exhibit sensory features, although only two PENs have been shown to respond to bacterial compounds to regulate feeding and foraging22,23.

Here we show that the single I3 PEN in C. elegans responds to multiple monovalent and divalent cations and regulates salt stress resistance. Salt detection is mediated by the I3-specific ionotropic receptor-related protein GLR-9 and the IR25a co-receptor orthologue GLR-7, which are necessary and sufficient for salt responses. I3 signals via cholinergic and peptidergic pathways to specifically increase tolerance of high salt in unacclimatized and acclimatized worms, respectively. Salt acts through GLR-9-dependent and GLR-9-independent pathways to reprogramme gene expression in distal tissues, including the epidermis and gut; disruption of this programme in glr-9 mutants likely underlies their salt-sensitivity phenotype. Together, our results identify a chemosensory role for an ionotropic receptor in nematodes and reveal how a single enteric neuron detects and relays the presence of ingested cations to counter salt toxicity.

GLR-9 localizes to the gut-exposed ending of I3

Seven of the 15 predicted ionotropic receptors related to glutamate-gated cation channels in C. elegans exhibit features that are characteristic of sensory ionotropic receptors in insects, including variable amino-terminal domains, absence of canonical glutamate-binding residues and loss of conserved pore sequences required for ion permeability15,19,24 (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a). Six of these ionotropic receptor-like genes are exclusively or highly expressed in subsets of PENs24,25 (Extended Data Fig. 1b). In particular, the W02A2.5 ionotropic receptor gene (hereafter referred to as glr-9) is expressed only in the single I3 PEN (Extended Data Fig. 1b), suggesting a specialized sensory role in this neuron.

Consistent with transcriptomics data, an endogenously GFP-tagged GLR-9 fusion protein was expressed only in I3, and localized to an asymmetric structure at the sensory ending projecting towards the interior of the worm (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Video 1). Electron microscope reconstruction of the sensory ending of I3 showed a corresponding expanded, curved structure2,3 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Video 2) with its internal side adjacent to the pharyngeal lumen. Although ionotropic receptor proteins localize to sensory cilia in Drosophila15,26,27, I3 is not ciliated and does not express ciliary genes25. The partial overlap of GLR-9::GFP with the membrane marker (Fig. 1b) suggests that GLR-9 localizes to a specialized membrane domain at the I3 sensory ending. We infer that GLR-9 may be positioned in I3 to directly sample ingested molecules in the pharyngeal lumen.

In insects, sensory ionotropic receptors typically function as heterotetramers comprised of a stimulus-specific tuning receptor and co-receptor(s) that traffic the complex to sensory cilia26,27. Similar to other tuning ionotropic receptors, GLR-9 lacks an extended amino-terminal domain (Extended Data Fig. 1a), suggesting that it acts as a tuning receptor. The IR25a co-receptor is deeply conserved in protostomes and is considered the ancestral chemosensory ionotropic receptor19. In C. elegans, the glr-7 IR25a orthologue is expressed in a few neurons with highest expression in I3 (ref. 25) (Extended Data Fig. 1b). The glr-7 locus is predicted to encode two isoforms (https://www.wormbase.org) that differ only in their N termini (Extended Data Fig. 1c). A GLR-7 protein with a fluorescent reporter fused to the C terminus of the genomic sequence localized to the I3 sensory ending and colocalized with GLR-9 (Fig. 1d,e). In glr-7 mutants, GLR-9 mislocalized to the soma and vice versa (Fig. 1f), similar to the reported reciprocal targeting of tuning and co-receptor ionotropic receptors to sensory cilia in Drosophila27. These results raise the possibility that GLR-9 and GLR-7 may comprise a sensory receptor complex in I3.

GLR-9 and GLR-7 mediate salt responses

To determine whether the GLR-9–GLR-7 complex detects ingested chemicals, we assessed intracellular calcium dynamics in I3 neurons expressing GCaMP6s. I3 responded robustly to bacterial supernatant and LB growth medium (Extended Data Fig. 2a) suggesting I3 responds to one or more chemicals in LB. Further analyses showed that I3 responded to multiple mono- and divalent salts (Fig. 2a). Substitution of Na+ with N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG) but not Cl− with gluconate abolished responses (Fig. 2b), suggesting that I3 responds non-selectively to cations. No responses were observed to high concentrations of fructose, sorbitol or glycerol (Fig. 2c), indicating that I3 does not respond to high osmolarity.

I3 responded to a range of salt concentrations with largely tonic responses that returned to baseline after stimulus removal (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 2b–f). Salt responses were maintained in unc-13 and unc-31 mutants, which abolish synaptic or neuropeptidergic transmission, respectively28,29 (Extended Data Fig. 2g), implying that I3 responds to salts cell-autonomously. PENs are densely interconnected via gap junctions2,3. Imaging of salt-evoked intracellular calcium dynamics across all twenty pharyngeal neurons showed that only I3 responded to salt under our conditions (Fig. 2d, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Video 3), further supporting the cell autonomy of I3 responses. Salt responses in I3 were also unaffected in worms that were mutant for genes required for chemosensation in ciliated chemosensory neurons, as well as for fate specification of the salt-sensing ASE chemosensory neurons5,30 (Extended Data Fig. 4a). All examined salt responses in I3 were abolished in glr-9 and glr-7 null mutants (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 4b). Salt responses were restored in glr-9 mutants after I3-specific expression of wild-type glr-9 sequences under its endogenous promoter or an I3-selective srg-42 promoter25 (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 4c). We conclude that GLR-9 and GLR-7 mediate I3 responses to a broad range of salts.

We next tested whether GLR-9 and GLR-7 are sufficient to confer salt responses. GLR-9, ectopically expressed in salt-sensing ASE neurons with or without GLR-7, did not to traffic to sensory cilia and instead accumulated in the soma (Extended Data Fig. 5a). However, co-expression of both proteins but not each protein alone led to localization to the sensory endings of the non salt-sensing I1 PENs (Fig. 2d), which are also apposed to the gut lumen2,3 (Extended Data Fig. 5b), and was sufficient to confer NaCl responses (Fig. 2f). In addition, heterologous expression of GLR-9 together with the GLR-7a and GLR-7b isoforms, but not each single receptor alone, in HEK293T cells resulted in dose-dependent increases in intracellular calcium (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 5c) that were decreased by chelating extracellular calcium with EGTA (Fig. 2g). Together, these results indicate that GLR-9 and GLR-7 are necessary and sufficient to mediate salt responses.

GLR-9 and GLR-7 promote salt stress resistance

To assess the functional consequences of I3-mediated salt sensation, we first examined salt-regulated behaviours in glr-9 mutants. Both wild-type and glr-9 mutant worms were robustly attracted to 75 mM NaCl but avoided 250 mM NaCl in microfluidics behavioural arenas31 (Extended Data Fig. 6a). glr-9 mutants also avoided high glycerol concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 6b), and exhibited no defects in pharyngeal pumping frequency in low and high salt concentrations on bacterial food (Extended Data Fig. 6c). These observations suggest that salt sensing by I3 may contribute to organismal responses other than taxis or feeding.

C. elegans is typically grown on medium containing 50 mM NaCl, which is considered isotonic. When moved to medium containing >500 mM NaCl, worms rapidly lose cell volume, become immotile from loss of turgor pressure and eventually die14. Since salt-specific pathways independent of hypertonicity have been implicated in salt toxicity14, we tested whether I3-mediated salt sensation regulates high-salt tolerance. Wild-type worms became immotile within 4–10 min when transferred from 50 mM NaCl to 500 mM NaCl or high concentrations of other I3-sensed salts (Fig. 3a). Both glr-9 and glr-7 mutants were paralysed more rapidly on high salt but not equiosmolar sugar (Fig. 3b,c and Extended Data Fig. 6d), suggesting decreased tolerance specifically to high salt. This phenotype was rescued after expression of wild-type glr-9 sequences under its endogenous promoter or the I3-selective srg-42 promoter (Fig. 3b). We conclude that I3 activation specifically by salt is partly protective against acute high-salt stress.

Pre-adaptation of C. elegans to 200–250 mM NaCl for 24 h increases high-salt tolerance14. This acclimatization and osmotolerance is thought to be mediated in part via increased expression of osmolyte biosynthetic enzymes14,32,33. Wild-type worms pre-exposed to 250 mM NaCl or 250 mM KH2PO4 for 3 h continued to move for more than 30 min when placed on 500 mM NaCl or 500 mM KH2PO4, respectively (Fig. 3d,e). By contrast, a significant fraction of glr-9 and glr-7 mutants became immotile within 5–10 min under the same conditions (Fig. 3d,e). The acclimatization defect of glr-9 mutants was rescued after I3-specific expression of wild-type glr-9 sequences under the glr-9 or srg-42 promoters25 (Fig. 3d). Mutations in genes required for sensory transduction in ciliated chemosensory neurons did not affect acclimatization (Extended Data Fig. 6e), indicating that these neurons do not contribute to high-salt resistance. Since I3 exhibits responses to multiple salts, we tested whether acclimatization to one salt confers tolerance to high concentrations of a different salt. Pre-exposure to 250 mM NaCl increased tolerance of wild-type but not glr-9 mutant worms to 500 mM KH2PO4 (Fig. 3f). We conclude that GLR-9 and GLR-7-dependent salt responses in I3 are necessary for tolerance of high salts in both unacclimatized and acclimatized conditions.

Signalling from I3 regulates salt tolerance

Osmoregulatory mechanisms operate in multiple tissues and organs to regulate osmotolerance in C. elegans32,33, suggesting that I3 may signal to one or more of these tissues to regulate salt stress resistance. I3 is a cholinergic neuron and also expresses multiple neuropeptides3,25. Knocking out the unc-17 vesicular acetylcholine transporter specifically in I334 resulted in rapid paralysis on high salt similar to glr-9 mutants (Fig. 4a), indicating that cholinergic signalling from I3 is necessary for acute salt tolerance. However, loss of unc-17 in I3 did not affect salt tolerance following acclimatization (Fig. 4b). By contrast, knocking out the egl-21 carboxypeptidase required for processing of a subset of neuropeptides34 specifically in I3 reduced high-salt tolerance after acclimatization but not in acute salt tolerance assays (Fig. 4a,b). I3 responses to 500 mM NaCl were unaltered after acclimatization (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We conclude that cholinergic signalling from I3 mediates acute salt tolerance, whereas peptidergic signalling is required for acclimatization-dependent salt stress resistance.

To determine when neuropeptide signalling from I3 is necessary to regulate salt tolerance following acclimatization, we expressed the auxin receptor TIR1 specifically in I3 in a strain in which the unc-31 CAPS-related protein required for dense core vesicle fusion28 is endogenously tagged with an auxin-inducible degron (AID) sequence35. Auxin treatment during both acclimatization and exposure to high salt resulted in reduced tolerance further supporting the necessity of peptidergic signalling from I3 for tolerance of high salt (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Addition of auxin only during the 3 h acclimatization period but not during high-salt exposure was sufficient to reduce salt tolerance (Fig. 4c), indicating that peptidergic signalling from I3 is necessary during acclimatization to promote high-salt tolerance.

Neuronal transcriptomics analyses indicate that I3 expresses at least 24 neuropeptide genes25. We found that worms mutant for the flp-6 FMRFamide-like neuropeptide did not tolerate high salt following acclimatization, but showed no defects in acute salt tolerance (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). The salt tolerance phenotype of acclimatized glr-9; flp-6 double mutants was similar to that of the single mutants, suggesting that these molecules act in a linear pathway (Fig. 4d). Moreover, the salt tolerance phenotype of worms lacking flp-6 expression specifically in I3 was similar to that of flp-6 mutants indicating that flp-6 acts in I3 (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 7d). Although FLP-6 has been shown to interact with multiple broadly expressed candidate neuropeptide receptors36, mutations in single candidate receptor genes had little effect on salt acclimatization (Extended Data Fig. 7e), suggesting that FLP-6 receptors may act redundantly to regulate salt tolerance. Together, our results indicate that cholinergic signalling from I3 increases tolerance of acute salt stress. By contrast, FLP-6 neuropeptide signalling from I3 during acclimatization is protective in response to high-salt exposure (summarized in Fig. 4f).

GLR-9 modulates salt-regulated gene expression

Increased salt resistance following acclimatization occurs in part owing to altered expression of molecules such as cuticular collagens and osmolyte biosynthetic enzymes that regulate the permeability of the cuticle of the worm and concentration of internal osmolytes, respectively14,37–40. We hypothesized that I3 signalling regulates the expression of a subset of these genes in distal tissues, including the cuticle, epidermis and gut, and that mis-regulated expression in glr-9 mutants contributes to their decreased salt resistance. To test this notion, we compared the transcriptional profiles of wild-type and glr-9 mutants before and after salt acclimatization.

Principal component analyses indicated that transcriptional profiles clustered by both genotype and condition (Extended Data Fig. 8a). We found that 92 genes were significantly up-regulated and 33 genes were significantly down-regulated in wild-type worms following acclimatization (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 1). A small subset of these differentially expressed genes overlapped with those identified previously using partly distinct experimental conditions40,41 (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Up-regulated molecules included gpdh-1 and gpdh-2 (glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase), pgph-2 (phosphoglycolate phosphatase) and tre-5 (trehalase), which encode enzymes contributing to the production of protective osmolytes such as glycerol14,40–44 (Fig. 5a,c and Supplementary Data 1). The expression of a subset of collagen genes was also altered, consistent with a role for cuticular components in regulating hypertonic stress responses32,33,38,40,41,45,46 (Fig. 5a, Extended Data Fig. 8c and Supplementary Data 1). Previous work reported up-regulation of fipr genes encoding predicted secreted anti-fungal peptides after exposure of embryos to 500 mM NaCl47; we noted up-regulation of fipr-26 after salt exposure in adult worms (Fig. 5a,c and Supplementary Data 1).

Acclimatization resulted in altered expression of a larger set of genes in glr-9 mutants (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 8c,d,f and Supplementary Data 1). However, the expression of gpdh-1, pgph-2, tre-5 and fipr-26 was not significantly altered in glr-9 mutants (Fig. 5b,c and Supplementary Data 1), indicating that I3-mediated signalling in response to salt is necessary for their regulation. The expression of a subset of genes, including those encoding cuticular collagens, was regulated similarly in both wild-type and glr-9 mutants following acclimatization, whereas the expression of a subset of genes involved in stress responses, metabolism and cuticle or hypodermis biogenesis was specifically altered in acclimatized glr-9 mutants (Extended Data Fig. 8c,d and Supplementary Data 1). We infer that altered gene expression after salt acclimatization is mediated via both salt-specific GLR-9-dependent pathways and general hypertonic stress-regulated GLR-9-independent pathways.

The transcriptional profile of glr-9 mutants was different from that of wild-type worms at 50 mM NaCl (Extended Data Fig. 8a,c,e,f and Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that chronic I3-mediated signalling regulates physiology even at isotonic salt concentrations. Up-regulated genes in glr-9 mutants included those involved in metabolism and stress, cuticle and hypodermis components such as collagen and chitinase-like genes, and nematode-specific groundhog-like (GRL) and ground-domain (GRD) secreted proteins of the apical extracellular matrix of the cuticle and precuticle48 (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 8c,e and Supplementary Data 1). These observations suggest that chronic glr-9 signalling may indicate the presence of salt under standard growth conditions, and that structural remodelling of the cuticular barrier in the absence of this signalling could contribute to the decreased resistance of these worms to acute salt stress.

To assess the role of salt- and GLR-9-dependent transcriptional changes, we examined the expression of a subset of regulated genes in vivo, and determined their contribution to salt tolerance. We generated strains in which the gpdh-1, tre-5 and the fipr-26 genes were tagged at their endogenous loci to co-express a nuclear-localized mStayGold reporter protein (Methods) that enables cell identification and quantification of transcriptional changes49. As reported previously14, gpdh-1 was up-regulated after acclimatization; this up-regulation was observed in intestinal but not hypodermal cells, and was significantly decreased in glr-9 mutants (Fig. 5d,e). tre-5 was expressed only in the amphid sheath glial cells (Fig. 5d). Although this gene is predicted to be transcriptionally up-regulated in acclimatized wild-type worms (Fig. 5a,c), we did not detect altered expression, possibly owing to regulation via post-transcriptional mechanisms (Fig. 5d,e). However, tre-5 expression was significantly down-regulated in glr-9 mutants after acclimatization, consistent with measurements from transcriptional profiling (Fig. 5c–e). Expression of fipr-26 was up-regulated after acclimatization in hypodermal cells in wild-type worms; this up-regulation was abolished in glr-9 mutants (Fig. 5d,e and Extended Data Fig. 8g).

Whereas knocking out gpdh-1 alone has little effect on glycerol accumulation after osmotic stress, worms doubly mutant for both gpdh-1 and gpdh-2 exhibit markedly decreased glycerol accumulation in hypertonic environments14,38. We found that gpdh-1; gpdh-2 double mutants, but not each single mutant alone, exhibited strong defects in salt acclimatization (Fig. 5f). Although tre-5 single mutants showed no defects in salt acclimatization (Fig. 5f), worms mutant for all five trehalase gene paralogues50 exhibited significant salt acclimatization defects (Fig. 5f). fipr-26 deletion mutants also exhibited strong defects in their ability to acclimatize to salt (Fig. 5f). Together, these results support the notion that regulated expression of a subset of genes after salt acclimatization contributes to salt tolerance, and that mis-regulation of these and other genes after salt exposure may in part underlie the salt tolerance defects of glr-9 mutants.

We tested two additional predictions that arise from the transcriptional profiling data. Wild-type worms moved from 50 mM NaCl to 400–500 mM NaCl shrink and become paralysed32,51, but whereas prolonged exposure to 500 mM NaCl is lethal14, worms regain body volume and motility after 2–4 h on 400 mM NaCl via modulation of ion transport and up-regulation of osmotolerance genes51. Consistent with the inability of glr-9 mutants to up-regulate osmotolerance genes, and their extensively remodelled cuticle in isotonic conditions, these worms regained motility more slowly on 400 mM NaCl (Extended Data Fig. 9a), supporting a critical role for I3 signalling-regulated gene expression changes in regulating salt tolerance. In addition, our finding that salt acclimatization results in partly GLR-9-dependent, salt-specific gene expression changes suggests that different hypertonic solutes may regulate distinct gene subsets, with only a shared subset being regulated by general osmotic stress. This hypothesis predicts that acclimatization to high salt would not be protective for high sugar and vice versa. We found this to be the case for both wild-type and glr-9 mutants (Extended Data Fig. 9b), suggesting that partly distinct pathways contribute to the detection of individual hypertonic solutes to regulate specific organismal stress responses.

Discussion

Here we show that salt sensing by the single I3 enteric neuron promotes salt stress resistance in C. elegans. I3 salt responses require the GLR-9 and GLR-7 ionotropic receptor proteins, which are necessary and sufficient for salt detection. I3 signals via cholinergic and peptidergic signalling to increase salt stress resistance in unacclimatized and salt-acclimatized worms, respectively. We find that signalling from I3 in response to high salt contributes to expression changes of a subset of molecules that regulate cuticle composition and internal osmolyte concentration, and that the combined gene expression changes driven by I3 and general hypertonic stress-sensitive pathways results in specific tolerance of high salt (summarized in Fig. 5g). Our observations describe the molecular and neuronal mechanisms by which an enteric neuron regulates physiological homeostasis in response to a specific abiotic stress.

Chemoreceptors are among the fastest evolving gene families in multicellular animals, with some gene families being lost or gained within specific lineages52,53. For example, ionotropic receptor sensory proteins are found in all protostomes but are absent in deuterostomes19,52. So far, chemosensory roles for ionotropic receptors have been described or suggested mainly in arthropods15,54,55. Our identification of a chemosensory function of an ionotropic receptor in C. elegans implies that ionotropic receptors may have retained chemosensory functions in the protostome lineage. Whether other PEN-expressed ionotropic receptors in C. elegans also function as chemosensors, or detect other sensory cues similar to some Drosophila and mosquito ionotropic receptors56 remains to be determined.

glr-9 mutants exhibit broad gene expression changes under isotonic conditions that may impact hypertonic salt resistance. Growth on 50 mM NaCl can influence ageing and lifespan, suggesting that even low salt concentrations are mildly stressful57. Since I3 responds tonically to salt, we propose that chronic activation of GLR-9 and GLR-7 and cholinergic signalling from I3 in low salt enhances resistance to acute high salt. Remodelling of the cuticle in the absence of I3 signalling may sensitize worms to high-salt stress, resulting in decreased acute salt tolerance (Fig. 5g). During acclimatization, prolonged exposure to 250 mM salt drives FLP-6-mediated signalling from I3, which acts with inputs from other neuronal and non-neuronal osmosensory pathways to regulate broad gene expression changes that promote high-salt tolerance. The failure of acclimatized glr-9 mutants to alter expression of a subset of these genes, along with their mis-regulated gene expression under isotonic conditions, may underlie their decreased salt tolerance phenotype (Fig. 5g). Consistently, we find that mutations in a subset of salt-regulated genes result in salt tolerance defects. Additional pathways are likely to contribute to salt tolerance under different conditions and timepoints.

Several lines of evidence suggest that C. elegans and other animals can discriminate among specific hypertonic solutes. First, equiosmolar concentrations of salt and sucrose result in different survival rates in C. elegans14,51,58. Second, glr-9 mutants exhibit decreased tolerance of high salts but not equiosmolar sugar. Third, acclimatization to NaCl does not confer resistance to sucrose and vice versa (although see ref. 58), and finally, hypertonic salt regulates the expression of a subset of osmotolerance genes in a GLR-9-dependent manner. In rodents, arterial blood pressure regulation and sympathetic nerve activity are more sensitive to central NaCl infusion than to equiosmolar sugars13, and subsets of OVLT neurons in the circumventricular organs of the brain preferentially respond to hypertonic NaCl over sugars12,13, indicating the existence of NaCl-specific, non-osmosensory pathways. Given the critical role of salts in regulating physiological processes, we suggest that dedicated neuronal pathways for detection of high salt provide flexibility in coordinating responses on the basis of internal state.

Enteric sensory neurons in C. elegans appear to utilize partly distinct sensory mechanisms from those of somatic exteroceptive sensory neurons. The GUR-3 gustatory receptor-like protein required for hydrogen peroxide detection by the I2 PEN, and the DEL-3 and DEL-7 acid-sensing ion channels that detect ingested bacterial cues in the NSM PEN are expressed exclusively or mainly in PENs22,23,25. Similarly, 6 of the 7 ionotropic receptors, including GLR-7 and GLR-9, are expressed mainly or highly in PENs. Unlike exteroceptive neurons, PENs are non-ciliated, suggesting novel mechanisms for receptor trafficking and localization. The enteric neuronal network has ancient evolutionary origins and is thought to have arisen prior to and independently of the central nervous system59. Investigating the mechanisms underlying enteric neuron development and function across species may provide insights into ancestral neuronal strategies, and broaden our understanding of interoceptive sensory functions.

Methods

Growth of C. elegans strains

C. elegans strains were grown on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 at 20 °C. unc-122p::dsRed or gfp were used as co-injection markers (at 40 ng µl−1) to generate transgenic strains unless noted otherwise. vha-6p::NLS::gfp or vha-6p::mCherry was used as a co-injection marker at 10 ng µl−1 with 65 ng µl−1 of 1 kb DNA ladder (New England BioLabs) to a final concentration of 100 ng µl−1 to generate PY12814, PY12815, PY12816, PY12821, PY12822 and PY12823 (Supplementary Table 1). Strains were generated using standard genetic crosses. The PY12829 strain was obtained by deleting glr-9 via gene editing to generate glr-9(oy224) in the PY12818 strain. The genotypes of all strains were verified by sequencing or phenotypic examination. All experiments were performed using one-day-old adult hermaphrodites grown under well-fed conditions for at least three generations. A list of all strains used in this work is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Molecular biology

1.1 kb of sequences upstream of and including the glr-9 START codon and 1.9 kb of sequences upstream of and including the srg-42 START codon were amplified from wild-type worm lysate and used to drive I3-specific or I3-selective expression. Expression in I1 was driven using 1.1 kb of sequences upstream of the lgc-8 START codon. To generate lgc-8p::glr-7::mStayGold::SL2::mScarlet-I3 and lgc-8p::glr-9::mStayGold::SL2::mScarlet-I3, mStayGold::SL2::mScarlet-I3 was amplified by PCR from gBlock fragments and used to generate PSAB1400 and PSAB1401 by Gibson assembly (Supplementary Table 2). glr-9 cDNA or genomic DNA was amplified from wild-type worm lysate and tagged with gfp for localization and functional rescue experiments. Since glr-7 has a and b isoforms, the genomic region of glr-7 from the START codon of the glr-7b isoform (X: 2,419,477) to the STOP codon of both glr-7a and glr-7b isoforms (X:2,414,064) was amplified from wild-type worm lysate. These sequences were tagged with fluorescent reporter sequences to examine localization of glr-7. Full-length cDNAs encoding GLR-9, GLR-7a and GLR-7b amplified from worm lysates were cloned into the pME18s vector for expression in HEK293T cells. All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing. A list of plasmids used in this work is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing

Deletion alleles of glr-9, osm-9 and fipr-26 were generated using an injection mix containing 0.5 μl of Cas9 (IDT), 1.5 μl of 100 μM trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA), 1 μl of 50 μM 5′ CRISPR RNA (crRNA), 1 μl of 50 μM 3′-crRNA, 1 μl of 17 μM dpy-10 crRNA as a co-CRISPR injection marker, and nuclease-free water, for a total volume of 10 μl. No DNA donor was used. Worms were maintained at 25 °C after injection. Three days following injection, dumpy or roller F1 progeny were segregated onto individual plates. After the singled F1 worms laid eggs, parents were lysed and then genotyped using PCR.

To generate the flp-6::loxP insertion strain (PY12288, Supplementary Table 1), the injection mix included 0.5 μl of Cas9 (IDT), 2.8 μl of 34 μM crRNA, 0.2 μl of 100 μM tracrRNA, 2 μl of 26 μM donor DNA, 50 ng µl−1 of column-purified unc-122p::gfp, and nuclease-free water for a total volume of 20 μl. Following injection, worms were kept at 25 °C. F1 progeny carrying the unc-122p::gfp co-injection marker were singled onto individual plates and genotyped. The C-terminal loxP site was inserted first, followed by insertion of the N-terminal loxP site. All tracrRNA, crRNAs, and donor DNAs were obtained from IDT.

To generate nuclear SL2::mStayGold insertions (PY12818, PY12819, PY12820, Supplementary Table 1), the injection mix included 0.5 μl of Cas9 (IDT), 2.8 μl of 34 μM crRNA, 0.5 μl of 100 μM tracrRNA, and pRF4 rol-6(gf) plasmid as an injection marker. Repair templates were obtained by PCR from each plasmid, gel purified and cleaned up using AMPureX beads, and 500 ng was added to the injection mix. Injected worms were singled and 96 F1 progeny were singled from plates that contained rollers, allowed to starve out the plate, and then screened by PCR for the expected change. The sequences of the crRNAs and donor DNA are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Fluorescent reporter imaging

One-day-old adult worms were immobilized with 10 mM (−)-tetramisole hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich L9756) and mounted on 10% agarose pads on microscope slides. Images in Fig. 1b, left, and Fig. 1d, left, were acquired on an inverted Zeiss Axiovert with a Yokogawa CSU-X1spinning disk confocal unit and a Photometrics Quantum 512SC camera. Slidebook 6.0 (Intelligent Imaging Innovations, 3i) software was used to acquire images with z-step sizes of 0.2 or 0.27 μm using a 63× oil immersion objective. High resolution images in Fig. 1b, right, Fig. 1d, right, and Fig. 1e were acquired at 0.21–0.23 μm z-intervals using a 100× oil immersion objective on an inverted LSM880 Airyscan confocal microscope using ZEN software (Zeiss). Histograms were adjusted for visualization after Airyscan processing to optimize resolution and contrast. Images in Figs. 1f, 5d and Extended Data Figs. 4c and 5a,b were acquired at 0.2 μm z-intervals using a 63× oil immersion objective on an inverted two-camera spinning disk confocal microscope (Leica DMI6000B with a CSU-W1 spinning disk head and two Andor Neo sCMOS cameras) and Andor IQ3.5 Software. All images were acquired in the Brandeis Light Microscopy Facility (RRID: SCR_025892) and processed using FIJI/Image J.

Reconstruction of I3 sensory ending

Serial section electron microscope (EM) images of I3 were obtained from Wormimage (https://www.wormimage.org). I3 was identified as ‘blue 14’ on the electron microscopy images (with the assistance of D. Hall), specifically from the sections N2T_114522 to N2T_114583 in the legacy series2,60. The TrakEM2 plugin in Fiji was used to manually align I3 images. Following the alignment, manual segmentation was performed using 3dmod v4.11 from the IMOD suite (https://bio3d.colorado.edu/imod/) to generate the 3D reconstruction of the I3 sensory ending.

Acute salt stress assay

Acute responses to high salt were examined using a protocol adapted from ref. 37. Six-centimetre unseeded NGM plates supplemented with the indicated salt concentrations were divided into two sections to assess the behaviours of wild-type and experimental worms in parallel on the same plate. Growth-synchronized 1-day-old adult hermaphrodites were transferred from NGM plates containing 50 mM NaCl to high salt-containing plates to initiate the assay. Each assay included ten worms of each genotype, and the time at which all ten worms were unresponsive to touch with a platinum wire was recorded. Unresponsive was defined as failure to move after prodding with a platinum wire at the nose and tail, and cessation of pharyngeal pumping.

Salt acclimatization assay

Acclimatization experiments were performed using an adaptation of a previously published protocol37. Thirty-five growth-synchronized 1-day adult hermaphrodites were transferred from NGM growth plates (50 mM NaCl) to NGM plates containing 250 mM NaCl, 250 mM KH2PO4 or 250 mM sucrose and seeded with 200 μl E. coli OP50. Worms were maintained at 20 °C for 3 h. Following 3 h of acclimatization, worms were transferred to unseeded plates containing 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM KH2PO4 or 500 mM or 1 M sucrose, and tested for responses after prodding with a platinum wire after 5, 10, 15 and 30 min. Control and experimental worms were examined in parallel. A subset of experiments was performed by researchers blinded to the genotype.

Salt recovery assay

Recovery from paralysis on 400 mM NaCl was examined using an adaptation of previously published protocols14,51. Bacteria-seeded growth plates were supplemented with 400 mM NaCl. Growth-synchronized 1-day-old adult hermaphrodites were transferred from growth plates containing 50 mM NaCl to the assay plates and were examined every 15 min up to 4 h. Each assay included ten control and experimental worms examined in parallel.

Glycerol avoidance assay

The avoidance assay was performed as previously described61. In brief a ring of 8 M glycerol with xylene cyanol (Sigma-Aldrich X4126) was pipetted onto an unseeded NGM plate dried overnight. For each assay, 10 one-day-old adult hermaphrodites were transferred to the centre of the glycerol ring. After 10 min, the number of worms inside and outside the ring was counted. Wild-type and glr-9(oy180) mutants were examined in parallel.

Auxin-induced degradation

To make 4 mM auxin-containing plates, 400 mM auxin (1-naphthaleneacetic acid, Sigma 317918) was dissolved in ethanol and added to NGM containing the indicated salt concentrations. Control plates contained equal concentrations of ethanol alone. Plates were seeded with 100 μl E. coli OP50, dried overnight, and stored at 4 °C wrapped in aluminium foil to prevent light exposure.

At least 70 young adult hermaphrodites were transferred from NGM growth plates to plates containing 250 mM NaCl supplemented with either auxin or ethanol. After 3 h acclimatization, worms were tested for movement on plates containing 500 mM NaCl supplemented with either auxin or ethanol. Worms were examined at 5, 10, 15 and 30 min for responses to touch with a platinum wire. Each assay included 30 worms per condition and per genotype and was conducted over multiple days. Experimental and control strains were examined together in the same assays in each experiment.

Quantification of pumping frequency

Pumping frequency was analysed on NGM agar plates containing 50 mM or 250 mM NaCl and seeded with bacteria. The bacterial solution was prepared by diluting the supernatant of centrifuged E. coli OP50 with LB broth at a 1:50 ratio and 1 ml of this solution was spread to form a thin bacterial layer on a 10 mm plate. Individual worms were moved to these tracking plates and pumping was recorded for 3 min at 40 frames s−1. Worm behaviour was captured using a custom worm tracker consisting of a 760 nm IR LED (LED760L, Thorlabs), a 4× objective lens (XLFLUOR 4× 340, Olympus), and a CMOS camera (BFS-U3-23S6M-C, FLIR). To calculate the pumping rates, 2 min of pumping events from the recording was analysed using custom software written in Python (https://github.com/venkatachalamlab/Yeon-2025-pumping-analysis). In brief, the contour of the worm’s body was extracted from every frame of the video, and the centre line passing through the head and tail vertices and the terminal pharyngeal bulb was identified. A two-dimensional kymograph was then generated using this centre line as the region of interest for each frame. Pumping defined as morphological changes in the pharyngeal grinder was analysed by observing the movement of the corresponding region on the kymograph.

Microfluidics behavioural assay

Behavioural assays using microfluidics devices were performed essentially as previously described62. After degassing the assembled microfluidic devices, the outlet port was loaded with 5% v/v poloxamer surfactant (Sigma P5556) containing 2 mg ml−1 xylene cyanol to eliminate bubbles. Twenty-to-thirty one-day-old adult hermaphrodites were transferred to unseeded NGM plates, washed with water, and loaded into two separate arenas within a single device. glr-9 mutants were assessed in the same device, with wild-type controls placed in the adjacent arena. After allowing the worms to disperse (<5 min), the stimulus flow containing either 75 mM NaCl or 250 mM NaCl and 2 mg ml−1 xylene cyanol for visualization was initiated. 20 min videos were recorded at 2 Hz using a PixelLink camera. Microfluidic devices were cleaned after each experiment by washing with water and 100% ethanol before reuse. Custom MATLAB software was used to process and analyse all recordings, and data were visualized using custom R scripts (10.5281/zenodo.13748735)63. Mean residency histograms and chemotaxis indices were analysed as previously described62.

Calcium imaging

Imaging in I3 and I1

Custom microfluidic devices for calcium imaging were fabricated as described previously62. Images were acquired using an Olympus BX52WI microscope with a 40× oil objective and a Hamamatsu Orca CCD camera at 4 Hz with 4 × 4 binning. All stimuli were diluted in filtered Milli-Q water, and worms were paralysed with 10 mM (−)-tetramisole hydrochloride (Sigma L9756) dissolved in water prior to loading on the unseeded NGM. Stimuli dilutions were prepared fresh in water on the day of each imaging session. Responses to 30 s of water, 30 s of stimuli and 30 s of water delivered by a perfusion system were recorded in I3. For GCaMP fluorescence analysis, recorded videos were processed using Fiji as previously described62. In brief, images were aligned using the Template Matching plugin, and cell body and background fluorescence were calculated using manually drawn ROIs. Background-subtracted fluorescence intensity values were used for analysis in R Studio. The baseline fluorescence intensity (F0) was calculated as the average fluorescence value over 5 s prior to stimulus onset. R Studio was used to compute the response mean and s.e.m. (10.5281/zenodo.13748735)63. Peak ΔF/F0 values were calculated as the maximum change in fluorescence relative to F0 within 10 s following stimulus addition. GCaMP6s was driven under the glr-9 promoter for imaging in I3, and under the pha-4-promoter264 for imaging in I1. I1 was identified by its unique anatomical position. Control and experimental worms were interleaved in each imaging session.

Pan-pharyngeal neuron imaging

Volumetric calcium imaging was performed using a microfluidic device as previously described65–67 using the OH18562 strain64. Responses were recorded to 60 s water, 30 s 50 mM or 250 mM NaCl, 30 s water delivered via a perfusion system. Pan-pharyngeal neuronal soma imaging was performed using a piezo-driven 60× objective lens (UPLSAPO60XS2, Olympus) and a sCMOS camera (Kinetix, Teledyne) mounted on a spinning disk confocal microscope (Dragonfly 200, Andor). Calcium responses were recorded at a rate of 2.67 volumes per second, with each volume consisting of 25 frames per 15 ms. For volumetric imaging analysis, each of the 20 imaged pharyngeal neurons was identified on the basis of the neuroanatomical map68,69. All 20 neurons were tracked across all frames using the ZephIR multi-object tracking algorithm70. F0 was calculated as the average fluorescence value over 30 s prior to stimulus onset, ΔF was calculated as F − F0.

Heterologous expression and calcium imaging in HEK293T cells

HEK293T cells were provided by L. B. Vosshall at the Rockefeller University. Frozen stocks were stored in liquid nitrogen or at −80 °C. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; High Glucose, Wako) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum. Cells were routinely passaged at least twice a week. For plasmid transfection, cells were seeded on Type I-C collagen-coated 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (Nitta Gelatin). Just before transfection, the culture medium was replaced with Advanced DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 1% (v/v) GlutaMAX and 2.5 µM 4-phenylbutyric acid. The vectors with or without the glr-9 and glr-7 cDNAs (1.0 µg per dish) were transiently transfected into HEK293T cells using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were analysed 24–36 h after transfection.

To perform calcium imaging, HEK293T cells in glass-based dishes were loaded with Fura-2/AM (2.5 µM; Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing the cells with Ringer’s solution (140 mM NaCl, 5.6 mM KCl, 2.0 mM MgCl2, 2.0 mM CaCl2, 1.25 mM KH2PO4, 9.4 mM D-glucose, 2.0 mM sodium pyruvate, and 5 mM HEPES; pH 7.4), the low-cation solution contained 266 mM sorbitol, 5 mM HEPES, 2.3 mM NMDG and 0.1 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4, adjusted with NMDG) was applied with a custom-built gravity-flow perfusion system. Salt stimulation was performed by 1 mM NaCl solution (1 mM NaCl, 259 mM sorbitol, 5 mM HEPES, 2.3 mM NMDG and 0.1 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4, adjusted with NMDG)), 50 mM NaCl solution (50 mM NaCl, 167 mM sorbitol, 5 mM HEPES, 2.3 mM NMDG and 0.1 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4, adjusted with NMDG)), and 140 mM NaCl solution (140 mM NaCl, 5 mM HEPES, 2.3 mM NMDG and 0.1 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4, adjusted with NaOH)) and 0.5 mM EGTA solution (0.5 mM EGTA-2Na, 282 mM sorbitol, 5 mM HEPES and 2.3 mM NMDG (pH 7.4, adjusted with NMDG)). Fluorescence at 510 nm excited at 340 or 380 nm provided by a LED light source (pE-340fura; CoolLED) was monitored with a CCD camera (ORCA-R2; Hamamatsu Photonics). Fluorescence ratios (F340/F380) were calculated using HCImage software (Hamamatsu Photonics). Positive and negative response magnitudes (ΔF ratio) were defined as the maximum change from baseline. The baseline was defined as the average fluorescence ratio during the 5 s immediately preceding stimulation.

RNA sequencing

Worms were synchronized by bleaching adults, collecting eggs, and then arresting hatched L1s in the absence of food for ∼16 h. Around 1,000 worms were cultured on E. coli OP50 seeded at 1 ml per 10 cm plate at 20 °C until the 1-day-old adult stage. Worms from a total of four 10 cm plates were used per sample. Worms were washed with M9 containing 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma T8787) and placed onto either 10 cm OP50 seeded NGM plates or NGM plates supplemented with 250 mM NaCl for 3 h at 20 °C. Worms were then washed off the plates with M9 containing 0.01% Triton X-100. Total RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol (Invitrogen 15596026) and chloroform (Sigma 288306), followed by RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 (Zymo Research R1014) to extract total RNA. The quality and concentration of total RNA was assessed using Agilent High Sensitive RNA ScreenTape analysis (Agilent 5067-5579).

For each sample, 100 ng µl−1 of total RNA was used as input to generate a sequencing library using the xGen Broad Range RNA library preparation Kit (IDT 10009865) with AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter A63880). Agilent DNA ScreenTape (D1000 5067-5582) assays were used to assess the quality of the library prior to sequencing. Libraries were paired-end sequenced at 75 × 75 bases on a NextSeq 1000 system (Illumina). Sequencing reads were adapter trimmed using Trim Galore (10.5281/zenodo.7598955), mapped to the WBcel235 C. elegans genome, and counted using STAR71 with alignMatesGapMax 2500. Differential expression analysis was performed in R studio with DESEQ2 (ref. 72). The principal component analysis, heat maps and volcano plots were generated using the ggplot package in R. Venn diagrams were created using VennDiagram in R. GO terms, Wormbase, WomCat, Uniprot and PANTHER were used to categorize individual genes listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism v10.4.1 and R Studio 2025.05.01 + 513. Comparisons to wild-type were performed with two-tailed unpaired or paired t-test, Kruskal–Wallis test, two-tailed Welch’s t-test, or one-way ANOVA across the genotype and condition unless indicated otherwise. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using Dunnett’s, Dunn’s or Tukey’s tests. Scatter plots and bar graphs were generated and analysed using Prism v10.4.1, and estimation plots were generated using R Studio 2025.05.01 + 513. The statistical tests used, significance values, the number of animals, and number of independent experiments are indicated in the legend to each figure. Exact P values for acclimatization assays are shown in Supplementary Data 2.
