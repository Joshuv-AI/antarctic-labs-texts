Diethyl aminoethyl hexanoate ameliorates salt tolerance associated with ion transport, osmotic adjustment, and metabolite reprograming in white clover
Authors: Muhammad Jawad Hassan, Min Zhou, Yao Ling, Zhou Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil salinization is a serious environmental hazard, limiting plant growth and production in different agro-ecological zones worldwide. Diethyl aminoethyl hexanoate (DA-6) as an essential plant growth regulator (PGR) exhibits a beneficial role in improving crop growth and stress tolerance. However, the DA-6-regulated effect and mechanism of salt tolerance in plants are still not fully understood. The objective of current study was to disclose salt tolerance induced by DA-6 in relation to changes in water and redox balance, photosynthetic function, ionic homeostasis, and organic metabolites reprogramming in white clover (Trifolium repens).ResultsA prolonged duration of salt stress caused water loss, impaired photosynthetic function, and oxidative injury to plants. However, foliar application of DA-6 significantly improved osmotic adjustment (OA), photochemical efficiency, and cell membrane stability under salt stress. In addition, high salinity induced massive accumulation of sodium (Na), but decreased accumulation of potassium (K) in leaves and roots of all plants. DA-6-treated plants demonstrated significantly higher transcript levels of genes involved in uptake and transport of Na and K such as VP1, HKT8, SOS1, NHX2, NHX6, and SKOR in leaves as well as VP1, HKT1, HKT8, H+-ATPase, TPK5, SOS1, NHX2, and SKOR in roots. Metabolomics analysis further illustrated that DA-6 primarily induced the accumulation of glucuronic acid, hexanoic acid, linolenic acid, arachidonic acid, inosose, erythrulose, galactopyranose, talopyranose, urea, 1-monopalmitin, glycerol monostearate, campesterol, stigmasterol, and alanine.ConclusionsThe DA-6 significantly up-regulated transcript levels of multiple genes associated with increased Na+ compartmentalization in vacuoles and Na+ sequestration in roots to reduce Na+ transport to photosynthetic organs, thereby maintaining Na+ homeostasis under salt stress. The accumulation of many organic metabolites induced by the DA-6 could be attributed to enhanced cell wall and membrane structural stability and functionality, OA, antioxidant defense, and downstream signal transduction in leaves under salt stress. The present study provides a deep insight about the synergistic role of DA-6 in salt tolerance of white clover.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05657-6.Keywords: Metabolomics, Sugars, Organic acids, SOS pathway, Ionic homeostasis, Gene expression

Background

Soil salinization is a critical environmental problem, restricting plant growth and productivity specifically in arid and semi-arid agro-ecological zones worldwide [1, 2]. Nearly 17% of the total arable lands of more than one hundred countries are drastically affected by salinization [3]. Salinity significantly disturbs numerous physiological and biochemical processes at all stages of plant life cycle such as germination, seedling establishment and growth, and reproduction [4, 5]. Excessive amount of sodium ion (Na+) and various other salt ions inside soils induce osmotic stress that hinders the capacity of root water uptake, thereby causing physiological drought. On the other hand, plants exposed to salt stress are forced to absorb superfluous Na+ leading to ionic toxicity in cells [6]. Salinity-induced osmotic stress and ionic toxicity result in massive liberation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) which are responsible for oxidative damage to plants [7]. In order to cope with salt stress, plants have developed various stress adaption responses or strategies such as accumulation of protective proteins and compatible solutes, increased transcriptional regulation of stress defensive genes, and maintenance of potassium ion (K+) and Na+ transport and homeostasis [8, 9]. However, it is still a great challenge to utilize salt-polluted soils in agricultural production because majority of economical crops are glycophytes in nature and highly vulnerable to salt toxicity.

Decreased uptake of Na+ by roots and Na+ compartmentalization into vacuoles are key mechanisms of plant adaptability to saline conditions. The salt overly sensitive (SOS) pathway serves as the chief regulation pathway associated with the maintenance of Na+ equilibrium in plants exposed to salt stress [10]. It has been reported that the overexpression of SOS1 could ameliorate salt tolerance of tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea) via exclusion of Na+ from the cytoplasm [11]. Na+/K+ antiporters (NHXs) are primarily involved in the inverse transport of monovalent hydronium ion (H+), thus resulting in dynamic equilibrium of K+ and Na+ [12, 13]. High affinity K+ transporter (HKT) performs a crucial function of Na+ refutation and uptake of K+ in plants [14]. H+-ATPase together with H+-PPase such as VP1 supply essential proton motive force for NHXs to shift K+ or Na+ from cytoplasm toward vacuole for osmotic adjustment (OA) [15, 16]. In addition, stelar K+ outward rectifier (SKOR) is also an important channel protein regulating the elimination and transport of K+ from xylem to arial parts of plant [17]. Previous studies have shown that transgenic alfalfa (Medicago sativa) [18], mungbean (Vigna radiata) [19], and Arabidopsis thaliana [20] overexpressing NHX1 or NHX2 demonstrated increased salt tolerance associated with Na+ and K+ homeostasis.

In addition to inorganic ions, the accumulation and metabolism of organic metabolites such as sugars, amino acids, and organic acids are of primary importance when plants undergo salt stress because of beneficial roles of these organic metabolites in pH regulation, OA, osmoprotection, antioxidant, and metal ion chelation [21]. Metabolomics supplies indispensable methodology to examine global metabolites remodeling and interlinked metabolic pathways in plants under unfavorable environmental conditions [22]. It has been reported that salt-tolerant wild soybean (Glycine soja) exhibited higher accumulation of organic acids and amino acids, whereas the salt-sensitive local soybean (G. max) genotype specifically increased the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to liberate extra energy under salt toxicity, which could be an ideal example of metabolomics analysis disclosing differential adaptation in response to salt stress [23]. Metabolites profiling has been also utilized to disclose alterations in global metabolites and stress-defensive metabolic pathways associated with salinity tolerance, heat tolerance, and water stress tolerance in white clover (Trifolium repens) or other economical plant species [24–26].

Being an imperative artificial tertiary amine, the use of diethyl aminoethyl hexanoate (DA-6) as an essential plant growth regulator (PGR) has become a common practice in agricultural sector nowadays [27–29]. Many studies have reported the promising role of DA-6 in improving seed germination, rapid seedling growth and development, photosynthetic performance, grain yield, and dry matter accumulation in different crops [28–30]. In addition, the application of DA-6 either alone or in combination with other PGRs has been proved to be highly beneficial to tolerance to various abiotic stresses in plants. For instance, exogenous supply of DA-6 effectively mitigated adverse effects of low temperature via amelioration of nitrogen metabolism in maize (Zea mays) seedlings [31]. Enhanced antioxidant defense system induced by the DA-6 contributed to chilling tolerance of strawberry (Fragaria ananassa) seedlings [32]. The DA-6-mediated lipidomic reprogramming improved adaptability to water stress in two white clover genotypes [33]. Moreover, combined application of DA-6 with 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA) successfully alleviated cadmium-induced toxicity in slim amaranth (Amaranthus hybridus) by withholding cadmium in cell wall [34]. In addition, a previous study found that the DA-6 application significantly reduced the salinity-prompted oxidative damage by ameliorating enzymatic antioxidants and photosynthetic functions in Cassia obtusifolia [35]. However, the mediatory function of DA-6 in ionic transport and metabolite-interlinked defensive pathways requires further exploration in plants exposed to salt toxicity.

White clover is a perennial herbaceous legume in temperate pasture and cultivated worldwide as a nutrient-rich feed source for ruminants [36]. Apart from forage, it has strong ability of nitrogen fixation in roots, therefore contributing significantly to soil nutritional enrichment and sustainable agriculture [36–38]. The betterment of white clover quality and quantity is crucial for improved livestock husbandry and soil management. However, white clover is usually considered as a salt-sensitive plant species, and salt toxicity is regarded as one of the major limiting factors to its production in arid and semi-arid regions [39, 40]. The present experiment focused on elucidating the impact of foliar application of DA-6 on physiological function, accumulation and transport of Na+ and K+, and metabolites profiling, which facilitated the adaptability of white clover to salt toxicity. These findings will supply comprehensive understanding of the DA-6-regulated salt tolerance in forage legumes.

Results

Effects of DA-6 on physiological changes under normal and saline conditions

Leaf relative water content (RWC) and osmotic potential (OP) were not significantly affected by DA-6 foliar pretreatment under normal condition (Fig. 1A and B). Salt toxicity induced significant decreases in RWC and OP, and DA-6-treated plants retained 18% higher RWC and 19% lower OP when compared with untreated plants under saline condition, respectively (Fig. 1A and B). In contrast to control, the content of total chlorophyll (Chl), Chl a, and Chl b were significantly increased by DA-6 application under normal condition, but Chl a/b ratio, the ratio of variable to maximal fluorescence (Fv/Fm), and performance index on an absorption basis (PIABS) remained unaffected under non-stress condition (Fig. 2A-F). Salt stress significantly decreased all above-mentioned photosynthetic pigments and parameters than control (Fig. 2). The DA-6-pretreated plants showed an 84%, 97%, 17%, 33%, 12%, or 96% upsurge in total Chl, Chl a, Chl b, Chl a/b, Fv/Fm, or PIABS when compared with DA-6-untreated plants under salt stress, respectively (Fig. 2A-F). The electrolyte leakage (EL), superoxide anion (O2.−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents significantly elevated in DA-6-treated and -untreated plants in response to salt toxicity (Fig. 3A-D). Exogenous application of DA-6 significantly decreased salt-induced increases in EL, O2.−, H2O2, or MDA by 13%, 18%, 10%, or 12% than DA-6-unpretreated plants under saline condition, respectively (Fig. 3A-D).

Effects of DA-6 on Na+/K+ content and transcript levels of genes associated with K+/Na+ transportation

Under normal condition, Na+ content was retained at a low level in leaves or roots, and foliar application of DA-6 exhibited no significant impact on Na+ content (Fig. 4A). Salt toxicity significantly enhanced accumulation of Na+ in leaves and roots. In response to salt stress, DA-6-treated plants possessed significantly higher Na+ content in roots, whereas reduced Na+ content in leaves when compared with untreated plants (Fig. 4A). Salt stress significantly decreased K+ content in leaves and roots of all treatments, but the K+ content in leaves and roots did not show any significant difference between DA-6-treated and untreated plants under normal and salt stress conditions (Fig. 4B). Similarly, salt stress induced a significant decline in K+/Na+ ratio in all plants, but the DA-6-treated plants maintained lower K+/Na+ ratio in roots than untreated plants under normal condition or salt stress (Fig. 4C). In term of changes in transcript levels of genes associated with Na+ and K+ transport in leaves, exogenous DA-6 significantly upregulated transcript levels of VP1, HKT8, H+-ATPase, and NHX2 under normal condition (Fig. 5A). Under salt stress, DA-6-treated plants demonstrated significantly higher transcript levels of VP1, HKT8, SOS1, NHX2, NHX6, and SKOR than non-treated plants in leaves (Fig. 5A). Salt toxicity significantly up-regulated expression levels of VP1, NHX2, and NHX6 in roots (Fig. 5B). Under non-stressed condition, foliar application of DA-6 caused significant upsurges in VP1, HKT8, and TPK5 in roots (Fig. 5B). Under saline condition, VP1, HKT1, HKT8, H+-ATPase, TPK5, SOS1, NHX2, and SKOR were significantly up-regulated by foliar DA-6 pretreatment in roots (Fig. 5B).

Effect of DA-6 on metabolic profiling under normal and saline conditions

A total of 58 metabolites were identified in different comparison groups (C + DA-6 Vs. C, S + DA-6 Vs. S, S Vs. C, and S + DA-6 Vs. C) including 18 organic acids, 15 sugars, 14 alcohols, and 11 other metabolites (Fig. 6A). Heat map depicted an overview of changes in relative contents of 58 metabolites generally or differentially regulated by DA-6 and salt stress (Fig. 6A). A 3%, 24%, 29%, or 19% metabolite significantly decreased in the C + DA-6 Vs. C, S + DA-6 Vs. S, S Vs. C, or S + DA-6 Vs. C, respectively (Fig. 6B). Nearly 22% metabolites significantly increased in S + DA-6 Vs. S, while 16%, 52%, or 57% metabolites significantly increased in C + DA-6 Vs. C, S Vs. C, or S + DA-6 Vs. C, respectively (Fig. 6B). Relative contents of sugars and alcohols increased significantly in both of DA-6-pretreated and untreated plants under salt stress (Fig. 6C). However, DA-6-pretreated plants showed 9% increase in sugars content, whereas 17% decrease in alcohols content than untreated plants exposed to saline condition (Fig. 6C). The DA-6 pretreatment had no significant effects on relative contents of organic acids, sugars, alcohols, and other metabolites under normal condition as shown in Fig. 6C.

Salt stress significantly reduced accumulations of inosose, erythrulose, lyxose, galactopyranose, and talopyranose in DA-6-untreated plants (Fig. 7A and B). However, foliar pretreatment of DA-6 significantly mitigated salt-induced decreases in inosose, erythrulose, galactopyranose, and talopyranose (Fig. 7A and B). In contrast to control, relative contents of all detected sugar alcohols increased significantly in both DA-6-treated and untreated plants under saline condition (Fig. 7C). No significant differences were recorded in relative contents of propylene glycol, 2,3-butanediol, 1, 2, 3-butanetriol, threitol, arabitol, pentitol, diethylene glycol, 1,5-anhydroglucitol, and galactinol between the DA-6-pretreated and untreated plants subjected to salt toxicity (Fig. 7C). The S + DA-6 treatment exhibited significantly higher glucuronic acid, hexanoic acid, linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid contents than the S treatment (Fig. 8A and B). On the contrary, the DA-6-treated plants demonstrated reduction in lactic acid, glycolic acid, 2, 4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and propanoic acid contents than non-treated plants under saline condition (Fig. 8A and B). In addition, the DA-6 application significantly increased accumulations of urea, 1-monopalmitin, glycerol monostearate, campesterol, stigmasterol, and alanine under salt stress (Fig. 8C). Discussion

Plants experience different types of inter-linked stresses including hyperosmotic, hyperionic and oxidative stresses due to continuous exposure to salt toxicity leading to physiological drought, ionic imbalance, and oxidative damage, which induces metabolic disturbance and growth retardation [6]. PGRs such as jasmonic acid (JA), melatonin (MT), abscisic acid (ABA), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and gibberellins (GA) perform critical functions in mediating salt tolerance of plants [41–43]. Many evidences have also reported that the DA-6 could display similar function of PGRs, hence directly or indirectly regulating tolerance to various abiotic stresses including salt stress in plant species [33, 35, 44]. In the current study, a prolonged period of salt stress significantly reduced leaf RWC, OP, photosynthetic functions (Chl content, Fv/Fm, and PIABS), cell membrane stability and also increased oxidative damage, which indicated that white clover plants suffered from extreme salt stress damage (Figs. 1 and 2). However, exogenous DA-6 pretreatment not only significantly mitigated salt-elicited leaf water loss, reduced cell membrane stability, and impaired photosynthetic function, but also effectively reduced oxidative damage (Figs. 1 and 2). These results indicated that DA-6 performed a crucial positive role in maintaining water balance and ROS homeostasis in white clover under saline condition.

One of key mechanisms of salt tolerance in plants is to quarantine surplus Na+ in roots to effectively impede upward transportation of Na+ to aerial photosynthetic parts [45]. Although salt stress significantly increased accumulation of Na+ in leaves and roots of all white clover plants, those plants with DA-6 application exhibited significantly lower Na+ content in leaves and higher accumulation of Na+ in roots when compared with untreated plants under salt stress (Fig. 4). It has been reported that HKT genes family regulates Na+ excretion in root zones and also restricts Na+ transport to aerial leaves during a prolonged salt stress [14, 46]. In addition, Wang et al. reported that the primary role of AtSOS1 is to squeeze out Na+ from cytosol into intercellular space in favor of Na+ excretion in root zones of A. thaliana that was subjected to salt toxicity [47]. Gene expression analysis revealed that DA-6 significantly enhanced HKT8 and SOS1 expressions in leaves and roots as well as HKT1 expression in roots of white clover plants under salt stress, which suggested that the DA-6-triggered HKT and SOS pathways might be associated with long-distance transport of Na+ from roots to leaves. Previous studies also found that exogenous application of spermine or chitosan effectively alleviated salt-induced accumulation of Na+ in leaves of creeping bentgrass (Agrostis stolonifera) through up-regulating transcript levels of HKT1 and SOS1 [48, 49].

The isolation of Na+ inside vacuoles and transport of K+ from vacuoles to the cytoplasm are another important regulatory mechanisms of salt tolerance in plant species, because this process prevents hazardous impacts of Na+ in the cytosol and K+ homeostasis in the cytoplasm [50]. The Na+ compartmentalization into vacuoles further contributes to increased OA, thereby improving water balance under salt stress [51, 52]. The DA-6-pretreated white clover plants (S + DA-6) exhibited significantly higher transcript levels of NHX2 and VP1 in leaves and roots, NHX6 expression level in leaves, and H+-ATPase expression in roots under saline condition (Fig. 5), indicating that the DA-6 could ameliorate the potential for Na+ compartmentalization within cells under salt stress. Our results are in agreement with the study of Geng et al. who found that exogenous application of chitosan significantly up-regulated transcript levels of H+-ATPase, NHX1, NHX5, and NHX6 in creeping bentgrass plants related to better salt tolerance [49]. Moreover, transgenic A. thaliana plants overexpressing an AVP1 exhibited higher salt and drought resistance when compared with wild type [53]. For K+ transport in plant cells, two-pore K+ channels (TPKs) play an indispensable role in maintaining cellular K+ homeostasis in plants by transporting K+ from vacuole to the cytoplasm [54]. Previous studies have proved that the TPK1a expression increased by two times in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) under osmotic shock or salt toxicity [55], and a TPK overexpression enhanced salt tolerance of tobacco cells [56]. Foliar application of DA-6 significantly upregulated the TPK5 expression in roots of white clover plants under salt stress (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that the DA-6-regulated salt tolerance could be associated with transport and distribution of K+ in different cellular components contributing to improved K+ homeostasis under salt stress, even though the DA-6-pretreated and untreated white clover plants exhibited same accumulation of K+ in leaves and roots.

High-salinity stress induces osmotic stress, oxidative damage, and ion toxicity resulting in metabolic disturbance in plant cells. Metabolic alterations associated with metabolites profiling are essential adaptive responses when plants experience salt toxicity [5]. The production of organic acids is one of the most vital defense strategies in plants because of their positive roles in the elimination of ionic toxicity, energy liberation via respiration, and pH adjustment under hazardous environmental conditions [57, 58]. In the present study, DA-6-pretreated plants demonstrated significantly higher glucuronic acid, hexanoic acid, linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid contents than untreated plants exposed to salt stress (Fig. 8). Glucuronic acid serves as a crucial precursor for biosynthesis of various polysaccharides, thereby constituting up to 50% of cell wall biomass in A. thaliana [59]. Many studies have reported the higher accumulation of glucuronic acid in favor of tolerance to salt stress in different plant species [60, 61]. In plants, linolenic acid is an unsaturated fatty acid which performs multiple advantageous functions under abiotic stresses by serving as essential ingredient of cell wall, constituent and regulator of cellular membranes, precursor of different biomolecules such as jasmonates and nitroalkenes, carbon and energy reserves, and modulator of stress signaling [62]. In addition, as an imperative polysaturated fatty acid, arachidonic acid is found abundantly in semi-permeable membrane as a constituent of phospholipids in plant cells [63]. Exogenous arachidonic acid activates biogenesis and accumulation of fatty acids in Laminaria digitata contributing to cell membrane stability [64]. It has been also showed that exogenous application of arachidonic acid significantly improved morphological traits, grain fill-in and unsaturated fatty acids profile of rice (Oryza sativa) under saline condition [63]. Our results indicated that DA-6-regulated increases in glucuronic acid, hexanoic acid, linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid could be connected with improved cell wall structural stability and better defensive response in white clover plants under salt stress.

In response to unfavorable environmental conditions, the biosynthesis of soluble sugars and sugar alcohols in plants will be conducive to maintain cell turgor by serving as essential osmolytes, stability of cellular membranes and proteins, and ROS homeostasis [65–67]. In contrast to non-stress control, salt stress induced the accumulation of most of sugars and sugar alcohol in DA-6-treated and untreated white clover plants, which indicated that enhanced accumulation of sugars and sugar alcohols could be an important adaptive response to salt stress in white clover. More importantly, the DA-6-treated white clover plants demonstrated significantly higher inosose, erythrulose, galactopyranose, and talopyranose contents than the untreated plants under salt stress (Fig. 7). Inosose and its derivatives such as inositol, myo-inositol, and inositol phosphatases perform beneficial roles in plant growth and regulation of stress tolerance. For example, foliar pretreatment of myo-inositol could significantly mitigate salinity-induced damages in Chenopodium quinoa by improving antioxidant defense, cell membrane stability, and up-regulation of stress-related genes [68]. In addition, co-expression of PcINO1 and McIMT1 ameliorated salt tolerance via higher accumulation of myo-inositol and methylated inositol in transgenic tobacco plants [69]. Erythrulose is an important reductive monosaccharide that regulates cellular energy metabolism, water balance, and ROS homeostasis in plants exposed to abiotic stress [70]. A previous study has found that higher accumulation of erythrulose could be associated with improved heat tolerance in creeping bentgrass [26]. In addition, a recent study related to metabolites profiling of two Italian Olea europaea genotypes demonstrated that UV-B stress enhanced accumulation of talopyranose [71]. Moreover, the study of Gundaraniya et al. based on metabolomics revealed that drought-tolerant peanut (Arachis hypogea) genotype enhanced accumulation of galactopyranose in leaf and root under drought stress [72]. Hence, enhanced salt tolerance in DA-6-treated white clover plants could be associated with sugar metabolism under salt stress.

Other metabolites including urea, 1-monopalmitin, glycerol monostearate, campesterol, stigmasterol, and alanine in white clover plants were induced by exogenous DA-6 under salt stress, which also could be involved in stress adaptation of plants. Urea performs dual roles in growth enhancement as a primary source of nitrogen as well as a source of stress signal in plants [73]. Foliar supplementation of urea delayed leaf senescence of creeping bentgrass via improvement in cell membrane and protein stability under high temperature stress [74]. Exogenous application of urea also significantly improved growth and gas exchange parameters in broccoli (Brassica oleracea) plants under salt stress [75]. The analysis of metabolic profiling has demonstrated that chitosan-induced drought tolerance could be associated with increased accumulation of 1-monopalmitin in white clover [25]. Sterols such as campesterol and stigmasterol are functional constituents of membrane lipid bilayer for maintaining the structural stability and functionality of membrane systems [76, 77]. It has been reported that the choline priming-induced upregulation of campesterol and cholesterol content was markedly linked with ameliorative salt tolerance of wheat (Triticum aestivum) [78]. Significant upsurge in stigmasterol content resulted in increased membrane adaption to salt toxicity in different Brassicaceae species [79]. Moreover, alanine has the capability to enhance stress tolerance including salt tolerance as it could be transformed to antioxidant and osmoprotective compounds such as homoglutathione and β-alanine betaine [80, 81]. A recent study reported that exogenous spermine mediated higher accumulation of alanine which could be related to increased salt tolerance of creeping bentgrass [21]. The current study indicates that the DA-6-mediated adaptability to salt stress could be connected with higher accumulation of urea, 1-monopalmitin, glycerol monostearate, campesterol, stigmasterol, and alanine in white clover, hence resulting in improved stability and integrity of cellular membrane systems, OA, and antioxidant defense under saline condition.

Conclusion

Physiological analysis demonstrated that foliar application of DA-6 was a cost-effective approach to mitigate salinity-induced stress injury in white clover plants through regulating water balance, photosynthetic function, and cell membrane stability. In response to salt stress, exogenous DA-6 significantly up-regulated transcript levels of VP1, HKT8, SOS1, NHX2, NHX6, and SKOR in leaves as well as VP1, HKT1, HKT8, H+-ATPase, TPK5, SOS1, NHX2, and SKOR in roots associated with increased Na+ compartmentalization in vacuoles and Na+ sequestration in roots to reduce Na+ transport to aerial photosynthetic parts, thereby maintaining Na+ homeostasis. In addition, metabolites profiling found that a total of 58 metabolites were generally or differentially regulated by DA-6 in leaves of white clover plants under saline condition. Under salt stress, DA-6 primarily induced accumulations of glucuronic acid, hexanoic acid, linolenic acid, arachidonic acid, inosose, erythrulose, galactopyranose, talopyranose, urea, 1-monopalmitin, glycerol monostearate, campesterol, stigmasterol, and alanine, which contributed to enhanced cell wall and membrane structural stability and functionality, OA, antioxidant defense, and downstream signal transduction when white clover plants were exposed to salt stress. The present study provides a deep insight about the synergistic role of DA-6 in salt tolerance of white clover.

Methods

Plant materials and treatments

Seeds of white clover genotype Haifa which was a commercial cultivar and purchased from Beijing Best Grass Industry Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) were disinfected with 75% ethanol solution for 10 min and rinsed twice with distilled water before sowing. A total of 0.05 g seeds were uniformly sown in each plastic container (15 cm depth, 24 cm length, and 20 cm breadth) which was filled with sterilized quartz sands as a growth medium. All containers were placed under controlled growth chamber conditions with 65% relative humidity, daily temperature of 23/19℃ (day/night), and 700 µmol photon m−2 s−1 PAR, respectively. Seeds were irrigated daily with deionized water for 7 days of germination and later with half strength Hoagland’s solution [82] for next 23 days. One-month-old seedlings were subjected to foliar pretreatment of 1.5 mM DA-6 solution or deionized water for 3 days so that seedlings could absorb adequate amount of DA-6 or deionized water before stress treatment. Seedlings were divided into four different treatments: (1) seedlings grown in half strength Hoagland’s solution as the control (C); (2) seedlings were pretreated with DA-6 and subsequently grown in half strength Hoagland’s solution (C + DA-6); (3) non-DA-6-pretreated seedlings grown in 200 mM NaCl solution for 3 days and 250 mM NaCl solution for 15 days as salt stress (S); (4) seedlings were pretreated with DA-6 and subsequently grown in 200 mM NaCl solution for 3 days and 250 mM NaCl solution for 15 days as salt stress plus DA-6 (S + DA-6). The NaCl was dissolved in half strength Hoagland’s solution. The concentration of DA-6 (1.5 mM) with the most promising phenotypic effect was selected on the basis of our previous study [33]. Leaf samples were taken after 18 days of salt stress for detecting various physiological functions and metabolomics. Each treatment comprised of four separate biological repetitions and was carried out under completely randomized design (CRD).

Estimation of water status and osmotic potential

For RWC, 0.1 g of leaf samples were taken and instantly weighed to record fresh weight (Fw). After this, samples were immersed in deionized water for 24 h to obtain saturated weight (Sw). Later, samples were dried in an electric oven at 75℃ for 3 days and dry weight (Dw) was recorded. Values of leaf RWC were calculated in percent by RWC (%) = [(Fw-Dw)/(Sw-Dw)]×100 [83]. For the estimation of OP, leaf samples were immersed in distilled water for 8 h at 4℃. Leaves were blotted to eliminate water on surface of leaves and leaf saps were extracted. Leaf saps (10 ul) were then poured into an osmometer (Wescor) to measure the osmolality (mmol kg−1). Later, OP (MPa) was evaluated based on the following equation: OP (MPa) = [0.001] × [2.58] × [osmolality] [84].

Estimation of chlorophyll content and photochemical efficiency

To determine leaf Chl content, fresh leaf samples (0.1 g) were excised, immersed in 10 ml of dimethyl sulphoxide, and placed under dark environment for 2 days. After this, leaf extract (200 µl) was measured spectrophotometrically by observing the absorbance value at 645 and 663 nm [85]. The Fv/Fm and PIABS were estimated with a Chl fluorescence system (Pocket PEA, Hansatech, United Kingdom). Before determination, leaf samples were kept in darkness with attached leaf clips for half an hour.

Measurement of electrolyte leakage and oxidative injury

To determine EL, 0.1 g of leaf samples were submerged in distilled water (40 ml) for 1 day and the initial conductivity (Ci) of the solution was measured. After this, leaves were kept in an autoclave at a high temperature (120 °C) for 20 min and the final conductivity (Cf) of the solution was recorded. The value of EL was obtained by using the following formula: EL (%) = Ci/Cf × 100 [86]. To evaluate contents of reactive oxygen species (ROS) including O2.−, H2O2, as well as MDA content, the assay methods of Elstner and Heupel, Velikova et al. or Dhindsa et al. were used, respectively [87–89]. The protocol and reagents used for the determination of MDA have been thoroughly illustrated in our earlier study [33].

Determination of Na+and K+contents

For the measurement of Na+ or K+ content, fresh leaf samples were collected and oven dried at 70 °C until a constant weight was achieved. Dry leaf samples were mechanically ground into fine powder. A total of 0.5 g of fine powder was taken and extricated in the HNO3 and HClO4 mixture (5:1, v: v) at 180 °C for 30 min. After digestion, a flame atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany) was used to detect the Na+ or K+ content, by monitoring the absorbance of digestive solution at 589 or 766.5 nm, respectively [21].

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNAs from fresh leaf samples were extricated with an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) by following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The reverse transcription of RNA to cDNA was achieved by an revert Aid First Stand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas). Primer sequences of different genes related to Na+ or K+ transport were mentioned in Table S1. The transcript levels were estimated using a quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) supported with SYBR GreenSupermix detection system (Bio-Rad). The detailed protocols have been clearly illustrated in our former studies [90, 91]. Relative expressions of all genes were computed with the formula 2−∆∆Ct described by Livak and Schmittgen [92].

Metabolomics analysis

The concentration of different metabolites was measured with a gas chromatography-time of flight mass spectrography (GC-TOFMS). Various interlinked processes including metabolite extraction, separation, and quantification were performed by following the procedure illustrated by Li et al. as mentioned thoroughly in our previous study [93].

Statistical analysis

Data was recorded and organized by using Microsoft Office Excel (version 2023, Washington, USA) and variance analysis (version 8.1, Statistix, USA) was performed to estimate significance among all treatments. Significant differences were examined with two-way ANOVA and Tukey's test at p < 0.05.
