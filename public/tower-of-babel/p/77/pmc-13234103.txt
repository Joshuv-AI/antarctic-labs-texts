Lightweight liquid neural networks decipher salivary metabolic fingerprinting for high-risk periodontitis screening in diabetes
Authors: Yizhou Liu, Ya Zhao, Zhenhe Chen, Qin Liang, Yajuan Lei, Lilei Zhu, Xiaodong Li, Chao Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes (T2DM) and periodontitis are bidirectionally linked diseases that both involve chronic inflammation; their co‑occurrence represents a high‑risk clinical condition requiring timely identification. In this proof‑of‑concept, dual‑center feasibility study (n = 426), we evaluate a rapid, non‑invasive approach for identifying individuals with co‑occurring periodontitis and T2DM by integrating salivary metabolic fingerprints acquired by probe electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (PESI‑MS, ~0.7 min per sample) with demographic covariates (age and sex) as inputs for a lightweight liquid neural network (LNN) classifier. A total of 426 participants (H: healthy controls, n = 114; P: periodontitis, n = 209; MP: periodontitis with T2DM, n = 103) were enrolled, and the data were randomly split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. We benchmarked the LNN against conventional classifiers (PLS‑DA, random forest, SVM) and other deep‑learning models (BiLSTM, MHA‑LSTM). While conventional models showed limited performance (AUC 0.73–0.78), deep‑learning models, which leverage the sequential structure of m/z‑ordered spectral data, achieved substantially higher accuracy. Notably, the LNN achieved the highest test accuracy (91.9%) with 100% recall for the MP group, while requiring approximately one‑third the trainable parameters of other recurrent networks. This study demonstrates the feasibility of combining PESI‑MS metabolic fingerprinting with efficient deep learning for high‑risk periodontitis screening.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and periodontitis are chronic inflammatory conditions of high global prevalence, collectively contributing to a substantial healthcare and socioeconomic burden worldwide1–3. Epidemiological and mechanistic evidence firmly supports a bidirectional relationship between the two diseases. On one hand, hyperglycaemia and diabetes-associated metabolic dysregulation increase host susceptibility to periodontal tissue destruction and are linked to more severe periodontal outcomes. On the other hand, persistent periodontal inflammation adversely influences systemic glycaemic control and elevates the risk of diabetic complications4–8. Notably, interventional studies have demonstrated that periodontal therapy can lead to clinically meaningful improvements in glycaemic parameters among patients with coexisting T2DM and periodontitis9. These observations collectively underscore that individuals with concomitant periodontitis and T2DM constitute a distinct, high-risk phenotype that necessitates timely identification and integrated, long-term management across dental and diabetic care.

It is recognized that in dental practice, essential systemic data like recent HbA1c levels are typically unavailable at the time of consultation, hindering comprehensive risk evaluation10. Moreover, comprehensive periodontal examination requires specialized training, dedicated chairside time, and is inherently subjective, making it more suitable for definitive diagnosis and staging than for large-scale screening or frequent monitoring11. Contemporary periodontal classification frameworks further emphasize the importance of assessing disease severity, complexity, and risk modifiers for personalized management12,13. These practical constraints highlight an unmet need for a rapid, non-invasive, and scalable tool that can be deployed at the initial point of clinical assessment. Such a tool may support exploratory risk stratification, inform future referral strategies, and facilitate evaluation within integrated care pathways addressing inflammatory and metabolic control.

Saliva represents a highly promising medium for this purpose. Its collection is straightforward and non-invasive, and its composition reflects the local oral microenvironment, encapsulating a dynamic interplay of host inflammatory markers, microbial constituents, and metabolic by-products14. Systematic reviews indicate that salivary biomarkers, particularly when combined into panels, can achieve clinically useful diagnostic performance for periodontitis, supporting the feasibility of saliva-based screening strategies15–17. Beyond targeted biomarker assays, untargeted metabolomics captures a broad metabolic fingerprint that reflects disease-associated physiological perturbations18. This untargeted strategy has gained increasing attention in oral disease research, including periodontitis, for its potential to reveal novel metabolic pathways and diagnostic signatures19. A key challenge for clinical translation, however, lies in obtaining robust and informative metabolic profiles rapidly, with minimal sample preprocessing, to enable near real-time decision support.

Recent advancements in ambient ionization mass spectrometry have significantly lowered the barriers to rapid, in-situ chemical profiling by enabling direct analysis of complex biological samples with minimal preparation20,21. Among these techniques, probe electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (PESI-MS) has emerged as a promising tool, demonstrating the feasibility of high-speed analysis of body fluids like saliva—often without extensive preprocessing—making it potentially suitable for rapid exploratory screening workflows22. However, PESI-MS generates high-dimensional, correlated spectral data, which may contain nonlinear patterns influenced by individual variability, matrix effects, and instrumental fluctuations20,23. Under such conditions, conventional statistical or feature-based machine learning approaches, which rely on manually curated variable sets, may fail to fully capture the discriminative information embedded within the complete spectral profiles.

Machine learning has become integral to mass spectrometry data analysis, especially for pattern recognition, calibration, and clinical predictive modeling24. Deep learning extends these capabilities through an end-to-end representation learning framework, capable of automatically extracting relevant features directly from raw or minimally preprocessed data, thereby enhancing robustness against noise and subtle confounding factors25,26. Recently, liquid neural networks (LNNs) have attracted growing interest in machine learning research27. Unlike traditional neural networks that assume feature independence (e.g., standard MLPs), LNNs are designed to capture inherent dependencies along ordered, continuous axes—such as the m/z dimension—through continuous-time recurrent dynamics, framing mass spectrometry data as an ordered sequence. Nevertheless, medical datasets are often limited in size and exhibit substantial heterogeneity across patients and sampling conditions, necessitating the use of lightweight model architectures and rigorous validation protocols to mitigate overfitting and ensure generalizability28.

To address these challenges, we designed a cross-sectional study to characterize salivary metabolic fingerprints acquired via PESI-MS from three distinct clinical groups: systemically and periodontally healthy controls (H), patients with periodontitis (P), and patients with both periodontitis and type 2 diabetes (MP). Our objective was to develop and internally evaluate an individualized classification framework using recurrent networks, aiming to improve the identification of high-risk periodontal status while exploring its feasibility for future risk-stratification applications. Using minute-scale, unstimulated whole-saliva profiles, we evaluated the model’s discriminative performance and robustness in differentiating the H, P, and MP cohorts within a cross-sectional design, and explored its potential utility for future screening-oriented evaluation. Ultimately, this study sought to generate evidence for a practical, non-invasive risk stratification tool that bridges machine learning with metabolic fingerprinting, thereby supporting earlier and more targeted clinical interventions.

PESI‑MS salivary metabolic fingerprints and exploratory multivariate structure

Untargeted metabolic fingerprinting in positive‑ion mode yielded 456 retained m/z features after initial detectability‑based filtering of the PESI‑MS data. Representative salivary mass spectra revealed distinct global profiles across the three groups, with each group exhibiting characteristic spectral patterns and relative intensities in specific m/z regions (Fig. 2A). Following the MetaboAnalyst preprocessing workflow, box‑and‑whisker plots of the extracted m/z features from 50 randomly selected samples (H = 16, P = 24, MP = 10) indicated comparable baseline intensity distributions across groups (Fig. 2B). Principal component analysis (PCA) performed on the full dataset (n = 426) using all 456 retained m/z features revealed considerable overlap among the three groups, with samples from the P group forming the most compact cluster despite being the largest group (Fig. 2C).

Variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis was employed within the PLS‑DA framework to rank features by their contribution to the model. A total of 32 m/z features with VIP scores >1.5 were identified as making substantial contributions to the PLS‑DA model’s discrimination (VIP > 1.5 was used here to highlight the most influential features; VIP > 1 was used for conventional model input construction). Sensitivity analyses across VIP cutoffs and an alternative normalization showed broadly consistent downstream trends (Supplementary Table 2). Notably, the majority of these high‑VIP features were localized within the m/z 150–400 range (Fig. 3C). Conventional machine-learning classification

Using the training set, we evaluated three conventional multivariate classifiers—partial least squares‑discriminant analysis (PLS‑DA), random forest (RF), and linear support vector machine (SVM)—for discriminating MP from the combined non‑MP group (H + P). All models used the 76 features with VIP > 1 (identified from the initial PLS‑DA) as input. Multivariate ROC analysis with internal cross‑validation was performed to rank features and to assess the predictive performance of biomarker panels of increasing size (top 3, 5, 10, 20, 38, and all 76 features). For all three classifiers, the cross‑validated area under the curve (AUC) increased with panel size, reaching the highest value when all 76 features were used (Fig. 4A, C, E). However, the incremental gain in performance diminished substantially beyond approximately 10 features. Therefore, for each classifier, a compact 10‑feature panel was defined by selecting the m/z variables with the highest selection frequency across cross‑validation folds. In the training set, heatmaps based on these 10‑feature panels showed intensity patterns and sample clustering that were broadly consistent with the class labels, though some inter‑class overlap remained (Fig. 4A, C, E, insets).

To evaluate the generalization ability of these panels, the three fixed 10‑feature panels (derived from PLS‑DA, RF, and SVM training, respectively) were applied to the independent test set. The PLS‑DA-derived panel achieved an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.58–0.96) (Fig. 4B), the RF‑derived panel an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.48–0.90) (Fig. 4D), and the SVM‑derived panel an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.51–0.93) (Fig. 4F). Corresponding heatmaps of the test set based on the same panels revealed discernible but incomplete separation between MP and non‑MP samples, with a subset of samples displaying overlapping profiles (Fig. 4B, D, F, insets). Deep learning-based identification of high-risk periodontal conditions

Given that conventional machine‑learning models based on 10‑feature panels demonstrated only moderate generalization on the independent test set (AUC 0.73–0.78), we next explored deep‑learning architectures to more fully leverage the sequential nature of PESI‑MS m/z fingerprints. As a commonly used baseline in deep learning, we established a two‑layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) using the same 237‑feature set together with age and sex (Supplementary Table 6). Under the fixed split (random state = 42), the MLP achieved a test accuracy of 86.0%, demonstrating improved generalization compared to conventional machine‑learning models.

Three sequence‑based models were then implemented: a bidirectional long short‑term memory network (BiLSTM), a liquid neural network (LNN), and an LSTM enhanced with multi‑head attention (MHA‑LSTM). Age and sex were incorporated as covariates where specified. The input feature set comprised 237 retained m/z features, with age (1 feature) and sex encoded using two one‑hot features, yielding 240 features in total. Sensitivity of the deep-learning input pipeline to normalization and chunking is summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

We first established a bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) as a widely used sequential baseline (Fig. 5A, D). Following sensitivity analysis of different sequence lengths, the sequence length for all recurrent models—including the BiLSTM—was set to three (Supplementary Fig. 3). Retained features were accordingly organized into ordered sequences. Including age and sex improved test accuracy from 86.0% (spectral features only) to 90.7% (full input).

We next evaluated the multi‑head attention LSTM (MHA‑LSTM) (Fig. 5C, F). Models with varying numbers of attention heads (2 to 16) were examined (Supplementary Fig. 4). We selected four attention heads based on validation performance and model simplicity. To assess sensitivity to data partitioning, we fixed the attention head number at four and repeated training under four different random train‑test splits (Supplementary Table 5). The resulting test accuracies were highly consistent (89.0 ± 2.0; Supplementary Table 6; Supplementary Fig. 5).

We then implemented a liquid neural network (LNN) comprising two stacked LTC cells (Fig. 5B, E). The hidden‑state dimension was optimized as a critical hyperparameter through systematic sensitivity analysis across a range of 32 to 128 (Supplementary Fig. 6). We selected a hidden dimension of 96 based on validation performance and model compactness. To determine whether the chosen model capacity is adequately supported by the available data, we performed a learning-curve analysis. Model performance improved with increasing training‑set size but plateaued toward the upper range of available samples, indicating diminishing returns from additional data under the current model configuration (Supplementary Fig. 7; Supplementary Table 7).

To isolate the contribution of spectral features, we also evaluated an LNN variant using only age and sex as input; this baseline performed near chance level (43.0% test accuracy under the fixed split with random state = 42), confirming that spectral fingerprints are the primary driver of predictive performance. To interpret the role of individual input variables, SHAP analysis was performed, suggesting that age was among the top contributors in H and MP, whereas sex contributed marginally (Supplementary Fig. 8). To comprehensively assess robustness to training stochasticity (random initialization), all three architectures were trained and evaluated 15 times under an identical protocol with a fixed split (Supplementary Table 9; Supplementary Fig. 9). Across the 15 matched runs, test accuracies did not differ significantly among the three models (Friedman p = 0.410; all Holm-adjusted pairwise p > 0.05), indicating comparable performance.

Across all three architectures, training loss declined rapidly and validation performance stabilized within the first 15–20 epochs, indicating rapid learning of core discriminative features. Thereafter, mild divergence between training and validation curves emerged, with the onset occurring later in the LNN compared to the BiLSTM and MHA‑LSTM (Fig. 5G–L). Misclassifications were largely confined to mutual confusion between H and P groups, whereas MP samples were consistently identified (Fig. 5D–F). Calibration of the LNN model was assessed using reliability diagrams and expected calibration error under both the original training distribution and a simulated low‑prevalence scenario (Supplementary Fig. 10; Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that rapid salivary metabolic fingerprinting using probe electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (PESI‑MS), combined with a lightweight liquid neural network (LNN), can accurately identify individuals with periodontitis concurrent with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) as a distinct high‑risk phenotype. In a dual‑center cohort of 426 participants, the LNN showed a test accuracy of 91.9% with 100% recall for the MP group (periodontitis with T2DM). Together, these results suggest that MS1-level spectral patterns, even without prior metabolite identification, may contain sufficient discriminative information for initial screening under the current internal-validation setting.

Previous efforts to refine periodontitis‑diabetes phenotyping have largely focused on targeted biomarker panels (e.g., ELISA‑based host or microbial markers) or comprehensive omics profiling (LC/GC‑MS metabolomics, microbiome sequencing)29–33. While mechanistically informative, these methods often entail lengthy workflows and are less suited to chairside or repeat testing. In contrast, ambient ionization MS techniques—including PESI—are designed to minimize sample preparation and accelerate analysis34–36. Our PESI-MS workflow required ~20 min per sample end-to-end (including preparation and data export), with per-sample time further reducible in batch mode, suggesting practical time advantages relative to representative ELISA (typically ≥90 min)37 and UHPLC-HRMS workflows (typically ~30–60 min)38 (Supplementary Table 11).

Conventional multivariate and classical machine‑learning models (PLS‑DA, random forest, SVM) applied to VIP‑selected feature panels yielded only moderate test performance (AUC 0.73–0.78), reflecting a known limitation of small, manually curated feature sets in capturing predictive information distributed across correlated spectral variables39–42. Deep‑learning architectures are inherently suited to leveraging the sequential structure of full‑scan MS data, as the m/z‑ordered intensity profile can be treated as a one‑dimensional sequence where neighboring bins exhibit local correlations and distant bins may capture broader metabolic covariations43.

To isolate the contribution of sequential modeling, we first benchmarked a non‑sequential multilayer perceptron (MLP) as a baseline; while it achieved reasonable mean accuracy (85.5% ± 6.1% across splits), it exhibited substantially higher variability, confirming the advantage of sequence‑aware architectures44. Among recurrent models, the bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) and multi‑head attention LSTM (MHA‑LSTM) served as established sequential baselines, both achieving stable performance exceeding 90% accuracy. As an innovative alternative, we introduced the liquid neural network (LNN)—a continuous‑depth recurrent architecture built on ordinary differential equations that treats the m/z axis as a continuous physical domain. This formulation may help mitigate practical issues such as mass-axis drift and variable sampling resolution, while offering improved parameter efficiency45. With only two LTC cells, the LNN reduced trainable parameters by approximately threefold compared to LSTM‑based models, while maintaining performance comparable to established sequence baselines under the same protocol. Overall, the LNN achieved the best balance of accuracy, parameter efficiency, and cross‑split stability, supporting its selection as the primary model in this work.

Although definitive metabolite identification awaits MS/MS confirmation, the chemical classes associated with top discriminative features—dipeptides, acylcarnitines, and fatty acids—align with prior reports linking periodontitis to proteolytic tissue degradation46 and T2DM to mitochondrial dysfunction and altered lipid metabolism31,47. This observation is consistent with the biological plausibility of an MP-associated spectral fingerprint, while metabolite-level confirmation is needed.

The improved calibration achieved through temperature scaling (ECE reduction from 0.057 to 0.049 under training distribution) enhances interpretability of model outputs as risk estimates, enabling flexible threshold selection for different clinical contexts—e.g., prioritizing specificity in low‑prevalence screening or sensitivity in high‑risk specialty clinics. Robustness checks confirmed stable performance across data partitions (mean accuracy 89.0% ± 2.0% across four random splits), and while multi‑head attention provided modest gains (optimal at four heads), attention mechanisms do not automatically confer biological interpretability48.

Model behavior also warrants clinical contextualization: the observed 10% false‑positive rate predominantly involved healthy individuals misclassified as periodontitis rather than periodontitis with diabetes. This pattern is biologically plausible, because under the 2017 World Workshop-based 2018 EFP/AAP case definition, periodontal health is operationally defined as bleeding on probing (BOP) < 10%49—a pragmatic threshold that reflects low-grade inflammation rather than complete inflammatory quiescence. In a potential future screening workflow, such false positives are expected and would be addressed through confirmatory periodontal assessment.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, this dual‑center, cross‑sectional study employed internal validation through random splitting of the same cohort; therefore, the reported performance reflects an optimistic upper bound, and broader applicability will depend on prospective external validation across diverse populations, operators, and PESI‑MS platforms. Second, despite including age and sex as covariates and evaluating a demographics‑only baseline (43.0% accuracy), residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as smoking status, BMI, medication use, diabetes duration, and other systemic conditions—cannot be fully excluded and may influence spectral profiles. Third, comprehensive metabolite identification was not performed for all discriminatory m/z features, as the primary focus was fingerprint‑based classification; all annotations remain at MSI Level 3 and require targeted MS/MS confirmation. Future work will prioritize high-resolution MS/MS acquisition and confirmation against authentic standards to enable definitive metabolite identification. Finally, all measurements were conducted by a single operator on a single instrument, underscoring the need for reproducibility testing across time, batches, and platforms.

Future studies should leverage larger, prospectively collected cohorts with independent external validation, with balanced enrollment across sites and diverse populations, operators, and instruments, while adopting demographically balanced or stratified designs where appropriate. Context‑specific calibration and threshold optimization may further enhance clinical utility for distinct use cases (e.g., population-level screening vs. clinic-enriched settings). Targeted validation of key discriminatory m/z features via MS/MS and authentic standards would strengthen biological interpretability. Collectively, these steps will advance translational robustness while preserving the rapid, low‑burden advantages of the PESI‑MS approach50,51.

Overall, this proof-of-concept study demonstrates the feasibility of using rapid salivary PESI-MS metabolic fingerprinting with a lightweight liquid neural network for non-invasive identification of a high-risk periodontitis phenotype in individuals with T2DM under internal validation, supporting further evaluation in prospective external cohorts.

Methods

Ethics approval and informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of both participating centers: Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology (Approval No. PKUSSIRB-202281149) and Changsha Stomatological Hospital (Approval No. 2023-011). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any study-related procedures.

Study population and recruitment

This dual-center, cross-sectional study consecutively recruited participants from the periodontology clinics of Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology and Changsha Stomatological Hospital between October 2022 and June 2025. A total of 426 individuals were enrolled and stratified into three groups: periodontally and systemically healthy controls (H, n = 114), patients with generalized periodontitis (P, n = 209), and patients with generalized periodontitis and type 2 diabetes mellitus (MP, n = 103). Participant flow and the main reasons for non-participation after initial approach are provided in Fig. 6.

Participant recruitment and flow of the dual-center cohort.

Participants were consecutively recruited from two centers (Beijing and Changsha). Screening counts prior to saliva collection were not systematically recorded. After initial approach, the main reasons for non-participation included ineligibility by age, xerostomia or inability to provide sufficient saliva, refusal to provide saliva, and other infrequent reasons. Saliva samples with corresponding clinical data were obtained from 172 participants at the Beijing center and 254 at the Changsha center. All collected samples yielded usable PESI-MS spectra and complete covariate data (age and sex) and were included in the final analytic dataset (n = 426), comprising healthy controls (H, n = 114), periodontitis (P, n = 209), and periodontitis with type 2 diabetes (MP, n = 103). Owing to the consecutive and competitive enrollment process, all MP participants were recruited at the Changsha center.

The eligibility criteria were defined as follows. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 20 and 70 years; (2) presence of at least 20 natural teeth (excluding third molars); (3) absence of any systemic disease other than T2DM; (4) for participants in the P and MP groups, a diagnosis of generalized periodontitis based on the 2018 classification system; (5) for participants in the MP group, a physician-confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for ≥1 year and stable treatment; (6) periodontal health in the H group was defined as probing pocket depth <4 mm and bleeding on probing ≤ 10%, and glycemic health was defined as no history of diabetes, no physician diagnosis of diabetes, and no use of glucose-lowering medication; and (7) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) major systemic conditions other than T2DM (e.g., renal or hepatic dysfunction, active rheumatoid arthritis, history of stroke); (2) severe diabetic complications, including acute cardiovascular events within the past 12 months, symptomatic peripheral vascular disease, or severe neuropathy; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) active oral infections (e.g., periodontal or periapical abscess), oral mucosal lesions, extensive untreated caries, or the presence of orthodontic appliances or removable dentures; and (5) use of antibiotics or immunomodulatory drugs within three months prior to enrollment.

Saliva collection, preparation, and PESI‑MS analysis

Unstimulated whole saliva (2 mL) was collected from all participants after a minimum fasting period of 1 h, following a standardized operating procedure at both centers. Samples were immediately placed on ice, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the clarified supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. For samples collected at the Changsha center, the clarified supernatant was aliquoted locally, transported to Beijing on dry ice (typically within ~1 day), and transferred directly to −80 °C storage upon arrival. During transport, cold‑chain integrity was maintained, and no thawing events were observed. All aliquots were handled to minimize freeze–thaw cycles, and PESI‑MS analysis was performed using a single thaw per aliquot whenever possible.

Prior to analysis, a protein precipitation step was performed: the clarified saliva supernatant was mixed with anhydrous ethanol at a 1:1 ratio, vortexed, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was then diluted five‑fold with 1:1 ethanol/water, and the final extract was stored at −80 °C until analysis. For each measurement, 9 µL of the prepared extract was used for injection.

Metabolic fingerprinting was performed using a probe electrospray ionization mass spectrometry system (DPiMS‑2020, Shimadzu) with a single quadrupole mass analyzer (LCMS‑2020). All measurements were conducted centrally on the same instrument by a single operator in a single analytical campaign. The cohort composition by center was as follows: Center 1 (Beijing): P (n = 99), H (n = 73); Center 2 (Changsha): P (n = 110), H (n = 41), MP (n = 103). Instrument parameters (positive ion mode) were: sampling time 50 ms; sampling position 46.0 mm; ionization time 200 ms; discharge voltage 2.3 kV; probe speed 250 mm/s; probe frequency 2.48 Hz; desolvation line temperature 250 °C; heating block temperature 30 °C; event time 0.36 s; scan time per probe cycle ~0.7 min; mass scan range m/z 50–1000. Data acquisition was managed using LabSolutions (version 5.120) and PESI MS Solution (version 1.0).

Before each run, the PESI probe was rinsed in 60% isopropanol for 20 s to minimize carryover. Throughout the sequence, the PESI probe was replaced approximately every 50 samples, and a solvent blank (50% ethanol/water) was acquired immediately after each replacement to monitor background signal and potential carryover before resuming sample analysis. A total of ten solvent blanks were acquired at intervals across the sequence for systematic evaluation; total ion current (TIC) stability and spectral profile consistency were assessed by comparing overlaid TIC traces and representative spectra from early, mid, and late positions. Center‑related variation was examined using principal component analysis (PCA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) on the dual‑center subset (H and P groups).

PESI-MS data processing and feature extraction

The entire cohort of 426 samples was randomly split into a training set (80%, n = 340) and an independent hold‑out test set (20%, n = 86) using the train_test_split function from scikit‑learn. Within the training set, 20% of the samples were further allocated as a validation set for model selection and early stopping during training. All subsequent preprocessing and feature selection steps were performed exclusively on the training data. The parameters learned from these steps (e.g., imputation values, selected feature lists) were then applied consistently to transform both the training and test sets, thereby preventing data leakage. This protocol is summarized schematically in Fig. 7. The primary analyses used a fixed split with random state = 42 (random_state = 42 in scikit-learn), unless otherwise specified for split-sensitivity analyses.

Data splitting, feature selection, and leakage prevention workflow.

The full dataset (n = 426) was split into a training set (80%, n = 340) and a held-out test set (20%, n = 86) using a fixed random seed (random state = 42), with a validation subset within the training set for model tuning. All steps involving parameter fitting—including preprocessing (imputation, normalization, and scaling) and feature selection (detectability filtering, univariate testing, and VIP-based selection)—were performed exclusively on the training set. The resulting frozen feature lists and preprocessing transforms were then applied unchanged to the validation and test sets without refitting, preventing information leakage and enabling unbiased estimation of generalization performance. After training-set-only feature selection, 237 m/z features were retained for downstream modeling.

Raw data processing was conducted using the instrument software (LabSolutions v5.120). For each sample, the total ion chromatogram was integrated, and the average mass spectrum across three consecutive stable peaks was exported in JSpell format. Spectral features within the m/z range of 50–1000 were extracted. To ensure robust feature detection, we retained m/z features detectable in ≥75% of samples within any clinical group (H, P, or MP) in the training set. This group‑wise, detectability‑based filtering produced a fixed list of 456 features, which was then directly applied to the held‑out test set without refitting. The resulting data matrix comprised samples as rows and the intensity values of these retained features as columns.

Prior to statistical modeling, the data matrix underwent preprocessing. First, any remaining missing values were imputed using the median intensity of the corresponding feature across the training set. Second, to account for variations in total signal intensity between samples, each sample’s intensity vector was L1‑normalized (i.e., divided by the sum of its absolute intensities) using the Normalizer (norm = “l1”) in scikit‑learn. Third, feature intensities were standardized via Z‑score transformation using StandardScaler from scikit‑learn. All preprocessing parameters were learned from the training set only and then applied unchanged to the validation and test sets, preventing data leakage.

A two‑step filtering strategy was implemented within the training set to reduce noise and redundancy. First, a detectability filter retained only those features with a detection frequency of 90% or higher in at least one clinical group (H, P, or MP). Second, a univariate significance filter was applied, whereby one‑way ANOVA (H vs P vs MP) was performed for each remaining feature; only features with a F-statistic >3 were retained for subsequent multivariate model training. This two‑step, training‑set‑only filtering reduced the feature space from 456 to 237 m/z features.

Exploratory multivariate analysis and conventional machine-learning modeling

Exploratory visualization and conventional machine-learning analyses were conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.052. To visually represent the spectral profiles of each group, one representative sample per group was selected based on the highest similarity (after log(1 + intensity) transformation) to the respective group’s median profile. These representative mass spectra were plotted as m/z‑intensity stick plots for illustrative purposes only and were excluded from all formal feature selection, model training, and performance evaluation steps.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was first applied to characterize the overall distribution of the entire cohort (n = 426). This analysis, along with all subsequent statistical modeling performed within the MetaboAnalyst platform, followed its standard metabolomic preprocessing workflow: median normalization, log transformation, and autoscaling. To visually assess the effect of this normalization, a total of 50 randomly selected samples from three groups was visualized using box‑and‑whisker plots of their processed intensity distributions.

All subsequent exploratory and model development work was confined to the training set (n = 340). PCA was employed to examine global, unsupervised clustering trends. For supervised pattern discovery, partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS‑DA) was performed. The risk of overfitting was evaluated through permutation testing (2000 permutations). Features were ranked by training‑set variable importance in projection (VIP) scores, and those with VIP > 1 (n = 76) were retained as the input feature set for conventional classifiers. As a robustness check for multiple testing, we additionally performed a Kruskal–Wallis univariate analysis with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05), confirming that all VIP‑selected features remained significant after FDR adjustment.

Three established classifiers—PLS‑DA, random forest (RF), and linear support vector machine (SVM)—were implemented using the 76 VIP‑selected features as input. For this modeling stage, samples from the H and P groups were combined into a single “non‑MP” class, and a binary classification task was defined to distinguish the MP group from non‑MP. Multivariate ROC analysis with internal cross‑validation was used to rank features and to construct classification panels of varying sizes (top 3, 5, 10, 20, 38, and all 76 features). Features were ranked by their frequency of selection across cross‑validation folds within each classifier. The final conventional biomarker panel for each method was defined as the 10 m/z features with the highest selection frequency. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated based on these 10‑feature panels.

To evaluate the generalization ability of these panels, the three fixed 10‑feature panels (derived from PLS‑DA, RF, and SVM training) were applied directly to the independent test set (n = 86). Their performance was evaluated by computing ROC curves and AUCs, and corresponding heatmaps were generated for the test samples.

Deep-learning modeling and evaluation

All analyses were performed in a Jupyter Notebook (v6.5.4) environment using Python (v3.11.0) and PyTorch (v2.5.1). As a non‑sequential baseline, we implemented a two‑layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) using MLPClassifier from scikit‑learn (v1.6.1) with ReLU activation functions, the ‘lbfgs’ solver for weight optimization, and an L2 penalty parameter (alpha) of 0.001. The MLP used the same 237‑feature set together with age and sex as input, providing a reference point for classification performance in deep learning.

We then developed and compared three recurrent neural network architectures: a Bidirectional Long Short‑Term Memory network (BiLSTM), a Multi‑Head Attention LSTM (MHA‑LSTM), and a Liquid Neural Network (LNN). The BiLSTM was implemented based on PyTorch (v2.5.1) documentation using a two‑layer LSTM with bidirectional processing; the final sequential representation was constructed by concatenating the hidden states from both directions, with a dropout rate of 0.3 applied for regularization. The MHA‑LSTM was adapted from Selvam et al.53, integrating a multi‑head attention mechanism to enhance the model’s capacity to capture complex dependencies. The LNN architecture was adapted from Hasani et al.45, comprising two stacked Liquid Time‑Constant (LTC) cells that implement continuous‑depth recurrent dynamics along the m/z‑ordered input sequence; the hidden state dimension was optimized as a critical hyperparameter.

All deep‑learning models were trained and selected using the training/validation split described above, with hyperparameters optimized based on validation performance. Final model performance was evaluated on the held‑out test set without refitting, using test accuracy as the primary metric for the three‑class classification task (H, P, MP). For hyperparameter tuning, we conducted systematic sensitivity analyses over prespecified ranges: sequence length (3, 5, 8, and 10) for the BiLSTM; number of attention heads (2 to 16) for the MHA‑LSTM; and hidden‑state dimension (32 to 128) for the BiLSTM and the LNN. Hyperparameters were selected based on validation performance, with preference given to more parsimonious configurations when performance was comparable.

To evaluate the contribution of demographic covariates, we trained a BiLSTM model using spectral features only and compared its performance to the full model incorporating age and sex. The MHA‑LSTM was used to examine model stability across different random data splits, leveraging its attention mechanism to assess robustness. The LNN served as the primary model of interest; to further assess the contribution of demographic information within this continuous‑depth framework, we also evaluated an LNN variant using only age and sex as input (demographics‑only baseline). Additionally, to assess whether model performance approaches saturation with the current sample size, we performed a learning‑curve analysis for the LNN under the fixed data split. The model was trained on random subsamples of the training set at increasing sizes (10–95% of the training set, corresponding to n = 34–323) and evaluated on the same held‑out test set (n = 86). Five repeats were performed at each training size using different random seeds to capture variability. We further conducted sensitivity analyses under the same fixed split to evaluate robustness to: (i) deep‑learning input preprocessing and sequence construction, including alternative sample‑wise normalization strategies and sequence chunking configurations; and (ii) VIP‑based feature selection choices in conventional modeling, encompassing alternative normalization for VIP derivation and varying VIP thresholds.

To evaluate robustness to training stochasticity arising from random initialization, each recurrent architecture (BiLSTM, MHA‑LSTM, and LNN) was trained and evaluated 15 times under an identical protocol using the fixed split (random state = 42). Differences in paired test accuracies across models were assessed using the Friedman test, followed by post‑hoc paired Wilcoxon signed‑rank tests with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons where applicable. Post‑hoc probability calibration was assessed for the LNN using reliability diagrams and expected calibration error (ECE). Temperature scaling was applied on the validation set to calibrate the predicted probabilities, and calibration performance was evaluated under both the original training distribution and a simulated low‑prevalence scenario by applying a prior‑probability shift to the classifier outputs.
