Successful isolation of arrhythmogenic superior vena cava via balloon-in-basket pulsed field ablation: Overcoming phrenic nerve proximity
Authors: Dimitrios Gerontitis, Dimitrios Katsaras, Vassiliki Tsonona, Evangelia Christoforatou, Apostolos Katsivas, Panagiotis Ioannidis
Published: 2026

Introduction

Atrial tachycardia (AT) originating from non–pulmonary vein (PV) sources remains a significant challenge for clinical electrophysiologists. Among these sites, the superior vena cava (SVC) is 1 of the most common locations for focal triggers that can initiate or maintain atrial arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation (AF). The specialized anatomic architecture and distinct electrophysiological properties of the cavoatrial junction are thought to provide the necessary substrate for these tachycardias, which are often characterized by high frequencies and resistance to antiarrhythmic drugs. The standard treatment for symptomatic SVC-AT has long been radiofrequency (RF) catheter ablation. However, the SVC is located in a high-risk anatomic “danger zone,” flanked by the sinus node inferomedially and the right phrenic nerve posterolaterally. Thermal ablation (RF or cryoablation) carries an inherent risk of collateral damage, such as permanent phrenic nerve palsy or sinus node dysfunction.1, 2, 3 Pulsed field ablation (PFA) offers a tissue-selective, nonthermal alternative that can effectively isolate the SVC while sparing the adjacent phrenic nerve. However, given that sinus node cells share a similar electroporation threshold with atrial myocytes, the risk of nodal injury from extensive field delivery remains a critical consideration.

This case report details the successful use of a novel balloon-in-basket PFA system in treating a complex SVC-AT. To the best of our knowledge, this represents 1 of the first reported clinical cases of direct SVC-AT ablation using this specific technology, highlighting the balance between achieving electrical isolation of an arrhythmogenic vessel and preserving sinus node function.

Case report

A 71-year-old man with a history of arterial hypertension and type 2 diabetes presented with a 12-month history of paroxysmal, highly symptomatic palpitations. Previous clinical 12-lead electrocardiogram documentation during these episodes had identified a rapid, regular, narrow-QRS tachycardia with heart rates reaching 220 beats/min, characterized by P waves in the mid-R-R interval without clear polarity (Figure 1). The patient reported intense palpitations, dizziness, and presyncopal phenomena. Previous attempts at rhythm control with beta-blockers had failed; consequently, therapy was discontinued for at least 5 half-lives before the electrophysiological study to ensure unhindered arrhythmia induction.Figure 112-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating the clinical atrial tachycardia with a rate of 220 beats/min.

The procedure was performed via the right femoral vein, with vascular access obtained under ultrasound guidance. A decapolar catheter (Inquiry™, Abbott, St. Paul, MN) was positioned in the coronary sinus, whereas 2 quadripolar catheters (Response™, Abbott) were placed at the His bundle position and the high right atrium or right ventricle, for recording and pacing as required. Upon admission to the electrophysiology laboratory, the patient was in sinus rhythm. However, the clinical tachycardia was easily induced after isoproterenol infusion and programmed atrial stimulation. The induced tachycardia exhibited a cycle length (CL) of 260–280 ms, with a proximal-to-distal activation sequence recorded in the coronary sinus (Figure 2). Using the EnSite X™ 3-dimensional (3D) mapping system and the Advisor™ HD Grid mapping catheter (Abbott), a high-density activation map of the right atrium was constructed. The earliest activation site was identified within the SVC, approximately 1.5–2 cm above the cavoatrial junction, suggestive of a localized reentry circuit. At this site, fragmented, high-frequency endocardial potentials were recorded, covering a significant portion of the tachycardia CL (Figure 3A). Entrainment maneuvers could not further confirm the diagnosis, given that pacing consistently resulted in tachycardia termination. Although the AT frequently degenerated into AF, necessitating electrical cardioversion, high-density mapping was successfully completed during periods of stable tachycardia, confirming the SVC’s role as the primary arrhythmic driver. During sinus rhythm, high-resolution mapping of the sinus node was performed, using the patient’s baseline heart rate as a reference. A critical step involved mapping the right phrenic nerve. High-output pacing (20 mA at 2.0 ms) along the lateral SVC wall identified a capture zone that directly overlapped with the earliest activation site (presumed localized reentry area) identified during the activation map. These points were meticulously marked on the 3D map to define the zones at risk (Figure 3C). Given the high risk of phrenic nerve injury with thermal energy, the Volt™ PFA catheter (Abbott) was selected. This 13F “balloon-in-basket” catheter was advanced via an Agilis™ NxT steerable sheath (Abbott). Before energy delivery, intravenous midazolam was administered for conscious sedation. Upon entry of the Volt PFA catheter into the SVC, the tachycardia immediately terminated owing to mechanical contact with the vessel wall (Supplemental Video 1).

PFA was then delivered using a bipolar/biphasic pulse protocol. The balloon-in-basket design ensured stable circumferential contact and allowed for complete electrical isolation of the SVC at a level superior to the mapped sinus node. A total of 3 applications were delivered to ensure sufficient overlap and achieve complete circumferential isolation. Each application was delivered using the system’s standard output (1.8 kV, biphasic), ensuring a robust and definitive isolation pattern (Figure 4, Supplemental Video 2). The delivery of pulsed energy was well tolerated by the patient, despite transient phrenic nerve capture during the applications. After the procedure, no tachycardia was inducible despite aggressive isoproterenol challenge combined with programmed atrial extrastimuli and burst pacing. Furthermore, high-output pacing after the ablation confirmed robust phrenic nerve capture, consistent with the baseline assessment. The total procedure time was 90 minutes, with a fluoroscopy time of 8.5 minutes and a total fluoroscopy dose of 85 mGy. Activated clotting time values were maintained above 300 seconds throughout the therapeutic phase of the procedure. The procedure was completed without complications, and the patient was discharged the next day in good clinical status with no recurrence of symptoms.Figure 4Pulse field ablation using the Volt catheter within the superior vena cava. Discussion

In this case, we demonstrated the successful and safe isolation of an arrhythmogenic SVC using a novel balloon-in-basket PFA system in a patient where the SVC-AT served as the primary driver for clinical symptoms. To the best of our knowledge, published clinical experience with this specific balloon-in-basket technology for isolating an arrhythmogenic SVC remains extremely limited. It is well established that the SVC is a significant site for the initiation of various arrhythmias, often acting as a trigger for AF. Several mechanisms have been implicated in SVC arrhythmogenicity, ranging from enhanced automaticity and triggered activity to various forms of reentry.4 The underlying substrate for these arrhythmias is often rooted in the complex local anatomy and the distinct electrophysiological properties of the SVC. Anatomically, the SVC contains myocardial sleeves extending up to 4–5 cm from the cavoatrial junction, with their thickness progressively decreasing cranially.5 This configuration induces conduction anisotropy, which, alongside marked conduction delays and the significant disparity in effective refractory periods between the right atrium and the SVC,6 establishes a substrate highly conducive to reentrant circuits. In the present case, the observation of fragmented potentials spanning nearly the entire tachycardia CL, combined with the reproducible induction of tachycardia via programmed atrial extrastimuli, provided strong clinical evidence of such a reentry mechanism.

In addition to the mapping findings, we observed that the baseline heart rate remained remarkably stable throughout the delivery of pulsed energy. Significant fluctuations in heart rate, including sudden acceleration or deceleration, would have been subject to various interpretations. For instance, an acceleration might have indicated the activation of the right anterior-superior atrial ganglionated plexus, but it could also have been a warning sign of impending damage to the sinus node.7 In contrast, a decrease in heart rate could have been attributed to direct sinus node injury or a transient neuromodulatory effect. The absence of these variations provided greater diagnostic clarity, underscoring the anatomic precision of the ablation. Although the tachycardia could theoretically have been managed with focal ablation at the site of slow conduction, a circumferential isolation strategy was deemed clinically superior. This approach not only mitigated the prohibitive risk of phrenic nerve injury associated with conventional RF energy but also ensured a more robust and definitive elimination of the arrhythmogenic substrate. Given that the SVC activity was proven to trigger AF, complete isolation was favored over a focal approach to prevent future clinical recurrences. This was executed rapidly and efficiently using the Volt balloon-in-basket catheter, whose design allowed for excellent apposition within the tubular anatomy of the SVC, in accordance with emerging feasibility data and recently established standardized workflows.8,9 A significant advantage of this system is the real-time feedback regarding tissue contact, ensuring that the basket is properly seated against the vessel wall before energy delivery. Furthermore, the “Sensor-Enabled” technology provided clear 3D visualization within the EnSite X map, allowing for precise orientation and the maintenance of a safe distance from the sinus node. Our experience suggests that this combination of contact indicators and advanced 3D visualization is essential for navigating the anatomic and safety challenges inherent in SVC ablation.

Addressing the broader clinical context, the identification of non-PV triggers remains a complex challenge, given that laboratory provocation with isoproterenol, adenosine, or postcardioversion observation does not always correlate with real-world clinical behavior. This was particularly evident in our patient, who presented solely with symptomatic AT and had no clinical history of AF. However, during the procedure, the SVC-originating arrhythmia was identified as a potent AF trigger, given that it repeatedly degenerated into AF. We hypothesized that although a strong trigger was present, the atrial substrate was likely not yet sufficiently remodeled to sustain AF. Thus, we opted for a targeted, trigger-based strategy, performing successful SVC isolation while deliberately refraining from prophylactic PV isolation in the absence of clinically documented AF.

Regarding safety, although PFA is widely regarded as a safe modality for preserving nerve function owing to its tissue-selective nature, recent evidence suggests that phrenic nerve injury may be more frequent than initially estimated. In particular, cases of delayed phrenic nerve palsy occurring up to 3 months after PFA have been reported.10 Nevertheless, large-scale clinical data confirm that PFA remains remarkably safe against permanent and clinically significant injury.11 In our case, despite observing phrenic nerve capture during energy delivery, postprocedural testing confirmed intact nerve function. Finally, the procedure was successfully completed under conscious sedation with midazolam, given that the Volt system’s unique pulse waveform was well tolerated, consistent with other clinical feasibility data.12 The present case highlights the potential of combining tissue-selective energy with high-density mapping to safely navigate the complex anatomy of the SVC, effectively bridging the gap between procedural precision and patient safety in the management of SVC-AT.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that isolating an arrhythmogenic SVC using the novel balloon-in-basket PFA system is highly effective and safe. A key factor was the system’s high-density mapping capabilities, ensuring precise localization and protection of the sinus node. Excellent catheter apposition combined with real-time visualization allowed targeted energy delivery that successfully preserved adjacent critical structures, including the phrenic nerve. Furthermore, the efficiency of the procedure under conscious sedation underscores the clinical advantages of this technology. For patients where the SVC is the confirmed arrhythmic driver, this approach provides a definitive, precise, and well-tolerated therapeutic solution.
