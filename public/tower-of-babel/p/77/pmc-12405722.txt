Prenatal Ultrasound Diagnosis and Prognosis Analysis of Fetal Congenital Cataract
Authors: Huizhu Wang, Xin Jin, Mengmeng Wang, Hezhou Li, Juan Wu, Ling Liu, Zhengfeng Zhu, Mu Qiao, Yingying Wang, Lili Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the prenatal ultrasound signs of fetal congenital cataract, study the potential etiologies, and evaluate the perinatal outcomes and the prognosis.Patients and MethodsThe study included 29 cases of fetal congenital cataract identified using prenatal ultrasound screening from January 2018 to September 2023 in our hospital. Ultrasound signs, the potential etiologies, the perinatal outcomes, and prognosis were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 29 cases, 18 (62.1%) showed complete hyperechogenicity of the lens (type I), 6 (20.7%) showed dot or cluster hyperechogenicity within the lens (type II), and 5 (17.2%) showed the “double-ring” sign (type III). Moreover 18 (62.1%) involved isolated cataract and 11 (37.9%) involved cataract associated with extraocular abnormalities, with central nervous system and cardiac abnormalities being the most common. In terms of potential etiology, five cases (17.2%) had a family history, and genetic testing was performed in eight cases, with five of them showing abnormalities. Cases 2 and 16 involved two fetuses of the same pregnant woman, both of which had a hemizygous mutation of the OCRL gene (c.1514G > A). Moreover Case 3 involved Trisomy 13, and Case 17 had heterozygous mutations of the RAB3GAP1 gene (c.718C > T and c.1879dupA). Furthermore, Case 21 had a heterozygous mutation of the NHS gene (c.375C > A). Among the 29 cases, pregnancy was terminated in 21 (72.4%) and was continued in 4 (13.8%).ConclusionCongenital cataract, defined as lens opacity, presents as complete hyperechogenicity of the lens, a double-ring sign, or dot and cluster hyperechogenicity within the lens on prenatal ultrasound. Prenatal ultrasound can help assess the eyes and other systems during prenatal or postnatal examinations and can provide additional information for prenatal counseling and early postnatal treatment of congenital cataract.Keywords: congenital cataract, ultrasound, prenatal, genetic mutation, perinatal outcome

Introduction

Congenital cataract manifests as partial or complete clouding of the eye’s lens that is present at birth or that develops within the first year of life. This condition, which may affect one or both eyes, is a significant cause of vision impairment and blindness in infants and children.1 The global prevalence of congenital cataract has been reported to range from 1.91‱ to 4.24‱, with Asia having the highest prevalence at 7.43‱.2 The pathogenesis of congenital cataract is complex, and are mostly associated with hereditary syndromes, intrauterine infections, and metabolic diseases.3 It can manifest as isolated congenital cataract or as a syndrome accompanied by other ocular and systemic abnormalities.4 Timely diagnosis and intervention are crucial because the success of vision recovery after cataract surgery is heavily dependent on the early identification and treatment of the condition. To prevent blindness or amblyopia due to congenital cataract, proactive screening, prompt diagnosis, and early treatment are essential, especially in high-risk children.5 Prenatal ultrasound diagnosis offers advantages such as being non-invasive and repeatable, providing more information for prenatal counseling of pregnant women, early referral for intervention and treatment after birth. This study conducted a retrospective analysis on the ultrasound signs, potential etiologies, perinatal outcomes, and prognosis of fetuses diagnosed with congenital cataract prenatally.

Material and Methods

Subjects

From January 2018 to September 2023, a total of 29 cases of fetal congenital cataract were diagnosed among 62,190 pregnant women who underwent prenatal ultrasonography in our hospital, corresponding to an incidence rate of 4.6‱. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (approval number: 2023–213-01).

Instruments and Techniques

Fetal prenatal ultrasound examinations: All ultrasound assessments were conducted by skilled practitioners using advanced systems, including Voluson E8 and E10 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and WS80A (Samsung Healthcare, Seoul, Republic of Korea) paired with abdominal transducers (RAB 6-D, RM7C, and CV1–8A). Pregnant women underwent ultrasound in the supine position following the systematic prenatal ultrasound examination standard. The ultrasound involved transverse, coronal, and sagittal sectional imaging for evaluation of the bilateral eye dimensions, interocular distance, lens characteristics, vitreous body, and eyelids.

Ultrasound classification standard of fetal congenital cataract: On ultrasound, a normal lens appears as a circular hyperechoic ring with an anechoic center (Figure 1). The presence of fetal lens opacity suggests cataract. Ultrasound findings of fetal congenital cataract are classified as follows: type I, complete hyperechogenicity of the lens (Figure 2), type II, dot or cluster hyperechogenicity of the lens (Figure 3); and type III, presence of a “double-ring” sign (Figure 4). 6 The mean gestational age at the time of diagnosis was 24.8 ± 2.7 weeks (range, 21–31 weeks).

Data Collection

We collected ultrasound images, genetic results, perinatal outcomes, and family history of fetuses with congenital cataract. In addition, we collected information on the age of the pregnant women, gestational age at initial diagnosis (weeks), history of medication use, and history of exposure during pregnancy.

Ultrasound Characteristics of Other Systems

Of the 29 cases, 18 involved isolated cataract (62.07%, 18/29) and 11 involved cataract accompanied by extraocular abnormalities (37.93%, 11/29), mainly involving the central nervous system (6 cases, 54.5%), cardiovascular system (5 cases, 45.5%), and urinary system (3 cases, 27.3%). (Table 1).

Potential Etiologies

No enrolled pregnant women reported exposure to toxins or harmful substances throughout their pregnancies. Among the 29 cases of fetal congenital cataract, two women were screened for infections including Toxoplasma, Cytomegalovirus, Rubella virus, and Herpes simplex virus, with all results returning negative. A familial predisposition was noted in five cases (designated as Case 2, 13, 16, 27, and 28). Specifically, Case 13, 16, 27, and 28 were identified with dominantly inherited isolated congenital cataracts. Affected family members in these cases presented with clinical signs of cataract, which points towards a potential genetic link. Refer to Table 2.

Among eight cases that underwent genetic testing, five had abnormal results. Cases 2 (type I, bilateral) and 16 (type I, bilateral) involved two fetuses of the same pregnant woman, both of which had a hemizygous mutation of the OCRL gene (c.1514G > A). Moreover, Case 3 (type II, unilateral) involved trisomy 13, and Case 17 (type I, bilateral) involved heterozygous mutations (RAB3GAP1 c.718C > T, c.1879dupA). Furthermore, Case 21 (type II, bilateral) involved a heterozygous mutation (NHS c.375C > A). (Table 3) (Figures 5–7).

Prenatal Outcomes and Prognosis

11 cases of non-isolated congenital cataract chose to terminate the pregnancy, while among the 18 cases of isolated congenital cataract, 10 cases chose to terminate the pregnancy, 4 cases chose to continue the pregnancy, and 4 cases lost follow-up. In Case 13 (type I, unilateral), the infant was delivered at full term. The child underwent removal of the right lens at 1 year of age, followed by implantation of an intraocular lens (IOL) at 2 years of age. Currently, at 3 years of age, the child’s right eye has a visual acuity of 4.4. In Case 14 (type I, unilateral), the infant was delivered at 33+2 weeks. The child, who is now 1 year of age, experiences blurred vision in the right eye. An ophthalmologist has strongly recommended surgery as soon as possible. In Case 17 (type I, bilateral), the infant was delivered at 38+3 weeks. The child underwent lens removal in both eyes at 3 months of age, followed by optic nerve atrophy at 1 year of age. Presently, at 4 years of age, the child exhibits poor postoperative vision in both eyes. In Case 23 (type III, unilateral), the infant was delivered at term. The child underwent removal of the right lens at 5 months of age, followed by implantation of an IOL at 2 years of age. Currently, at 3 years of age, the child has poor vision in the right eye.

Discussion

Congenital cataract is a leading cause of treatable childhood blindness worldwide. The sensitive period of visual system development is often affected by congenital cataract, which reduces the quality of sensory information obtained by children and leads to irreversible visual defects. Therefore, early detection and timely intervention are crucial for good vision outcomes.7

Ultrasound is the preferred imaging method for the prenatal diagnosis of fetal congenital cataract. With improvements in ultrasound machine resolution, bilateral fetal lenses can be clearly visualized on ultrasound as early as 14 weeks of gestation if the fetus is in the appropriate position.8 However, prenatal ultrasound screening for congenital anomalies typically occurs at 20–24 weeks of gestation, making it easier to screen for abnormalities directly in the middle or late stages of pregnancy. In this study, congenital cataract was identified by prenatal ultrasound at a mean of 24.8 ± 2.7 gestational weeks. The incidence rate of congenital cataract was 4.6‱ of the study, which is consistent with the incidence rates reported in previous literature.9

Prenatal ultrasound findings of congenital cataract can be divided into three cataract types. Prenatal ultrasound findings of congenital cataract can be divided into three cataract types (types I–III), which have been described in the “Ultrasound classification standard of fetal congenital cataract subsection”. Other ultrasound findings associated with congenital cataract include irregular thickening and enhancement of echogenicity at the lens margin. In some cases, the vitreous artery may not be visualized. In mild-to-moderate cases, a positive ultrasound image of the lens may not be obtained, but the cataract gradually becomes visible after birth.8 Previous studies have reported that Type I ultrasound sign is the most common in congenital cataract Additionally, congenital cataract tends to affect both eyes simultaneously in a significant number of cases.5,6 In this study, type I cataract (62.1%) was the most common. This study also explored the ultrasound characteristics of fetal congenital cataract in the eyes and other systems. According to previous studies, congenital cataract can occur in isolation or in combination with other extraocular and intraocular abnormalities. Among the cases analyzed, 37.9% had extraocular anomalies, primarily affecting the central nervous system and cardiovascular system, and the findings are consistent with those of previous studies.6,10,11 Therefore, when fetal congenital cataract is suspected, detailed systematic ultrasound should be performed to evaluate and exclude other fetal abnormalities.

The causes of congenital cataract are complex and varied, and they are mainly associated with abnormalities in ocular genes, which result in abnormal synthesis of lens proteins. These abnormalities can be caused by genetic factors, intrauterine infections, and metabolic disorders, etc.11 Previous studies have shown that approximately one-third of cases are hereditary, one-third are associated with environmental risk factors, and the remaining one-third are idiopathic.12 Congenital cataract can be inherited in different ways, including autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and X-linked inheritance patterns. In addition, several chromosomal disorders have been shown to be associated with congenital cataract.8 Many chromosomal disorders, including Down syndrome (Trisomy 21), Trisomy 18, cridu-Chat syndrome (5p deletion), and 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, have been associated with congenital cataract.13

In this study, a total of seven patients (Case 2, 13, 16, 17, 21, 27, and 28) were identified with a genetic predisposition. While Case 13, 27, and 28 had not undergone an exhaustive genetic examination, the presence of cataract within their family lineage was noted. Particularly noteworthy are Case 2 and 16, which represent two successive offspring from a single couple. Upon review of the ultrasound findings and subsequent genetic analysis, these cases were clinically determined to have Lowe syndrome. This is a rare genetic disorder that is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern.14 Case 17 was found to have a connection to Warburg-Micro syndrome (WARBM), a rare neuro-ophthalmologic condition inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.15 This diagnosis was established through a combination of genetic testing, ultrasound observations, and the recognition of a comprehensive developmental delay postnatally. Furthermore, Case 21 was clinically diagnosed with Nance-Horan syndrome, another rare X-linked genetic disorder. This determination was made in light of the genetic testing outcome, fetal ultrasound findings, the clinical signs of mental retardation and strabismus in the mother, and a history of mental retardation in the maternal grandmother.16 In addition to these findings, the study also identified a case of Trisomy 13, which may be an aneuploidy abnormality that led to congenital cataract in the developing fetus. This case represents a new finding of this study, and the relationship between Trisomy 13 and congenital cataract awaits further analysis in studies with larger sample sizes.

Case 2, 16, 17, and 21 in the study exhibited unusual echogenicity within the fetal eyes upon ultrasound examination. Subsequent whole-exome sequencing (WES), a sophisticated method within the next-generation sequencing paradigm, uncovered genetic mutations pertinent to these conditions. WES is a comprehensive diagnostic tool that scrutinizes all exons in the genome, facilitating the identification of genetically diverse diseases and the evaluation of the likelihood of disease recurrence.17 Compared to conventional genetic testing methods, WES offers a faster and more accurate genetic diagnosis. It has the capability to identify variants of uncertain significance within the coding regions of the genome, which in turn increases the rate of disease detection.18 For patients with congenital cataract, WES not only aids in earlier diagnosis and treatment for the patients but also enables genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis.

When congenital cataract is detected in a fetus through ultrasound, a thorough clinical examination should be conducted to identify the underlying cause of the condition and determine if there are any genetic or chromosomal abnormalities inherited from the parents. In families with a known genetic cause, there is a certain probability of having a child with the disease during each pregnancy. Therefore, it is recommended to undergo preimplantation genetic testing for eugenic fertilization. By integrating WES with pre-implantation genetic screening, there is a significant potential to prevent the propagation of pathogenic mutations to the next generation. In addition to this preventive measure, proper monitoring throughout the pregnancy, coupled with routine ultrasonic assessments, can contribute to a reduction in the abortion rate within obstetric care.

The prognosis for children with congenital cataract depends on the cause, severity of the accompanying anomalies and the timing of surgery. The outcome is worse when combined with severe structural anomalies in other systems. Therefore, once congenital cataract is identified, early surgical intervention is necessary to improve the child’s vision and prevent irreversible deprivation amblyopia.19 The surgical technique of microincision cataract extraction and primary IOL implantation can improve the prognosis.20 At the same time, the treatment of amblyopia is of paramount importance. Studies have shown that congenital cataract surgery appears to be effective when combined with early, conscientious amblyopia treatment.21

In this study, the prenatal outcomes with congenital cataract varied. All 11 cases of non-isolated congenital cataract chose to terminate the pregnancy. Among the 18 cases of isolated congenital cataract, 10 opted to terminate the pregnancy, while 4 chose to continue. Of these, 3 cases all underwent surgical treatment after birth. Due to China’s national conditions, most families prefer to have healthy children. As a result, many pregnant women opt for induced labor, which is why this study failed to obtain more postnatal information about the fetuses.

This study conducted a detailed retrospective analysis of the ultrasound signs, potential etiologies, perinatal outcomes, and prognosis of congenital cataract, with complete clinical data. Additionally, it provided a detailed analysis of the genetic diagnostic results. This study offers support to ultrasonographers in diagnosing congenital cataract. Due to the low incidence of congenital cataract, the number of cases included in this study is relatively small. Among them, the number of cases that received treatment was even smaller, and the follow-up period was relatively short, with no sustained monitoring of the children’s postoperative conditions. Future research will further incorporate more cases through multi-center collaboration, while continuously tracking the postoperative conditions of children to obtain more objective and reliable evidence.

Conclusion

Therefore, ultrasound is the preferred imaging method for the prenatal diagnosis of congenital cataract, along with related tests to determine the etiology. Early prenatal diagnosis and timely postnatal treatment are crucial to achieve a good prognosis regarding the child’s vision.
