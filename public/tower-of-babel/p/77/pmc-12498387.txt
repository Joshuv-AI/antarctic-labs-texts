Cooperative Magnetic Phase Evolution via Oxygen Spin Coupling in a Layered Metal–Organic Framework
Authors: Jun Zhang, Yang Cao, Wataru Kosaka, Masaki Mimura, Yasutaka Kitagawa, Hitoshi Miyasaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) constructed from appropriate building units can exhibit both porosity and long-range magnetic order, enabling the modulation of magnetic states via mass transport. In most guest-responsive MOF magnets, the guest inclusions perturb the framework electronically or structurally and trigger abrupt phase transitions. In contrast, spin-active guests can directly mediate exchange pathways, enabling the continuous and controllable evolution of the magnetic order; however, the kinetic pathways of guest adsorption and spin mediation remain poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrated that the entrapment of O2 dimers in bottlenecked isolated pores between ferrimagnetic layers triggered a gradual evolution from ferromagnetism to antiferromagnetism in an isostructurally layered MOF. Systematic O2 sorption studies revealed a time-resolved shift in the Néel temperature (T N) from 17 to 28 K, correlating with the extent of O2 loading and indicating a cooperative growth of antiferromagnetic (AFM) domains. In contrast, the insertion of diamagnetic CO2 dimers preserved the original ferromagnetic (FM) ground state. Density functional theory calculations showed that the antiferromagnetically coupled O2 dimer acted as an efficient superexchange bridge between adjacent ferrimagnetic layers, stabilizing the AFM ground state. These findings provide direct evidence of the guest-induced, spin-mediated evolution of the magnetic phase in porous magnets and establish the entrapment of molecular oxygen as a versatile strategy for finely tuning the magnetic order in MOF magnets.

Introduction

The field of molecular magnetism involves the study of spin phenomena across molecular systems, ranging from discrete molecular units to extended three-dimensional (3D) frameworks. This discipline encompasses various quantum spin behaviors, including those found in single-molecule magnets, − single-ion magnets, , and potential spin-qubit materials. Additionally, molecular magnetism addresses spin crossover , and magnetic ordering phenomena at multidimensional scales, ranging from short-range ordering, such as in single-chain magnets, , to long-range magnetic ordering, such as in magnets. − A key advantage of molecular magnetic materials is their synthetic tunability and structural precision. Their spin ordering and anisotropic alignments can be rationally designed and controlled via bottom-up synthetic chemistry, enabling the integration of multiple functionalities coexisting or coupled with magnetism. Notably, magnetic metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) exhibit both porosity and magnetic properties, − offering promising avenues for the development of stimuli-responsive magnetic switches, memory elements, and sensor materials associated with mass transport. By judiciously selecting magnetic building blocks and assembling them into extended porous structures with π-conjugated frameworks that mediate superexchange interactions, creating “MOF magnets” with robust long-range magnetic ordering is feasible.

Common gases such as nitrogen (N2), carbon dioxide (CO2), and oxygen (O2) have garnered significant interest as chemical triggers for magnetic phase switching in MOF magnets (Figure a). − Their small molecular size, thermal stability over a broad temperature (T) range, and biological compatibility offer distinct advantages. However, these gases pose challenges owing to their extremely small kinetic diameters, complex adsorption behavior near the boiling points, and generally low chemical reactivity. In low-dimensional layered MOF magnets, four principal mechanisms have been proposed to account for guest-induced magnetic phase transitions (Figure b): (i) structural modification, which alters interlayer interactions; , (ii) intrahost charge transfer, ,, involving electron redistribution within the framework upon gas adsorption; (iii) host–guest charge transfer, involving electron transfer between the framework and adsorbed guest species; and (iv) spin mediation, wherein paramagnetic guest molecules directly mediate magnetic coupling between magnetic layers through spin-exchange interactions. Mechanism (iii) is primarily observed in the presence of chemically active guest molecules, such as iodine, whereas mechanisms (i), (ii), and (iv) are observed in the presence of common inert gases. In particular, mechanism (iv) often involves paramagnetic oxygen (O2), whose triplet spin ground state (S = 1) makes it highly effective at bridging spins between MOF layers or sites. , To date, only one prior study has provided direct evidence of spin mediation in O2-loaded MOFs. However, a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic processes driving guest-induced magnetic transitions, especially the transient spin interactions and structural rearrangements during guest-in and guest-out before equilibrium, remains elusive. Capturing these kinetic processes, particularly the spin-coupling behavior of paramagnetic O2 before full adsorption equilibrium is reached, is crucial for advancing the design of responsive magnetic materials and realizing their potential for magnetic sensing applications.

(b) Four mechanisms driving guest-induced magnetic (phase) transitions: (I) structural modification, (II) intrahost charge transfer, (III) host–guest charge transfer, and (IV) spin mediation. Each 2D layer produces a ferrimagnetically ordered layer (unequal antiparallel spins within a layer); the interlayer alignment determines a global FM or AFM stack.

In this study, we report that the dynamic nonequilibrium insertion of O2 dimers into a layered MOF ferromagnet induces a magnetic phase transition that converts the original ferromagnetic (FM) ground state into an antiferromagnetic (AFM) state in an O2-incorporated isostructure. By precisely regulating the O2 dosing duration, we monitored the spin-coupling dynamics and magnetic domain evolution in real time. In contrast, the introduction of CO2 dimers into an analogous framework preserved the initial FM order, highlighting the unique ability of paramagnetic O2 molecules to mediate magnetic interactions. The target layered MOF ferromagnet was [{Ru2(m-FArCO2)4}2TCNQ­(OMe)2] (1; m-FArCO2 – = m-fluorobenzoate; TCNQ­(OMe)2 = 2,5-dimethoxyl-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane, Figure a), which featured a rigid, fishnet-like hexagonal network stacked in a layered form, where each layer provided a heterospin ferrimagnetic layer and interlayer interaction was ferromagnetic, defining it as a layered ferromagnet. Compound 1 exhibited long-range magnetic ordering with a Curie temperature (T C) of 30 K (Figure b). Isolated pores between the ferrimagnetic layers accommodated up to two molar equivalents of O2 or CO2, forming dimeric guest species within the isostructural host lattice, denoted as 1⊃O 2 and 1⊃CO 2 , respectively. Although 1⊃CO 2 retained the original FM state with T C = 24 K, 1⊃O 2 exhibited an AFM ground state with a Néel temperature (T N) of 28 K, indicating that O2 dimers within the isolated pores may facilitate AFM coupling across the ferrimagnetic layers. Notably, the phase evolution from the pristine ferromagnet to an antiferromagnet in 1⊃O 2 occurred gradually; as O2 loading increased, T N continuously shifted from 17 to 28 K. This phase change occurred without detectable structural modifications, indicating that the overall magnetic response in a cooperative bulk-phase evolution was governed by the dynamically enlarging AFM domains. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further revealed that guest O2 molecules not only bridged adjacent ferrimagnetic layers via weak AFM coupling but also drove the phase evolution dynamically. O2 within narrow pores exhibits quite unique characteristics. A notable example in a microporous coordination polymer has demonstrated the formation of one-dimensional O2 dimer arrays with strong antiferromagnetic coupling. In contrast, in the present layered MOF, we observe the effect stemming from isolated O2 dimers that mediate interlayer AFM coupling. Thus, the continuous and precise control of cooperative signals and phenomena associated with phase transitions is a novel approach for controlling the bulk physical properties of MOFs via mass transport.

Results and Discussion

Solvent-Free MOF and Its CO2 and O2 Adsorption Behavior

The solvent-free compound 1, which was obtained by heating and evacuating the solvated form [{Ru2(m-FArCO2)4}2TCNQ­(OMe)2]·4DCE (1–4DCE) to remove the crystallization solvent 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE), crystallized as a robust two-dimensional (2D) fishnet-like framework. In this structure, two distinct paddlewheel-type diruthenium centers, [Ru(1)2] and [Ru(2)2], each coordinated by four m-fluorobenzoate groups, are bridged by a single TCNQ­(OMe)2 moiety (Figure a). The [Ru2] and TCNQ­(OMe)2 units exhibit inversion symmetry at their respective midpoints, resulting in a symmetrically ordered arrangement. Detailed packing analysis revealed that the 2D zigzag layers were assembled into a 3D architecture with well-defined interlayer spacings, characterized by an interlayer vertical (l V) distance of 7.75 Å and translational (l T) distance of 10.51 Å. Within each layer, isolated cavities are located in the vicinity of the TCNQ­(OMe)2 moieties, indicating intrinsic porosity and diffusion pathways for guest molecules along the [11̅0] direction (Figure b,c).

Single-component sorption isotherms for N2, CO2, and O2 revealed the significant adsorption of CO2 and O2, whereas the adsorption of N2 was negligible at 77 K in 1 (Figure S1a), indicating that N2 does not fill the bottlenecked pores under these conditions. The CO2 adsorption isotherm at 195 K exhibited typical physisorption behavior (Type I based on the IUPAC classification), which is characteristic of microporous materials. The CO2 uptake reached approximately 27.0 mL (STP) g–1 (2.1 mol/mol) at PCO2=5.4kPa , followed by a linear increase to 53.7 mL (STP) g–1 (4.3 mol/mol) at PCO2=99kPa . The adsorption of more than 2 mol of CO2 per mol of formula unit (mol/mol) at 100 kPa can be attributed to its adsorption in mesopores, which were formed by the aggregation of crystalline particles, or on crystal surfaces. To further elucidate the CO2 adsorption behavior, we recorded isotherms at multiple T values (Figures a and S2), as well as isobars, under PCO2=100kPa (Figure S3a). These measurements revealed a systematic decrease in CO2 uptake with increasing T value, consistent with the exothermic nature of physisorption. The variable-pressure powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern of 1 (vacuumed) recorded at 200 K under PCO2=3kPa (Figure b) revealed that some peaks corresponding to the (01–1), (110), (101), and (10–1) indices slightly shifted from their pristine 2θ positions, without the appearance or disappearance of peaks. These peak shifts indicate only minor changes in the unit cell parameters, with no visually apparent differences in the structure (vide infra). Structural modifications occurred due to the introduction of CO2 at a low PCO2 of 3 kPa, indicating that they resulted from the insertion and diffusion of a single CO2 molecule. The variable-temperature PXRD patterns of 1 recorded in the range of T = 200–300 K under PCO2=100kPa were nearly identical (Figures S3b–d), despite the amount of adsorbed CO2 varying from one molecule to approximately four molecules (Figure S3a). This observation was further corroborated by the time-resolved PXRD patterns of 1 recorded at PCO2=100kPa (Figure S4), which revealed that the structural transformation occurred within 1 h of CO2 dosing time. Specifically, adsorption equilibrium was achieved more rapidly within this temperature range of 200–300 K owing to faster CO2 diffusion, effectively accounting for the isotherm (Figures a and S2) and isobar adsorption characteristics (Figure S3a).

(a) CO2 adsorption isotherms of 1 at different temperatures; the solid lines are guided to the eye. (b) Variable-pressure PXRD patterns recorded at 200 K under different CO2 pressures.

The O2 sorption isotherms of 1 were measured at 90, 120, 140, 160, and 200 K (Figures a and S5). Across all measured temperatures, the isotherms exhibited a nearly linear increase in O2 uptake with increasing pressure, except for a slight deviation from linearity at low PO2 when the isotherm was measured at 160 K. At PO2=100kPa , the O2 uptake reached approximately 1 mol/mol at 90 and 120 K, increased significantly to approximately 2 mol/mol at 140 and 160 K, and decreased to approximately 1 mol/mol at 200 K. This temperature-dependent trend is further illustrated by the O2 adsorption isobar at 100 kPa (Figures b). The isobar exhibits a concave-down profile: as the T value increases from 95 to 160 K, O2 uptake increases from 3 mL g–1 to a maximum of 27 mL g–1; at 200 K, O2 uptake decreases to 14 mL g–1. These results indicate that O2 adsorption is predominantly kinetically controlled in the 95–160 K range, whereas above 160 K, the process is governed by the thermodynamic stability of the O2-accommodated phases within approximately 1 h of O2 dosing time. The linear isotherms and concave-down temperature dependence indicate that O2 adsorption proceeded through constricted cavities, specifically bottlenecked pore channels, as revealed by the structural analysis of 1 (Figure b,c). Furthermore, the adsorption of CO2 at 195 K occurred more readily than the adsorption of O2 at 160 K; this behavior can be attributed to the smaller kinetic diameter of CO2 (3.3 Å) compared to O2 (3.46 Å), along with the increased framework flexibility at high T values.

O2 adsorption (a) isotherms and (b) isobars of 1 at different temperatures; the solid lines are guided to the eye. (c) PXRD patterns measured under PO2=100kPa at various temperatures during cooling and after evacuation (black) at 120 K. (d) Time-dependent O2 adsorption at fixed temperatures under PO2=100kPa .

Temperature-dependent PXRD patterns of 1 under PO2=100kPa were recorded in the range of 120–200 K, revealing no structural transitions (Figure c). The diffraction patterns were nearly identical to those of the guest-free framework obtained under vacuum at 120 K, indicating that O2 adsorption, regardless of the loading level (1 or 2 mol/mol), did not induce any phase transformation, such as a gate-opening transition at a threshold pressure (Figure ). The dynamic time-resolved O2 adsorption curves recorded at fixed T of 120, 140, and 160 K under PO2=100kPa (Figure d) exhibited notable differences in O2 uptake behavior. Saturation was reached more rapidly at 160 and 140 K than at 120 K. Additionally, the total O2 uptake over 12 h was greater at higher temperatures. This behavior aligned with the trends observed in the isotherms and isobars (Figure a,b), indicating more favorable adsorption kinetics at higher T values, although the thermodynamic stability decreased as T increased above 160 K. The PXRD patterns of 1⊃O 2 were nearly identical to those of 1, and 1⊃CO 2 also exhibited a similar PXRD pattern, although some peaks were slightly shifted (vide supra). This isostructural nature was further confirmed by single-crystal XRD (SCXRD) measurements carried out under gas-loading conditions, as described in the following section. To monitor the structural evolution of the framework during O2 uptake, we recorded time-dependent PXRD patterns at 120, 140, and 160 K under continuous O2 dosing at 100 kPa. As shown in Figure S6, no discernible changes were observed in the positions or intensities of the diffraction peaks, confirming the absence of any structural transformations or formation of intermediate phases during the dynamic gas uptake (Figure d). These results confirm the structural robustness and isostructural nature of 1 during gas sorption.

Crystal Structures of 1⊃CO2 And 1⊃O2

To elucidate the structures of 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 , we obtained their SCXRD patterns under CO2 and O2 gas atmospheres (PCO2/O2=100kPa) , which refines 1⊃CO 2 as C72H40F8N4O22Ru4 (2 CO2 per formula unit at 195 K) and 1⊃O 2 as C70H40F8N4O19.6Ru4 (∼1.6 O2 per formula unit at 140 K) in Table S1. Both frameworks crystallized in the triclinic space group P–1 with unit cell parameters similar to those of 1, as summarized in Table S1. The layered framework consisted of two crystallographically independent [Ru2] units, [Ru(1)2] and [Ru(2)2], and one TCNQ­(OMe)2 molecule, each of which had an inversion center (Z = 1) (Figure S7). The interlayer void space around the TCNQ­(OMe)2 moieties, identified in 1 with a solvent-accessible volume of 126 Å3, was occupied by two potential CO2 or O2 adsorption sites. These sites were centered around an inversion point, with site-occupancy volumes of 140 and 133 Å3 for 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 , respectively (Figures d–g and S8). SCXRD data revealed that 1⊃CO 2 accommodated two molar equivalents of CO2 per unit cell, whereas 1⊃O 2 contained approximately 1.6 mol equiv of O2. The lower O2 loading relative to the theoretical maximum of the two equivalents is consistent with the adsorption behavior of O2 at 140 K (Figure a,b,d).

The structure of 1⊃O 2 closely resembled that of 1, while that of 1⊃CO 2 exhibited slight deviations. Notably, the m-fluoro substituent on the benzoate groups of [Ru(2)2] exhibited positional disorder in both 1 and 1⊃O 2 but was ordered in 1⊃CO 2 . No other significant differences were observed between the structures of the three compounds. For example, no significant differences were observed in the Ru–N–C angles; the average Ru–N–C angles for 1, 1⊃CO 2 , and 1⊃O 2 were 157.1°, 154.9°, and 156°, respectively. These subtle differences, corroborated by the PXRD patterns (Figures S1b, S3, S4, and b, c), indicate that the framework of 1⊃CO 2 is similar to those of 1 and 1⊃O 2 . The fishnet-like layered arrangements in both 1⊃O 2 and 1⊃CO 2 were oriented along the (110) plane, as shown in Figures and S8. Gas insertion resulted in subtle changes in l V and l T: from 7.75 and 10.51 Å in 1 to 7.85 and 10.46 Å in 1⊃CO 2 and 7.87 and 10.53 Å in 1⊃O 2 , respectively. Overall, the values were almost identical.

Compound 1 has a charge-transfer ferrimagnetic layer in which the [Ru2] and TCNQ­(OMe)2 components function as electron donors and acceptors, respectively. Therefore, comparing the electronic states of [Ru2] and TCNQ­(OMe)2 in 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 with those in 1 is fundamental to understanding their magnetic properties. The local bond distances around these units reflect their electronic states, and the relevant bond lengths and angles for 1⊃CO 2 , 1⊃O 2 , and 1 are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. The Ru–Oeq (Oeq = carboxylate oxygen atom) bond lengths fall within narrow ranges depending on the [Ru2] oxidation state, typically 2.06–2.07 Å for [Ru2 II,II] and 2.01–2.03 Å for [Ru2 II,III]+. − The oxidation states of [Ru(1)2] and [Ru(2)2] units in 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 were [Ru(1)2 II,III]+ and [Ru(2)2 II,II/II,III]0.5+, respectively, which were identical to those in 1. The electronic state of TCNQ­(OMe)2 moiety, i.e., TCNQ­(OMe)2 ρ, was evaluated using the Kistenmacher relationship (ρ = A[c/(b + d)] + B), where b, c, and d represent the respective bond distances for the 7,9-, 1,7-, and 1,2-positioned C–C sets in TCNQ­(OMe)2 moiety (Table S3). The estimated ρ charge values (for 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 were −1.3(5) and −1.4(5), respectively, indicating an intermediate charge state between TCNQ­(OMe)2 •– and TCNQ­(OMe)2 2–. These assessments indicate that both 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 maintained a charge-disproportionation state analogous to that of 1, which is formulated as [{Ru(1)2 II,III}+–{TCNQ­(OMe)2}1.5––{Ru­(2)2 II,II/II,III}0.5+]. , In situ infrared spectroscopy further confirmed the charge state of TCNQ­(OMe)2: the CN stretching peaks, which are highly sensitive to the oxidation state of TCNQ­(OMe)2, remained unchanged under both CO2 and O2 atmospheres ( PCO2/O2=100kPa , Figure S9). These findings indicate that the ferrimagnetic layers observed in 1 persisted in 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 , which exhibited identical packing forms.

Magnetic Properties of 1⊃CO2

Given that variations in the spin number or spin coupling within an MOF magnet can be sensitively detected using a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID), we investigated the effects of CO2 and O2 adsorption at local dimeric cavities between the ferrimagnetic layers of 1. A freshly synthesized 1–4DCE sample was sealed in a custom-made SQUID measurement cell connected to an atmosphere-controlled apparatus equipped with a turbomolecular pump (TMP). The sample was evacuated in situ to obtain the desolvated form 1, ensuring that it was not exposed to ambient atmosphere before the magnetic measurements.

To investigate the magnetic changes induced by CO2 dosing, we recorded field-cooled magnetization (FCM) curves under an applied magnetic field (H) of 100 Oe after dosing CO2 gas at PCO2=100kPa (Figure ). Upon cooling, the magnetization (M) increased sharply at approximately 25 K, indicating the onset of long-range magnetic ordering, and continued to increase down to 1.8 K. A similar abrupt increase in M was also observed under a lower H value of 5 Oe (Figure S10a), confirming the FM ground state of 1⊃CO 2 . Additionally, the zero-field-cooled magnetization (ZFCM) curves at H = 5 Oe exhibited a single peak and merged with the corresponding FCM curves at approximately 24 K, clearly defining the T C for 1⊃CO 2 at 24 K (Figure S10a). This T C value for 1⊃CO 2 was slightly lower than that observed for the pristine form 1 (T C = 30 K), reflecting the influence of structural modification caused by CO2 adsorption (Figure S10). In [{Ru2(RCO2)4}2TCNQR x ]-layered ferromagnets, T C is primarily governed by superexchange spin coupling in the intralayer. These interactions can be evaluated by examining the Ru–N bond lengths and Ru–N–C bond angles within the layer; shorter Ru–N lengths and larger Ru–N–C angles typically indicate stronger intralayer superexchange interactions and can be effective for obtaining higher T C. In 1⊃CO 2 , the Ru–N bond length varied minimally compared with that in 1; however, the Ru–N–C angles in 1⊃CO 2 (average: 154.9°) were smaller than those in 1 (average: 157.1°) (Table S2). Consequently, these minimal angular differences, which are potentially critical in this case, are likely responsible for the decrease in T C from 30 to 24 K upon CO2 uptake. T C could be switched repeatedly between 30 and 24 K via reversible CO2 desorption–adsorption cycles (inset of Figure a). Although this shift in T C is relatively small, it highlights the intralayer structural modifications arising from the dense packing of CO2 molecules (see the structural section). The M–H curve of 1⊃CO 2 was similar to that of 1. Although the remnant magnetization (RM) value was slightly lower than that of 1, the saturated magnetization (M s) and coercive field (H c) were nearly identical to those of 1. The identical M s values agree with the existence of the same spin system in 1⊃CO 2 as that in 1 (Figure b).

Magnetic Properties of 1⊃O2

FCM curves for 1⊃O 2 were measured at various H values after dosing the sample of 1 with 100 kPa of O2 at 160 K for 12 h, ensuring adsorption equilibrium before the initial scan. A pronounced cusp appeared at 18–28 K at H values below 2 kOe, indicating the stabilization of an AFM ground state in 1⊃O 2 (Figures a and S11), a characteristic of metamagnetic behavior. This spin-flip phenomenon was corroborated by the magnetization hysteresis (M–H) loops (Figure b); an abrupt transition in M was observed during the initial sweep from 0 to 7 T. This transition was visible as a sharp peak in the plot of dM/dH vs H (Figure c). Based on these findings, an H–T phase diagram (Figure d) was constructed, confirming the typical metamagnetic behavior of 1⊃O 2 . Notably, at 1.8 K, M continues to rise up to 7 T without fully saturating (Figure b). This phenomenon may be attributed to the strong anisotropy of the [Ru2 II,II] and [Ru2 II,III]+ component units, − as well as the antiferromagnetic coupling in dimerized O2 molecules (vide infra). Assuming the formation of O2 dimers within the framework cavities, the expected difference in M (ΔM s) would be 4 NμB (g = 2.0), consistent with the two isolated O2 molecules per dimer. To isolate the paramagnetic contribution from the adsorbed O2 molecules, we subtracted the M–H curve of 1 from that of 1⊃O 2 at 1.8 K. Figure S12 shows the M–H curve corresponding to the contribution of the adsorbed O2 (the small hysteresis in the ±1 T field range is negligible and likely results from minor differences in the subtracted data). However, the M–H curve representing the magnetic contribution of O2 does not align with the Brillouin function model, which involves two S = 1 spins with g = 2.0, as its slopes increase more gradually. This behavior indicates that the dimeric O2 spins were coupled antiferromagnetically between the ferromagnetically aligned layers, regardless of whether they were isolated or frustrated. This observation is consistent with our conclusion that O2 dimers act as magnetic mediators between ferrimagnetic layers.

The magnetic phase transformations induced by the inclusion of O2 were fully reversible during the desorption–adsorption cycles (Figure S13). Given the isostructural nature of 1⊃O 2 and 1, the spin of the adsorbed O2 molecules, located between the TCNQ­(OMe)2 moieties as revealed by structural analysis (Figure ), is pivotal for driving the magnetic phase shift from the FM state of 1 to the AFM state of 1⊃O 2 . In particular, the newly established interlayer magnetic exchange pathway mediated by the O2 spins superseded the original interlayer FM coupling, as illustrated by mechanism (IV) in Figure b. This interpretation is further supported by the behavior of 1⊃CO 2 , which featured a similar dimeric structure but retained its FM ground state, highlighting the unique role of the paramagnetic O2 species in mediating magnetic interactions.

Variation of Magnetic Properties Induced by Time-Resolved O2 Adsorption

The O2 adsorption behavior of 1 exhibited a concave-down temperature dependence, with O2 adsorption below 160 K being predominantly kinetically controlled. To investigate the dynamic magnetic properties during O2 uptake, we regulated O2 adsorption at various temperatures, particularly at T ≤ 160 K, and monitored the corresponding magnetic changes over time. Compound 1, which was prepared in situ and sealed within the measurement cell, was cooled to 160, 140, and 120 K, followed by O2 dosing at PO2=100kPa for a specified period (referred to as dosing time). The sample was rapidly cooled to 120 K after dosing, and magnetic measurements, including FCM, RM, and M–H curves, were acquired for each kinetically stabilized O2-adsorbed state (Figure ).

As fundamental information for ferromagnet 1, the M values obtained from the FCM at temperatures below T C reflect the extent of long-range FM domain ordering. The substantial size of the FM domains was confirmed by an M value exceeding 4 × 103 emu G mol–1 at 1.8 K. Upon dosing with O2, the long-range ordered M value at T < 30 K gradually decreased with increasing dosing time (Figures a,b,c,g and S14, S15), regardless of the dosing temperature and the amount of O2 introduced. This decrease indicates the emergence of new magnetic domains that coexist with the original FM domains during the initial stage, consistent with the formation of the AFM phase in 1⊃O 2 . As shown in the insets of Figure a–c, the normalized M values (relative to those at 1.8 K) clearly illustrate the transformation from a FM state to an AFM state during dynamic O2 sorption, with the temperature-dependent magnetization curves showing the characteristic peak for the AFM phase. These results demonstrate that the evolution of the magnetic phase induced by O2 adsorption progressed gradually over time.

Regarding the domain structures formed by the kinetic migration of O2 through bottlenecked isolated pores, three plausible intermediate states (cases (ii)–(iv)) are proposed, as illustrated in Figure , in addition to cases (i) and (v) for the pristine structure (1) and the fully pore-filled structure (1⊃O 2 ), respectively. In case (ii), if less than 1 mol/mol of O2 is adsorbed, the paramagnetic O2 monomers (S = 1) may form a single O2 layer between the ferrimagnetic layers. Previous studies by our group have shown that the presence of highly symmetrical paramagnetic spins within interlayer spaces can be critical for achieving long-range ordering. ,− Although this scenario is plausible, the present study did not obtain direct experimental evidence for its occurrence. The remaining two cases represent scenarios of incomplete O2 diffusion: (iii) partially blocked pores and (iv) partially defective pores. These configurations typically occur when O2 uptake is less than 2 mol/mol. Domain (iii) is likely to emerge during the early stages of adsorption, whereas domain (iv) may form in the later stages as the adsorption approaches saturation, as in case (v).

Because the structures of 1 and 1⊃O 2 are highly similar, distinguishing the dominance of either magnetic domain over time, even from PXRD patterns, is difficult. (Figure S6). Nevertheless, the appearance of a distinct peak in the FCM curves within the temperature range of T N = 17–28 K during O2 dosing at 160 K indicates the presence of imperfect diffusion states that evolve over time (Figure h). Despite peak broadening, the FCM peak remained visible when the O2 dosing time exceeded 3 h at 140 K and 12 h at 120 K. These findings indicate a gradual phase transition from the FM state of 1 to AFM domains, eventually resulting in the fully O2-loaded phase 1⊃O 2 with T N = 28 K (case (v) in Figure ). A similar phase evolution was also observed in the O2-induced phase transition of the layered ferromagnet [{Ru2(3,5-F2ArCO2)4}2{TCNQ­(OMe)2}], which was simulated using the Monte Carlo method.

These phase evolution events occurred in an isostructural form. In addition, 1⊃CO 2 , which adopted a similar structure, retained a FM ground state. This fact indicates that the arrangement of the overall magnetic order is mediated by the spins of the O2 dimer layers between the ferrimagnetic layers. At 160 K, O2 adsorption reached equilibrium more rapidly at T N = 28 K than at lower dosing temperatures (Figure a,b,c,h). At 120 and 140 K, the AFM phase transition temperature T N gradually increased with time (Figure h), indicating that the AFM ground state evolved cooperatively in response to the diffusion of the O2 dimer, denoted as T N = f(x n), where x n is the degree of O2 dimer diffusion, and a longer time was required to achieve the equilibrium AFM phase with T N = 28 K. Additionally, the magnetic hysteresis loops (Figure d,e,f) revealed that M s progressively increased with increasing dosing time, consistent with the continuous dynamic adsorption of spin-carrying O2 molecules into the framework (Figures d and g).

Computational Study of Exchange Coupling via O2 Spins

To investigate the magnetic interaction between layers mediated by O2 spins, we conducted DFT calculations on model structures of 1⊃O 2 and 1⊃CO 2 . Given that two triplet O2 molecules are located between two TCNQ­(OMe)2 •– moieties from adjacent layers, we constructed the following four-spin-site model: [TCNQ­(OMe)2 •–···O2···O2···TCNQ­(OMe)2 •–], as shown in Figure a. This model was based on the atomic coordinates obtained from single-crystal X-ray crystallography. The coordinates are listed in Table S4. We calculated the total energies of the four states with varying spin configurations, as illustrated in Figure b, using spin-unrestricted (U)­B3LYP/6-31G* level theory. The results, summarized in Table S5, indicate that state 4, in which all spins in adjacent molecules were antiferromagnetically aligned, was the most stable. To analyze the nature of the exchange coupling between molecules, we defined two effective exchange parameters for the Ising Hamiltonian: J TO (TCNQ­(OMe)2···O2) and J OO (O2···O2) interactions, expressed by the following equation: HIsing=−2JTOSTCNQ(1)ZSO2(1)Z−2JOOSO2(1)ZSO2(2)Z−2JTOSO2(2)ZSTCNQ(2)Z where STCNQ(x)Z and SO2(x)Z (x = 1 or 2) represent SZ values of TCNQ­(OMe)2 •– and O2. As detailed in Table S5, J TO and J OO were −1.0 and −4.7 cm–1, respectively, based on the energy differences among states 2, 3, and 4. Although these values were weak, they were large enough to support the AFM coupling between the spins of adjacent molecules. Given that the B3LYP functional can sometimes overestimate the stability of antiferromagnetically coupled states, we performed additional calculations using BHandHLYP/6-31G* level of theory, which was reported to provide more reliable estimates for exchange interactions with fully spin polarized systems. The J TO and J OO values calculated using BHandHLYP/6-31G* were −1.1 and −2.8 cm–1, confirming the presence of weak AFM interactions. The spin size in the model was also simulated by Boltzmann simulation in Table S5. To evaluate the validity of the Ising model and account for spin contamination error in spin-unrestricted calculations, we estimated J TO and J OO values using Yamaguchi’s approximate spin projection method, which is based on two-spin-site Heisenberg Hamiltonians. These calculations were performed using two-spin-site models (models 1–3), as shown in Figure S16 and Table S6. The results agreed with those obtained from the four-spin-site Ising model. Furthermore, the results revealed that the exchange coupling between the two TCNQ­(OMe)2 •– moieties was zero in the absence of O2 molecules. This fact indicates that AFM coupling between layers does not occur in the absence of oxygen molecules at the level of local exchange interactions. The magnetic behavior of 1 indicates that interlayer dipole interactions are ferromagnetic in nature. Therefore, AFM coupling in 1⊃O 2 arises from spin-mediated interactions facilitated by oxygen molecules. In other words, the AFM couplings of J TO and J OO are essential for the observed magnetic order. The weak AFM coupling between adjacent molecules, i.e., TCNQ­(OMe)2 •–···O2 and O2···O2, can be attributed to the overlap between their SOMOs (SOMO = singly occupied molecular orbital). As summarized in Table S7, small SOMO–SOMO overlaps were detected between adjacent molecules, contributing to the stabilization of the AFM state.

Conclusions

Understanding the dynamics of guest-responsive magnetic MOFs is essential for understanding host–guest interactions in which paramagnetic adsorbates directly couple to framework spins. In this study, we demonstrated that guest adsorption droves spin-mediated magnetic phase evolution. The nonequilibrium insertion of O2 dimers into the bottlenecked interlayer cavities of the layered ferromagnet [{Ru2(m-FArCO2)4}2TCNQ­(OMe)2] droves a continuous, isostructural transformation to an AFM state. The phase evolution was experimentally demonstrated by a gradual increase in T N from 17 to 28 K with increasing O2 loading, indicating a cooperative phase transition depending on O2 dimer domains. In contrast, the analogous insertion of diamagnetic CO2 did not alter the ground state of the ferromagnet. DFT calculations on the spin configuration of the [TCNQ­(OMe)2···O2···O2···TCNQ­(OMe)2] moiety revealed that AFM interactions occurred between TCNQ­(OMe)2 and O2 and between adjacent O2 molecules, with the AFM ground state being the most stable. This spin arrangement between TCNQ­(OMe)2 and O2 differed from that of a previously reported compound, in which a TCNQ­(OMe)2···O2 interaction coexisted with AFM O2···O2 coupling, ultimately yielding an AFM ground state. These findings establish molecular oxygen as an efficient spin mediator in porous magnets and highlight mass-transport-controlled spin coupling as a versatile strategy for post-tuning magnetic order in framework materials that interact with chemically inert but spin-active gases.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of 1

All synthetic procedures were performed in an inert atmosphere using a commercial glovebox and standard Schlenk-line techniques. All reagent-grade chemicals were obtained from commercial suppliers. The solvents were distilled under an N2 atmosphere using common drying agents. Compound [{Ru2(m-FArCO2)4}2TCNQ­(OMe)2] (1) was synthesized following a previously reported method. A 2 mL solution (3.61 mg or 0.004 mmol) of [Ru2(m-FArCO2)4(THF)2] in dichloromethane (DCM) was carefully introduced into narrow glass tubes with an inner diameter of 8 mm to form the bottom layer. A 1 mL buffer layer containing a DCM/DCE mixed solvent (1:1) was added. Concurrently, 5.3 mg of TCNQ­(OMe)2 (0.02 mmol) was dissolved in 20 mL of DCE, divided into 2 mL portions, and gently layered above the initial solution in each tube. The tubes were left undisturbed for 1 week, resulting in the formation of dark brown block-shaped crystals of [{Ru2(m-FArCO2)4}2TCNQ­(OMe)2]·4DCE (1–4DCE). To prepare solvent-free (1) crystals, we heated 1–4DCE crystals under vacuum at 353 K for 12 h.

Gas Adsorption Measurements

Gas sorption isotherms, isobars, and time-resolved O2 adsorption measurements were performed using an automatic volumetric adsorption apparatus (BELSORP Max, MicrotracBEL) connected to a cryostat system. Approximately 25 mg of 1 was placed in a sample cell. The cell was evacuated at 353 K for 12 h to degas the sample before measurements. The adsorption capacity was determined by monitoring pressure changes and calculating the pressure decrease at equilibrium.

In Situ Infrared (IR) Spectral Measurements

In situ IR spectral measurements were conducted using a transmission configuration on a JASCO FT-IR 4200 spectrometer. The spectrometer incorporates diamond and CaF2 windows in a custom-built cryostat system based on an Oxford microstat, which was connected to a gas-handling and pressure-monitoring system. The neat samples were sandwiched between the two CaF2 plates.

In Situ PXRD Measurements

A ground sample of 1 was sealed in a silica glass capillary with an inner diameter of 0.5 mm. The PXRD pattern was obtained using an Ultima IV diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) in 0.02° steps. To obtain PXRD patterns under gas-adsorbed conditions, we connected the glass capillary to stainless-steel lines with valves to a gas-handling system (BELSORP max; BEL Inc.) that enabled gas dosing and evacuation. The temperature was controlled using an N2 gas stream.

In Situ Magnetic Measurements

Magnetic susceptibility measurements were conducted using a Quantum Design SQUID magnetometer (MPMS-XL) in the temperature range of 1.8–300 K and DC field ranging from −7 to 7 T. The diamagnetic contributions from the samples were corrected using Pascal constants. Samples of polycrystalline 1–4DCE were secured in a gelatin capsule with cotton and placed at the center of a plastic straw. The straw was affixed using fluorocarbon tape to the edge of a custom-made sample rod constructed from an SUS tube and a brass male thread. The sample was sealed inside a closed-ended brass tube screwed onto the open end of the sample rod to maintain an airtight environment. Silicon sealant (CAF4, Bluestar Silicones) was applied at the connection point between the thread and the brass tube. The SUS tube was connected to a gas-handling system equipped with a TMP and a manometer. CO2 adsorption equilibrium was established by maintaining the sample at approximately 100 kPa and 195 K for 12 h. For O2 adsorption experiments, the dynamic magnetic properties were monitored at 100 kPa O2 and temperatures of 120, 140, and 160 K for various exposure durations. The temperature was set to 120 K for FCM measurements, followed by magnetic hysteresis loop measurements from −7 to 7 T. Although cotton was used to stabilize the sample, a minor step was observed at zero magnetic field during magnetic hysteresis loop measurements, likely due to slight sample movement because fixation agents such as Nujol oil were avoided to enable gas adsorption at cryogenic temperatures.

Crystallography

A single crystal of 1 was mounted on a thin glass capillary using a minimum amount of epoxy adhesive and then attached to the inner wall of a soda glass capillary with an inner diameter of 1.0 mm. The capillary was connected to the gas-handling system using an O-ring seal connector fixed to a diffraction goniometer. The temperature was controlled using a stream of N2 gas. Crystal data for 1⊃CO 2 were collected at 195 K under a 90-kPa CO2 atmosphere, as well as for 1⊃O 2 at 140 K under a 100-kPa O2 atmosphere, using a CCD diffractometer (Rigaku Saturn724) with a multilayer mirror monochromated Mo–Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). The structures were solved by the direct method using SHELXT Version 2014/5 for 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 and expanded using Fourier techniques. Full-matrix least-squares refinements on F 2 were based on observed reflections and variable parameters and converged with unweighted and weighted agreement factors as follows: R 1 = Σ||F 0| – |F c||/Σ|F 0| (I > 2.00σ­(I)) and wR 2 = [Σ­(w­(F 0 2 – F c 2)2)/Σw­(F 0 2)2]1/2 (all data). Nonhydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically. The hydrogen atoms were refined using the riding model. These data have been deposited as CIFs at the Cambridge Data Centre as Supplementary Publication Nos. CCDC-2472692 and CCDC-2472693 for 1⊃CO 2 and 1⊃O 2 , respectively. Copies of the data can be obtained free of charge upon application to the CCDC, 12 Union Road, Cambridge CB21EZ, UK (fax: (+44 1223–336–033; e-mail: deposit@ccdc.cam.ac.uk). Structural diagrams were generated using the VESTA software. The void volumes were estimated using the PRATON software.

DFT Calculations

Quantum chemical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) and Gaussian-type basis functions were performed on isolated model clusters of 1⊃O 2 and 1⊃CO 2 . The all atomic coordinated were taken from the X-ray crystallographic analyses data without any geometry optimization. To consider the static correlation effect, the spin-unrestricted (U) method was introduced. Two hybrid DFT functionals were employed: the Becke three-parameter Lee–Yang–Parr (B3LYP) and the Becke two-parameter (50% Hartree–Fock exchange) Lee–Yang–Parr (BHandHLYP) functionals. All calculations were performed using the 6-31G* basis set. Dispersion interaction such as van der Waals (vdW) was not included because it was eliminated by the energy subtraction for the calculation of the J values. For calculations of small |J| values, we used an ultrafine grid and self-consistent-field (SCF) convergence with 10–7 atomic unit for electron density to ensure the reliability of the DFT calculations. All calculations were performed in the gas phase using Gaussian 09 Rev. D01.
