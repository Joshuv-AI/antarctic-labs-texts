Global, regional, and national trends in burden of lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and associated risk factors, 1990–2021: results from the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Jing Qian, Sheng Zhou, Rong Wang, Qian Wu, Jinliang Meng, Jun Zhang, Yunhui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer (LC) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are fatal respiratory diseases with shared pathophysiological mechanisms. However, their global burden patterns remain insufficiently characterized within a comparative framework. This study aimed to conduct a comprehensive comparative global burden analysis of LC and COPD.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study data(1990–2021), we analyzed LC and COPD burden (incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)), identified their associated risk factors, elucidated temporal trends, and projected future burden from 2022 to 2050.ResultsIn 2021, significant geographical variations in the burden of LC and COPD were observed, with LC predominantly burdening high-income European and Oceanian regions and COPD concentrated in low-to-middle-income Asian and Oceanian countries. Ten shared risk factors were identified for both diseases, among which tobacco use, smoking, ambient and household air pollution, occupational carcinogen exposure, and particulate matter pollution were the most common and prominent. Demographically, both diseases were concentrated in the elderly, peaking at slightly older ages, with LC showing more pronounced male predominance than COPD. From 1990–2021, females LC burden, rost most regions,whereas COPD burden declined globally across genders. Socioeconomically, socio-demographic index (SDI) was positively correlated with LC burden, but negatively correlated with COPD mortality and disability. High-SDI regions experienced a decline in LC and COPD declines, contrasting with the rising COPD prevalence in low-to-middle SDI regions. Projections (2022–2050) indicate declining LC incidence, mortality, and DALY rates (age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) from 26.43 to 22.18 per 100,000 by 2050), with stable prevalence. All COPD metrics are projected to decrease (age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) from 45.22 to 20.83 per 100,000 by 2050).ConclusionThis study presented a comprehensive comparative analysis of the global burden of LC and COPD, offering descriptive and comparative epidemiological evidence to inform tailored, disease-specific and region-specific prevention and control efforts.

Introduction

As a malignant neoplasm, lung cancer(LC) exhibits elevated age-standardized rates for both incidence and mortality, maintaining its position as the leading cause of all malignancies internationally and within China’s borders1,2. Global statistics for 2022 documented nearly 2.48 million newly diagnosed cases with mortality reaching approximately 1.82 million individuals, demonstrating the substantial public health challenge this disease presents3. The prognosis remains grim, especially for patients diagnosed at advanced stages, with a five-year survival rate of only 10%4.

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) ranks among chronic conditions demonstrating remarkably high age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPR) on a worldwide scale. Each year, approximately 300 million people worldwide suffer from COPD, resulting in nearly 3.3 million deaths annually and ranking it among the top three leading causes of death worldwide5. Beyond its respiratory symptoms, COPD significantly increases the risk of multiple comorbidities, such as lung cancer, cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, etc6,7, thereby compounding the health and economic burden on patients and societies.

LC and COPD are closely related across several dimensions. Firstly, patient-level comorbidity is well documented, with reported comorbidity rates ranging from 30% to 70% in lung cancer populations7,8 and 5% to 9.1% in COPD patients9,depending on diagnostic criteria and study settings. These comorbidities also influence treatment selection (particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors) and prognosis, where patients may experience both potential benefits and heightened risks of adverse events10. Secondly, they are often driven by shared biological or environmental risk factors such as smoking, air pollution, and occupational hazards11–13, as well as underlying pathological processes including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune microenvironment dysregulation14,15. Recent research also highlights that non-smoking factors, including household air pollution, have emerged as critical contributors, particularly in low- and middle-income countries16. The high comorbidity rate presents a formidable public health challenge, particularly in aging populations, urgently demanding for more effective prevention and control strategies10,17.

However, these individual-level clinical observations and shared risk profiles are distinct from parallel population-level burden patterns. Although LC and COPD share several established population-level risk determinants, such as smoking and air pollution, the inherent heterogeneity of both diseases across diverse geographies means that their overall epidemiological distributions cannot be simply extrapolated from shared risks or clinical coexistence alone. The large-scale, aggregated data are therefore needed to characterize the epidemiological distributions of the two diseases across regions and populations. The Global Burden of Disease(GBD) investigation offers a comprehensive framework for assessing disease patterns, injury metrics, and risk determinants spanning 204 nations and territories18. While current research largely focuses on the burden and intervention strategies of lung cancer or COPD in isolation, the systematic comparative assessment of their respective epidemiological characteristics at the population level remain limited.

Consequently, the present investigation leverages GBD datasets covering the period from 1990 through 2021 to perform a comparative analysis of the global disease burdens imposed by LC and COPD. The analytical approach initially focuses on characterizing parallel spatiotemporal patterns regarding incidence metrics, mortality statistics, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) at global, regional, and country-specific scales. These analyses are further stratified by age, sex, and socio-demographic index (SDI) groups for each disease to reveal dynamic changes and disparities. In addition, the contribution of common risk factors to the burden of each condition is quantified separately, and a projection framework is constructed to anticipate future epidemiological shifts. These multifaceted analytical outputs establish a comprehensive knowledge foundation that may guide the formulation of targeted prevention approaches, strategic resource deployment, and improve patient care practices for these two major respiratory conditions on a global scale.

Materials and methods

Data sources

In the GBD database, COPD was defined as a group of chronic respiratory diseases characterized by persistent airflow limitation, identified based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (ICD-10: J41–J44.9)19, including chronic bronchitis and emphysema. LC was defined as malignant neoplasms of the trachea, bronchus, and lung identified by ICD codes (ICD-10: C33–C34)20. This study collected data on the burden of LC and COPD from 204 nations and regions globally, spanning the years 1990 to 2021, sourced from the GBD database (http://www.healthdata.org). Epidemiological indicators collected for analysis encompassed incidence measures, prevalence metrics, mortality statistics, and disability-adjusted life years. These data were stratified by age, years (1990, 2021, and the period 1990–2021), gender (male, female, and both genders), and country. Meanwhile, countries and regions were grouped based on the SDI. The GBD database was accessed on August 22, 2025.

Estimation of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs

Incidence and prevalence quantification was performed through the DisMod-MR 2.1 (Disease Model - Bayesian Meta-Regression) analytical framework21. Mortality estimates were generated via the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) methodology22. The DALY rate was calculated by dividing DALYs by the corresponding population. The DALY rate was expressed as an estimate per 100,000 population. A higher DALY rate was indicated to reflect a more severe disease burden in the region or population.

SDI

The SDI values about LC and COPD are accessible on the Global Health Data Exchange platform website (GHDx; https://ghdx.healthdata.org/ihme_data)23. Investigating the association between sociodemographic index values and both LC and COPD becomes essential for recognizing disease burden inequalities spanning different socioeconomic hierarchies. Countries and territories were stratified into five distinct SDI quintiles—specifically low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high classifications—enabling exploration of how disease burden patterns for LC and COPD correlate with varying levels of socioeconomic advancement.

Age-standardised rate (ASR)

Given that the included data involved different age groups and varied over time, ASRs were employed in this study for assessment. The calculation of ASRs was based on a population of 100,000, and heterogeneity was estimated via 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Estimating temporal shifts in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates for LC and COPD across the 1990–2021 timeframe represents a central objective, accomplished through EAPC calculation. Additionally, the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for EAPC were also calculated using the linear regression model. An upward trajectory in ASR was determined when both the EAPC estimate and the lower confidence interval boundary at the 95% level demonstrated positive values. Conversely, declining trajectories in ASR were identified when both the EAPC estimate and the upper confidence interval boundary at 95% demonstrated negative values. Situations where neither criterion was fulfilled indicated stable age-standardized rates26.

Risk factor estimation

In the GBD 2021, risk-outcome pairs that had been assessed as meeting the “convincing evidence” or “probable evidence” grades of the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF) were included to collate relative risk data, and the relative risk values for these risk-outcome pairs were then determined.

Age-standardized metrics encompassing incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates for LC and COPD—linked to an extensive array of risk determinants—were extracted from the GHDx (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/ihme_data). The attributable risk factors encompassed major categories such as air pollution (ambient particulate matter, household air pollution), behavioral risks (smoking, diet), occupational exposures (to carcinogens like asbestos, silica, and diesel exhaust), and metabolic factors (e.g., high fasting plasma glucose).

Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model

For projecting future trajectories of age-standardized incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates spanning 2022 through 2050 for LC and COPD, an autoregressive integrated moving average modeling framework was constructed. Model calibration utilized GBD datasets covering the 1990–2021 temporal window. All model estimations and visualizations were performed using the forecast (v8.23.0) and tseries (v0.10–55) packages in R27.

Statistical analysis

R software (v 4.3.1) facilitated data preprocessing, computational analyses, and visual representation generation throughout the investigation. Spearman’s rank correlation methodology was employed to examine the relationship linking SDI with ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) metrics for LC and COPD conditions, utilizing the stats computational package (v4.3.1). Statistical significance criteria were defined as absolute correlation coefficient values exceeding 0.3 coupled with p-values below 0.05.

For the ASPR, the top increase were Egypt (EAPC: 3.68, 95% CI: 3.19 to 4.17), France (EAPC: 3.29, 95% CI: 2.93 to 3.65), and the Republic of Korea (EAPC: 2.96, 95% CI: 2.41 to 3.50), whereas the most substantial reduction occurred in Kazakhstan (EAPC: −3.23, 95% CI: −3.34 to −3.12), Uzbekistan (EAPC: −2.85, 95% CI: −3.18 to −2.51), and Kyrgyzstan (EAPC: −2.59, 95% CI: −2.90 to −2.28). For COPD, the most rapid increase of ASIR were in Morocco (EAPC: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.07 to 1.12), Lebanon (EAPC: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.12), and Antigua and Barbuda (EAPC: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.93 to 1.02), whereas the largest decrease in ASIR was observed in Singapore (EAPC: −1.65, 95% CI: −1.75 to −1.54), Ukraine (EAPC: −1.42, 95% CI: −1.49 to −1.34), and Turkmenistan (EAPC: −1.15, 95% CI: −1.30 to −1.00). A different set of countries led the changes in the ASPR, which rose most in Saudi Arabia, Lebanon, and Morocco, and fell most in Singapore, Brunei Darussalam, and Ukraine. In contrast, mortality and disability metrics showed more pronounced changes. The ASMR rose most in Georgia (EAPC: 2.84, 95% CI: 2.13 to 3.56), Norway, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines. However, the ASMR fell dramatically in Belarus (EAPC: −7.33, 95% CI: −7.99 to −6.66), Ukraine, and Singapore. A parallel trend was noted for the ASDR, which increased largely in the same three countries- Georgia, Norway (EAPC), and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines. The largest decrease in ASDR was identified in Belarus, Ukraine, and Singapore (Fig. 3E-H) (Tables S4).

Of particular interest, despite observable disparities in regional distribution patterns concerning global burden modifications between LC and COPD, Ukraine exhibited comparatively substantial reductions in the burden of both conditions. Singapore was also identified among the countries with the most pronounced decreases in COPD incidence-related metrics and DALY rates. These patterns may be interpreted in the context of the progressive implementation of tobacco control policies during the study period. In Singapore, tobacco control measures began as early as 1970, when smoking in public places was first regulated and a tobacco advertising ban was implemented; subsequent measures included tobacco taxation, comprehensive advertising and promotion bans, smoke-free legislation covering most indoor public places, public education campaigns, plain packaging, and raising the minimum legal age for tobacco purchase, use, and possession to 21 years. Over this period, daily smoking prevalence in Singapore declined from 25.0% in 1974 to 10.6% in 201928. Singapore was also among the first countries to ratify the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC).In Ukraine, the WHO FCTC was ratified in 2006, followed by progressive tobacco control implementation over the subsequent decade; the second round of the Global Adult Tobacco Survey in 2017 reported a 20% relative decline in current tobacco smoking, while Global Youth Tobacco Survey data from 2005 to 2017 showed a reduction of more than 40% in youth smoking rates, reflecting the period following comprehensive legislative changes since 201229. Therefore, the observed reductions in LC and COPD burden in these countries align temporally with the implementation and strengthening of tobacco regulation frameworks. In addition, public health policies for tobacco control generally exert obvious lagged effects, and the reduction of disease burden can only be achieved after long-term policy enforcement. Further quantitative analysis will be conducted to explore the correlations between policy intervention timing and long-term respiratory disease trends in subsequent studies.

Trends in the Burden of LC and COPD in different SDI regions

Analyses spanning global territories alongside five SDI-stratified regions throughout the 1990–2021 temporal interval reveal substantial heterogeneity in burden trajectory modifications for LC and COPD across territories characterized by divergent developmental stages.

From 1990 to 2021, the burden of LC in high SDI regions exhibited considerable fluctuations: ASDR and ASMR experienced large reductions, while ASIR demonstrated an intense and rapid decline since 2010. Conversely, ASPR increased markedly from 2000 to 2010, followed by a significant decrease from 2010 to 2020. Discussion

LC and COPD are the two most lethal respiratory diseases worldwide. They not only pose a serious threat to human health but also impose a heavy social and economic burden. Although these conditions share common risk determinants and certain pathophysiological pathways, their epidemiological distributions exhibit substantial heterogeneity across different regions and populations. Rather than focusing on a single condition, this study leverages the GBD database to provide a systematic, comparative assessment of the LC and COPD burden. Here, we discuss the distinct spatiotemporal patterns, demographic disparities, risk factor profiles, and future burden trajectories of these two major respiratory diseases, aiming to distill actionable public health insights.

Geographical differences in the burden of LC and COPD

Our analysis reveals a stark geographical divergence in the burden of LC and COPD. In 2021, the regions with the highest ASIR, ASPR, and ASMR of LC were concentrated in Europe (Monaco, Hungary, France, etc.) and Oceania (Greenland, Palau). This pattern likely reflects the legacy of historically high smoking prevalence, with its carcinogenic effects manifesting after a considerable latency period13,15. Specific local exposures, such as indoor coal burning in Greenland (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, PAHs) and occupational dust in island nations like Palau, may act as synergistic risk factors14,33. Furthermore, high screening coverage (e.g., low-dose computed tomography (LDCT)) in many European nations could contribute to higher reported incidence rates, underscoring the impact of healthcare system capacity34.

In contrast, the high burden of COPD is mainly concentrated in low- and middle-income countries in Asia, such as Nepal, India, and Papua New Guinea. This is primarily driven by widespread exposure to household and ambient air pollution from solid fuel use35,36, which remains an important contributor to COPD burden in low- and middle-income settings. Compounding this issue, insufficient medical resources and delayed diagnosis in these regions likely amplify the disease burden37,38. This geographical disparity underscores the need for tailored prevention strategies: high-income countries should prioritize tobacco control and early LC detection, whereas low- and middle-income countries require urgent interventions focused on environmental governance and the strengthening of primary healthcare for COPD.

Age and gender differences in burden: both LC and COPD show significant age dependence

The disease burden of both LC and COPD is mainly concentrated in the elderly population. For instance, the new incidence and mortality peaks of LC occur at the age of 70–74, respectively, whereas the prevalence and DALY peaks advance to the age of 65–69, reflecting the temporal mismatch between functional impairment and mortality risk39. The new onset of COPD and the peak of DALYs are concentrated at the age of 70–74, while the peak of death is delayed to 80–84, reflecting its chronic progression characteristics40. This difference highlights the distinct nature of the two diseases: LC typically progresses rapidly with a poor prognosis. Patients experience a shorter survival time after disease onset, leading to a close proximity between the onset, death, and peak age of DALYs. In contrast, COPD is a chronic and progressive disease. Patients often survive for a prolonged period but eventually die at an older age due to complications such as respiratory failure or acute exacerbation. As a result, the peak age of death occurs much later than the peak age of onset.

Gender disparities were also prominent. For both diseases, the LC burden remains significantly higher in males than in females, which is mainly due to historical differences in smoking and occupational exposure3,41,42. However, the sustained increase in female mortality is concerning, potentially reflecting both rising smoking prevalence among women and a greater biological susceptibility to carcinogenic exposure43,44. For COPD, while men had higher DALYs and mortality, women’s health is disproportionately affected by indoor air pollution36,45. The potential modulatory role of estrogen on female lung function may partly explain sex-specific differences in disease progression46, warranting further mechanism investigation.

Global burden change trends (1990–2021)

Throughout the 1990–2021 interval, these diseases demonstrated pronounced geographical redistribution regarding global burden. The most accelerated LC burden escalations materialized within developing territories across African regions (exemplified by Egypt) and Asian regions. The EAPC of ASIR, ASPR, and ASMR for LC in Egypt were 3.99, 3.68, and 4.09, respectively, reflecting the superimposed effect of local high smoking rate and air pollution35,47. Limited access to early detection and timely treatment in Uganda/ selected African settings may contribute to late-stage diagnosis and high mortality. The regions with the fastest-growing burden of COPD include the Middle East (Morocco, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia) and high-income countries in Europe (Norway). The former is driven by both indoor biomass fuel and smoking30,32, while the latter is partly associated with population aging and the coexistence of multiple diseases with COPD23,48. In a positive contrast, the substantial decrease in both diseases’ burden in Ukraine underscores the profound impact of robust and sustained tobacco control policies29.

The relationship between LC and SDI reflects the health costs under the past development model. The relationship between COPD and SDI is more like a story of “management and future challenges”: High SDI successfully addresses the “consequences” (death and disability) of COPD, but still deals with its “causes” (new cases and illness). This disparity clearly indicates that economic development alone cannot resolve all public health challenges, particularly those involving chronic disease prevention and long-term management. For LC, regions with medium and low SDI must learn from the lessons of regions with high SDI, avoid repeating the mistake of “polluting first and treating later”, and vigorously implement primary prevention measures such as tobacco control. For COPD, in areas with high SDI, while being satisfied with management effectiveness, it is still necessary to attach importance to prevention. Low SDI regions, however, are confronted with dual challenges: they must control risk factors from the source and strive to build a medical system to reduce mortality and disability. The task is arduous and the road is long.

Moreover, despite the overall positive global trend, the disease burden is increasingly shifting toward low- and middle-income regions, presenting a paradoxical pattern of “decline accompanied by growth”. Both LC and COPD are now concentrated in low- and medium-low SDI regions, which have become hotspots for the rising burden, highlighting the severe challenge of global health inequality.

ARIMA model prediction (2022–2050)

Assuming current control measures remain effective, our forecasting model suggests a potential overall decline in the global burden of LC and COPD by 2050, indicating that ongoing interventions may be producing tangible effects. The global ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of LC are expected to decline, while the ASPR remains basically stable. This is closely related to the popularization of tobacco control policies, early screening (LDCT), and precision treatment (targeted and immunotherapy)49,50. A stable or rising prevalence rate may reflect an extended survival period of patients51. However, the slower decline in the global female LC burden is a critical warning, emphasizing the need to address non-smoking risk factors like household air pollution52,53. For COPD, global age-standardized prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates have all exhibited downward trends, with significantly greater reductions observed in ASMR and ASDR. Combined with clinical evidence, these findings indicate that specific pharmacotherapies and standardized management strategies for COPD have achieved substantial beneficial effects54,55. However, delayed mitigation of upstream determinants such as smoking prevalence and ambient air pollution may constrain further reductions in incidence rates56.The decline in the COPD incidence rate in men will be greater than that in women, suggesting that female exposure (such as indoor fuel smoke) remains a gap in prevention and control57. The more significant drop in mortality compared to incidence suggests that while management of existing disease is improving, primary prevention, such as tackling smoking and air pollution, lags behind.

Risk factors for LC and COPD

This study identified 10 shared risk factors for LC and COPD, offering a strong epidemiological foundation for understanding their frequent clinical coexistence. The results confirmed 10 risk factors shared by both diseases. This result is highly consistent with the pathophysiological association between LC and COPD that was based on the core pathological basis of chronic respiratory injury and abnormal repair58. This common risk factor explains the phenomenon that the two types of diseases often coexist in clinical practice59. The differentiation of risk factors for LC and COPD further highlights the differences in prevention and control strategies: The core risk factors for LC are concentrated on behavioral factors (primarily tobacco and smoking), which are closely related to the direct carcinogenic effects of carcinogens such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines in tobacco60. COPD is mainly driven by environmental/occupational risk factors, air pollution, and particulate matter pollution, reflecting chronic inflammatory damage to the airways and lung parenchyma caused by long-term inhalation of pollutants61. Collectively, this distinction suggests that the prevention and management of LC needs to concentrate on behavioral intervention (smoking cessation) while the prevention and control of COPD requires stronger emphasis on mitigating environmental and occupational exposures.

Conclusions and limitations

The investigation draws upon GBD research repositories encompassing the 1990–2021 temporal span. This study presents a comparative global burden analysis of LC and COPD from 1990 to 2021, based on the GBD database. We systematically compared their epidemiological patterns, temporal trends, geographic disparities, and shared and disease-specific risk factors. A systematic comparative analysis was conducted on ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR and related risk factors (including shared risk factors and disease-specific risk factors). Furthermore, this study integrated an ARIMA model to seamlessly connect the analysis of current disease burden characteristics with the projection of future trends (2022–2050), thereby bridging a critical gap in the existing literature, which frequently focuses on the current burden of a single disease or fails to integrate situational analysis with forecasting.

Despite providing a comprehensive comparative overview between LC and COPD burden globally, several critical limitations that should also be acknowledged. First and foremost, this is an ecological, population-level analysis based on aggregated national and regional estimates. As such, it cannot infer patient-level comorbidity between LC and COPD, nor can it establish causal links or mechanistic relationships between the two diseases. Interpretations are restricted to population-level epidemiological associations and parallel burden patterns, without implying individual-level causality or clinical interactions. Second, the findings are limited by the inherent assumptions, data sources, and modeling structure of the GBD 2021 study, including varying data availability, underreporting, diagnostic heterogeneity, and estimation uncertainty in regions with sparse surveillance data or weak health information systems. Third, the study did not fully incorporate potential confounding factors such as genetic background, individual clinical phenotypes, comorbidity profiles, or access to specialized treatment, which may modify disease expression and outcomes at the patient level. Fourth, the ARIMA projections rely on historical temporal trends and assume the continuation of current policies, risk factor exposures, and healthcare delivery; unforeseen changes in public health interventions, medical advances, or environmental exposures may introduce uncertainty into future forecasts. These limitations warrant caution in interpretation and highlight the need for subsequent individual-level clinical and mechanistic studies to validate and extend the present findings. Despite the above limitations, this study also identified epidemiological linkages between LC and COPD. The two diseases shared 10 key risk factors, and showed contrasting correlations with socioeconomic development, indicating opportunities for integrated prevention strategies targeting common risk determinants.
