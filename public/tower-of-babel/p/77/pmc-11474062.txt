Complexity of confined water vitrification and its glass transition temperature
Authors: Jorge H. Melillo, Daniele Cangialosi, Valerio Di Lisio, Elisa Steinrücken, Michael Vogel, Silvina Cerveny
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe current understanding of the glass transition temperature (Tg) in confined water is highly debated, hindering our comprehension of various biological and terrestrial phenomena. This study resolves this controversy by identifying three distinct contributions to the calorimetric response of supercooled confined water. We demonstrate that confined water has a Tg in the 170 to 200 K range, depending on the confinement size and wall interactions. The fast and standard calorimetry and dielectric spectroscopy results linking the glass transition with the dynamics support this observation. Furthermore, our findings reveal thermal events at temperatures higher and lower than the Tg, which are connected to the adsorption/desorption of water at the inner surfaces and an “annealing prepeak” or, equivalently, “shadow glass transition,” respectively.

The glass transition refers to the transformation of a liquid, previously supercooled below the melting temperature, into a glass. The underlying nature of this process is a central puzzle in condensed matter physics because understanding the mechanisms underpinning vitrification and the time-dependent evolution of the resulting glass is essential for gaining insights into fundamental properties and predicting the long-term behavior of glassy materials (1). In particular, the glass transition of bulk water presents a distinctive and significant phenomenon in materials science. While bulk water typically solidifies into ice upon reaching its freezing point, certain conditions allow for the bypass of crystallization and, instead, lead to the transformation into a glass at lower temperatures. The study of this particular event holds considerable importance in fundamental physics and practical applications spanning diverse fields, such as cryopreservation and atmospheric science. It also contributes to our understanding of the second critical point hypothesis (2, 3), which suggests that the two amorphous states, known as high-density amorphous (HDA) and low-density amorphous (LDA) ice, transform into two distinct liquid states, high-density liquid (HDL) and low-density liquid (LDL) when heated (4). Two different liquid states are consistent with experiments using ultrafast heating/cooling and detection schemes (5, 6) and computer simulations (3, 7). However, several studies have been published against this scenario (see refs. 8–10). Therefore, investigating the intricacies of water’s glass transition provides valuable insights into the behavior of one of the most essential substances for life on Earth.

Glassy bulk water can be prepared, e.g., by hyperquenching at rates higher than 105 K/s, which prevents crystallization. However, hyperquenched water tends to crystallize upon heating at ~150 K. A calorimetric experiment on hyperquenched bulk water (11) identified its glass transition temperature (Tg) at approximately 136 K at a heating rate of 30 K/min on the basis of the observation of a specific heat increase, interpreted as the onset of the glass transition, before crystallization takes over. In addition, Tg values of ~126 K and ~116 K were determined for LDA and HDA (12), respectively, where analogous to hyperquenched water, a tiny increase in the heat capacity (ΔcP ∼1 J/Kmol) was observed before crystallization. The observation of different Tg values confirms the existence of two distinct liquids. The assignment of these weak calorimetric signatures to the glass transitions of LDA and HDA received support from observing an onset of diffusive motion above these temperatures (13). Moreover, the aforementioned ultrafast cooling/heating and detection schemes provided evidence for a phase transition between LDL and HDL (5, 6). In addition, other variants of amorphous ices can be prepared in the laboratory (14). Andersson (15) studied the calorimetric response of very HDA ice and reported a glass transition at 140 K. Moreover, LiCl solutions prepared as LiCl-HDA or LiCl-LDA, presented Tg values of 116 K and 140 K, respectively (16). However, when the calorimetric response of LDA under isotopic substitution (16O → 18O and H2O → D2O) was studied, Shephard and Salzmann reported that the heat capacity at approximately 136 K is not affected by the substitution of 16O for 18O, whereas the signature shifts when H2O is replaced with D2O. They concluded that the feature in the calorimetric response in this temperature range does not involve translational motions of the oxygen atoms but is due to an unfreezing of molecular rotational degrees of freedom. Therefore, these results cast doubts on the conjecture that the observed thermal event is due to a true glass transition, as the latter should result in the freezing/unfreezing of both rotational and translational degrees of freedom. Other reports also indicated that the feature observed at ~136 K does not represent the proper Tg of water, which, instead, would be located in a hitherto ill-defined range between 165 and 228 K (8, 17–20). Yue and Angell (21) and Giovambattista et al. (22) explained the weak effect at 136 K as an “annealing prepeak,” whereas the “real” glass transition of bulk water is located at higher temperatures but is experimentally inaccessible because of crystallization. Thus, the value of the glass transition of bulk water and the molecular motions at the Tg have been discussed for decades but have remained elusive until now.

Dynamic measurements probing the time scale of spontaneous thermal fluctuations can also shed light on the problem of the glass transition of water. The main α-relaxation generally reflects the spontaneous fluctuations associated with the glass transition, and an estimate of Tg is generally obtained by extrapolating its relaxation time to approximately 100 s. When LDA and HDA are heated, a broadband dielectric spectroscopy (BDS) study unveiled a relaxation process whose correlation times are consistent with the features found in the calorimetric response (12), providing Tg = 126 K and Tg ≈ 116 K, respectively. Therefore, this relaxation was identified with α-relaxation. However, its characteristics are similar to those shown by the local relaxations of other glasses, i.e., the Arrhenius temperature dependence and symmetric spectral shape; and, in addition, very similar to those observed in confined water (9), for which this process was interpreted as local relaxation by many authors (23–27). Thus, it is still debated whether the BDS process observed for LDA and HDA actually corresponds to structural α-relaxation or local β-relaxation, which mainly involves rotational degrees of freedom.

All these controversial results originate from the propensity of bulk water to rapidly crystallize (on cooling at 235 K or heating at 150 K). Hence, it was suggested that they could be resolved by avoiding crystallization under confinement. Moreover, since the relaxation of water under confinement coincides with that of LDA (8), the results of water under confinement are vital for understanding bulk behavior. When the confinement size is small enough (generally ≤2 nm), it is possible to maintain water molecules in an amorphous state at all temperatures. Alternatively, when partial crystallization occurs in slightly larger confinements, this typically results in small crystals with negligible effects on the calorimetric response of the amorphous fraction, as we will demonstrate here. However, similar to bulk water, we hitherto lack precise observations of the glass transition of water under confinement (18). The exception is a report by Oguni et al. on water confined in nanopores of silica glass (Mobil Composition of Matter - Number 41 (MCM-41)), considered a model for studying the water behavior) via adiabatic calorimetric measurements (28, 29). However, Johari et al. (30) later challenged these results (measured at a slow rate of 0.2 K/m) because they do not show an overshoot due to the enthalpy recovery before the equilibrium state is reached. As similar observations have not been reported for other confinements and there is no agreement on the interpretation of Oguni’s results, the experimental evidence for the glass transition of confined water is very scarce, and the debate continues. In addition, numerous studies have explored the dynamics of confined water, investigating scenarios such as mixing water with solutes (soft confinements) or incorporating water into small rigid cavities (hard confinements). In these studies, the relaxation time of confined water showed dynamic crossovers, during which the activation energy increased upon heating at about 175 to 200 K or at ~220 K if it was determined by BDS (24, 31, 32) or quasielastic neutron scattering (33, 34), respectively. Furthermore, two crossovers were determined at 175 and 225 K via NMR (35, 36). Different arguments have been used to explain these dynamical crossovers: a liquid–liquid transition (33, 34), finite-size effects (37), partial crystallization, or α–β splitting (35). In agreement with the NMR results for confined water (33, 34), the diffusion coefficients obtained from the crystallization kinetics of bulk water showed two dynamical crossovers at ~180 K and ~233 K (38). In contrast, the diffusion coefficients associated with the isotope intermixing of bulk water did not yield evidence for a change in the temperature dependence and supported a glass transition near 136 K (39).

In this work, we measured the calorimetric response, both in linear and nonlinear regimes, of water confined in MCM-41 or molecular sieves (MS) with different pore sizes. To this end, we employed standard differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), including measurements in the modulated mode, and fast scanning calorimetry (FSC) covering a wide range of time scales and complemented these measurements with BDS. Through DSC annealing experiments in the glassy state, conveying insights into the nonlinear equilibration kinetics, we provide compelling evidence that the glass transition of confined water is located at Tg = 170 to 200 K, depending on the type of confinement. The identification of the glass transition of confined water is corroborated by calorimetry and BDS in the linear regime showing a relaxation attaining values of ~100 s at approximately the Tg obtained by annealing experiments. In addition, we show the presence of an extra low-temperature endotherm in the temperature range of 120 to 130 K, identified with a sub-Tg thermal event, also referred to as a shadow glass transition, which was previously reported for a variety of glasses. The latter is located near the temperature where the β-relaxation, observed in all water-containing systems, has a relaxation time of ~100 s, as detected by BDS. Another step in the heat capacity is observed at temperatures above Tg. It is insensitive to annealing and, therefore, attributed to a true thermodynamic transition. Possible implications of these findings for the fundamental properties of bulk water are discussed.

Upon heating, the calorimetric response was measured in modulated mode at 1 K/min (see details in SI Appendix, section 2). The corresponding reversing heat capacity (cP, rev) is shown in Fig. 1 C and D. For MCM-41 and MS, a very broad step in heat capacity is detected, which is independent of the degree of crystallization, and extends over several tens of degrees, as indicated in the figure. This extended temperature transition observed in the cP step markedly differs from the typical glass transition behavior observed in ordinary glasses. In the Inset of Fig. 1A, we see that the step observed for confined water is much wider than that of the glass transition of a typical fragile glass former, PG, in bulk and confined in MS-II, which extends over about 10 and 14 K in the bulk and under confinement, respectively. Therefore, at first glance, the findings suggest a complex vitrification behavior of confined water, possibly hinting at the presence of two or more convoluted steps.

Annealing Experiments.

Our attention now turns to the physical origin of the observed anomalously broad step in the heat capacity presented in Fig. 1. The samples were subjected to thermal procedures based on isothermal annealing over a wide temperature range, which is a valuable approach for gaining insights into the characteristics of different thermal events (40, 41). Specifically, subjecting a nonequilibrium glass to annealing below its Tg induces a reduction in the glass enthalpy; see scheme in Fig. 2A. The subsequent calorimetric heating scan exhibits a typical endothermic overshoot in proximity to the step in the specific heat associated with the glass transition, as shown in Fig. 2B. Thus, annealing allows us to clarify the presence of a weak kinetic thermal event that is not easily discernible in heating scans of unannealed samples, which do not show any endo excess.

Schematic representation of the enthalpy (A) and the heat capacity (B) versus temperature for a glass-forming system upon physical aging. The blue dashed line represents the thermodynamic equilibrium. The green line is the aged sample-heating scan. Taging is the aging temperature.

Annealing experiments were conducted at various temperatures and times via standard DSC techniques (refer to the procedure outlined in SI Appendix, section 3 and Fig. S1). The cooling process involved rapid quenching (~30 K/min) of the samples to low temperatures, while heating was always carried out at a rate of 20 K/min. After cooling, the samples were heated to a specific annealing temperature (Ta) and held there for a particular annealing time (ta). After annealing, the samples were cooled to 100 K and reheated to 293 K for data recording. The absence of annealing-induced permanent changes in the properties of the samples was confirmed by measurements both before and after all aging cycles.

A comparison of the results for the unaged and aged MCM-41 samples in Fig. 3 A and B, clearly reveals that the broad step in the heat capacity observed in Fig. 1 actually results from two well-distinguished thermal events, which can be discriminated by judiciously selecting the annealing temperature. In the following, we refer to these events as Step II and Step III. To demonstrate that the existence of the two thermal events is independent of water crystallization, samples with different ice contents are shown in Fig. 3 (for the MCM-41 samples, the melting of small fractions of crystalline confined or external water is observed at higher temperatures, whereas for MS-I and MS-II no melting is observed).

Steps II and III are seen, whereas Step I is not visible on this scale. Units are expressed in J/Kg, where g represents grams of water. When the samples are annealed just below the cP step onset (e.g., 168 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm; see the blue dot in Fig. 3A), an increasingly prominent endothermic overshoot is observed at the upper end of the Step II as the annealing time (ta) increases. This overshoot indicates that the enthalpy recovery process results from the well-established nonequilibrium nature of glasses, exhibiting excess free energy with respect to the equilibrium state. Therefore, when a glassy material is held isothermally below its Tg, its thermodynamic properties (e.g., volume, enthalpy, etc.) evolve toward equilibrium through a spontaneous process called glassy-state structural relaxation or physical aging (42, 43), as schematized in Fig. 2B. Similar features are also present for water confined in MSs of different sizes see Fig. 3 C and D. Particularly strong and systematic annealing effects are observed for MS-I. Importantly, aging just below Step II onset results in a relatively narrow endothermic overshoot at the upper end of this step, encompassing a range smaller than 15 °C, which is typical of the glass transition.

In addition to Step II exhibiting aging effects, we observe another increase in cP at higher temperatures between 200 and 250 K (Step III). To gain insights into the nature of the latter feature, we performed annealing at temperatures between Step II and Step III, specifically at Ta = 193 K and 190 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm and MCM-41 2.4 nm, respectively (see red points in Fig. 3). In both cases, annealing fails to result in any aging effects, indicating that Step III cannot be attributed to a kinetic event such as a second glass transition of confined water. Instead, this lack of annealing effects implies that this additional cP increase exhibits the hallmark characteristics of a true thermodynamic transition.

The fact that Step II exhibits aging effects is a signature that this increase in cP represents a glass transition of confined water. Moreover, the onset point (Tg,onset), where cP starts to increase at a heating rate of 1 K/min (165.7 K; see Fig. 1C), is lower than that observed at a faster heating rate of 20 K/min (167.8 K; see Fig. 3A). This behavior is consistent with the concept that lowering the heating rate reduces the glass transition temperature (see SI Appendix, section 5 for a more detailed study on the heating-rate dependence of the Tg). We also analyze the change in the heat capacity (ΔcP) at the Tg, although accurately determining the water contribution to ΔcP is difficult. While the total water content is well determined, it is unclear whether all confined water vitrifies alike; hence, differentiating between interfacial and internal water molecules may be necessary, which is, however, challenging. Due to this uncertainty, the measured ΔcP values should be treated as lower bounds of the actual values. The ΔcP values range between 0.89 and 1.16 J/gK (or 16 to 20.9 J/Kmol) for the different samples as shown in Fig. 3. Thus, they are of the same order as those of glycerol (0.97 J/gK = 90 J/Kmol), a moderately fragile liquid (44), ruling out the superstrong nature of confined water, but in contrast to hyperquenched glassy bulk water, which has a much lower ΔcP = 0.055 J/gK (45).

We finally explored the effects of annealing at low temperatures of Ta = 115 and 120 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm and MCM-41 2.4 nm, respectively. Fig. 4 shows that these annealing temperatures are near the onset of a weak low-temperature increase in cp (Step I) but well below the glass transition temperature (Step II). After annealing, the calorimetric response in the low-temperature range of Step I displays a weak endothermic excess relative to that of the unannealed sample, exhibiting features analogous to those previously observed by others and associated with the glass transition of bulk water (12, 45). However, it is worth noting that the magnitude of this excess due to aging is relatively tiny compared with that observed for Step II in Fig. 3. Furthermore, the temperature range of the endothermic thermal event resulting from low-temperature annealing is approximately 50 K, which is much wider than that conventionally observed for a glass transition. Consequently, this low-temperature endotherm (Step I) differs in important aspects from the conventional glass transition phenomenon observed in Fig. 3 (Step II).

Molecular Relaxation Determined by Standard and Fast Calorimetric Techniques, and Dielectric Spectroscopy.

We now focus on the dynamics of confined water via the BDS. This technique applied to glass-forming materials provides information about α-relaxation, which is typically associated with the Tg, and possible β-relaxation. However, samples containing water often have strong conductivity or other nonrelaxation contributions to the BDS spectrum, which may mask the relaxation processes in the temperature range where the α-process is expected. To overcome these problems, we complement our BDS studies with calorimetric techniques, including next-generation FSC. Using step response protocols, we obtain information on spontaneous thermal fluctuations via the thermal complex susceptibility, i.e., the complex specific heat capacity. In this way, we obtain information on the time scale of the α-relaxation at low temperatures, where the BDS fails due to overwhelming conductivity contributions. In this section, we will present the BDS and FSC results obtained from two samples: MS-I, with a water content of 23 wt%, which crystallized upon cooling, and MCM-41 2.1 nm with a water content of 22 wt%, where ice is absent.

Fig. 5 A and B shows the imaginary part (ε´´) of the complex permittivity for water in the indicated confinements. For MCM-41 2.1 nm, where water remains fully amorphous at all temperatures, we observe a peak shifting to higher frequencies with increasing temperature (Fig. 5 A, Upper). No further relaxation is observed in this representation. We determined the relaxation time from this peak: τ = 1/ (2πfm), where fm is the frequency corresponding to the maximum of the loss peak. The results are displayed as filled symbols in the Arrhenius diagram of Fig. 5E. They show a typical crossover at T = (170 ± 3) K. These results are fully consistent with previous BDS (46) and 2H NMR (47) findings for water in MCM-41 pores with various diameters. However, both methods have difficulty determining whether a slower relaxation exists, which is potentially associated with α-relaxation of confined water. In particular, the strong conductivity produced by water interferes in BDS. One BDS attempt in this direction (36) reported a weak relaxation peak in the temperature range of 170−190 K. However, it was not possible to conclude whether or not this weak relaxation is related to the α-relaxation of water confined in MCM-41.

Here, by analyzing the derivative of the real part (ε´) of the complex permittivity (ε* = ε´(ω) + ε´´(ω), which is proportional to ε″ but not affected by conductivity, we detect an additional slower relaxation process (Fig. 5 A, Lower). It is also observed in the dielectric modulus (M*), although a decade slower, as is typical in this representation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and section 6). While we cannot precisely determine the relaxation time of this process by BDS, it is possible to access these relaxation times via a methodology, which is completely unexplored for studying the dynamics of confined water and is based on a DSC step response protocol (43, 48) that uses both fast and standard calorimetric techniques. These techniques overcome the limitations of other experimental methods that hinder the observation of α-relaxation. We obtain the complex specific heat, which is typically exclusively sensitive to α-relaxation (49, 50). Fig. 5C shows cPrev, which is approximately equal to cP′(ω), for different frequencies f = ω/2π. We see a step shift to higher temperatures with increasing frequency. From the inflection point of the cPrev step (see the Inset of Fig. 5D), we can determine the temperature at which the α-relaxation time corresponds to the inverse of the respective angular frequency [τ = ω−1 = 1/(2π f)]. The relaxation map (Fig. 5E) shows that the relaxation times determined by the DSC step response protocol are consistent with those of the low-frequency relaxation observed by the BDS.

MS-I displays partial crystallization, and, therefore, in addition to the relaxations of amorphous water (similar to that observed in MCM-41), we detect two BDS processes due to the relaxation of ice (51). We determine the relaxation time as the inverse of the frequency of the peaks’ maximum in Fig. 5B only for temperatures below 190 K since, at higher temperatures, the contributions due to ice and conductivity mask the response of amorphous water. However, similar to MCM-41, we can determine the relaxation time of the slow relaxation using the DSC step response protocol (see Fig. 5D and open points in Fig. 5F).

For both samples, the slow relaxation follows a Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) behavior [τ = τo exp [DT0/(T − T0)]], where D is the fragility index, and T0 is the temperature at which τ would become infinite. Given its VFT behavior, this relaxation can be attributed to the α-relaxation of water molecules confined in a rigid matrix. From the VFT fit, we obtain τ0 = 10−12.5 s, D = 21, and T0 = 103 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm, whereas τ0 = 10−12.5 s, D = 9.4, and T0 = 156 K for MS-I. This means that water in MCM-41 2.1 nm is slightly stronger than water in MS-I (see the Angell plot in the Inset of Fig. 6). Applying the criterion τα(Tg) ∼ 100 s, the Tg values are 169.5 and 200.8 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm and MS-I, respectively. Importantly, these temperatures approximately coincide with those of the endothermic event observed after annealing at temperatures just below the cp increase (Step II; see Fig. 3). This coincidence implies that the latter endothermic event is intimately associated with α-relaxation and, therefore, can be assigned to the calorimetric Tg of confined water.

With respect to the faster BDS relaxation, we observe a dynamic crossover from a liquid-like VFT behavior toward an Arrhenius activated motion, which occurs at a temperature of T = (170 ± 2) K for MCM-41 2.1 nm, which is compatible with previous BDS and NMR results (9, 36, 56). Below this temperature, the relaxation times of the faster process can be described by the Arrhenius equation [τ = τo exp (ΔE/kBT)] with an activation energy of ΔE ~ 0.48 eV and a pre-exponential factor of log (τo/s) = −17.4, again in agreement with previous results for confined water. The relaxation times of MS-I are similar to those of MCM-41 at low temperatures, with a somewhat lower activation energy of 0.40 eV. Fig. 6 compares these times with those obtained by BDS for LDA and HDA (12), water in vermiculite clays (52), aqueous solutions of PVP (53), microporous glass (32) (MIP), and NMR for water confined in aqueous solutions of collagen (54) and PVP (55), indicating the universality of this relaxation time for confined water as previously discussed (56, 57).

Discussion

The main findings of our work, summarized in Figs. 3 and 4, indicate the occurrence of three distinct thermal events in the heat capacity of confined water (Steps I, II, and III), which occur in different temperature ranges. While, with Steps I and II exhibit time-dependent responses to aging, Step III remains completely unaffected by aging.

Step II in the specific heat capacity of the confined water (~175 K and ~200 K for MCM-41 2.1 nm and MS-I, respectively) exhibits striking hallmarks of a glass transition phenomenon. Multiple observations support this conclusion. First, annealing experiments (Fig. 3), just below Step II, reveal a pronounced and narrow endothermic overshoot in specific heat upon heating, as typically observed at the glass transition. Hence, this peak is attributed to the recovery of enthalpy previously decreased by aging. Notably, the temperature at which this endothermic peak occurs approximately coincides with the temperature where the VFT process (derived from BDS, FSC, and DSC) extrapolates to a characteristic time of 100 s. This concordance across independent techniques and different approaches (nonlinear, in annealing experiments versus linear measurements, where spontaneous fluctuations are probed) provides compelling arguments that Step II at 175 to 200 K reflects the glass transition of confined water within the pores.

Valuable insights also emerge from annealing confined water well below the often reported glass transition temperature of bulk water, Tg = 136 K. Conducting annealing experiments at 115 or 120 K, i.e., near the onset of Step I, for 4 h revealed an intriguing excess endothermic in heat capacity, situated just above the annealing temperature, as depicted in Fig. 4. The excess endotherm shown in the Inset of Fig. 4 covers a broad temperature range of approximately 50 K, vastly surpassing the width of the excess typically associated with the glass transition (as indicated in Fig. 3, approximately 20 K). Notably, this phenomenon extends beyond water and appears to be universal, as multiple experiments illustrate that annealing various glasses–including hyperquenched inorganic glasses (21), polymers (58, 59), metallic glasses (60, 61), and plastic crystals (61, 62)—well below their Tg induces a similar thermal event, which is well distinguished from a standard glass transition and alternatively referred to as a “shadow glass transition,” “annealing prepeak,” or “sub-Tg peak” (21, 63, 64). Consistently, confined water shows a broad relaxation process with Arrhenius temperature dependence typical of a secondary relaxation, which extrapolates to a characteristic time of 100 s in the temperature range of the thermal event observed postannealing at low temperatures. Thus, this thermal event is related to secondary equilibration mechanisms in glassy materials, originating from a secondary relaxation process, which bears potential in glass equilibration (65, 66). At these low temperatures, HDA and LDA (obtained from solutions of pure water) were identified as strong and superstrong liquids in terms of Angell’s classification (67), because of their tiny ΔcP and their relaxation times following an Arrhenius temperature dependence up to crystallization (12). However, considering that the relaxation times of HDA and LDA are very similar to those of confined water (Fig. 6), the present results suggest a scenario where bulk water at these temperatures is still glassy, and the small increase in ΔcP is due to the well-known phenomenon referred to as sub-Tg peak, as suggested by Angell (21) but later challenged by himself (68).

The question is why the α-relaxation of water differs in the studied confinements, as shown in Fig. 6. α-relaxation is a cooperative phenomenon associated with increasing length scales. The correlation length of this relaxation could be on the order of ~1 or 2 nm (69, 70) at sufficiently low temperatures. Therefore, when nanopores with similar diameters are used, it is inevitable to consider not only interactions with the inner surfaces but also possible finite-size effects. Both effects are more prominent in MS because this confinement system is more disorganized than MCM-41. In addition, the presence of cations in the cavities of MS (sodium and potassium for MS-I and MS-II, respectively) may affect water dynamics.

The high-temperature event (Step III) is insensitive to annealing, strongly suggesting that it is not related to a kinetic event, such as the glass transition, but rather is a thermodynamic transition. However, the origin of this jump remains elusive. While the widely discussed liquid–liquid transition (33, 34, 71), an intrinsic feature of water's behavior, is a possible explanation for this phenomenon, we must also consider that a fraction of water molecules is located at the inner surfaces of porous materials. Hence, an alternative interpretation comes into play, where the adsorption/desorption of water molecules may be related to this thermal event, in ways analogous to polymer layers in contact with an inorganic substrate (72). In this latter case, it has been shown that adsorption/desorption of polymer chains exhibits the features of a true thermodynamic transition, taking place at a specific temperature.

It is evident that the Tg of confined water, as studied here, is intricately linked to the type of confinement. This allows us to determine a temperature range between ~170 and 200 K, where the Tg of confined water occurs, dependent on its interactions with the walls. At the same time, the Johari–Goldstein β-relaxation of confined water exhibits a crossover at temperatures between 170 and 220 K across various confinement systems. Following our findings, the crossover indicates the presence of a glass transition in the confined water (the temperature where the glass transition occurs can be detected using the methodology developed in this work). Therefore, confined water is expected to display a Tg within this range for all confinement systems, with the exact temperature depending on the confinement type. Thereat, the Johari–Goldstein β-relaxation time starts to deviate from the common low-temperature Arrhenius law and exhibits a higher temperature dependence above the Tg, which is typical of many glass formers. Thus, the literature interpretation (33) that connects the observed change in the temperature dependence of the relaxation times to the evolution from a high-temperature HDL to a low-temperature LDL must be reconsidered. Additionally, the idea presented by some of us that this crossover is attributed to finite-size effects must be revisited (37). This change in the dynamics occurs at the glass transition of confined water, similar to what is observed for secondary relaxations in other liquids, and therefore does not support the original interpretations. Hence, because the β-relaxation rather than the α-relaxation is probed, this dynamical crossover around Tg does not inform about the fragility and, thus, should not be misinterpreted as a transition where water transforms from a strong to a fragile liquid.

Our findings raise intriguing questions about the behavior of bulk water at supercooled temperatures, particularly in comparison to confined water. As shown in Fig. 6, the relaxation times of heated LDA and HDA are similar to those of the Johari–Goldstein β-relaxation of confined water. Furthermore, similar relaxation times determined by FSC in thin water films and bulk water glasses (73) (see a complete discussion in SI Appendix, section 5) also correspond to the JG β-relaxation of confined water. This suggests that the relaxation in different bulk water forms also represents a (Johari–Goldstein) β-relaxation rather than an α-relaxation. Whatever the underlying molecular mechanism behind this β-relaxation, it must bear the features of a more localized process because water is deep in the glassy state at this temperature. Hence, although we must be cautious when drawing conclusions from confined water to bulk water, the present findings imply that the tiny and broad cP-endotherm observed for LDA at an onset temperature of 126 K should also be interpreted as a nonequilibrium enthalpy recovery triggered by a local molecular mechanism with potential for glass equilibration rather than ascribed to the structural glass transition of water.

Materials and Methods

Characterization of MCM-41 and MS.

The mesoporous silica MCM-41 (2.1 and 2.4 nm) were synthesized following the protocol of Grünberg and coworkers (74) and previously characterized (47) by nitrogen adsorption at 77 K. For MCM-41 2.1 nm and MCM-41 2.4, the pore diameter amounts to 2.1 and 2.4 nm, respectively, whereas the specific pore volumes are 0.3 and 0.49 cm3/g, respectively. MS were purchased from Merck (CAS: 308080-99-1 and 70955-01-0, respectively). The supplier provided a cell size of 0.3 nm for MS-I and 0.4 nm for MS-II. All the samples were dried under high vacuum for 24 h. After that, samples were weighed and hydrated for 4 wk at different RH values. The water fraction, [Mw/(Mw+Md)], was calculated on the basis of the masses Mw and Md of the absorbed water and the dry confinement system, respectively. Information related to the water contents of all the samples can be found in SI Appendix, section 2.

Calorimetric Studies.

Standard DSC was performed with a TA Instruments DSC Q2000 with liquid nitrogen cooling, operating in a temperature range between 100 K and 300 K. The chamber was purged with dry nitrogen at a flow rate of 50 mL/min. The samples were prepared by pressing approximately 15 to 20 mg of powder in an aluminum pan.

FSC was conducted using a Mettler-Toledo Flash DSC1 instrument equipped with a Hubert TC100 intracooler and a Leica M60 optical microscope (40×), with operating temperatures between 173 K and 723 K. The cooling stage of the instrument was purged with dry nitrogen at a flow rate of 20 mL/min. Samples were prepared by depositing about 300 ng of sample powder on a Mettler Toledo UFS 1 chip. A drop of silicon oil was placed on the chip to achieve good thermal contact with the sample.

A step response protocol was performed for the FSC and DSC techniques to assess the spontaneous thermal fluctuations of confined water in terms of the frequency-dependent complex specific heat. The temperature protocol comprised a repetition of 2 K down-jumps at a nominal cooling rate β of 1,000 K s−1 for FSC and 15 K min−1 for DSC followed by isotherms. The period tp (0.1024 s and 128 s for FSC and DSC, respectively) required to perform a jump/isotherm step defines the base angular frequency of perturbation ω0 = 2π/tp. The step was looped, spanning from 253 K to 173 K for the FSC protocol, while reaching temperatures as low as 123 K for DSC measurements.

The frequency-dependent complex specific heat cp*(ω) was calculated for ω0 and high-order harmonics (ω = kω0, where k = 1, 2, …) by using a discrete fast Fourier transform for each period of perturbation in the form ofcp*ω=∑t=0tpHFte-iωtΔt∑t=0tpq(t)e-iωtΔt,

where HF(t) and q (t) are the sample heat flow and instantaneous cooling rate, respectively. Finally, the frequency-dependent glass transition temperature Tg(ω), which is assessed in the linear perturbation regime, is calculated at the inflection point of the reversing specific heat step cprev(ω), i.e., the modulus of cp* (ω).

BDS.

In BDS experiments, the complex dielectric permittivity, ε∗(ω) = ε′(ω) − iε′′(ω), is measured over a wide frequency range (∼10−2 to 1010 Hz) by combining several dielectric techniques (10−1 to 106 Hz: A Novocontrol Alpha Analyzer was used; 106 to 109 Hz: An Agilent rf impedance analyzer 4192B was used). The thickness of the samples was ~0.1 mm, and the sample diameters were 30 and 10 mm for the low- and high frequencies, respectively. In the BDS, we measured unaged samples (after cooling at 10 K/min) every degree in the temperature range from 160 to 250 K and every 2.5° between 252 and 300 K.
