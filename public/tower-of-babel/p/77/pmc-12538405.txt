Sox17 mediates venous adaptive remodeling after arteriovenous fistula creation
Authors: Yukihiko Aoyagi, Andrew W. Schwartz, Zhuo Li, Hualong Bai, Bryan Ho, Cayetana Lazcano-Etchebarne, Luis Gonzalez, Yuichi Ohashi, Masaki Kano, Bogdan Yatsula, Cassius Iyad Ochoa Chaar, Kathleen Martin, Roberto Vazquez-Padron, Guohao Dai, Alan Dardik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMature arteriovenous fistulae (AVF) are the gold standard vascular access allowing hemodialysis for patients with end-stage kidney disease. The rate of AVF failure remains high, reflecting an incomplete understanding of the biology of AVF maturation and failure. Sox17 is a transcription factor indispensable for the acquisition and maintenance of endothelial arterial identity. Because endothelial cells (EC) acquire dual arteriovenous identity after AVF creation, we determined whether Sox17 mediates venous remodeling in AVF.MethodsHuman preaccess vein, failed AVF, and mature AVF from second stage basilic vein transpositions were analyzed using immunofluorescence and single cell sequencing to determine Sox17 expression. Aortocaval AVF created in 9- to 11-week-old C57BL/6J mice were harvested on day 7 or 21 for analysis with Western blot, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, histology, or immunofluorescence to assess Sox17 immunoreactivity in EC. Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed AVF patency and measured inferior vena cava and aortic flow velocity and diameter. Sox17 knockdown was performed using short hairpin RNA lentivirus delivered perivascularly immediately after AVF creation. Analysis of variance and t tests were used for statistical analyses.ResultsSox17 was significantly elevated in mature human AVF compared with control veins (P = .02) and in mature human AVF compared with failed human AVF (P = .04). In the mouse AVF, Sox17 expression was also significantly upregulated compared with sham-operated mice (P = .0012 in male mice; P = .0062 in female mice). Sox17 immunoreactivity was highest on days 7 and 21 and returned to near sham levels at day 42. In both human and mouse AVF, Sox17 expression was localized to the nucleus of EC. Sox17 knockdown mice had impaired venous remodeling, characterized by a smaller inferior vena cava diameter (P = .001) and thinner intima-media layers relative to control AVF (P = .015). Sox17 knockdown mice had decreased JAG1 expression (P = .008) and decreased smooth muscle cell proliferation (P = .03) on day 7.ConclusionsSox17 is a novel mediator of venous remodeling during AVF maturation. Sox17 is upregulated in the venous outflow of AVF, and Sox17 knockdown causes impaired outward remodeling and wall thickening. Manipulation of endothelial vascular identity may be a translational approach to regulate venous remodeling and improve AVF patency.Clinical RelevanceArteriovenous fistula (AVF) failure remains a major barrier to long-term hemodialysis access in patients with end-stage kidney disease. This study identifies Sox17 as a key transcription factor mediating venous remodeling after AVF creation. Sox17 expression is elevated in mature human AVF and affects outward remodeling, wall thickening, and smooth muscle cell proliferation in a murine model. Sox17 knockdown results in impaired AVF maturation. These findings highlight Sox17 as a critical regulator of flow-mediated vascular adaptation and suggest that modulation of endothelial arterial identity through Sox17 may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to improve AVF outcomes.

End-stage kidney disease is a highly prevalent and morbid condition, with approximately 800,000 patients in the United States who require a significant amount of healthcare resources.1, 2, 3, 4 Creation of an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is a lifesaving and commonly performed procedure to create hemodialysis access for patients with end-stage kidney disease.5 However, the rate of AVF failure remains high despite continued clinical and translational efforts.6 Between 30% and 50% of AVF fail to mature within 6 months owing to insufficient luminal dilation and wall thickening (primary failure).7 In mature AVF, approximately 40% will fail within the first year after creation owing to stenosis caused by neointimal hyperplasia (secondary failure).8,9 During AVF maturation, the venous outflow of the AVF remodels by dilating and increasing wall thickness owing to both myofibroblast differentiation and proliferation and extracellular matrix remodeling; in addition, the venous outflow retains venous identity and gains expression of arterial identity and thus takes on a mixed arterial-venous molecular identity.10

Sox17 is a transcription factor and member of the SoxF protein subfamily that are essential regulators of cell differentiation.11 Sox17 is necessary for endothelial cell (EC) arterial specification during embryogenesis; Sox17 is expressed in arteries, but not in veins, and is an upstream regulator of arterial specification throughout development.12 Knockout of Sox17 in EC causes embryonic death and a lack of proper vascular development, including an undifferentiated vascular system with a dysfunctional or absent arterial network. Sox17 is a necessary regulator for remodeling of the coronary vessels, and introducing genetic deletion of Sox17 in EC causes aberrant coronary artery formation.13 Sox17 also plays a critical role in maintaining arterial identity and function postnatally.12,14 Deficiency of Sox17 expression in adulthood is associated with the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension and intracranial aneurysms owing to EC dysfunction.15,16 Importantly, Sox17 mediates EC adaptation to altered hemodynamics and promotes the expression of arterial identity markers after altered shear stress.17 Previous RNA sequencing analyses of human AVF identified increased expression of Sox17 mRNA in human AVF compared with the control vein.18

Because Sox17 regulates endothelial arterial identity in response to hemodynamic changes, Sox17 may play a mechanistic role in venous remodeling after AVF creation. In a mouse model of AVF, we previously showed that several markers of arterial identity were upregulated in the venous outflow tract after AVF creation.10 This acquisition of an arterial molecular signature and phenotype may be driven by Sox17, given its upstream function as a key regulator of arterial differentiation and maintenance of arterial identity in adulthood. We hypothesized that Sox17 expression is increased in the venous outflow after AVF creation and regulates venous remodeling in AVF.

Methods

Human AVF sample collection

Control (basilic) vein and mature and failed brachiobasilic AVF were collected.18, 19, 20 AVF were determined as patent or failed at the time of the second stage procedure; failure was defined clinically as an AVF that never reached a luminal diameter of 6 mm or greater or anatomically as an AVF that required a short transposition, ligation, or graft extension rather than the standard second stage transposition. None of the AVF underwent surgical or endovascular interventions to assist maturation. For immunofluorescence analyses, we included longitudinal vein and second stage AVF samples (n = 18; 9 preaccess vein, 4 with matured AVF, 5 with failed AVF) randomly selected from the University of Miami Vascular Access Biorepository.21 Samples were preserved in RNAlater (QIAGEN) and stored at −80°C. A 1- to 2-mm cross-section from each sample was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich) for paraffin embedding and sectioning. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the regulatory requirements of Jackson Memorial Hospital and the University of Miami Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at the University of Miami.

Single-cell RNA sequencing bioinformatic analysis

Bioinformatic analysis was performed on single-cell RNA sequencing data from 70,281 cells derived from human veins and AVF collected from patients requiring two-stage AVF creation.19 Cells were isolated from veins before and shortly after anastomosis, as well as from mature and failed AVF collected before transposition. Data were processed in R (v4.2.2) using Seurat v4 and standard bioinformatic pipelines.20,22 Overall clustering was performed using the first 30 principal components with the FindNeighbors, FindClusters, and RunUMAP functions at a resolution of 0.5. Major clusters were manually annotated based on canonical cell type markers.20 For downstream visualization and cluster-specific analyses, we selected the 20 samples included in this study from the complete Seurat object.

Aortocaval fistula surgery

Male or female C57Bl/6 aged 9 to 11 weeks were treated with either sham surgery or AVF creation via an infrarenal aortic puncture using a 25G needle as described previously.23 For induction of anesthesia, 3.0% isoflurane in 1 L/min oxygen was used followed by 2.0% to 2.5% isoflurane for maintenance. A midline laparotomy was performed and the infrarenal aorta and inferior vena cava (IVC) were carefully dissected free from surrounding structures and exposed. A distal aortic puncture site was exposed with dissection. An aortic clamp was placed using a bulldog clamp inferior to the left renal artery. A 25G needle was then used to puncture the aorta into the IVC, followed by gentle compression of the aortic puncture site using surrounding retroperitoneal tissue. Technical success of AVF creation was determined by the presence of visible arterial flow in the IVC and adequate hemostasis. Abdominal musculature and skin were closed with a simple running suture. Mice recovered on a heated mat on a back table. Sham surgery was a midline laparotomy, bowel manipulation, dissection, and exposure of the aorta and IVC, but without needle puncture of the aorta into the IVC. For analgesia, animals received preoperative subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.5 mg/kg) and postoperative subcutaneous 2% lidocaine injection surrounding the midline laparotomy incision.

Ultrasound examination

Aorta and IVC diameter and flow velocities were measured preoperatively and postoperatively with Doppler ultrasound. The Vevo F2 Imaging system (VisualSonics) was used for all data points. AVF were considered to be technically patent based on the presence of disturbed waveforms in the IVC and increased end diastolic velocities in the aorta.24 Nonpatent AVF at postoperative day 3 were excluded from analysis and considered to be technical failures. All measurements of the aorta and IVC were done just caudal to the left renal vein. Measurements were collected serially in mice at day 0 (baseline), day 7, and day 21 after AVF or sham surgery. Relative diameter was the ratio of the diameter at the specified time point relative to the day 0 diameter. Velocities were obtained at the same anatomical location using the pulse wave mode to measure waveforms. The average of the waveform was taken to calculate a mean velocity for the aorta and IVC. Shear stress was calculated using the Hagen-Poiseuille equation τ = 4ηV/r, where τ is the shear stress, η is the blood viscosity, V is the mean velocity (cm/s), and r is the radius (cm). The blood viscosity was assumed as a constant of 0.035 poise.

Mouse tissue collection and processing

Mice were euthanized in compliance with local Veterinary Clinical Services Policies and consistent with the American Veterinary Medical Association. Mice were euthanized via exsanguination under anesthesia. The abdominal aorta just caudal to the left renal artery and the IVC just caudal to the left renal vein were dissected away from surrounding tissue. For histology, the infrarenal aorta and IVC were extracted en bloc after being flushed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and placed in 10% NBF for at least 12 hours. NBF was replaced with 70% ethanol for transport and sent to Yale Histology Service for paraffin embedding. The IVC and aorta were embedded to be cut transversely using a microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For RNA and protein extraction, the aorta was dissected away from the IVC, and the aorta and IVC were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Immunofluorescence and histology

Tissue sections of en bloc IVC and aorta were deparaffinized in xylene and a graded series of alcohols. Sections were washed with PBS then heated with citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) at 100°C for 20 minutes for antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table, online only) diluted in 3% BSA overnight in 4°C. Sections were washed with PBS then treated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour with goat anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sections were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Life Technologies), and a coverslip was placed. Fluorescent images were captured with EVOS FL Auto 2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Positive cells, immunofluorescent density, and percent area were calculated using ImageJ software. Histological staining was performed by Yale Pathology Tissue Service, including Elastin Van Gieson and trichrome staining. The combined intimal-medial thickness was assessed because mouse veins lack an internal elastic lamina and only have an external elastic lamina; therefore, medial thickness alone cannot be assessed.25 Light microscopy of stained slides was captured. Image processing was carried out using ImageJ.

Western blotting

Protein from AVF was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer. The protein concentration was calculated using colorimetric assay (Bio-Rad). We loaded 30 μg of protein onto 8% to 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred it to polyvinylidene difluoride microporous membrane (0.45 μm pore size; Immobilon, Millipore) and blocked it with 5% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated it with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After primary antibody incubation, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was added and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were developed using the Western Lightning ECL reagent (PerkinElmer). As needed, membranes were stripped with Restore Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Pierce Biotechnology) and reincubated as described.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

After AVF creation, the IVC was dissected with care to remove surrounding arterial and connective tissue. Total RNA was isolated from the IVC by using RNeasy Mini Kit (74,106, Qiagen) from each IVC.26 The quality of the RNA was confirmed with NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine the 260/280 nm ratio. Reverse transcription was performed with SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis Supermix (11752250, Invitrogen). Real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed using iQ SYBR Green Supermix (1708880, Bio-Rad) and amplified for 40 cycles using the iQ5 Real-Time polymerase chain reaction Detection System (Bio-Rad). All samples were normalized to GAPDH amplification and graphed in arbitrary units. The primers used are as follows: Sox17 forward primer: GTGGGGGACGAGTACGAGAT; reverse primer: TGGTCACCACATCAAAAGCAT; GAPDH forward primer: GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG; and reverse primer: CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT.

Aortocaval Sox17 knockdown

For knockdown experiments, periadventitial tissue was dissected away from the infrarenal IVC to expose the adventitia before AVF creation. Equivalent concentrations of Sox17 lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (ACATTTGACGGTTGCCGAC, GCAAGCAGGTGAAGCGCAT, TCAGCGGTCTACTATTGCA) or nontargeting lentiviral shRNA (Horizon) were dissolved in 200 μL of Pluronic gel, using 2 × 106 copies of either lentiviral Sox17 shRNA or lentiviral nontargeting control based on previously optimized lentiviral shRNA concentrations.10 After successful AVF creation, either Sox17 lentiviral shRNA or nontargeting lentiviral shRNA in Pluronic gel were applied directly to the infrarenal IVC (Supplementary Fig 1, online only). The use of lentiviral shRNA to target luminal EC protein expression has been previously described in this model.10

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Data analysis was conducted using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Statistical significance was determined using Student's t test or analysis of variance. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for nonparametric data or for small sample sizes (n < 6). A P value of less than .05 was considered significant.

Results

Increased Sox17 in human AVF

To determine whether the arterial-specific transcription factor Sox17 is present in mature human AVF, we analyzed human control veins without an AVF and matched AVF collected after maturation as part of a two-stage AVF surgical procedure, as previously described.18 Briefly, AVF were determined as failed at the time of the second stage procedure (time of harvest); failure was defined clinically as an AVF that never reached a luminal diameter of 6 mm or greater or that required a short transposition, ligation, or graft extension rather than the standard second stage transposition. Control veins before creation of an AVF showed typical venous morphology, including an endothelial monolayer and a single elastic lamina with a thin medial layer; mature human AVF had a thicker venous wall composed of neointima; failed AVF had either thick neointimal hyperplasia consistent with occlusive failure or lack of wall thickening and outward remodeling consistent with failure to mature (Fig 1, A). Increased Sox17 expression in mature compared with failed human AVF

To evaluate for potential differences in Sox17 expression between mature and failed AVF, we queried Sox17 expression in a single-cell RNA sequencing database of preaccess vein, mature AVF, and failed AVF that was created in an unbiased fashion.27 As expected, there was minimal Sox17 expression in control veins; interestingly, there was increased endothelial Sox17 expression in mature AVF compared with failed AVF (Fig 2, A). Our analysis also confirmed that Sox17 expression is mostly in the EC population and not in the other cell types in the blood vessel. Among failed AVF, Sox17 expression was significantly more likely than mature AVF to be found within a subpopulation of cells that arose postoperatively (EC4) that expressed shear stress responsive and hemostatic regulatory proteins such as endothelial nitric oxide synthase and prostaglandins (Supplementary Fig 2, online only). To confirm the single-cell RNA findings, we analyzed cross-sections of human vein, mature AVF, and failed AVF using immunofluorescence. Both mature and failed AVF showed some EC loss, consistent with these cross-sections of AVF being taken from near the juxta-anastomotic area, which is characterized by disturbed flow and EC loss.8 Within the CD31+ cells (EC), Sox17 expression was significantly increased in mature AVF compared with failed AVF (Fig 2, B and C), consistent with the single-cell sequencing analysis.

Increased Sox17 expression in a mouse AVF model

Because human AVF shows increased Sox17 immunoreactivity (Figs 1 and 2), we confirmed these data in a mouse AVF model, using a sutureless mouse aortocaval fistula model that recapitulates human AVF maturation and is characterized by outward remodeling and wall thickening by day 21 (Fig 3, A and B and Supplementary Fig 1, online only).23 In the venous outflow of the AVF, Sox17 mRNA transcripts were increased at day 7, in AVF in both male and female mice, compared with sham-operated mice (Fig 3, C). There was a trend toward increased Sox17 protein at day 7 in AVF compared with sham-operated mice (Fig 3, D). Similarly, Sox17 was also significantly increased at day 7 after AVF creation in mice treated with 5/6 nephrectomy (Supplementary Fig 4, online only). In addition, the juxta-anastomotic area, which is characterized by disturbed flow and endothelial loss at the fistula exit, showed high levels of Sox17 expression along the fistula exit channel in wild-type mice (Supplementary Fig 5, online only).8,28 The flow-responsive expression of Sox17 in vivo is consistent with prior in vitro studies showing increased expression of endothelial Sox17 and arterial identity markers after exposure to laminar shear stress.17 Temporal evaluation of Sox17 expression in the AVF wall showed a lack of Sox17 expression in the control IVC (day 0 preoperative IVC); Sox17 expression was increased in EC at days 7 and 21, with decreased expression at day 42 (Fig 3, E and F). At days 7 and 21, Sox17 expression was significantly increased in AVF and was colocalized to CD31+ cells, consistent with the human AVF data (Fig 3, G). Knockdown of Sox17 impairs AVF maturation

Because Sox17 is increased in both human (Figs 1 and 2) and mouse (Fig 3) venous remodeling, we determined whether inhibiting Sox17 expression altered venous remodeling. Murine lung carcinoma cells, which have high levels of Sox17 expression, were treated with either lentiviral nontargeting shRNA or lentiviral Sox17 shRNA (108 particles/mL); EC were not used to test lentiviral shRNA knockdown owing to expected loss of Sox17 expression in EC in vitro. Cells treated with lentiviral shRNA showed significantly decreased Sox17 immunoreactivity (Fig 4, A), with an efficiency of knockdown of 99.5%. For in vivo experiments, lentiviral shRNA (control and Sox17) was dissolved in Pluronic gel and applied directly to the adventitia immediately after AVF creation. Local lentiviral shRNA delivery to the IVC, which is known to have some systemic absorption, did not have any noticeable effect on mouse weight or behavior; there was no significant difference in relative body weight change between control and Sox17 knockdown mice at day 21 (0.98 vs 0.99 body weight normalized to day 0; P = .81).29 Serial ultrasound examinations after AVF creation showed that AVF with Sox17 knockdown had impaired outward remodeling compared with control AVF (Fig 4, B); however, despite the small change in AVF diameter, there was no difference in shear stress in the IVC between mice treated with control and knockdown lentivirus (Fig 4, C). Immunofluorescence of AVF cross-sections confirmed persistent knockdown of Sox17 expression at days 7 and 21 (Fig 4, D). Arterial identity markers and downstream targets of Sox17 activity were assessed on day 7. Delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4) and Notch3 were not significantly different between control and Sox17 knockdown AVF; however, Jagged1 was significantly decreased in the AVF wall (Fig 4, E). At day 21, intimal-medial thickness was significantly decreased in Sox17 knockdown AVF compared with control AVF (Fig 4, F). Knockdown of Sox17 impairs AVF mural cell proliferation

Because AVF with reduced Sox17 expression show less outward remodeling and thinner walls (Fig 4), we determined whether there was altered cell turnover in the remodeling vein. At day 7, there were significantly fewer proliferating cell nuclear antigen/SM22α dual-positive cells in AVF with Sox17 knockdown compared with control AVF, with no difference at day 21 (Fig 5, A). There were few cells showing apoptosis in the AVF wall at day 7 and day 21, and there was no significant difference between control or knockdown AVF (Fig 5, B); minimal apoptosis in the AVF wall is consistent with our previous data.29,30 We then determined whether Sox17 knockdown influenced EC proliferation. There was no difference in proliferation or in apoptosis in EC in control or Sox17 knockdown AVF at day 7 (Supplementary Fig 6, online only); positive controls for proliferation and apoptosis in small bowel are shown in Supplementary Fig 5, B, online only. These data are consistent with reduced smooth muscle cell (SMC) proliferation in AVF with reduced Sox17 expression. There were no significant differences in α-smooth muscle actin (contractile marker) and vimentin (proliferative marker) immunoreactivity between Sox17 knockdown AVF and control AVF (Fig 5, C and D). Similarly, there were no differences in collagen staining (Fig 5, E) or collagen I and III immunoreactivity (Fig 5, F) between the groups. Discussion

This study shows increased expression of the arterial transcription factor Sox17 in the EC of mature human AVF and in a mouse model of AVF (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3). Failed human AVF have decreased Sox17 expression compared with patent human AVF on single-cell and immunofluorescent analyses (Fig 2). Knockdown of Sox17 in a murine model of AVF causes impaired AVF outward remodeling and reduced wall thickening (Fig 4) and reduced SMC proliferation (Fig 5), as well as decreased JAG1 expression, a key marker of arterial identity (Fig 4). These data show that Sox17 is a mediator of the flow-dependent endothelial programming necessary for adaptive remodeling and arterial identity specification after AVF creation.

We show that the transcription factor Sox17 is highly expressed in mature human AVF and in an aortocaval mouse AVF model. Prior in vitro work established that induced Sox17 activation in human umbilical vascular EC is sufficient to induce an arterial gene expression profile and induced Sox17 expression enhances adult EC adaptability to hemodynamic changes.12,17 Interestingly, in that study, arterial levels of laminar shear stress alone were not sufficient to induce Sox17 expression and arterial marker gene expression. Our work builds on these findings and further shows that Sox17 expression is induced in venous EC in vivo after AVF creation (Figs 1 and 3). The AVF hemodynamic environment is characterized by supra-arterial magnitudes of flow and shear stress, as well as altered frequencies, such as disturbed flow; thus, Sox17 expression may be increased in response to these supra-arterial hemodynamic changes. It is also possible that Sox17 expression is increased in response to the inflammation seen in early AVF maturation, potentially secondary to the increased oxygen in the shunted arterial blood, because Sox17 mediates EC regeneration after inflammation induced vascular injury.31,32 Although Sox17 was significantly upregulated in mature human AVF compared with human control vein (Figs 1 and 2), failed human AVF had significantly reduced Sox17 expression compared with mature AVF (Fig 2), raising the interesting possibility that the Sox17 level may correlate with the AVF success rate.

Another important finding is that Sox17 knockdown after AVF creation impairs the venous remodeling that occurs during AVF maturation. Our prior data showed that arterial identity markers are upregulated after AVF creation, such as EphrinB2, JAG1, and DLL4, as well as and members of the Notch pathway such as Notch1 and Notch3.10 We also showed that the activation of EphrinB2 signaling promotes adaptive venous remodeling, thus improving fistula maturation.33 Sox17 is a master transcriptional regulator controlling arterial identity and exerts upstream control of arterial identity markers, and our data suggest that Sox17 is an important mediator of adaptive venous remodeling in response to the increased shear stress in the fistula environment.12 The mechanisms of outward remodeling involve vasodilation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and vascular SMC migration and proliferation.34,35 Interestingly, we found significantly decreased SMC proliferation in Sox17 knockdown AVF compared with control veins (Fig 5, A), consistent with impaired outward remodeling and wall thickening. Because Sox17 knockdown decreased SMC proliferation (Fig 5, A) without altering α-smooth muscle actin levels (Fig 5, C), it may be that Sox17 regulates proliferative capacity without fully suppressing contractile marker expression; however, it is not clear whether static in vitro models will be sufficient to model this in vivo response. Decreased SMC proliferation after Sox17 knockdown in vivo appears to reflect impaired early adaptive remodeling and failure of maturation, rather than protection from or absence of neointimal hyperplasia; our data (days 7 and 21) are from before days 28 to 42, when neointima hyperplasia develops in this model.23,36 In contrast, the failed human AVF were obtained at variable time points based on routine clinical care. Thus, it is likely that these cases included both early failure of maturation and/or subsequent stenotic failure driven by neointimal hyperplasia, and it is possible that the decreased Sox17 expression observed in failed human fistulae may represent an early upstream defect that contributes to later stenotic failure. Nonetheless, the decreased SMC proliferation in the mouse Sox17 knockdown model suggests failure of AVF maturation, which is only one aspect that contributes to human AVF failure.

The mechanisms by which endothelial Sox17 affect flow-mediated remodeling in the AVF wall, including multiple cell types that probably include SMC and immune cells, likely involves a complex interplay between shear stress, EC signaling, SMC phenotypic changes, and extracellular matrix remodeling.37 Sox17 functions as a key upstream modulator of this process by regulating endothelial responses to altered hemodynamics and promoting downstream EC arterial identity and SMC proliferation.38 As a transcription factor, Sox17 controls the expression of numerous shear stress responsive genes that exert paracrine effects and are involved in cell-cell signaling with the surrounding EC and SMC populations.17,38 It is possible that Sox17 expression after AVF creation controls the expression of important modulators of EC and SMC phenotype, such as members of the Notch family (Fig 4). This finding is consistent with our previous data showing that EC secrete SMC chemoattractants in response to shear stress.39 In addition, Sox17 regulation of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition may also play a role in AVF wall remodeling.36 Thus, it is possible that, after AVF creation, endothelial Sox17 expression promotes a protein expression profile that facilitates adaptation to the altered hemodynamic environment through paracrine and cell-cell signaling in the AVF wall.

In vitro models have established that Sox17 is a critical mediator of the arterial response to fluid shear stress.17,38 This study assessed the role of Sox17 as a mediator of acquiring arterial identity in an in vivo, postnatal model that assesses venous adaptation to supra-arterial flow; we showed that Sox17 knockdown is associated with significantly decreased JAG1 expression compared with control AVF, without significant differences in DLL4 or Notch3 expression (Fig 4). This finding was somewhat unexpected, however, because in vitro models of Sox17 knockdown display significant loss of most if not all arterial markers. Vascular EC and SMC in vivo are continuously influenced by paracrine factors, hemodynamics, inflammatory cells, and cell-cell interactions that are not present within in vitro systems; however, one in vivo study using an inducible EC-specific knockout of Sox17 in mice showed that both JAG1 and DLL4 were significantly reduced in the mouse retina at postnatal day 9.12 In vivo models have greater regulatory complexity and compensatory potential, and our study assessed the potential for adaptation to hemodynamic changes in the venous system in adult mice rather than during arterial vascular development, which may account for the differences seen in the lack of downregulation of DLL4. As such, the specific decrease in JAG1 suggests JAG1 may be uniquely sensitive to Sox17 loss in the context of venous remodeling and AVF maturation.

Conclusions

Sox17 expression is increased in both human AVF and in a murine aortocaval fistula model. Knockdown of Sox17 impairs AVF maturation, evidenced by reduced outward remodeling and attenuated wall thickening. Mechanistically, Sox17 deficiency disrupts early SMC proliferation during maturation. These findings suggest that Sox17-mediated transcriptional regulation is needed for venous adaptation to altered hemodynamic forces. Regulation of vascular identity may represent a translationally relevant target to enhance AVF maturation for patients undergoing hemodialysis.
