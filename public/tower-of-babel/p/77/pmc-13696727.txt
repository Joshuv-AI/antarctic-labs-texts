Resorantel: a dual-targeting therapeutic with potent efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus with low potential for drug resistance
Authors: Pengfei She, Shaowei Guo, Dan Xiao, Guanqing Huang, Yiqing Liu, Mengna Li, Lihua Lu, Yelan Hong, Yong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has emerged as a significant public health concern. The biofilm formation capability of MRSA is often associated with high levels of antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic strategies to combat MRSA is urgently warranted. By drug repurposing, resorantel, originally developed as an antiparasitic agent, has demonstrated significantly bactericidal activity against MRSA and its biofilms with effectively inhibitory activity against the emergence of drug resistance. Furthermore, resorantel displayed significant in vivo antibacterial efficacy in both topic and systemic infection models. Mechanistic investigations employing transcriptomics, fluorescent probes, molecular dynamics simulations, and surface plasmon resonance analysis revealed that proton motive force and MurB are likely the potential targets of resorantel. Collectively, this study identifies resorantel as a promising novel antimicrobial agent with potential for use in the treatment of MRSA and its biofilm-related infections.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a versatile Gram-positive bacterium that colonizes approximately one-third of the human population as part of the normal microbiota1,2. It functions both as a commensal organism and opportunistic pathogen, capable of causing a broad spectrum of infectious diseases in humans3,4. In clinical settings, S. aureus has emerged as a significant pathogen associated with both hospital-acquired and community-acquired infections5. Upon entering the bloodstream, S. aureus can disseminate to various organs and lead to severe complications, including pleuropulmonary infections, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, toxic shock syndrome, sepsis, and infections related to intravascular devices6,7. This bacterium demonstrates the ability to adhere to biotic surfaces, such as cardiac tissue, cartilage, and chronic wounds, and both abiotic surfaces, such as medical implants, facilitating the formation of biofilms8,9. Biofilms largely enhance bacterial survival, increase antimicrobial resistance by up to 1000 times compared to their planktonic forms, as the extracellular polymeric substances within biofilms protect bacteria from host immune responses and antibiotic attack4,10–12. Thus, biofilms are often associated with persistent, chronic, and recurrent infections6,13. The emergence of antimicrobial resistance by Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and its biofilms poses a serious threat to both human and animal health, resulting in therapeutic failure, limited treatment options, increased morbidity and mortality13. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic agents with bactericidal properties has become increasingly critical.

Bacterial proton motive force (PMF) is a transmembrane energy-generating force, playing a pivotal role in regulating various biological processes, including ATP synthesis, signal transduction, and macromolecular synthesis14,15. The electric potential (ΔΨ) and the transmembrane proton gradient (ΔpH) are typically two major components of the PMF16–18. Although several potent antimicrobial agents, such as tridecaptin A1, bacaucin-1, halicin, WRK-12, α-mangoside, and ibavaroxone, targeting PMF have been identified, the therapeutic potential of PMF as an antimicrobial target still remains largely underexplored19,20. The bacterial PMF represents a novel and promising target, offering a broad-spectrum strategy for combating antimicrobial resistance21.

Peptidoglycan is a crucial component of the cell wall in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria22,23. The primary function of peptidoglycan is to confer structural rigidity to bacterial cells and protect them from osmotic stress24,25. The peptidoglycan synthesis pathway is a potential antimicrobial target, because the defects of the peptidoglycan layer render cells susceptible to hypotonic environments, leading to cellular swelling and eventual lysis23. MurB is a key enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of peptidoglycan26, which catalyzes the final essential step in the production of UDP-N-acetylmuramic acid, a critical precursor of peptidoglycan27. Thus, MurB represents a promising target, associated with the peptidoglycan synthesis, for the development of antimicrobial agents22,23,28.

In this study, we repurposed the antiparasitic compound resorantel as an antimicrobial agent against MRSA. Resorantel is commonly used for the treatment of Paramphistomum and Moniezia expansa infections in ruminants29,30. It is also employed in the treatment of trematodiasis as well as in Aedes aegypti31. However, no study has reported the antibacterial activity of resorantel. In the present study, we demonstrate that resorantel exhibits both in vitro and in vivo antibacterial potential against MRSA or its biofilms without resistance occurrence. Mechanistic studies indicate the bactericidal effects of resorantel were mediated by PMF disruption as well as cell wall synthesis inhibition. Our study highlights the potential of resorantel in the development of antimicrobial agents against MRSA and its highly resistant phenotypes.

Results

Antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of resorantel against S. aureus

Resorantel is a halogenated salicylamide compound, and its structural formula is illustrated in Fig. 1A. As shown in Table 1, resorantel exhibited potent antimicrobial activity against both MRSA and methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) type strains as well as clinical isolates with the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values ranging from 2 to 4 μg/mL and ≥4 μg/mL, respectively. Meanwhile, resorantel also demonstrated antimicrobial efficacy against S. epidermidis and vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE), with the MIC and MBC values of 2–8 μg/mL and 8–32 μg/mL, respectively. However, no antibacterial activity was observed by Gram-negative bacilli. Like conventional antibiotics VAN and daptomycin (DAP), resorantel also produced distinct growth inhibition zones (Fig. 1B, C). Subsequently, we assessed the antimicrobial activity of resorantel by measuring the diameter of inhibition zones across a concentration gradient and observed a clear dose-dependent increase in inhibitory efficacy (Fig. S1). Resorantel could significantly reduce the viability of S. aureus MW2 and ATCC 43300 in TSB at the concentration of 1-2×MIC (Fig. 1D). Time-kill assay results demonstrated that resorantel exhibited rapid and potent bactericidal activity at a concentration of 4×MIC, achieving a ≥3 log₁₀ reduction in viable bacterial counts within 12 h. However, this bactericidal effect was not sustained beyond 12 h, as bacterial regrowth was observed (Fig. 1E). In accordance, the viable cell proportion of S. aureus was markedly reduced after treatment with 2×MIC of resorantel, as assessed by SYTO 9/PI dual staining (Fig. 1F). Further, the development of resistance occurrence by resorantel was evaluated using both multi-step and one-step induction methods. As shown in Fig. 1G, resorantel exhibited extremely low propensity to induce resistance mutation, even after 15 passages in sub-MICs. In contrast, ciprofloxacin (CIP) exhibited a 32-fold increase in MIC at the endpoint. Similarly, one-step resistance induction assay also indicated the lower resistance induction rate of resorantel compared to CIP (Fig. 1H). The CIP-induced highly resistant S. aureus strains retained susceptibility to resorantel at a concentration of 2 μg/mL (Fig. 1I). In contrast, resorantel-induced S. aureus strains exhibited no alterations in susceptibility to ciprofloxacin, vancomycin, or amikacin, indicating the absence of cross-resistance among these antimicrobial agents (Fig. 1J). Mechanisms of action

To investigate the underlying antimicrobial mechanisms, we firstly examined the ultrastructural changes in S. aureus following resorantel treatment by using transmission electron microscope (TEM). As shown in Fig. 3A, resorantel induced cellular collapse and decreased cell density, which suggested that resorantel may target the membrane or cell wall of S. aureus. Although no membrane permeability was observed by SYTOX Green probe after treatment with resorantel (Figs. 3B and S4A), significant changes of ΔΨ leading to membrane depolarization were observed by DiSC3(5) probe (Figs. 3C and S4B). The PMF consists primarily of the ΔΨ and ΔpH32. However, only moderate disruption of ΔpH was evident by BCECF-AM probe (Figs. 3D and S4C). In accordance, the antimicrobial effects of resorantel were slightly changed under varied pH-adjusted mediums (Figs. 3E and S4D). Thus, resorantel dissipates the PMF by primarily targeting the ΔΨ. As reported by Bonnie et al.33, PMF is a key energy source for ATP synthase-dependent processes. Consistently, resorantel treatment resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction in intracellular ATP levels in our study (Figs. 3F and S4E). As we expected, a decrease in ROS production was observed after the treatment, probably also due to the energy metabolism inhibition activity of resorantel (Figs. 3G and S4F). Laurdan staining revealed decreased GP values after the resorantel treatment, indicating the occurrence of membrane disorder (Fig. 3H). As reported by Kyle et al.34,35, aminoglycosides typically enter bacterial cells via active transport driven by the electrochemical gradient, including PMF, across the cell membrane. Resorantel did not exhibit synergy with conventional antibiotics (Fig. S5) but demonstrated antagonism against aminoglycosides (Fig. 3I, J). The antagonism effects were also verified by time-growth assay (Fig. 3K, L). This is consistent with our findings that resorantel depolarizes the cell membrane, disrupts the PMF, and reduces ATP synthesis, thereby limiting the entry of aminoglycosides into bacterial cells. As reported by Li et al.36, the depletion of the PMF and ATP may result in the impaired function of efflux pumps. Thus, we further investigated the effects of resorantel against S. aureus efflux pumps. As shown in Fig. S6, resorantel is a more potent efflux pump inhibitor than the known compound CCCP. To identify the specific membrane targets responsible for resorantel-induced PMF disruption, we performed a competitive inhibition assay using exogenous supplementation of membrane components. Four cell membrane components—phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), and cardiolipin (CL)—were incorporated into the assay. In addition, competitive inhibition assays, performed by adding the peptidoglycan constituents, N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) or N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM), also resulted in a complete loss of antibacterial activity (Fig. 4A), which indicated an additional mechanism of resorantel involves the inhibition of peptidoglycan synthesis. Transcriptome analysis revealed that resorantel significantly up/down-regulated the expression of genes associated with energy metabolism as well as certain genes related to the cell membrane and cell wall (Fig. 4B). KEGG enrichment indicated that these differentially expressed genes were primarily involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis, ABC transporter regulation, bacterial secretion systems, and various metabolic pathways (Fig. 4C). Further, these differentially expressed genes were confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 5A). To explore the potential targets related to peptidoglycan biosynthesis, molecular docking was conducted between resorantel and key enzymes. As shown in Fig. 5B, MurB (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine-enolpyruvate reductase) exhibited the strongest potential binding affinity to resorantel. While other enzymes, including GlmS (glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase), GlmM (phosphoglucosamine mutase), GlmU (glucosamine-1-phosphate acetyltransferase), and MurA (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase), demonstrated relatively high binding energies. The stable complex formation between resorantel and MurB is predominantly mediated by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 5C). To assess the functional relevance of this interaction, we examined MurB expression levels via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). As shown in Fig. 5D, resorantel treatment led to a statistically significant downregulation of MurB mRNA expression. Subsequently, recombinant MurB was heterologously expressed, purified to homogeneity, and subjected to surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis to quantify its binding affinity for resorantel. The resulting equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was determined to be 1.92 μM (Fig. 5E). Meanwhile, the catalytic activity of MurB could also be significantly inhibited by resorantel treatment (Fig. 5F). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that resorantel may target the MurB enzyme to inhibit the cell wall synthesis. Low cytotoxicity by resorantel

Resorantel showed no hemolytic activity against human erythrocytes even at the concentrations up to 512 μg/mL (Fig. 6A). All tested cell lines (HepG2, RAW264.7, HSF, HaCaT) were well-tolerated to resorantel, showing minimal viability inhibition and IC₅₀ values above 30 μg/mL (Fig. 6B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed no significant increase in the proportion of early or late apoptotic HaCaT cells following resorantel treatment (Fig. 6C). In consistence, Calcein-AM/PI staining also revealed none significant apoptotic changes in HaCaT cells after the treatment (Fig. 6D, E). The scratch wound healing assay demonstrated that treatment with 2× MIC of resorantel enhanced the migration capacity of HaCaT cells (Fig. 6F, G). Additionally, resorantel exhibited a low risk of hERG channel inhibition (IC₅₀ > 10 μM), which stands in sharp contrast to the high potency of cisapride (IC₅₀ = 13.7 nM) (Fig. 6H, I). Effective in vivo antibacterial efficacy by resorantel

Prior to evaluating the in vivo antimicrobial efficacy of resorantel, its capacity to eradicate intracellular S. aureus was first assessed at the cellular level. A well-characterized intracellular bactericidal model was established using HEUVC cells infected with MRSA strain ATCC 43300. As illustrated in Fig. S7, treatment with resorantel at its MIC resulted in a statistically significant reduction in intracellular bacterial burden—as quantified by CFU enumeration—relative to the untreated control group. These findings demonstrate that resorantel effectively penetrates mammalian cell membranes and exerts potent intracellular antibacterial activity against S. aureus.

The in vivo antibacterial activity of resorantel was evaluated using both the subcutaneous abscess model, the wound infection model, and the peritonitis-induced sepsis model, respectively (Fig. 7). In the abscess model, a single administration of resorantel at a dose of 30 mg/kg resulted in a marked reduction in abscess size compared to the vehicle group (Figs. 8A and S8A). Representative images of viable bacterial counts further confirmed the potent antibacterial activity of resorantel (Figs. 8B and S8B). As illustrated in Figs. 8C and S8C, the number of viable bacteria was significantly reduced following the treatment. In the wound infection model, improved wound healing was observed in the group treated with the ointment containing 2% (wt/wt) resorantel or fusidic acid (Figs. 8D and S8D) and the area of the infected wound also showed an obvious reduction (Figs. 8E and S8E). As shown in Figs. 8F and S8F, the bacterial load in the resorantel -treated group was significantly decreased. H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that resorantel could significantly reduce inflammatory infiltration and the expression levels of cytokines (including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), compared with the vehicle group (Figs. 8G and S8G). Further, a peritonitis-induced sepsis model was employed to evaluate the systemic antimicrobial efficacy of resorantel in vivo. As we expected, resorantel significantly decreased the bacterial burdens in livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys after 24 h of treatment (Figs. 8J and S8J). Next, hematological parameters, serum biomarkers, and histopathological changes were assessed to evaluate the in vivo safety profile of resorantel. Resorantel-treated groups showed no significant differences in key hematological and biochemical parameters compared to the vehicle group, including WBC, N%, RBC, HGB, PLT, and the serum biomarkers of ALT, urea, and CK (Fig. S8A). In addition, histopathological analysis of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney by H&E staining revealed no notable abnormalities (Fig. S9B). These findings collectively suggest that the therapeutic regimen of resorantel exhibits a favorable in vivo safety profile.

Discussion

In this study, resorantel disrupted the PMF of S. aureus by altering the membrane potential. Specifically, resorantel impaired the PMF, a critical energy source for ATP synthesis via ATP synthase. Following disruption of the PMF, bacterial ATP production was significantly reduced. Additionally, treatment with resorantel led to a decrease in ROS generation. This reduction is likely attributable to the inhibition of energy metabolism, which in turn suppresses ROS formation. Notably, this mechanism contrasts with that of many conventional antibiotics, which rely on ROS induction to exert bactericidal effects. However, under anaerobic conditions or when bacterial metabolism is inhibited, the reduced capacity to generate ROS can diminish the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics and potentially contribute to the emergence of drug resistance16,35. Resorantel bypasses this mechanism by directly inhibiting energy production and disrupting cell wall integrity, suggesting that it may be particularly effective in complex infection environments such as abscesses and hypoxic regions within biofilms.

Given the escalating antimicrobial resistance of S. aureus, there is an imperative need for the development of novel antimicrobial agents. Antimicrobials that target bacterial cell wall synthesis have historically represented the most successful class of antibiotics. However, the majority of drug development efforts have focused on later stages of this process26. For example, currently established targets include β-lactams (penicillins and cephalosporins), which inhibit transpeptidases, also known as penicillin-binding proteins, and thereby prevent cross-linking of peptidoglycan chains37. Glycopeptides, such as VAN, bind directly to peptidoglycan precursors (D-Ala-D-Ala termini), blocking transpeptidase activity38. Lipopeptides, such as daptomycin, disrupt the later stages of peptidoglycan synthesis and interfere with membrane potential by binding to the bacterial cell membrane. Drug resistance to existing cell wall synthesis inhibitors is increasingly prevalent, posing a significant challenge in antimicrobial therapy39. This present study is shifting toward earlier biochemical steps in this pathway, aiming to identify novel therapeutic agents that are unaffected by existing resistance mechanisms. The early stages of bacterial cell wall synthesis, particularly the biosynthesis of peptidoglycan precursors (UDP-MurNAc-pentapeptide), are mediated by the MurA-F family of enzymes27. Thus, targeting MurB represents a promising direction at this research frontier.

Resorantel specifically targets MurB, an enzyme that is essential for bacterial survival and for which there are currently no clinically approved drugs available40. Targeting MurB suggests that resorantel may possess the potential to combat multidrug-resistant bacterial strains, particularly MRSA. Because the murB geneis crucial for bacterial growth, and its inhibition or inactivation directly leads to bacterial death. Importantly, MurB is a bacterial-specific enzyme with no homologues in human cells40. In this study, the inhibition of MurB by resorantel was evaluated through an in vitro assay, revealing an IC50 value of approximately 100 μM. Therefore, drugs targeting MurB are expected to exhibit low cytotoxicity and offer a high therapeutic index. However, the existing evidence is primarily derived from in vitro biochemical assays, which are insufficient to conclusively establish MurB as the direct intracellular target of resorantel. Rigorous target validation, therefore, requires complementary genetic approaches. Specifically, future studies should generate MurB-overexpressing strains and assess their susceptibility to resorantel; a reduced sensitivity would support target engagement. Conversely, strains with MurB knockdown or conditional knockout are expected to exhibit heightened sensitivity to resorantel. Furthermore, orthogonal biophysical methods—including cellular thermal shift assay and drug affinity-responsive target stability assay —can be employed to directly confirm intracellular binding of resorantel to MurB at the protein level.

Resorantel exerts its antibacterial effect through a dual mechanism involving disruption of PMF as well as bacterial cell wall synthesis. Compared to conventional antibiotics, agents employing dual targets demonstrate superior bactericidal efficacy, rapid action, and minimal potential for resistance development or cross-resistance17,21. For example, DAP has emerged as one of the “last-line” therapeutic agents for the management of severe infections, including bacteremia and endocarditis, caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens such as MRSA and VRE. The potent bactericidal activity and low propensity for drug resistance associated with DAP are primarily attributed to its unique and efficient dual mechanism of action, which indirectly disrupts cell wall synthesis and directly impairs PMF41. However, it remains unclear whether the two antibacterial mechanisms of resorantel operate independently and in parallel or exhibit a hierarchical relationship—specifically, whether disruption of the PMF indirectly impairs MurB activity within bacterial cells. Furthermore, the temporal sequence of these mechanisms during the bactericidal process has yet to be established. To address these questions, future studies could employ genetically engineered strains—such as PMF-restoration mutants or MurB-overexpression mutants—to dissect the individual contribution of each mechanism to resorantel sensitivity. Complementary time-kill kinetic analyses across a range of subinhibitory concentrations may further clarify which mechanism predominates under clinically relevant dosing conditions. A mechanistic understanding of the interplay between PMF disruption and MurB inhibition will inform rational optimization of resorantel therapeutic application in clinical practice.

In this study, resorantel exhibits significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus and demonstrates no resistance development with a favorable safety profile. Resorantel exhibited no antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacteria, likely attributable to the structural complexity of their cell envelope—particularly the presence of an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharides, outer membrane proteins, and a thin peptidoglycan layer25. Lipopolysaccharides has been well documented to impede antibiotic penetration and contribute substantially to intrinsic resistance in Gram-negative organisms42. Collectively, these structural and functional features of the Gram-negative cell envelope may account for the observed lack of antimicrobial efficacy of resorantel against this bacterial group. Furthermore, compared to resorantel, the small molecules of resorantel analogues exhibit enhanced anti-biofilm activity. These findings suggest that resorantel represents a promising novel antibacterial agent with potential for the treatment of MRSA-related infections.

Materials and methods

Strains, culture conditions, and chemicals

Type strains of Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606, E. faecalis (ATCC 29212andATCC 51299), E. faecium ATCC 19434 and S. aureus (ATCC 43300, USA300, ATCC 29213, ATCC 25923) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) center. S. aureus RJ-2, RN4220, and MW2 were given by Min Li (Shanghai Jiaotong University, Shanghai, P.R. China). Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 12228 and RP62A were provided by Di Qu (Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University). Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 was provided by Minqiang Qiao (College of Life Sciences of Nankai University). E. faecium clinical isolates of SRYEFM2 and SRYEFM9 were provided by Liangyi Xie (Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital, Hunan, China). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922、Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603、Vancomycin resistant E. faecium U101 and other Staphylococci clinical strains were collected from the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University and identified by both VETIK 2 Compact (bioMérieux, France) and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (Bruker, USA). Gram-positive cocci of Enterococcus and Staphylococci strains were grown in brain-heart infusion (BHI) and trypsin soybean broth (TSB), respectively, while Gram-negative bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth. All culture media were autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min before use. All bacteria were propagated at 37 °C, 200 rpm. Cell lines of HaCaT, HepG2, HSF, and RAW264.7 were cultured in RPMI medium or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The chemicals used in the present study were mainly purchased from MedChem Express (NJ, United States). For in vitro experiments, all compounds were prepared in distilled water or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For in vivo experiments, compounds were formulated using a solvent consisting of Cremophor EL and ethanol in a ratio of 1:1 (vol/vol)17,43.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

The MIC values were determined using the standard microdilution method in accordance with the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. S. aureus was cultured to the middle stage, and the bacterial suspension was diluted with Mueller-Hinton (MH) broth to an approximate concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Then, added 50 μL of the bacterial suspension to the 96-well plate and neutralized 50 μL of resorantel at a specific concentration. Resorantel was dissolved in DMSO and then diluted with MH broth. Incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 16–18 h. MIC was defined as the minimum concentration that inhibited the growth of visible bacteria. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that resulted in no visible bacterial growth after 24 h. In addition, the MICs of resorantel against S. aureus ATCC 43300 at different pH values were determined by adding NaOH or HCl. Meanwhile, the MIC of resorantel against S. aureus ATCC 43300 was also determined under various environmental conditions by incorporating specific concentrations of artificial urine, saliva, and serum43,44.

Time-killing assay

The log-phased bacterial suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland (McF) and subsequently diluted 100-fold in fresh MH broth. Following this, resorantel stock solution was added to 5 mL of the bacterial suspension to the final concentrations ranging from 1/4 to 4×MIC, with 0.1% (vol/vol) DMSO as a control. After incubation at 37 °C, 180 rpm, the number of viable bacterial cells was enumerated at the time points of 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h, respectively, by using the plate counting method45.

Biofilm inhibition and eradication

The crystal violet and XTT [2,3-bis-(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide] reagents were utilized to assess the impact of resorantel on both biofilm formation and disruption, respectively. For biofilm inhibition assay, S. aureus was cultured overnight, then diluted 1:100 with TSB, and transferred into a 96-well microtiter plate containing two-fold serial dilutions of resorantel. Following incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, non-adherent bacterial cells were removed by washing with 1×PBS (pH = 7.4). The remaining biofilm was stained with 0.25% (wt/vol) crystal violet for 15 min. After washing and solubilizing in 95% ethanol for an additional 20 min, the total biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the absorbance (A) at 570 nm. In addition, to evaluate the biofilm metabolic activity, 100 µL of XTT solution (0.2 mg/mL) supplemented with phenazine methosulfate (0.2 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by an incubation period of 3 h in the dark, and the A490 nm was recorded. For the pre-formed biofilm eradication, 200 µL of the overnight cultured bacterial suspension was seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h to facilitate biofilm development. Planktonic cells were subsequently removed through PBS washing, and 200 µL of indicated concentrations of resorantel were introduced into each well. After an additional incubation period of 24 h, the remaining biofilms were assessed by crystal violet and XTT reduction assay, respectively, as described above46.

Cell viability determination by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) kit

A cell viability assay employing CCK-8 (Tongren, Japan) was conducted to evaluate the cytotoxicity of resorantel against human cell lines. Briefly, cell lines of HaCaT, HSF and RAW264.7 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10%fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The adherent cells were treated with resorantel at specified concentrations for a duration of 24 h in 96-well plates. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After an additional incubation period of 3–4 h, the A450nm was measured47.
