Sensitizing carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae using a compact Cascade-Cas3 system
Authors: Jun Huang, Jiahui Chen, Luyao Huang, Shaofu Qiu, Ligui Wang, Xinying Du, Chao Wang, Zhengquan Yuan, Ping Li, Hongbin Song, Hongbo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKlebsiella pneumoniae is a ‘superbug’ characterized by high virulence and carbapenem resistance. CRISPR-Cas3 is capable of performing large-scale deletions in bacterial genomes. The purpose of this study is to sensitize Klebsiella pneumoniae A324 to carbapenem antibiotics by eliminating the multidrug resistance plasmid pA324-IMP using a compact Cascade-Cas3 system.MethodsRecombinant plasmids were constructed using molecular cloning techniques. Three sgRNAs targeting different positions within the blaIMP−38 gene were designed. Recombinant plasmids were separately transformed into host bacteria carrying blaIMP38 genes. The elimination efficiency of each CRISPR-Cas3 system target was determined by PCR and qPCR. Fluorescence confocal microscopy was used to observe the fluorescence intensity. Bacterial drug susceptibility phenotypes were determined using E-test drug sensitivity strips.ResultsIn this study, we successfully constructed the high copy number plasmid, the fluorescent plasmid, and the recombinant CRISPR plasmids. PCR and qPCR results demonstrated that the CRISPR-Cas3 system could efficiently eliminate blaIMP−38 genes, with pCas3-T3 showing the highest elimination efficiency. Fluorescence confocal microscopy indicated that the fluorescence intensity of the experimental group significantly decreased compared to the control group. Additionally, drug susceptibility tests revealed that the resistance of Klebsiella pneumoniae to imipenem and meropenem was significantly reduced. Furthermore, CRISPR-Cas3 could enhance the effectiveness of meropenem in killing Klebsiella pneumoniae A324.ConclusionIn conclusion, this work demonstrates that the compact Cascade-Cas3 system can serve as a novel tool to eliminate the resistance gene blaIMP−38 and restore the sensitivity of host bacteria to antibiotics. This strategy shows great potential to combat the spread of the blaIMP−38 gene in Klebsiella pneumoniae and holds significant application value in the field of combating drug-resistant bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10123-026-00846-9.

Introduction

The global prevalence of antibiotic resistance has become an urgent public health problem (Spellberg et al. 2008). Klebsiella pneumoniae is a ‘superbug’ characterized by high virulence and carbapenem resistance (Pu et al. 2023). Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae has been increasingly prevalent and rapidly spreading in China in recent years. National surveillance data (CHINET) demonstrated that the resistance rate of Klebsiella pneumoniae to meropenem increased from 2.9% in 2005 to 26.0% in 2023 (Pu et al. 2023). It is often associated with multiple drug resistance determinants, such as extended-spectrum β-lactamases and carbapenemase genes (Zohra et al. 2021). Infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae impose a significant economic burden and result in extremely high mortality rates (Bartsch et al. 2017). Plasmids, serving as mobile genetic elements (MGEs), promote the spread of antibiotic resistance genes in Klebsiella pneumoniae and other Gram-negative bacteria (Buckner et al. 2018). In Klebsiella pneumoniae A324, IncHI5 plasmids carrying blaIMP play a crucial role in the global transmission of antimicrobial resistance (Liang et al. 2018).

Therefore, novel antibacterial strategies are urgently needed to combat antibiotic resistance. The CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats)-Cas (CRISPR-associated) system can induce double-strand DNA breaks on target DNA and facilitate the removal of multidrug-resistant plasmids, and has been applied to combat antibiotic resistance (Buckner et al. 2018). The CRISPR-Cas9 system has been widely used for gene editing in various organisms, especially in eukaryotes (Fagerlund et al. 2015). However, its application in prokaryotes is quite limited, partly because the expression of Cas9 in different hosts is often affected by various factors, including host codon usage bias, promoter compatibility, protein toxicity, and restriction-modification systems (Xu et al. 2019, Pyne et al. 2016, Baker et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2018). Additionally, the exogenous CRISPR-Cas9 system is challenging to exploit due to its large size and cytotoxicity for most prokaryotes (Zheng et al. 2019). The off-target effects of the CRISPR-Cas9 system have also been a bottleneck in its biomedical and clinical applications (Zhang et al. 2015). Consequently, the application of the CRISPR-Cas9 system in some prokaryotes still faces many challenges (Xu et al. 2019 ). Endogenous CRISPR-Cas systems, which are widely distributed in prokaryotes, are emerging as a new type of CRISPR system for gene editing (Hidalgo-Cantabrana et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2021). Cascade-Cas3 system has shown great potential for large genetic element deletion compared to CRISPR-Cas9 (Csörgő et al. 2020). The most significant difference between CRISPR-Cas3 and CRISPR-Cas9 lies in the cleavage of the target DNA strand. The CRISPR-Cas3 system cuts and degrades DNA through 3’-to-5’ exonucleases, whereas the CRISPR-Cas9 system only cuts DNA (Wu et al. 2021). The CRISPR-Cas3 system can further improve the efficiency of DNA cleavage by interfering with DNA repair processes (Wu et al. 2021).

In this work, we first verified the effect of the CRISPR-Cas3 system on the elimination of multidrug-resistant plasmids and demonstrated its application value in controlling the spread of blaIMP−38 resistance. The results showed that the compact Cascade-Cas3 system can effectively eliminate multidrug-resistant IncHI5 plasmids carrying blaIMP and identify the most efficient target. After the elimination of multidrug-resistant plasmids, Klebsiella pneumoniae regained sensitivity to carbapenem antibiotics. In conclusion, this study developed a compact Cascade-Cas3 system for genome editing in Klebsiella pneumoniae, demonstrating significant application value in combating drug-resistant bacteria.

Materiais and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

All strains and plasmids used or constructed in this study are listed in Supplementary Information Table S1. E. coli Top10 was transformed with plasmid pUC57-blaIMP−38 to serve as a model drug-resistant strain. E. coli MG1655 with the SFGFP expression plasmid expressed bright green fluorescence at a wavelength of 475 nm. Klebsiella pneumoniae A324 is a wild strain containing the blaIMP−38 gene (Liang et al. 2018). The plasmid pCas3cRh was a gift from Joseph Bondy-Denomy (Addgene plasmid #133773; http://n2t.net/addgene:133773 (pCas3cRh)(Plasmid ); RRID: Addgene_133773) (Csörgő et al. 2020). E. coli and K. pneumoniae were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (2 g yeast extract, 4 g NaCl, and 4 g tryptone per 400 mL) or on LB agar plates (LB supplemented with 6 g agar per 400 mL) at 37 °C (Tao et al. 2023). If necessary, the following antibiotics were supplemented at the specified concentrations: streptomycin (50 µg/mL), ampicillin (100 µg/mL), gentamicin (50 µg/mL), and meropenem (4 µg/mL).

Plasmid construction

All oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Supplementary Information Table S2. The recombinant CRISPR-Cas3 plasmid was constructed via molecular cloning. First, three single crRNAs targeting distinct regions of the blaIMP−38 gene were designed: crRNA-T1 (positions 405–438), crRNA-T2 (positions 327–360), and crRNA-T3 (positions 290–323). The corresponding oligonucleotide pairs (T1-F/R, T2-F/R, T3-F/R) were synthesized and used for plasmid construction. These sgRNA sequences, each 34 nucleotides in length, were synthesized as oligonucleotides with sticky ends. Specifically, GAAAC or G was added to the 5’ end of the forward oligonucleotides, and GCGAC or G was added to the 5’ end of the reverse oligonucleotides. The paired oligonucleotides were then annealed and ligated into BsaI-digested pCas3 using a rapid ligation kit, resulting in pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into the corresponding host cells, incubated overnight at 37 °C, and selected on plates containing 50 µg/mL gentamicin. The spacer sequences were confirmed by bacterial colony PCR using primers pCas3-F/R, followed by sequencing.

The blaIMP−38 fragment was obtained from NCBI (GenBank accession number MF344566) (Liang et al. 2018 ). This fragment was amplified using primers blaIMP−38 (EcoRI)-F and blaIMP−38 (HindIII)-R, then digested with EcoRI and HindIII, and ligated into the corresponding site in pUC57, yielding pUC57-blaIMP−38. This high-copy model drug-resistant plasmid was transformed into E. coli Top10 to create the model drug-resistant strain.

For seamless cloning, the blaIMP−38 fragment was amplified using primers SF-blaIMP−38-F and SF-blaIMP−38-R. The fragment was digested with EcoRI. The linearized pR65S-SFGFP and blaIMP−38 fragments were ligated using a seamless cloning kit, yielding pR65S-SFGFP-blaIMP−38. The constructed plasmid was transformed into E. coli MG1655 to generate fluorescent drug-resistant model bacteria.

Bacterial transformations

Transformations of E. coli and K. pneumoniae were conducted using standard electroporation protocols. Fifteen milliliters of overnight cultures were centrifuged and washed twice with an equal volume of pure water, followed by two washes with an equal volume of 10% glycerol, and finally resuspended in 1 mL of 10% glycerol. One hundred microliters of the resulting competent cells were electroporated with 1000 ng of plasmid using an electroporation system set to 3000 V, 200 Ω, and 25 µF with a single pulse, using 2 mm gap electroporation cuvettes (BTX). The electroporated cells were cultured in antibiotic-free SOC medium at 37 °C for 1 h, then spread on LB agar medium containing 50 µg/mL gentamicin, and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The empty pCas3 plasmid was used as a negative control.

PCR and qPCR experiment

After transformation, five colonies were randomly picked to evaluate the elimination efficiency in each experiment. The pCas3 plasmid was detected by PCR with primers pCas3-F/R. Simultaneously, primers blaIMP−38-F/R were used to detect colonies with blaIMP−38 deletion by PCR. Host strains transformed with the empty vector pCas3 plasmid served as positive controls. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

To further analyze the eradication efficiency of pCas3-T1/T2/T3 on the blaIMP−38 gene, the copy numbers of pUC57-blaIMP−38 and pA324-IMP at each time point were determined by qPCR. Bacterial genomic nucleic acids were extracted using the Maga Bio Pathogen DNA/RNA Purification Kit (Bioer, Hangzhou, China). The qPCR reaction was performed using a probe qPCR mix (TaKaRa, Beijing, China) with specific primers and probes for the blaIMP−38 gene and the chromosome 16 S ribosomal RNA (16 S rRNA) gene. All reactions were repeated five times. The copy number changes of pUC57-blaIMP−38 and pA324-IMP in the experimental group were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Drug sensitive experiment

The drug susceptibility E-test strip (Liofilchem, Italy) and Phoenix drug susceptibility plate were used to detect changes in drug susceptibility phenotype in the control group and the experimental group. According to the guidelines of the Institute for Clinical Research and Laboratory Standards (Tao et al. 2023), the MIC values of imipenem and meropenem in the control and experimental groups were compared. The bacterial solution of the control and experimental groups was spread on LB plates containing meropenem, the bactericidal effect of meropenem was evaluated by bacterial colony counting after CRISPR-Cas3–mediated elimination of the resistance plasmid.

Observation of fluorescence signal in drug-resistant model bacteria

Single colonies of bacteria were inoculated into 5 mL LB liquid medium and cultured at 37 °C with shaking mid-logarithmic phase (approximately 3–4 h). The bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 2 min at room temperature, then resuspended in PBS and washed twice to remove impurities. The bacterial suspension was diluted with PBS to 10^8 CFU/mL. Ten microliters of bacterial droplets were placed on the center of a glass slide, passed quickly over an alcohol lamp flame twice to accelerate liquid evaporation, and immediately covered with a layer of sealing liquid. A coverslip was placed over the bacteria without trapping bubbles between the glass surfaces. The morphology and number of bacteria were observed under a confocal microscope, selecting the appropriate field of view and magnification. Scanning observation was performed under the corresponding fluorescence channel.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Student’s t-test was used to calculate P values. The bars represent the average values of three biological replicates, and the error bars show the standard deviation. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001).

RESULTS

Validation of the effect of different targets on eradicating multidrug resistance gene blaIMP−38

The crRNA coding sequence of pCas3 was replaced by a 34 nt oligonucleotide of the sgRNA sequence to obtain pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3. The high-copy plasmid pUC57 was selected as the backbone to construct the high-copy mode drug-resistant plasmid pUC57-blaIMP−38. pR65S-SFGFP was used as the plasmid backbone to construct the fluorescent drug-resistant plasmid pR65S-SFGFP-blaIMP−38.

To assess the elimination efficacy of the three targets on plasmid pUC57-blaIMP−38 in E. coli and plasmid pA324-IMP in Klebsiella pneumoniae, five single colonies in the experimental group and the control group were randomly selected, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A, the natural drug-resistant plasmid pA324-IMP was eliminated by all three targets. Similarly, Fig. 1B shows that the high-copy mode drug-resistant plasmid pUC57-blaIMP−38 was eliminated by the three targets. In contrast, due to the lack of sgRNA guidance, the Cas3 nuclease in the control group showed no specific clearance of blaIMP−38. Changes in Klebsiella pneumoniae A324 resistance phenotype

One of the greatest challenges in the clinical treatment of blaIMP−38-producing bacteria is their resistance to carbapenems. An E-test susceptibility test strip was used to detect changes in the MIC values of imipenem and meropenem after pA324-IMP was eliminated by CRISPR-Cas3. As shown in Fig. 2A, the resistance of bacteria to imipenem decreased significantly. The average MIC value of the control group was 3.7 µg/mL, whereas the average MIC values of pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3 were reduced to approximately 0.38 µg/mL. Similarly, Fig. 2B shows that the resistance of bacteria to meropenem decreased significantly. The average MIC value of the control group was 30 µg/mL, while the average MIC values of pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3 were reduced to approximately 0.064 µg/mL.Fig. 2 Elimination of blaIMP-38-mediated drug resistance by CRISPR-Cas3 systems. After the pA324-IMP plasmid was eradicated by the CRISPR-Cas3 system, the bacterial solutions of the experimental group and the control group were spread on LB agar plates containing meropenem. Analysis of the eradication efficiency of the CRISPR-Cas3 system on pA324-IMP in Klebsiella pneumoniae and pUC57-blaIMP38 in Escherichia coli

To more accurately evaluate elimination efficacy, the relative fold change of the natural drug-resistant plasmid pA324-IMP and the high-copy drug-resistant plasmid pUC57-blaIMP38 at each time point was determined by qPCR. Significant differences in the relative fold change of plasmid pA324-IMP or pUC57-blaIMP−38 can be observed between pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the eradication rate of the three targets on pA324-IMP was extremely fast. At 2 h, the eradication efficiencies of pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3 were 99.1%, 99.6%, and 99.4%, respectively. The eradication efficiency of pCas3-T2 and pCas3-T3 reached 99.9% at 16 h and continued until 32 h. However, the eradication efficiency of pCas3-T1 only reached 99.8% at 32 h. Therefore, compared with pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2 and pCas3-T3 showed more efficient eradication of pA324-IMP.Fig. 3 The relative fold change of plasmid pA324-IMP or pUC57-blaIMP-38 at each time point. As shown in Fig. 3B, at 2 h, the eradication efficiencies of pCas3-T1, pCas3-T2, and pCas3-T3 on pUC57-blaIMP38 were 93.9%, 55.3%, and 99.5%, respectively. The eradication efficiency of pCas3-T3 reached 99.9% at 8 h and continued until 32 h. Therefore, compared with pCas3-T1 and pCas3-T2, pCas3-T3 exhibited more efficient eradication of pUC57-blaIMP38.

The above experimental results demonstrate that pCas3-T3 shows extremely high clearance efficiency in eradicating both the natural drug-resistant plasmid pA324-IMP and the high-copy drug-resistant plasmid pUC57-blaIMP38.

Discussion

Klebsiella pneumoniae resistance to carbapenems has emerged as a serious problem hindering the treatment of bacterial infections (Goel et al. 2021). Many drug resistance genes are horizontally transferred through various mobile genetic elements (MGEs). For example, plasmids are one of the carriers of drug resistance genes, which can promote the spread of antibiotic resistance genes between bacterial hosts (Jian et al. 2021). Efficient strategies are urgently needed to combat multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, and the eradication of drug-resistant plasmids may be a promising method to eradicate bacterial resistance and make bacteria sensitive to antibiotics (Buckner et al. 2018). The CRISPR-Cas system is an adaptive immune system of bacteria that can target the cleavage of double-stranded DNA (Choi and Lee 2016). According to the characteristics of the CRISPR-Cas system, it provides an effective strategy for the targeted eradication of multidrug-resistant plasmids and assists antibiotics in killing drug-resistant bacteria (Tao et al. 2023).

In this study, we exploited a compact Cascade-Cas3 system to eliminate high-copy resistant plasmids and natural resistant plasmids, providing an efficient strategy to tackle antibiotic resistance in Klebsiella pneumoniae. According to a report from Nature Methods (Csörgő et al. 2020), the minimal Cascade-Cas3 system (type I-C), programmed using a single crRNA, was optimized to produce nearly 100% deletions. In contrast, Cas9 produced smaller deletions and point mutations. The CRISPR-Cas9 system uses a single DNA endonuclease, Cas9, to recognize double-stranded DNA substrates and employs a unique nuclease domain (HNH or RuvC) to cleave each strand (Jiang and Doudna 2017). The double-strand breaks created by Cas9 are then repaired by error-prone non-homologous end joining (Lieber 2010) or high-fidelity homology-directed repair (San Filippo et al. 2008). However, CRISPR-Cas3 generates a single-stranded gap at the DNA sequence, followed by continuous exonuclease degradation of the target strand, whereas CRISPR-Cas9 only causes DNA double-strand breaks without additional degradation (Wu et al. 2021). Additionally, CRISPR-Cas3 can further improve the efficiency of DNA cleavage by interfering with DNA repair mechanisms (Zhou et al. 2023). Therefore, CRISPR-Cas3 can cleave double-stranded DNA more efficiently than CRISPR-Cas9.

Currently, only a few studies have reported the use of the CRISPR-Cas3 system to eliminate bacterial resistance. Zhou et al. exploited a conjugative endogenous CRISPR-Cas3 system to tackle multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (Zhou et al. 2023). In this study, we utilized a compact Cascade-Cas3 system to eradicate natural resistant plasmids, high-copy resistant plasmids, and fluorescent resistant plasmids, respectively, and restore the sensitivity of resistant bacteria to antibiotics. The off-target effects of the CRISPR-Cas9 system have always been a bottleneck for gene editing (Zhang et al. 2015), especially in eukaryotes, where different target sites can lead to differences in CRISPR cleavage efficiency and varying degrees of off-target effects (Tao et al. 2023). In order to improve the activity and specificity of sgRNA, considerable time is needed to design the optimal sgRNA (Doench et al. 2016). The off-target effect has long been a major bottleneck restricting the biomedical and clinical application of the CRISPR-Cas9 system. In eukaryotic cells, abundant DNA repair pathways enable the survival and detection of cells with off-target chromosomal mutations. However, prokaryotes represented by Klebsiella pneumoniae lack efficient DNA double-strand break repair mechanisms. Once the CRISPR nuclease mistakenly targets and cleaves the bacterial chromosome, it will cause irreversible chromosome degradation and direct bacterial death. Therefore, any off-target targeting event will lead to cell lethality rather than the formation of editable and detectable surviving clones, which means that off-target mutations cannot be detected in surviving edited strains in prokaryotic systems (Westra et al. 2014, Stern et al. 2010).

Notably, a large number of studies have confirmed that the Type I CRISPR-Cas3 system has higher specificity and lower off-target risk than Cas9 (Dolan et al. 2019, Morisaka et al. 2019, Selle et al. 2020). The multi-subunit Cascade complex requires more stringent base-pairing matching and specific PAM recognition, with extremely low mismatch tolerance. In this study, all surviving edited colonies accurately eliminated the blaIMP−38 resistance plasmid without any detectable chromosome damage, further confirming the high specificity and safety of the compact Cascade-Cas3 system in antimicrobial resistance reversal application.

However, the eradication efficiency of pA324-IMP and pUC57-blaIMP−38 differed, which may be attributed to differences in their plasmid backbones. The pA324-IMP possesses a typical IncHI5-type backbone encoding replication, horizontal transfer, maintenance, and stability functions (Liang et al. 2018). In contrast, pUC57 is a commonly used cloning vector that displays a relatively high copy number based on ColE1-like replication machinery. Previous studies have shown that the curing rates of target plasmids with similar backbones but different replicons are comparable, while the curing efficiencies of target plasmids with similar replicons but different backbone contents vary (Lauritsen et al. 2017). Considering changes in copy number, other studies have indicated that there is no clear correlation between copy number and curing efficiency (Lauritsen et al. 2017, Yuen et al. 2017). Together, these findings suggest that the backbone of the target plasmid is a critical factor affecting curing efficiency.

Finally, the delivery efficiency of the system is a critical factor affecting the overall efficiency of the CRISPR system (Park et al. 2017). Since plasmid transformation is rarely spontaneous, achieving autonomous diffusion of the CRISPR system between bacteria remains a significant challenge for its application in clinical treatments. According to a report from mBio, phage-delivered CRISPR-Cas3 has been used as an antibacterial agent to target and lyse Clostridium difficile (Selle et al. 2020). Zhou et al. used conjugation to deliver an endogenous CRISPR-Cas3 system to treat multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (Zhou et al. 2023). However, CRISPR systems based on phage and bacterial conjugation delivery face limitations such as the narrow host range of phages and low conjugation efficiency (Liu et al. 2020). Previous studies have found that nanomaterials as non-viral vector delivery carriers provide a promising method for CRISPR-Cas system delivery (Xu et al. 2021). Various innovative polymers, lipids, and gold nanoparticles have been developed. Nevertheless, the integration of these materials with the CRISPR system is still in its infancy (Kong et al. 2021). Therefore, significant challenges remain in applying these techniques to clinical research. Future research should focus on finding efficient vectors to achieve autonomous diffusion of CRISPR-Cas3 between bacteria.

Conclusion

This study proposes strategies to eliminate bacterial resistance by exploiting a compact Cascade-Cas3 system. Three targets of the CRISPR-Cas3 system were designed to target high-copy drug-resistant plasmids, natural drug-resistant plasmids, and fluorescent drug-resistant plasmids containing the drug-resistant gene blaIMP−38. The sensitivity of Klebsiella pneumoniae to antibiotics was restored, and the most efficient target for eradicating drug-resistant plasmids was identified. In conclusion, the compact CRISPR-Cas3 system is an innovative alternative to combat antibiotic resistance and holds significant clinical application value. Further research is needed to optimize delivery methods to enhance the efficiency of clinical applications.
