Biosensor-driven strain engineering reveals key cellular processes for maximizing isoprenol production in Pseudomonas putida
Authors: Javier Menasalvas, Shawn Kulakowski, Yan Chen, Jennifer W. Gin, Emine Akyuz Turumtay, Nawa Raj Baral, Morgan A. Apolonio, Alex Rivier, Ian S. Yunus, Megan E. Garber, Corinne D. Scown, Paul D. Adams, Taek Soon Lee, Ian K. Blaby, Edward E. K. Baidoo, Christopher J. Petzold, Thomas Eng, Aindrila Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synthetic biology generates vast combinatorial designs, yet high-throughput analytical methods to screen them are poorly matched to interrogate this search space. We address this challenge by developing a biosensor-driven, growth-coupled selection strategy in Pseudomonas putida for isoprenol, a potential aviation fuel precursor. We found and characterized a noncanonical signaling pathway, revealing a functional and physical complex between a hybrid histidine kinase and an alcohol dehydrogenase, whose activity is tuned by heterodimerization. Leveraging this biosensor in a pooled CRISPRi library selection, we identified key host limitations. Iterative combinatorial strain engineering derived from these hits yielded a 36-fold titer increase to ~900 milligrams per liter. Integrated omics analysis revealed that metabolic rewiring toward amino acid catabolism was crucial for this improvement. This observation was found to be beneficial by technoeconomic analysis. Our modular workflow provides a powerful strategy for optimizing complex heterologous pathways and uncovering emergent host biology.

INTRODUCTION

Microbially derived fuels offer a strategic opportunity to enhance energy security, reduce the environmental footprint of industrialization, and drive innovation in biomanufacturing (1, 2). Synthetic biology can produce advanced fuel molecules with superior combustion properties (3–5). However, the development of biofuel molecules (6, 7), particularly short-chain to medium-chain alcohols, is hindered by several challenges. Unlike other products (8, 9), alcohols have physical properties that impede development cycles from both analytical and biological standpoints. Alcohols are colorless and volatile molecules, properties that make their measurement challenging with high-throughput methods, requiring trade-offs between speed, cost, and measurement accuracy for biological samples (10). For accurate quantification, the gold standard is gas chromatography (11, 12). Although this methodology is highly robust, throughput is limited due to serial analysis and time-consuming sample preparation. This is especially relevant in the context of the combinatorial mutant search space from a genomics perspective. Within the framework of biological systems, heterologous production of alcohols can be limited due to their inherent inhibitory nature, which causes growth defects (13). Expression of heterologous pathways can also lead to the accumulation of toxic intermediate pathway metabolites in the host organism (14, 15), restricting their potential as production platforms. The additional burden in these strains often result in phenotypic drift (16) where production is lost after several generations of growth. Unpredictable performance increases the risk when evaluating a strain for industrial scale-up (13). Therefore, it is crucial to develop new tools, specific for the challenges of screening and producing alcohols in biological systems. This development would constitute a substantial scientific advance to enable the rapid development of new biofuel production processes that can meet the demands of emerging biorefinery configurations.

We evaluated the production of isoprenol, a platform commodity chemical (17) with attractive properties specifically as a diesel fuel blendstock (18) and precursor for the bio-jetfuel dimethyl cyclooctane (DMCO) (19, 20). Because of its importance, this target has been developed across a number of microbial hosts (21–24). Although each host has its strengths and weaknesses, Pseudomonas putida KT2440 was historically discouraged for use as a microbial chassis because it consumes the final product (25). Initial work in this microbe focused on inactivating isoprenol catabolism using the pathway elucidated via random barcode transposon sequencing (RB-TnSeq) functional genomics (25). However, we recognized that the ability of P. putida to catabolize isoprenol could be leveraged as an advantage, rather than a limitation: We could refactor the isoprenol-responsive signaling cascade on the basis of the natural catabolic pathway and provide a readout for intracellular isoprenol levels. This approach could also enable screening methodologies providing alternatives to traditional strain engineering methods.

We adhere to a common definition for the term “biosensor”: A system in which a ligand is recognized by a transcriptional activator, which then binds to a cognate DNA sequence, driving transcription of a downstream reporter gene (26, 27). The resulting gene expression should increase linearly in response to the ligand concentration. There are undoubtedly other biosensor modalities, such as directly converting the ligand into a colored molecule (28), but the advantage of a biosensor that activates a genetic circuit is that, apart from an efficient screening assay, the response can also be integrated back into cellular physiology for high-throughput selection assays. For example, strains can be devised where cell growth is concomitant with the increased production of the ligand.

Although understanding the molecular interactions between ligands and their partner proteins is important for biosensor design, crystallographic evidence for short-chain alcohol binding is scarce (29). The absence of structural information necessitates alternative strategies for biosensor development. For example, Bahls et al. recently developed an isopentanol (3-methyl-1-butanol) biosensor by error-prone polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mutagenesis of P. putida GP01 alkS to broaden its substrate specificity in Escherichia coli (30). Another report on isopentanol using rational engineering approaches has also demonstrated isopentanol titers exceeding 9.5 g/liter in batch-mode cultures (31). Biosensor-derived findings can be synergistic or additive with other approaches, providing a high-throughput route to such genome-wide targets necessary for higher titers.

Here, we show the identification, development, and optimization of an isoprenol biosensor, mediated by a native P. putida hybrid histidine kinase (abbreviated as HHK) signaling cascade that is responsive to short-chain alcohols including isoprenol and diols. Intriguingly, a failure to complement revealed a requirement for a specific alcohol dehydrogenase activity as a key component of the signaling cascade. The refactored biosensor identified bottleneck processes limiting heterologous isoprenol production using a pooled deactivated Cfp1(dCpf1) guide RNA (gRNA) library targeting nearly all open reading frames (ORFs) in the genome, leveraging a cost-efficient and rapid Oxford Nanopore–based whole-genome sequencing (WGS) method. An antibiotic-free, genomically integrated isoprenol pathway was the basis for combinatorial mutant screening of 70 previously untested gene loci and over 165 mutant strains, integrating known rational engineering targets with discoveries that emerged from our unbiased biosensor selection assay. An integrated omics analysis of high-producer strains revealed previously unknown intracellular amino acid carbon sinks enhancing isoprenol titer in the production phase. Our modular methods provide a key scientific advance toward commercial viability and are relevant to other analytically and biologically challenging molecules for biotechnology applications.

RESULTS

Development of a dose-dependent isoprenol biosensor

We developed a generalizable approach to building biosensors from RB-TnSeq fitness data (Fig. 1A and note S1). Although these fitness data have historically been used to probe enzymatic function, we reanalyzed this dataset of over 200 aggregated genome-wide fitness experiments, enabling powerful cofitness analysis to identify functionally linked regulatory genes on the basis of subtle correlations across diverse conditions. Our method extends the tunability of biosensors described in an earlier report (32) by adding a ribosome binding site (RBS) optimization step to match the exogenous ligand concentration to a proportionate fluorescent response. Using our updated assay, we searched for transcriptional regulators from published RB-TnSeq datasets to identify genes necessary for growth in the presence of exogenously added isoprenol (25, 33) (Fig. 1B; Materials and Methods). Using a cofitness analysis and cross-referencing the RB-TnSeq datasets with predicted ethanol degradation pathways in Pseudomonads (34, 35), we identified several two-component signaling systems for analysis (Fig. 1B), including an HHK yiaZ (or PP_2683), a response regulator PP_2665, and several metabolic enzymes including PP_2674, pedF (PP_2675), and yiaY (or PP_2682) (Fig. 1B). Specific protein components of a putative isoprenol signaling cascade were not immediately clear as there were several probable kinases and regulators implicated by the catabolic steps deduced from the RB-TnSeq data.

To test for a ligand-induced signaling cascade, we first determined whether the promoter upstream of pedF (PpedF) was responsive to isoprenol. Our assay quantifies the fold change in reporter fluorescence for strains subjected to isoprenol versus an untreated control (Materials and Methods). We initially constructed a reporter by placing the PpedF sequence upstream of an mCherry gene; however, the translational efficiency dictated by the RBS is critical for reporter expression. A construct lacking a well-defined RBS showed poor activation, with a nonlinear and irreproducible response to isoprenol (Fig. 1C). To overcome this, we incorporated an optimized RBS sequence previously identified as enabling robust fluorescent protein expression in P. putida (36). The optimized RBS PpedF-RBS-mCherry construct was highly improved, exhibiting a strong, 20-fold dose-dependent activation in response to exogenous isoprenol (1 g/liter) (Fig. 1C). The consistent activation of this intracellular biosensor by an external ligand also indicates that, under these conditions, isoprenol import is not a rate-limiting factor. To demonstrate the generalizability of this RBS-tuning strategy, we applied the same principle to a second biosensor for the aromatic monomer p-coumarate (note S1). Here again, incorporating a tuned RBS was essential, improving the biosensor’s dynamic range from 5x to 200x (note S1 and fig. S1). Together, these results establish that rational RBS optimization is a critical and broadly applicable step for converting native regulatory parts into robust, functional biosensors.

Discovery of key components in the isoprenol biosensor regulatory network

The biosensor assay revealed the necessary native signaling components among all implicated regulatory targets in isoprenol catabolism in P. putida. We used recombineering (Materials and Methods) to generate isogenic gene deletions of HHKs and response regulators identified in the cofit analysis in Fig. 1B. Knockout strains were transformed with the PpedF-RBS-mCherry construct to examine differences in the activation of the isoprenol biosensor (Fig. 2A). We compared fold activation between all implicated mutants, enabling a facile assessment of which encoded genes were required versus dispensable for pedF-RBS-mCherry activation.

We observed that deletion of PP_2665, encoding a response regulator, ablated the biosensor response, indicating that this was the primary regulator involved in the isoprenol signaling cascade. The three most promising candidate histidine kinases (HKs) evaluated were PedS2 (or PP_2671), PP_2664, and YiaZ (or PP_2683). Only the ∆yiaZ mutant showed loss of the biosensor activation, pinpointing this to be the specific HHK required for the isoprenol-induced signaling cascade. Conversely, deletion of PP_2664 slightly increased the biosensor fold induction compared to a wild-type (WT) control (Fig. 2A), denoting a possible interaction that attenuates the response to isoprenol. Both yiaZ and PP_2664 encode HHKs containing receiver domains and are largely uncharacterized (35). Such HHKs can function directly via a response regulator (e.g., PP_2665) or involve additional phosphorelay proteins (37). Although PP_2671 was the only other signaling-related candidate with a potential role in this cascade, the ΔPP_2671 mutant did not alter the biosensor activation (Fig. 2A), indicating a separate and complementary alcohol signaling system functionally distinct from the PedS2-PedR2 system used in the rare earth element methanol responsive signaling pathway (35). Whereas it is possible that other phosphorelay proteins not revealed in RB-TnSeq cofitness analysis are involved in the signaling response to isoprenol, our results indicate that the minimal isoprenol signaling cascade that was able to activate the biosensor included the HHK YiaZ, which either directly or indirectly activates the PP_2665 response regulator, which, in turn, binds to and drives gene expression at the pedF promoter sequence.

Complementation analysis was used to test genetic linkage with the observed phenotype. Plasmid-based reintroduction of PP_2665 in the ∆PP_2665 strain resulted in constitutive mCherry fluorescence with basal induction from the BAD promoter (fig. S2). This indicates the construct’s sensitivity to PP_2665 gene stoichiometry for the optimal dynamic range of the system and confirms PP_2665 as a key component in the signaling cascade.

In contrast, transforming the ∆yiaZ strain with a plasmid expressing yiaZ did not restore biosensor activity (Fig. 2B). Other yiaZ expression constructs were built using the native yiaYZ promoter and a constitutive PJ23119 Anderson promoter, but they also failed to complement biosensor activity. Proteomics analysis confirmed that, in all plasmid-borne constructs, YiaZ was expressed at comparable levels and well above native levels in WT, which was below the detection limit (fig. S3B). None of the HKs examined in this study (YiaZ, PP_2664, and PP_2671) are detectable by our proteomics pipeline at endogenous expression levels (data S2-1). The failure to complement the yiaZ deletion with plasmid-based reintroduction was unexpected. However, we identified that yiaZ is encoded in an overlapping frame with the alcohol dehydrogenase yiaY. We hypothesized that a truncated gene sequence of yiaZ could have been subcloned if the annotated start codon was incorrect, likely resulting in the expression of a nonfunctional protein. To address this possibility, we tested the plasmid-borne complementation of ∆yiaZ with a larger genomic 1.5-kb upstream sequence including PP_2681, yiaZ, and yiaY. The larger sequence was sufficient to activate the biosensor providing successful complementation of ∆yiaZ (Fig. 2B and fig. S3A). Notably, inactivating the yiaY start codon in the PP_2681-yiaZ-yiaY DNA sequence failed to complement, indicating that the yiaY and yiaZ gene products were both required for biosensor activity and ruling out any contribution from PP_2681 (fig. S3A). Because there is an existing genomic copy of yiaY in the ∆yiaZ strain, coordinated tuning of expression levels of yiaY and yiaZ may be important for the biosensor activation.

To contextualize the unexpected requirement for both YiaY and YiaZ in restoring biosensor activity, we further characterized YiaY activity. The reciprocal yiaY deletion strain showed similar requirements for complementation. Although plasmid expression of yiaY failed to restore the biosensor activity, the reintroduction of both yiaY and yiaZ together showed complementation (Fig. 2B). Domain analysis with PFAM revealed that the YiaY protein contains an iron-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase domain and is 41% identical to an iron-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase from Zymomonas mobilis ZM4 [Protein Data Bank: 3OWO (38)]. Iron-dependent alcohol dehydrogenases oxidize short-chain alcohols to aldehydes, but the product generated by YiaY from isoprenol is unknown and the role of an alcohol dehydrogenase in the signaling cascade is uncommon. Bacterial alcohol dehydrogenases form multimeric homocomplexes and participate in multilayer regulatory functions [reviewed in (39)] but are not known to be stoichiometric constituents of two-component signaling systems (40). Closely related homologs of yiaY and yiaZ are only detected in 20 Pseudomonad genomes (41) and no other genera (fig. S4 and data S4), indicating that functionally similar homologs (42) of this HHK and alcohol dehydrogenase operon are phylogenetically restricted. Future availability of new Pseudomonad genomes containing this gene cluster may contribute to a deeper understanding of this atypical taxonomic distribution.

YiaY may play a potential structural or enzymatic role in the isoprenol signaling cascade. These functions are not mutually exclusive. Because biosensor activation was only observed when yiaY and yiaZ were both genomically or episomally expressed in the cis configuration, we hypothesized a model in which both would be constituent subunits of a multimeric complex with a specific stoichiometry for activity (Fig. 2B). AlphaFold3 multimer analysis (43) provided the potential interaction domains between YiaY and YiaZ (fig. S5A and data S3). The leading N-terminal 14 amino acids of YiaY were predicted to interact with the N terminus of YiaZ (figs. S4 and S5A), and truncating these residues from YiaY blocked biosensor activation but did not destabilize YiaY protein levels (Fig. 2B and fig. S3C). In addition, when YiaY and YiaZ are coexpressed in E. coli they cosediment by gel filtration analysis, consistent with the formation of a protein complex via a direct physical interaction (fig. S6A). We also used AlphaFold3 ligand analysis (43) to determine the potential active site by identifying where the NAD+ cofactor would reside in YiaY as NAD+ is generally required for enzyme activity (fig. S5B and data S3). One of the key residues that could form a coordinating salt bridge with NAD+ in YiaY was mutated (YiaY-H281A), and we noted no biosensor activation in this resulting point mutant (Fig. 2B). Together, these separation of function mutants indicate that YiaY is an integral accessory factor in the YiaZ/PP_2665 two-component signaling pathway and that the YiaY and YiaZ complex formation as well as YiaY catalytic activity is essential for isoprenol to activate the regulatory cascade.

We reevaluated the isoprenol signaling cascade to identify how the biosensor response could be optimized. With the native yiaYZ operon, detection of a fluorescent signal required 24 to 48 hours of incubation for signal maturation and the variation between biological replicates was high (Figs. 1C and 2, A and C). Constitutive expression of the yiaYZ operon may lead to a more rapid isoprenol detection by priming the two key signaling components for activation. We generated a PJ23119-yiaYZ or PBAD-yiaYZ variant strains via recombineering; without introducing araC into the PBAD-yiaYZ strain, the BAD promoter should also show constitutive expression. The linear dynamic range of these constructs compared to a WT control strain (Fig. 2C) showed that the kinetics of detectable fluorescence and linear dynamic range improved in the PJ23119-yiaYZ promoter variant. In contrast, the BAD promoter variant had only a negligible improvement (Fig. 2C). Differential proteomic analysis of the PJ23119-yiaYZ promoter mutant identified increased cellular protein expression at multiple gene operons, suggesting that the YiaYZ signaling cascade activated a diverse range of processes including many ped-family operons (i.e., PP_2663-PP_2680 and PP_4064-PP_4067), glucose uptake proteins and an uncharacterized transporter (KguDTKE and PP_3210), and PQQ synthase proteins (PP_0375-PP_0379) (data S2-2).

Flow cytometry of the activated biosensor strains revealed kinetic improvements in signal maturation (Materials and Methods). In the PJ23119-yiaYZ promoter variant strain, a distinct bimodal population emerged ~4 hours postexogenous isoprenol addition with 25% of the population expressing at least 10-fold more mCherry and, by 7 hours, nearly 80% of cells were mCherry positive (Fig. 2D, fig. S7, and data S5). In contrast, only 20% of the WT control strain population was mCherry positive 24-hour post–isoprenol addition and had a maximum of fivefold mCherry induction (fig. S7). Overall, the synthetic constitutive Anderson promoter upstream of yiaYZ had successfully improved biosensor performance in terms of dynamic range, kinetics, and coefficient of variation (%CV) between biological replicates.

Although the biosensor effectively detected isoprenol in the log phase (Fig. 2E), the signal was lost in the stationary phase when using the WT control or the PJ23119-yiaYZ biosensor strain (Fig. 2F). Deleting PP_2664 restored biosensor activity in stationary-phase cultures (Fig. 2F), suggesting that PP_2664 acts as a competitive inhibitor, potentially forming heterodimers with YiaZ that are less efficient in activating the signaling cascade, especially in the stationary phase. The ΔPP_2664 strain, as well as the PJ23119-yiaYZ ΔPP_2664 strain, showed response to isoprenol in the stationary phase, although the native promoter showed more variability (Fig. 2F). The formation and persistence of YiaZ homodimers are likely critical for enabling biosensor activity in the stationary phase.

The previous genetic analysis suggested that both YiaY and PP_2664 can modulate YiaZ HHK activity. Although HKs are known to form homodimers, the analogous interaction of any HK with an alcohol dehydrogenase has not been reported elsewhere. We confirmed several biochemical aspects of the YiaY-YiaZ complex, mainly using expression of a truncated YiaZ PAS domain protein (fig. S6, A and B); size exclusion chromatography was consistent with the key PAS domain in YiaZ-forming homodimers and observed YiaY-YiaZ oligomers (fig. S6, A and B). Evidence of a physical interaction was observed between YiaZ and PP_2664 using an affinity pull-down and detection by mass spectrometry assay (Materials and Methods). An N-terminal in-frame 6HIS epitope tagged allele of PP_2664 was coexpressed with YiaZ in P. putida (fig. S6C). Affinity purification from induced cells showed that YiaZ was enriched by 3.6-fold on nickel beads when 6HIS-PP_2664 was present in the sample over the YiaZ-only control, indicating that these two HHKs could stably interact consistent with the formation of a larger complex. This evidence of protein affinity between PP_2664 and YiaZ supports a potential PP_2664-YiaZ heterodimer as a functional unit in the cell that can exist alongside the PP_2664 and YiaZ homodimers. We also used AlphaFold3 multimer analysis to predict whether YiaZ and PP_2664 could interact and observed that the full-length YiaZ and PP_2664 proteins could form heterodimers along contacts between their C-terminal response regulator head domains (fig. S5C and data S3).

Our genetic and biochemical experiments shed insight into the mechanism underlying the isoprenol signaling cascade driving downstream expression of the larger transcriptional response induced by YiaZ. We combine our findings in a model (fig. S8), where P. putida expresses both PP_2664 and YiaZ HHKs under the presence of compatible ligands, which are able to form either homodimer or heterodimer at an unknown dynamic rate. YiaY binds to YiaZ and might enzymatically reduce isoprenol to an aldehyde (i.e., 3-methyl-3-butenal) and help physically stabilize the modified ligand for recognition by YiaZ. In turn, activated YiaZ autophosphorylation or transphosphorylation would cause the subsequent phosphorylation of PP_2665 (its active conformation) and allow it to bind to several genomic loci, facilitating the gene expression in conjunction with a sigma factor. The degree of isoprenol pathway activation in a population is variable as both the monomer protein expression level and ratio of homodimer to heterodimer can differ, contributing to pathway activation heterogeneity (fig. S8). Because we previously observed that the deletion of yiaZ abolishes the biosensor response (Fig. 2A), we concluded that the PP_2664 homodimer is not activated by isoprenol. In addition, we note that cells restricted to expressing the YiaZ homodimer have an improved response to isoprenol (Fig. 2, A, E, and F).

Alcohol signaling regulation is tuned by HHK dimer configuration

We next determined the specificity of this biosensor to other potential ligands, surveying a broader range of short-chain alcohols in WT, ∆yiaZ, and ∆PP_2664 strains. We included these mutants to examine whether alcohol activation was modulated by preferential formation of YiaZ or PP_2664 homodimers (Fig. 3). Using equimolar alcohol concentrations, we observed that branched-chain alcohols with the branch in the 1 position (isoprenol and isopentanol) showed strong, 25-fold induction in the ∆PP_2664 strain and was ~2-fold stronger than in WT (Fig. 3). Tested short-chain alcohols with the branch at the 2 position like 3-methyl-1-pentanol had the opposite preference, showing greater activation in WT over the ∆PP_2664 strain background. Linear alcohols like ethanol and hexanol had variable fold responses ranging from 4-fold up to 40-fold and were preferentially activated in the WT strain background. Isopropanol had similar fold activation levels to isoprenol but with the opposite dimer preference where activation in WT was preferred. In all cases, the absence of YiaZ blocked downstream biosensor activation, indicating that PP_2664 homodimers were not able to activate PP_2665 to drive expression of the PpedF-RBS-mCherry readout with other potential ligands. In addition, on the basis of the YiaY CLEAN reannotation (44) as a propanediol oxidoreductase, we confirmed that the biosensor can also be activated by both 1,2-propanediol and 1,5-pentanediol (fig. S9B). Activation of the sensor by the tested diols showed little preference for either homodimer or heterodimer forms in the deletion mutants (fig. S9B). Outside of these alcohols and diols, amino acids or carbon sources including lysine, succinate, valine, acetate, and glycerol showed no biosensor activation (fig. S9A). We include a summary of the WT to ∆PP_2664 response ratio for alcohols as an inset panel in Fig. 3.

Deploying a unified biosensor-producer strain to identify nonpathway bottlenecks

Having developed and characterized a robust isoprenol biosensor, we next sought to deploy it as a tool for unbiased, systems-level discovery. We hypothesized that the primary constraints on heterologous isoprenol production were not the pathway enzymes themselves but rather a complex network of nonintuitive host-intrinsic factors. To test this hypothesis, we engineered a unified production and sensing strain by integrating the isoprenol pathway into the P. putida genome alongside our biosensor module. For high-throughput selection, the biosensor’s fluorescent reporter (mCherry) was replaced with the conditionally essential gene pyrF to create a growth-coupled output. This section describes the construction of this integrated strain and its deployment with both fluorescent and growth-based readouts to identify the host-native elements limiting isoprenol production.

Isoprenol production in P. putida was previously described (22) with a five-step plasmid-borne pathway (Fig. 4A). We integrated both the isoprenol pathway and the PpedF-RBS-mCherry biosensor construct into the genome to bypass the limited availability of antibiotic markers optimized for P. putida and to move away from plasmid-borne production systems. As outlined in Fig. 4B, we first integrated the biosensor and tested several synthetic promoters to constitutively express either yiaYZ and/or PP_2665 for increased biosensor sensitivity. Contrary to the plasmid-based biosensor, here the best signal-to-noise isoprenol response was with the constitutive J23100 promoter for the PP_2665, PP_2666 operon. We then replaced the original arabinose-inducible BAD promoter in the isoprenol pathway with a more economical crystal violet–inducible promoter, PJEx1 (45), and split the pathway across two integration loci (Fig. 4B) with no further isoprenol pathway modifications. An additional copy of the biosensor was integrated at a second locus to increase sensitivity. Last, PP_2664 was deleted to enable stationary-phase biosensor activation (strain TEAM-2650), and subsequently, PP_2675 was deleted to block isoprenol catabolism (strain TEAM-2669). Although the PP_2675 deletion proved effective for increasing net isoprenol accumulation, we recognize that PP_2675 is involved in PQQ cofactor regeneration (25) and its removal may have broader uncharacterized effects on PQQ-dependent enzymes. However, the continued robust function of our isoprenol biosensor in the ΔPP_2675 background demonstrates that the YiaY-YiaZ signaling pathway is independent of this PQQ-dependent enzymatic activity, a key finding that enabled its use throughout our engineering workflow.

At intermediate stages of strain development, we assessed both isoprenol productivity and biosensor sensitivity (Fig. 4, C and G, and fig. S10). Although we observed a linear dynamic response in the lower range of isoprenol concentrations, the signal was generally saturated concentrations above 1 g/liter (Fig. 4C). This genomic strain provided a baseline for further isoprenol optimization.

To have a better understanding of this integrated production-biosensor strain (TEAM-2595), we used proteomics to evaluate cellular response under production conditions. Aeration differences from headspace can affect strain productivity, and thus we assayed isoprenol titers in both deep-well plates and culture tube formats. TEAM-2595 grown in 5-ml culture tubes produced up to 200 mg/liter of isoprenol, whereas the same strain grown in the deep-well plate format only produced ~25 mg/liter (Fig. 4D), suggesting that cell physiology was favorable for isoprenol production due to aeration differences in the test tube format. Samples were harvested from these two formats to compare differentially expressed proteins at the log phase and stationary phase (Fig. 4E). With a Bonferroni adjusted P value, roughly 2% of detectable proteins showed differential abundance at the 8-, 24-, and 48-hour time points, with about twice as many proteins exhibiting significant expression changes at the 24-hour time point (71 proteins) compared to the 8-hour time point (37 proteins).

The proteomics dataset revealed differential expression across a range of cellular functions, including those involved in aromatic catabolism, alcohol dehydrogenases, membrane transporters, and cold-shock response (data S2-3). We also observed differential abundance of several uncharacterized transcriptional regulators including PP_1697, PP_4197, and PP_4100 from the GntR and Cro/Cl family, with no fitness defects by RB-TnSeq analysis on isoprenol-containing M9 medium. Although PP_4100 expression was only statistically significant at the 24-hour time point, PP_1697 and PP_4197 were significantly overexpressed in the 5-ml format at all time points (Fig. 4E and data S2-3). We postulated that overexpressing a global regulator of multiple processes might have a greater impact on isoprenol titer than targeting a single metabolic reaction. To test this, we compared the above uncharacterized global regulators along with PP_2211, a regulator of acyl–coenzyme A (CoA) synthesis, for overexpression.

Overexpression of the uncharacterized global regulators under PBAD in TEAM-2650 was analyzed to compare isoprenol titers directly and mCherry fluorescence and showed a distribution of both true and false positives (Fig. 4F). From these observations, we incorporated a constitutive PJ23119-PP_1697 promoter modification into TEAM-2669, generating TEAM-2777, and this strain increased titers to 220 mg/liter in the deep-well plate format, a 7x increase over the initial titer (Fig. 4G). These experiments demonstrate the complexity of monitoring dynamically produced isoprenol in vivo compared to a fixed concentration of isoprenol added exogenously at the start of a time course and indicate the potential of using global regulators as drivers for improved isoprenol titer.

CRISPRi-based selection using a growth-coupled isoprenol biosensor

A major challenge in optimizing heterologous pathways is that their interactions with native metabolism are not yet fully predictable. Thus, we transitioned the optimized sensor system to an unbiased selection system to efficiently identify cellular processes that were rate limiting for isoprenol production. Specifically, we coupled higher concentrations of isoprenol to cell growth by replacing mCherry with pyrF, a URA3 homolog from Teredinibacter turnerae T7901, under the pedF promoter linking activation of the biosensor to growth (instead of fluorescence). Next, via a CRISPRi library, we aimed to select candidate gene knockdowns that improved pedF-induced growth as a proxy for higher isoprenol titers (Fig. 5A).

Without modifying the existing RBS from the mCherry reporter sequence, the pedF-pyrF construct was sufficient to complement viability in a ∆pyrF strain in M9 minimal medium, even without any added isoprenol (Fig. 5B). This indicated that leaky expression was sufficient for growth and unacceptable for the selection assay. It is critical to note that, in this selection scheme, isoprenol concentration is the sole variable determining cell survival, and therefore exogenous uracil is omitted from all media to maintain selective pressure. We mutagenized the pedF-pyrF RBS sequence as before (Materials and Methods) to identify a retuned RBS sequence that was isoprenol dose-responsive, selecting for clones that demonstrated that growth was proportionally induced by the presence of several exogenous isoprenol concentrations (Fig. 5B and fig. S11). Two constructs from this screen were selected for validation. The resulting RBS mutants were reassayed for growth in media containing either 300 mg/liter and 1 g/liter of isoprenol, and we identified two optimal RBS sequences, named RBSmut-1 and RBSmut-2, with different isoprenol sensitivity profiles. Whereas the RBSmut-1 had a comparatively lower isoprenol threshold and supported growth in the presence of both concentrations, RBSmut-2 only displayed robust growth in the presence of the higher isoprenol concentration (Fig. 5B). In addition to the selected RBS sequences, the mutant plasmids contained additional modifications in the pyrF coding sequence that suggested that additional mutations were necessary to reach the desired inducible range (fig. S11).

We used triparental conjugation to transform a plasmid-borne dCpf1/gRNA CRISPRi library in the isoprenol-producing ∆pyrF strain. The dCpf1/gRNA CRISPRi library contains ~16,500 gRNAs targeting nearly all genes with three unique gRNAs per gene (data S1-2). Analysis of the library confirmed the expected insert size and distribution (fig. S12 and data S1-3), and all variants but 40 were detected (data S1-4). Clones were expected to show higher fitness for growth if they produced more isoprenol, in turn, becoming enriched in abundance by sequencing. As CRISPRi knockdowns may not be fully penetrant (36, 46), the selection for improved growth lessens the risk for false positives. Nonfunctional gRNAs are unlikely to dampen gene expression sufficient to increase isoprenol titers at the thresholds necessary to activate expression of pyrF. Enriched gRNA sequences were identified from pooled populations with direct colony PCR with universal primers common across all gRNA plasmids and Oxford Nanopore sequencing enabling rapid, ~16- to 20-hour workflows for each biological replicate (Fig. 5C; Materials and Methods).

This selection identified several pathways that indirectly influenced isoprenol production. The pooled CRISPRi-∆pyrF selection regime was applied in two sequential rounds using four PpedF-RBS-pyrF variants and four different strains, each with varied base isoprenol titers and isoprenol activation thresholds (Materials and Methods). Overall, 61 genes were selected and spanned functional categories including membrane efflux pumps, uncharacterized transcriptional regulators, unrelated metabolic reactions, or had an unknown function. It appeared that these pathways indirectly influenced isoprenol production by affecting competing pathways or regulatory networks. It is unclear whether any other current methodology would efficiently allow genome-wide identification of cellular mechanisms that impede isoprenol production.

Validation and combinatorial stacking of hits from the CRISPRi selection

We evaluated the genes identified from the CRISPRi selection on isoprenol production by testing the corresponding loss-of-function deletion mutant (Fig. 6A and table S1). Gene deletions were introduced into the background isoprenol producer strain TEAM-2777 and assayed for isoprenol production in deep-well plates (Materials and Methods). In the first isoprenol-production run comparing 10 single deletion mutants, we identified four different deletion strains that increased titers from 150 to 250 mg/liter (fig. S13). We next investigated whether the identified mutations had synergistic interactions with respect to isoprenol titer. As deletions were chosen from disparate functional categories, we selected the highest producing strain to modify further. We observed both additive, neutral, and inhibitory interactions as deletions were combined (Fig. 6A).

The targets recovered from this unbiased selection provided a list of both untested and previously validated host-intrinsic bottlenecks. A crucial finding was that this CRISPRi selection successfully identified flagella-related genes as a high-confidence hit (table S1), guiding us to choose fleQ as a target to improve isoprenol titers in a later round as flagella generation is an established competing cellular process that consumes a substantial portion of cellular resources (46–48). The successful identification of this key target from the known literature served as an important validation of our biosensor-guided selection platform and demonstrated its sensitivity to find impactful, single-gene targets from a genome-wide library. To further assess the selection accuracy and potential false negatives, we tested reported rational engineering targets that were not identified in our selection (22, 49). These included the polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) synthesis operon (phaAZC-IID), its regulator (phaF), a hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA lyase (mvaB), a putative dioxygenase (catCBA), and a lactate dehydrogenase (ldhA). Of all these targets, only the deletion of mvaB significantly increased isoprenol production in our strain background (Fig. 6A and figs. S13 and S14). This result suggests that the lack of enrichment for many of these rationally chosen targets was true-negative results in our specific context, further validating the selection assay’s fidelity.

Because the mvaB deletion proved highly effective and increased the isoprenol titer to ~500 mg/liter in the stacked deletion strain, we chose this specific strain for the second gRNA enrichment analysis starting with generation of a pedF-RBS-pyrF with a higher isoprenol activation threshold and subsequent transformation with the pooled gRNA library for selection and sequencing. Analysis of gRNAs by sequencing showed no overlap with the first set, as expected (Fig. 5 and table S2). We built more recombineering plasmids and oligos to test more deletion strains as before.

Whereas single deletions derived from the second round of CRISPRi selection enhanced isoprenol titer, combinatorial modifications showed minimal additive effects, suggesting that the isoprenol pathway could be rate-limited in activity. MvaS is a common rate-limiting reaction for isoprenoids (50, 51). Switching the NADPH to NADH cofactor used in mvaS could alleviate the metabolic cofactor imbalance (50). We tested a small set of mvaS variants (Fig. 6A) and identified overexpression of Enterococcus faecalis mvaS as the most successful for isoprenol production improvement (Fig. 6A). We concluded the combinatorial strain engineering workflow as observed titers now exceeded 850 to 900 mg/liter in strains, a 5.6x increase from the base strain (TEAM-2777) in isolates TEAM-3185 and TEAM-3174 sampled in two production phase time points (Fig. 6B). These titers are comparable to other high-producing batch-mode plasmid-borne isoprenol systems in Corynebacterium glutamicum (21), E. coli (24), and P. putida (22) and exceed those observed in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (23).

Our two highest producer strains TEAM-3185 and TEAM-3174 contain gene deletions in PP_2428, PP_4622, PP_3540/mvaB, and PP_4373/fleQ. TEAM-3174 also overexpresses mvaS and includes ∆PP_2710. TEAM-3185 lacks the mvaS overexpression, and ∆PP_2710 but PP_2074 is deleted. Table S3 contains a summary of putative encoded functions. The overexpression of mvaS in TEAM-3174 leads to a kinetic growth delay upon isoprenol pathway induction (Fig. 7A and fig. S15A) that may be related to its ability to produce isoprenol during the growth phase, which is not observed in TEAM-3185 nor in the parental TEAM-2595 (Fig. 4D and fig. S16). Enhancing isoprenol titer did not sensitize the strains to exogenous isoprenol concentrations relative to the base strain (fig. S15B). WGS identified 74 additional nonsynonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of unknown function in both improved producer strains, and a 26.1-kb deletion surrounding the fleQ/PP_4373 locus containing 24 flagella-related genes. A comprehensive tabulation of all polymorphisms is described in data S1-5.

Functional genomics analyses reveal metabolic shifts in high isoprenol producers

To characterize the cellular changes in these enhanced isoprenol producer strains, we used an omics approach with proteomics and metabolomics. We assessed the most optimized final strains TEAM-3174 and TEAM-3185 to the starting strain TEAM-2595 to characterize cellular changes as isoprenol production increased from 50 to 850 mg/liter (Fig. 6, B and C). We analyzed parallel samples from the growth phase [optical density at 600 nm (OD600) ~ 1] and the 24-hour time point in the production phase (Materials and Methods). The central conclusion from this analysis indicated a broad metabolic shift beyond increasing isoprenol pathway expression or reducing product degradation and hinted instead to a previously undescribed role in amino acid metabolism. A detailed systems-level analysis is summarized in Fig. 7 along with a comprehensive assessment of the proteomics results (note S2, fig. S17, and data S1-6 and S2-4). Proteins linked to amino acid metabolism showed significant abundance changes, including up-regulation of PP_2088 (a sigma factor involved in nitrogen utilization) and PP_4621 (homogentisate dioxygenase for phenylalanine degradation) and down-regulation of PP_4401 and PP_4403 (BKDC proteins involved in branched amino acid catabolism to acyl-CoA). PHA protein levels were also down-regulated (note S2 and Fig. 7D).

Additional proteomics analysis ruled out that the isoprenol pathway expression was rate limiting. When proteomics samples were visualized as a fraction of the total proteome for each sample, the abundance of isoprenol pathway proteins was unchanged despite producing 16-fold more isoprenol (Fig. 6B), even when overexpression of mvaS led to a modest 1.4x protein increase in TEAM-3174 (Fig. 7D). Consistently high metabolite concentrations of mevalonate and mevalonate monophosphate (Fig. 7E) are described in further detail in Discussion. Contrary to the simple total pathway-dosage hypothesis (52), additional plasmid expression to increase pathway protein abundance further decreased isoprenol titers (fig. S18 and data S2-5). These results corroborate that pathway expression is not the primary driver of isoprenol production.

The key finding from the metabolomics analysis was that leucine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine accumulated during the production phase (and not the growth phase) in the enhanced isoprenol producer strains. We calculated relative metabolite concentrations for both growth and production phases and visualized them using a metabolic map (Fig. 7E). Although there were some notable changes with regard to the activation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) glyoxylate shunt in the production phase, we were drawn to changes in amino acid concentrations corroborating the enriched amino acid pathways from proteomics as an attractive explanation for the metabolic changes we observed.

Leucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan are all derived from the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) through the chorismate biosynthesis pathway (53). We observed a 2-fold increase in phenylalanine and a 15-fold increase in leucine concentrations comparing TEAM-3174 and TEAM-3185 to the base strain (Fig. 7E and data S1-1). In contrast, tryptophan concentrations showed inconsistent changes between the two producers as TEAM-3185 had no change whereas TEAM-3174 showed a 7x decrease (Fig. 7E and data S1-1). P. putida encodes both known catabolic routes for phenylalanine: It can both use a CoA-thioester intermediates (54) to generate succinyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA (the pheBA gene cluster), as well as the more conventional homogentisate route (55) (the paa gene cluster) to release fumarate and acetoacetate. Fitness data for P. putida grown on phenylalanine indicate that the two pathways are not functionally redundant; mutations in either pathway is sufficient to compromise fitness on phenylalanine as a carbon source (56). In addition, the proteomics data indicated an increase in the homogentisate pathway proteins. Whereas most of the Paa operon proteins are not detectable by this proteomics method, the Paa transcriptional regulator, PaaX, was up-regulated 8x to 9x in both strains, suggesting that the homogentisate pathway is also active. These results indicate that phenylalanine is likely being routed into the TCA cycle with potentially either or both enzymatic pathways. Either leucine and phenylalanine could be used as the replacement nitrogen or carbon source after ammonium sulphate was depleted, consistent with PP_2088 (Sigma-70 Factor, SigX) activation and the nitrogen starvation response identified by proteomics.

Our multiomics analysis revealed that particular intracellular amino acid pools are drivers for isoprenol production. This led to a testable model where phenylalanine could provide a carbon source for isoprenol synthesis after glucose depletion, and leucine could act as a nitrogen source for initial biomass formation. We validated this model with a media supplementation experiment adding the amino acids at different points of the cultivation. Phenylalanine supplementation increased isoprenol titers by 15% in a dose-dependent manner when added after glucose was consumed, whereas leucine boosted titers by 10% when introduced at the start of the production assay (fig. S19). Above 3 mM, leucine supplementation reduced titers, indicating a sensitivity to supplied amounts. In contrast, phenylalanine supplementation added at T0 did not change isoprenol titers and leucine supplementation during the production phase was likewise ineffective (fig. S19).

Targeting a related enzymatic reaction in channeling flux toward isoprenol was also beneficial: When tyrosine aminotransferase PP_3590 was overexpressed, a 40% increase in isoprenol titer was observed in the parental strain TEAM-2777, confirming the role of driving tyrosine to acetoacetate in improved isoprenol titers (fig. S20). A smaller but subtle increase was observed when PP_3590 was overexpressed in the high-producer strain TEAM-3185, differentiating the role of enzymatic activity versus amino acid pools as potentially rate limiting (fig. S20). Together, these results underscore the importance of timing and type of amino acid or enzymatic activity needed to optimize flux toward isoprenol, opening new rational engineering strategies, or large-scale media optimization studies, based on the identification of uncharacterized precursor pools postglucose use.

In summary, specific amino acid–focused experimental approaches provide an impactful synergistic strategy for improving production phase titers by using previously unidentified carbon and nitrogen sinks in engineered strains to further improve isoprenol production. Amino acid amendments already present in renewable carbon streams can be economically viable for biomanufacturing as supported by a technoeconomic analysis (Materials and Methods; note S3 and fig. S21).

DISCUSSION

Although powerful methods exist to measure the response of microbial systems in a cultivation environment, there are few approaches that enable direct identification of mechanisms that improve bioproduction, in a genome-wide manner. By coupling a bioproduct-detecting sensor to a selection, we were able to facilitate this goal. Establishing an optimized biosensor is key to this objective. Our modular workflows leverage functional genomics to find biosensors, refining their activity with rational approaches. Growth-coupled biosensors can then be developed and paired with mutant libraries beyond CRISPRi or promoter titrations (57) [i.e., chemical mutagenesis (58) and Tn-Seq (33)] or media optimizations (59) to enable rapid scanning of a large search space for a desired phenotype.

The isoprenol biosensor in this study (summarized in fig. S8) makes use of a noncognate pair of sensor HHK (YiaZ) and downstream response regulator (PP_2665). The sensor-response function was modulated with both the alcohol dehydrogenase YiaY and HHK PP_2664, a relatively uncommon configuration but one that effectively enabled the development of a highly responsive isoprenol biosensor in P. putida KT2440. Unlike the direct inhibition of the RetS-GacS two-component system (60), the YiaZ-PP_2664 system could use a temporally restricted competition between homodimers and heterodimers (as shown in Figs. 2 and 3 and diagrammed in fig. S8), which may facilitate population-level bet-hedging by diversifying the response to different alcohols, mirroring the functional complexity of ethylene sensing in Arabidopsis (61). As isoprenol titers remain high in our best-producing strains, YiaY activity does not measurably contribute to bulk isoprenol degradation nor do other encoded alcohol dehydrogenases substitute for YiaY in activating YiaZ.

We revealed three key mechanisms that dictate high isoprenol production. First, isoprenol production is an emergent cellular property (62) arising from complex, unpredictable interactions. Although individual gene deletions often boosted isoprenol titers, combining deletions did not predictably produce additive effects and sometimes resulted in lower titers (refer to Fig. 6A). Although this property has been reported in other engineered systems (63), our panel of systematically generated isogenic mutants offer a unique opportunity to elucidate regulatory network connections relevant to terpenes using the omics methods described here. Second, during the growth phase, our high-producer isoprenol strains express all isoprenol pathway enzymes at comparable levels, but only mevalonate (and not isoprenol) accumulates. Only when glucose is exhausted does a “switch” occur, unlocking isoprenol accumulation. It is possible that the accumulation of mevalonate monophosphate (MVAP) beyond a certain concentration threshold causes feedback inhibition (64) of mevalonate kinase (MK) and diphosphomevalonate decarboxylase (PMD) activity during the growth phase. Isoprenol production appears to be restored during the production phase after MVAP levels drop below this concentration threshold. This is exemplified with mvaS overexpression in TEAM-3174 (refer to fig. S16). The cost of this phenotype is severe: Cell growth is slowed and kinetically delays isoprenol formation in the production phase by 24 hours. Mutants that improve the rate of isoprenol production are rare but valuable in the context of fed-batch production regimes (65), but the decreased growth rate for this strain suggests that computational methods for rate improvements (66) should be compared. Last, phenylalanine and leucine accumulated in the production phase, but only phenylalanine supplementation enhanced isoprenol titers in the production phase. This metabolic rewiring is consistent with favoring leucine as a nitrogen source rather than use as a carbon source. Medium amendments do not modulate isoprenol production in the parental strain TEAM-2595, implying that metabolic rewiring is specific to genetic modifications generated here and may not be general properties of the amino acids themselves. The underlying change in stationary-phase physiology may be related to feedback inhibition (67) from higher extracellular concentrations or perhaps different metabolic profiles as hinted from fitness datasets with comparing leucine as a nitrogen versus carbon source (56).

Our strains require further optimization but have the potential to play an important role in the burgeoning bioeconomy by producing isoprenol as a useful end product and platform chemical for upgrading to fuels and commodity chemicals. We demonstrated production in economical M9 minimal salt media without the use of antibiotics or plasmids with this genomically integrated design, which sets our strains apart from other plasmid-borne isoprenol designs for long-term stability (52) in various fed reactor modes. These advances lay the groundwork for process-focused improvements required for optimal strain behavior in stirred tank, fed-batch production modes as we aim for cost-competitiveness in fuel and commodity chemical markets. The fluorescent biosensor module could also provide real-time inline diagnostic measurements during culture seed generation or provide single-cell resolution to identify regions of a bioreactor where cells are differentially active. The genotypic and phenotypic characterization provided here provides a baseline record facilitating future comparative studies as additional isoprenol producer strains are developed.

In conclusion, the use of the refined biosensor in conjunction with our straightforward workflow (RBS tuning and CRISPRi-mediated selection) identified competing cellular functions limiting heterologous isoprenol generation, linking peripheral cellular processes back to central metabolism. A direct genome-wide assessment of such mechanisms was previously not possible. With two rounds of screening with different activation thresholds for pedF-RBS-pyrF, we increased isoprenol titers stepwise from ~25 to 400 mg/liter and then to 900 mg/liter. Repeating this workflow for other process parameters can also be facilitated more easily. For example, utilization of mixed carbon sources containing amino acids under microaerobic conditions (68) could benefit from this unbiased optimization strategy. In tandem, machine learning (69) or constraint-based metabolic modeling methods (66) refined from omics information (70) could suggest modifications that force growth coupling. Parameters used in biomanufacturing present a large and dynamic design space for optimization, the interrogation of which is facilitated by high-throughput methods that use either the growth or fluorescence modality to accelerate strain engineering.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial growth media and cultivation parameters

P. putida KT2440 (and its derivative strains) were routinely cultured using standard laboratory conditions described previously (36). Bacterial strains were revived from cryostorage by streaking to singles on LB Miller agar plates and maintained for no longer than 2 weeks. The LB Miller (Luria-Bertani) medium [tryptone (10 g/liter), yeast extract (5 g/liter), and NaCl (10 g/liter)] was purchased from Becton Dickinson (BD Difco, product no. 244620). When cells were cultured on petri dishes, LB medium was supplemented with 2% (w/v) solid agar (Becton Dickinson, Bacto Agar) and incubated at 30°C. Cryostocks were prepared by diluting bacterial cultures grown to saturation overnight to 25% (w/v) glycerol before storage at −80°C. At the 1X working concentration, M9 medium contains 47.9 mM Na2HPO4, 22 mM KH2PO4, 8.56 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgSO4, 100 μM CaCl2 with 1X trace metal solution (catalog no. T1001, Teknova Inc., Hollister, CA), 2% glucose, 70 mM (NH4)2SO4, and 30 mM Mops (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. M1254) adjusted to a pH of 7.0. This formulation of M9 used for P. putida is sometimes referred to as “NREL M9” or “Modified M9” (71, 72). Crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, product no. 61135) was used at a concentration of 1000 nM (1 μM) to induce production of the integrated isoprenol pathway. The plasmid-borne isoprenol pathway (pIY670) (22) was induced at 0.2% (w/v) arabinose, and kanamycin sulfate (50 μg/ml) was added to the media for those specific experiments to maintain the plasmid. Extended details on the generation of strains and plasmids are included in Supplementary Methods.

Nomenclature

When discussing HKs, HHKs, and response regulators in the context of two-component signaling systems, such as YiaZ and PP_2665, we use the “/” notation to indicate the functional relationship between the two proteins as is the convention for the field. For all other cases, we use the “/” to indicate common synonyms (usually gene names and gene IDs) used to refer to the same gene or protein in P. putida KT2440 as annotated in NIH GenBank record AE015451.2.

RB-TnSeq data and cofitness analysis

Candidate regulatory components for biosensor development were identified by analyzing a large, previously generated RB-TnSeq fitness database for P. putida KT2440 (33). This database contains quantitative barcoded fitness data from a 200,000-member P. putida barcoded transposon mutant library collected across more than 200 experimental conditions. Published datasets are available for analysis at the fitness browser, accessible at fit.genomics.lbl.gov. To identify functionally linked genes, we performed a cofitness analysis. A cofitness score is the Pearson correlation coefficient calculated for the fitness values of two different genes across all experiments in the database. A high cofitness score (approaching 1) indicates that the two genes share a similar pattern of fitness gains and losses across many conditions, suggesting that they are functionally related (e.g., part of the same pathway or regulatory system). We reanalyzed this aggregated RB-TnSeq dataset for P. putida KT2440 for uncharacterized transcriptional regulators with high cofitness scores to identify candidate sensor-regulator pairs for our biosensor development pipeline.

Isoprenol biosensor activity assay: Standard assay and stationary-phase assay

Isoprenol induction assays were performed by picking a single colony from a strain transformed with pTE518 into 5-ml test tubes in LB medium supplemented with gentamicin (30 μg/ml) at 30°C overnight. Saturated cultures were diluted 1:10 into 2 ml of fresh M9 minimal medium with 2% glucose in 24-deep-well plates, with or without isoprenol supplementation at the desired concentrations. After 24 and 48 hours, 100-μl samples were taken and transferred to black opaque 96-well plates for fluorescence measurements. mCherry fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 587 nm and an emission wavelength of 610 nm using a monochromator-based Spectramax M2e Microtiter plate reader (Molecular Devices Inc., San Jose, CA).

For stationary-phase assays, strains were grown in the 24-deep-well plate format for 24 hours as in the conventional assay described above. Isoprenol was then added to the desired concentrations, the plate was resealed, and mCherry fluorescence was measured 24 hours later.

Flow cytometry

High-throughput flow cytometry experiments were performed using the Accuri C6 flow cytometer equipped with a microtiter plate autosampler (BD). Cells were prepared for isoprenol induction assays and sampled at the indicated time points. Upon sampling, cells were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1 in 500 μl of phosphate-buffered saline medium. A total of 30,000 events were recorded at a flow rate of 66 μl/min and a core size of 22 μm. mCherry was excited at 552 nm at 70 mW, and emission was detected at 610 nm with a 20-nm bandpass. Data acquisition was performed as described in the Accuri C6 Sampler User’s Guide and analyzed with Treestar FloJo V10.1. No sample gates were applied during analysis.

Metabolomics

Organic acids were quantified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) using the method described previously (73). All other metabolites were quantified by LC-MS via an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1-mm inner diameter, 100-mm length, and 1.7-μm stationary-phase particle diameter; Waters, MA, USA) and the appropriate guard column (Waters, MA, USA) using exactly as described previously (74, 75). Metabolites were quantified via external calibration curves or chemical standards (for relative quantification). Data acquisition was performed via the Agilent MassHunter Workstation software (version 8). Data processing and analysis were performed via Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis (version 6) and Profinder (version 8) software, respectively.

Shotgun proteomics analysis

TEAM-2595, TEAM-3175, and TEAM-3184 P. putida strains were grown for isoprenol production in deep-well plates. The log-phase samples were harvested when each strain reached an OD600 of 0.7 (roughly 8 to 14 hours post–back dilution and induction) as monitored by a spectrophotometer. The production-phase samples were harvested at the 24-hour time point. Washed cell pellets for each sample were stored at −80°C until sample preparation. The protein was extracted from cell pellets, and tryptic peptides were prepared by following an established proteomic sample preparation protocol (76). Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in Qiagen P2 Lysis Buffer (Qiagen Sciences, Germantown, MD, catalog no. 19052) for cell lysis. Proteins were precipitated with addition of 1 mM NaCl and 4 x volume acetone, followed by washing the protein precipitant with two additional washes of 80% acetone in water. The recovered protein pellet was homogenized by pipetting mixing with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate in 20% methanol. Protein concentration was determined by the DC protein assay (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, CA). Protein reduction was accomplished using 5 mM tris 2-(carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) for 30 min at room temperature, and alkylation was performed with 10 mM iodoacetamide (final concentration) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Overnight digestion with trypsin was accomplished with a 1:50 trypsin:total protein ratio. The resulting peptide samples were analyzed on an Agilent 1290 UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer for discovery proteomics (77). Briefly, peptide samples were loaded onto an Ascentis ES-C18 Column (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and separated with a 10-min LC gradient [10% Buffer A (0.1% formic acid in water)–35% Buffer B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile)]. Eluting peptides were introduced to the mass spectrometer operating in positive-ion mode and were measured in data-independent acquisition mode with a duty cycle of three survey scans from mass/charge ratio (m/z) 380 to m/z 985 and 45 MS2 scans with a precursor isolation width of 13.5 m/z to cover the mass range.

Isoprenol production assays

For each experiment, control strains were revived from a cryostorage concurrently with test strains. Overnight LB cultures were prepared in test tubes (5 ml) from single colonies and incubated at 30°C with 200 rpm orbital shaking and 40% humidity. Strains were adapted to M9 minimal media through two serial transfers. The first adaptation involved inoculating 200 μl of overnight LB culture into 5 ml of M9 minimal media in test tubes. The second adaptation was performed the following day, where 100 μl of saturated culture was inoculated into 1.5 ml of fresh M9 medium in 24-deep-well plates. These plates were sealed with a gas-permeable film and incubated at 30°C with 1000 rpm linear shaking and 70% humidity. After 24 hours, strains were inoculated into production media at a starting OD600 of 0.15. Production runs were performed in 24-deep-well plates (1.5 ml) with a gas-permeable film and M9 minimal media supplemented with 1 μM crystal violet as an inducer. To minimize evaporation, the gas-permeable film was additionally sealed with tape that covered half of each square well. All production runs were performed in quadruplicate, with two biological replicates prepared for each strain when multiple clones were tested. M9 minimal medium was freshly prepared for each production run and discarded when precipitation occurred. When supplemented with amino acids, 100 mM stock solutions of molecular grade chemicals (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared and filter sterilized before use. To measure isoprenol titers, samples (200 μl) were collected after 24 and 48 hours and stored at −80°C for analysis. For strains harboring overexpression plasmids, gentamicin (30 μg/ml) was added to maintain plasmid stability, and arabinose was supplemented to a final concentration of 1% (w/v) to induce the isoprenol pathway on pIY670, unless otherwise indicated. Isoprenol titers reported at the 48-hour time point postinduction were corrected for evaporation in the deep-well plate format with additional tape coverage accounting for loss of the authentic standard as previously described (24). A similar 43% isoprenol loss was observed for all tested concentrations ranging from 1 g/liter down to 62 mg/liter. The formula applied for the 48-hour time point was ([detected isoprenol 24 hours] x 0.43) + [detected isoprenol 48 hours]. Glucose consumption was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography exactly as previously described (46). Glassware was triple washed with ddH2O and ethanol (46) as previously described.

Quantification of isoprenol by gas chromatography

Isoprenol quantification was performed using the gas chromatography–flame ionization detection (GC-FID 8890, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a DB-WAX column (15 m, 0.32-mm inner diameter, and 0.25-μm film thickness, Agilent, USA) as previously described (78). Briefly, the gas chromatography oven was programmed as follows: 40° to 100°C at 15°C/min, followed by 100° to 230°C at 40°C/min, and last, a hold oven step at 230°C for 2 min. The inlet temperature was set to 200°C. Samples harvested for isoprenol analysis were thawed from cold storage on ice mixed with an equal volume of ethyl acetate (Fisher Scientific, no. E196SK) with n-butanol (100 mg/liter; Sigma-Aldrich, no. 281549) as the internal standard as described previously (78).

Optimizing RBS variants for PpedF-pyrF, PpobR-mCherry, and PJ23119-PP_1697

Candidate RBS sequences for the PpedF-pyrF design were calculated using the de novo DNA RBS calculator (“RBS Calculator Design Mode) available at www.denovodna.com (79), and a degenerate oligo with ~40 potential RBS designs was selected. The degenerate oligo was synthesized with 3′ and 5′ overhangs compatible for single-stranded DNA HiFi assembly as described previously (80). Approximately 0.25 to 1 μl of the HiFi assembly reaction was directly used for transformation via electroporation into a P. putida strain without outgrowth and isolation in E. coli. For growth-based selection assays, single colonies were picked into wells of a 96-well microtiter plate and incubated with exogenous isoprenol as indicated when strains contained the isoprenol production pathway, and crystal violet was added to the media at a concentration of 1 μM. Microtiter dishes were incubated at 30°C for 2 to 6 days to assess growth under varied isoprenol concentration. Clones with the desired behavior were isolated, and plasmid DNA was extracted and sequenced to identify RBS sequences and any other mutations. For the case of PP_1697 promoter modification, there was no statistical increase in isoprenol titer between RBS-PP_1697 clones screened compared to the RBS control, and so we used the RBS design in this study.

gRNA library design, construction, and validation

Three gRNAs targeting upstream of each of the 5591 coding sequences in the P. putida KT2440 were designed using gRNASeqRET (81) (data S1-2). The 20-nucleotide oligomer guide sequences were flanked with linkers containing AarI sites yielding 5′ AGAT and 3′ TCAA overhangs and ordered as a pool of oligonucleotides (Twist Bioscience, CA, USA). The pool was PCR amplified for nine cycles (Kapa polymerase, Roche) and Golden Gate assembled into the AarI sites of pTE219_dCpf1_AarI, modified to include oriT for downstream conjugation. To help ensure that all designed guide species were represented in the library, >160k (10x the number of variants) E. coli colonies were picked. The library was validated by PCR amplification (oligonucleotides 5′-CCACGTGACACACTcacctgc-3′ and 5′-GTTAGCCGTCTCCAcacctgc-3′), and the resultant amplicon library was sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform and analyzed using custom pipelines (data S1-4 and S1-5).

Analysis of enriched gRNAs from the library under PpedF-pyrF selection

P. putida ∆pyrF strains transformed with a PpedF-pyrF plasmid were subject to triparental conjugation with the gRNA library harbored in E. coli DH10 with an E. coli pRK2013 tra+ helper strain. (The transformation efficiency by electroporation in ∆pyrF strains is about three orders of magnitude lower than WT P. putida strains.) The three strains were spotted onto solid LB agar media and allowed to incubate overnight at 30°C. The next day, a small amount of biomass from the conjugation was isolated with a sterile toothpick and used to inoculate 1.5 ml of M9 medium kanamycin with or without 1 μM crystal violet in 24-deep-well plates with four replicates from each conjugation. Samples were grown for 24 hours at which point we examined the cultures for growth. If the cultures showed turbidity or in the best case saturation after 24 hours postinoculation, 30 μl of the culture was prepared for colony PCR and the gRNA sequences present on the dCpf1/dCas12a/CRISPRi plasmid were amplified by OneTaq PCR. gRNA amplicons were amplified using oligos TEAM-1174 (5′-gaccagttgcgcctgtcggtgttcagtg-3′) and TEAM-644 (5′-gatcttccccatcggtgatgtcg-3′). Biomass from the LB conjugation spot preselection, and the E. coli DH10 strain harboring the library was also amplified and sequenced to verify the diversity of the initial distribution of gRNAs. gRNAs were sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore linear DNA amplicon service by Plasmidsaurus Inc. (South San Francisco, CA). The rapid Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing service was chosen over other sequencing platforms and providers because it provided gRNA sequencing results as quickly as within 24 hours from sample submission, enabling rapid data analysis for future experimental planning. Raw reads were mapped to the pTE219 reference gRNA plasmid map using Geneious Prime, and the aligned gRNA sequences downstream of the 5′-TTTN-3′ PAM sequence were extracted as a CSV file. Targeting spacer sequences were filtered to remove sequences that were 19 bases or fewer. Sequences were compared to the known gRNA targeting sequences (data S4), and implicated genes were selected on the basis of the following criteria: (i) if a particular gRNA was enriched (>5 reads in one biological replicate), (ii) if there are multiple gRNAs targeting the same gene, (iii) gRNAs target genes functionally related (i.e., generation of a specific process) or targets in the same operon, and (iv) the repeated occurrence of gRNAs or gene targets across multiple replicates. All selected targets from both rounds are described in tables S2 and S3. Verification of gRNA knockdown on isoprenol titers was analyzed in isogenic deletion strains to both reveal a fully penetrant phenotype and eliminate gene perturbations from potential off-target gRNA repression that would complicate interpretation of changes to isoprenol titers (82). Candidate genes from the gRNA enrichment were first grouped by function using HMMer and COG to identify nonredundant cellular processes. At random, we picked several from each category to design new gRNA plasmids and recombineering oligos, choosing 28 targets for the first enrichment analysis and 30 for the second analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were computed using GraphPad Prism 10.5 (GraphPad Software LLC, Boston, MA) for MacOS. The statistical analysis described in Figs. 2 to 7 use all data points shown in the figure to calculate the SD from the mean. For growth curves, the SD of the mean OD values as a proxy for growth under the tested conditions is plotted as a shaded area between two solid lines, where the centered line represents the average value. The exact number of replicates averaged is described in the corresponding figure legend. The adjusted P value for proteomic dataset analysis is calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple t tests. Last, the error bars indicating the SD from the mean for isoprenol titer reflect are calculated using all data points shown in the figure panel.
