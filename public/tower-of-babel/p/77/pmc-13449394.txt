Deep learning-based neuroanatomical profiling reveals population-specific brain changes in multiple sclerosis: a large-scale Middle Eastern study
Authors: Mahdi Bashiri Bawil, Mousa Shamsi, Abolhassan Shakeri Bavil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) affects 2.8 million individuals worldwide, with Middle Eastern populations remaining underrepresented in neuroimaging research despite elevated regional prevalence rates. Large-scale comparative studies between MS patients and healthy controls (HC) are essential for characterizing disease-specific brain changes and establishing normative biomarkers. This study aimed to perform comprehensive statistical characterization of brain structural changes in MS patients compared to HC using automated deep learning-based segmentation, establishing population-specific reference ranges for a Middle Eastern cohort.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed 1,381 subjects (1,000 HC, 381 MS patients) from Northwest Iran. Four deep learning architectures were evaluated on multi-center training data (local cohort plus MSSEG dataset), with U-Net selected for automated FLAIR sequence segmentation. Neuroanatomically-informed lesion classification identified periventricular, deep, and juxtacortical white matter hyperintensities. Statistical analyses employed age group- and gender-stratified comparisons with comprehensive correlation assessments across ventricular and lesion load measures.ResultsU-Net achieved optimal segmentation performance (DSC = 88.8%, HD95 = 2.8 mm), supporting its selection for population-level analysis. Compared to healthy controls, MS patients exhibited markedly elevated structural burden across all age strata: ventricular load was 1.7-fold higher (normalized ratios: 1.39%→2.37%; t = − 18.92, Cohen’s d = 1.139) and white matter lesion load was 2.4-fold higher (normalized ratios: 0.315%→0.749%; U = 11,963.0, rank-biserial r = 0.310), both with p < 10⁻⁶. Among lesion subtypes, periventricular lesions predominated (53.91 ± 20.62% of total burden), while anatomical distribution patterns showed no significant gender differences. Age-related structural changes were more pronounced in MS patients than in controls, with stronger correlations observed for both ventricular load (r = 0.403, p = 2.50 × 10⁻¹⁶) and lesion load (r = 0.266, p = 1.33 × 10⁻⁷).ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary population-specific reference ranges for MS neuroimaging biomarkers in Middle Eastern populations, revealing lesion accumulation patterns with periventricular predominance. The automated segmentation and statistical framework address gaps in global MS research demographics.

Background

Multiple sclerosis: global burden and pathophysiology

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, representing the leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults worldwide [27, 49]. The global prevalence has reached approximately 2.8 million individuals, with rates continuing to increase across diverse geographical regions [27]. This disease burden exhibits pronounced geographical heterogeneity, with prevalence rates ranging from 158 to 219 per 100,000 population in high-risk regions including North America and Western Europe, while demonstrating emerging patterns in previously underrepresented populations [43].

The pathophysiology encompasses focal inflammatory lesions leading to demyelination, chronic axonal degeneration, and progressive neurological dysfunction [13]. Contemporary understanding has evolved beyond traditional discrete phenotype classifications toward a clinical continuum model, recognizing that relapsing and progressive features coexist variably from early disease stages [39, 56].

Neuroimaging in MS diagnosis and management

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, serves as the diagnostic gold standard for MS through superior lesion visualization and cerebrospinal fluid suppression [40, 53]. The McDonald 2017 criteria fundamentally depend on MRI demonstrations of disease dissemination in space and time, enabling earlier diagnosis and treatment initiation compared to previous iterations [21, 25, 37, 46].

MRI biomarkers encompass lesion topography, morphological characteristics, and enhancement patterns that correlate with clinical phenotypes and disability progression [3, 20]. However, conventional manual lesion segmentation faces critical limitations including time-intensive workflows, operator dependency, and significant inter- and intra-rater variability, compromising reproducibility in both clinical trials and routine practice [45]. These limitations necessitate automated approaches for large-scale neuroimaging studies and clinical implementation [19].

Deep learning advances in MS neuroimaging

Deep learning architectures have transformed medical image analysis, offering automated lesion detection and quantification capabilities. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly the U-Net architecture, have established themselves for biomedical image segmentation through their contracting-expanding design that captures multi-scale anatomical features [41]. The integration of attention mechanisms has enhanced performance by enabling selective focus on diagnostically relevant structures while suppressing background noise [35].

In MS applications, deep learning approaches have demonstrated consistent advantages over traditional methods [7]. Brosch et al., [6] established 3D CNN architectures with multiscale feature integration, while subsequent work has addressed scattered lesion distribution through attention mechanisms and ensemble approaches [10, 44]. Comparative evaluations across architectures including U-Net variants, DeepLabV3Plus, and transformer-based models have revealed architecture-specific advantages for different segmentation challenges [10, 23]. Recent advances have achieved superior precision rates using single-modality FLAIR sequences, supporting clinical accessibility [8, 55].

Population-specific research gaps

Despite technical advances, significant gaps persist in population-specific MS research. Multiple sclerosis demonstrates marked geographical variation, with Iran exhibiting elevated prevalence rates. Meta-analytical evidence reveals pooled MS prevalence of 100 per 100,000 in Iran, substantially exceeding global estimates of 35.9 per 100,000 [32, 57]. Regional studies demonstrate varying rates across Iranian cities, establishing the country as a medium-to-high risk region with rising temporal trends [5, 17, 18, 42]. However, Middle Eastern populations remain underrepresented in large-scale neuroimaging.

Understanding what distinguishes this population is essential for interpreting neuroimaging findings. Genetically, the HLA-DRB1*1501–DQB1*0602 haplotype constitutes a particularly strong susceptibility haplotype in Iranian patients (OR = 7.79), with additional risk conferred by DRB1*03 and DRB1*04 and protection by DRB1*07 and DRB1*11 — a pattern broadly paralleling, yet distinct from, Northern European profiles [2, 30]. Gene–gene interactions between HLA-DRB1*1501 and cytokine polymorphisms further amplify susceptibility and may modulate lesion accumulation in this population [33]. Clinically, the MENACTRIMS registry (n = 6,885) established that MS in the MENA region presents approximately four to five years earlier than in Western cohorts, with a more aggressive course and earlier disability accrual — implying that age-stratified neuroimaging norms derived from Western datasets may systematically underestimate lesion burden at equivalent age strata in Iranian patients [47, 58]. Environmentally, paradoxical vitamin D deficiency — driven by intentional limited sun exposure and low dietary intake — is prevalent in this population and has been associated with higher MRI T2 lesion load; case-control data from East Azerbaijan, the region of the present cohort, confirm significantly higher vitamin D deficiency rates in MS patients versus healthy controls [1, 24]. Finally, multifactorial diagnostic barriers including social stigma, inconsistent application of McDonald criteria, and limited specialist access contribute to an estimated mean diagnostic delay of 7 months in Iran, meaning patients in this cohort are more likely to present for neuroimaging at advanced disease stages [28, 34].

This underrepresentation is compounded by limited clinical integration of automated MS lesion detection tools, with systematic reviews revealing that only 3% achieve clinical validation and merely one tool reaching clinical implementation [52]. Furthermore, persistent misapplication of McDonald 2017 criteria underscores the need for standardized, automated diagnostic support in this setting [51].

Study rationale and objectives

This study addresses gaps in population-specific MS neuroimaging through comprehensive statistical characterization of brain structural changes in an underrepresented Middle Eastern population. By evaluating multiple deep learning architectures and integrating optimal model selection with statistical methodology, we enable automated, reproducible analysis of a large-scale database comprising 1,381 subjects from Northwest Iran.

Primary objective

To perform detailed statistical characterization of brain structural changes, specifically white matter hyperintensities and ventricular patterns, in MS patients compared to healthy controls using automated deep learning-based segmentation.

Secondary objectives

(1) Evaluate multiple deep learning architectures for automated segmentation of both ventricular regions and white matter hyperintensities, and select the optimal model based on validation performance; (2) Quantify lesion burden across neuroanatomically-informed classifications including periventricular, deep, and juxtacortical lesions; (3) Analyze ventricular patterns as potential biomarkers; (4) Identify gender and age-specific differences in lesion distribution patterns; and (5) Establish preliminary population-specific reference ranges for clinical and research applications.

This analysis provides population-specific normative values that may serve as reference data for future clinical studies and genetic-neuroimaging investigations in this underrepresented population.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted with institutional review board approval from Tabriz University of Medical Sciences Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and comprehensive data anonymization protocols were implemented to ensure patient confidentiality, with subjects identifiable only through anonymized patient IDs. The study cohort comprised 1,381 subjects recruited between 2021 and 2024: 1,000 healthy controls (HC) acquired during 2021–2022, and 381 MS patients recruited from 2021 to 2024. All participants were scanned at Golghasht Medical Imaging Center, Tabriz, Iran, using standardized MRI protocols. HC: Absence of neurological disorders confirmed by clinical history and normal neurological examination. MS Patients: Clinically definite MS diagnosis according to revised McDonald 2017 criteria. Clinical records confirmed the diagnosis but did not systematically include patient history; these variables are therefore not available for analysis in this retrospective cohort. Subjects were excluded for MRI contraindications, presence of other neurological comorbidities, significant motion artifacts compromising image quality, or scanner-related anomalies detected during quality control assessment.

MRI datasets and ground truth

Local data acquisition

All imaging was performed using a 1.5-Tesla TOSHIBA Vantage scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Japan) with standardized acquisition protocols. T1-weighted sequence (standard spin-echo, SE): TR = 540 ms, TE = 15 ms, flip angle = 70°, field of view = 230 × 230 mm², slice thickness = 6 mm, voxel size = 0.45 × 0.45 mm², acquisition matrix = [0, 256, 176, 0]. T2-weighted sequence: TR = 4,800 ms, TE = 105 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 230 × 230 mm², number of slices = 20, slice thickness = 5 mm, voxel size = 0.33 × 0.33 mm², acquisition matrix = [0, 352, 256, 0]. T2-FLAIR sequence: TR = 10,000 ms, TE = 100 ms, TI = 2,500 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 230 × 230 mm², slice thickness = 6 mm, voxel size = 0.9 × 0.9 mm², acquisition matrix = [0, 256, 192, 0]. All images were acquired in the axial plane following standardized positioning protocols.

Ground truth annotation process

Manual segmentation was performed on FLAIR images by a board-certified neuroradiologist with over 20 years of expertise, with assistance of a trained medical imaging specialist. A custom Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) facilitated the annotation process, displaying T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences simultaneously to enable cross-modal verification. One hundred MS patients were randomly selected for manual annotation, with three distinct classes delineated: (1) background, (2) ventricles, and (3) abnormal white matter hyperintensities (WMH). The segmentation methodology followed a systematic two-step approach: Ventricle Segmentation: Initial manual delineation of ventricular boundaries, followed by statistical morphological post-processing for refinement and final manual verification. Abnormal WMH Segmentation: Identification of hyperintense regions representing MS lesions with subsequent post-processing and expert verification following the same refinement protocol as ventricular segmentation [9]. Figure 1 demonstrates samples of expert annotations, showing ventricular regions and abnormal WMH lesions. Manual segmentation was performed on raw, acquired images without any preprocessing.

Public dataset

To enhance the generalizability and robustness of our findings, we incorporated the publicly available MSSEG 2016 challenge [12]. This dataset comprises 15 MS patients’ MRI data acquired from 3 different imaging centers with standardized acquisition protocols. The original MSSEG 2016 dataset provides expert-annotated ground truth segmentations only for white matter hyperintensities (MS lesions). Since our methodology requires simultaneous segmentation of both ventricles and WMH, we supplemented this dataset by having our annotating board manually segment the ventricular regions using the same protocol described in Sect. "Ground truth annotation process". This comprehensive annotation enables evaluation of our proposed multi-class segmentation approach across diverse imaging conditions and patient populations.

Preprocessing pipeline

The preprocessing workflow comprised four sequential steps to standardize image characteristics across the dataset: (1) Noise Reduction: A 3 × 3 median filter to suppress salt-and-pepper noise followed by selective Gaussian smoothing (σ = 1.0) to reduce remaining artifacts without compromising structural detail. (2) Intensity Normalization: Slice-wise z-score standardization applied independently to each 2D axial slice, providing robust standardization across inter- and intra-patient variability and correcting for the systematic variation in tissue composition and expected intensity distribution across anatomical levels inherent to thick-slice FLAIR acquisitions. For each slice, the mean intensity is subtracted and the result is divided by the standard deviation of that slice. This normalization is applied exclusively for model input conditioning; all quantitative measurements are derived from the original non-normalized images after binary segmentation masks are generated, preserving the statistical distribution of the source data throughout downstream analysis. (3) Final Matrix Standardization: Resizing to 256 × 256 pixels in float format, preserving original tissue contrast characteristics for subsequent deep learning analysis. This restrained preprocessing approach supports fair comparative analysis by limiting excessive manipulation while still providing clean, standardized inputs for downstream model training, inference, and analysis.

Deep learning architectures

Architecture selection and implementation

Four established architectures were implemented to evaluate performance across different computational paradigms for simultaneous ventricle and white matter hyperintensity segmentation, enabling objective model selection through comparative analysis.

U-Net Architecture

The baseline encoder-decoder network featured symmetric contracting and expansive pathways with skip connections [41]. The encoder incorporated four downsampling blocks (3 × 3 convolutions, ReLU activation, 2 × 2 max pooling) with progressive feature channel expansion (64, 128, 256, 512). The decoder mirrored this structure using transposed convolutions for upsampling. Skip connections preserved spatial information essential for precise boundary delineation, with progressive dropout regularization (0.1–0.3) applied throughout network depth.

Attention U-Net

Enhanced the standard U-Net through attention gate incorporation that selectively focused on target structures while suppressing irrelevant features [35]. Attention mechanisms utilized gating signals from coarser scales to emphasize salient features at higher resolutions. Attention coefficients (α) were computed using 1 × 1 convolutions and sigmoid activation, enabling spatial attention maps that highlighted pathological hyperintensities while reducing false positives from normal periventricular areas. The network accepted preprocessed FLAIR images (256 × 256 slices) and generated multi-class segmentation masks for background, ventricles, and abnormal white matter hyperintensities.

DeepLabV3Plus

Employed atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) for multi-scale contextual information extraction without resolution degradation [11]. The encoder utilized dilated convolutions with rates {1, 6, 12, 18} for multi-scale feature extraction, followed by encoder-decoder architecture with low-level feature integration. The ResNet-50 backbone provided robust feature extraction capabilities suitable for variable lesion sizes and ventricular morphologies.

Trans-U-Net

Combined convolutional neural networks with transformer self-attention mechanisms for global context modeling [10]. The architecture employed CNN encoding (matching U-Net structure with dropout regularization) for low-level feature extraction, followed by transformer bottleneck with 768 dimensions and 4 multi-head attention layers (8 heads each). Positional encoding was applied to 16 × 16 feature patches processed as sequences, integrating CNN efficiency with transformer long-range dependency modeling.

Training protocol

Dataset composition

The training dataset integrated manually segmented data from two sources: 100 randomly selected and annotated MS patients from our local dataset [9] and 15 patients from the MSSEG dataset. This multi-center approach enhanced model generalizability across acquisition protocols and population demographics. Patient-level stratified splitting prevented data leakage between sets: Training set (80 patients from local dataset and 9 patients from MSSEG dataset, accumulating 2,050 images), Validation set (10 patients from local dataset and 3 patients from MSSEG dataset, accumulating 350 images), Testing set (10 patients from local dataset and 3 patients from MSSEG dataset, accumulating 350 images). All four architectures operate on individual 2D axial slices; the image counts reflect this slice-level input. The local dataset contributes approximately 20 brain-covering slices per patient (non-brain slices, including neck and empty slices, were excluded), yielding approximately 1,600 images from the 80 local training patients. The MSSEG dataset, acquired at approximately one-fifth the slice thickness of the local protocol, contains 150–200 usable slices per patient; to avoid near-duplicate slices and to match the effective inter-slice spacing of the local data, approximately 50 evenly-spaced slices were selected per MSSEG patient, yielding approximately 450 images from the 9 MSSEG training patients. Patient-level stratified splitting was applied throughout, ensuring all slices from a given patient remained within the same partition and preventing data leakage between sets. Following architecture selection based on validation performance, the best-performing model was applied to automatically segment the entire cohort of 1,381 subjects (1,000 HC + 381 MS patients) for comprehensive population analysis.

Training hyperparameters

Consistent hyperparameters ensured fair comparison across architectures. Training utilized Adam optimizer with initial learning rate 2 × 10⁻⁴, β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, and weight decay = 1 × 10⁻⁴. Batch size was constrained to 4 by GPU memory limitations for 60 epochs with early stopping (patience = 10 epochs). Learning rate scheduling employed ReduceLROnPlateau with reduction factor = 0.5, patience = 5 epochs, and minimum learning rate = 1 × 10⁻⁷.

Hybrid loss function strategy

A two-stage loss function approach was implemented to optimize training stability and convergence. The initial 15 epochs employed weighted categorical cross-entropy loss to establish stable convergence toward optimal parameter space. Class weights were computed using inverse class frequency normalization to address imbalance between background, ventricular, and lesion pixels. Following this stabilization phase, unified focal loss [59] was employed for epochs 16–60 to enhance learning of challenging examples and refine segmentation boundaries. This hybrid approach leveraged the rapid convergence properties of weighted cross-entropy while capitalizing on focal loss’s ability to focus on hard-to-classify pixels and class boundary refinement.

Neuroanatomical lesion classification

The WMH mask underwent neuroanatomically-informed classification into three clinically significant subcategories according to standardized MS lesion topography criteria established by the Consortium of MS Centers and International Panel on MS Diagnosis [4, 54]. This classification methodology implemented Euclidean distance calculations from ventricle and gray matter boundaries to ensure anatomically accurate categorization. Gray matter masks were extracted directly from preprocessed FLAIR images by our previously trained model [7].

Periventricular White Matter Hyperintensities (PEWMH)

Lesions with closest boundary distance ≤3 mm from ventricular surface, approximating periventricular locations relevant to McDonald 2017 criteria while accommodating 6 mm slice thickness constraints [14, 29, 31, 53].

Juxtacortical White Matter Hyperintensities (JCWMH)

Lesions within 3 mm of estimated gray-white matter junction with cross-sectional area ≤ 20 mm², approximating juxtacortical locations given imaging resolution limitations [48].

Deep White Matter Hyperintensities (DWMH)

Remaining pathological hyperintensities not meeting periventricular or juxtacortical classification criteria, representing lesions in deep white matter parenchyma distant from ventricular and cortical boundaries.

The classification algorithm processed each identified WMH object through sequential distance calculations and morphometric assessments, storing results with spatial coordinates for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis framework

Our detailed analytical strategy incorporated multi-dimensional statistical approaches to examine age-stratified patterns and gender-specific contributions across neuroimaging measures. Age stratification was systematically implemented using intervals of (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60 + years) to capture developmental and degenerative processes throughout the adult lifespan.

Demographic analysis

Participant stratification into discrete age cohorts facilitated the examination of age-related neuroanatomical patterns throughout the adult lifespan. Gender distribution was analyzed within each age stratum for both HC and MS patients. Descriptive statistics were computed for continuous variables (mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed data) and categorical variables (frequencies and percentages). Gender distribution analysis included calculation of female-to-male ratios and Chi-square testing to evaluate distribution differences between groups.

Primary outcome measures

Ventricular area analysis

All structural measurements in this study were computed on a 2D slice-by-slice basis. Ventricular area was quantified as the sum of segmented ventricular pixels across all brain-covering axial slices for each subject, expressed in mm². Volumetric estimation was deliberately avoided given the 5–6 mm axial slice thickness. Also, through-plane interpolation here introduces reconstruction errors. Area-based measurements normalized by the corresponding brain area provide internally consistent measures appropriate for population-level comparative analysis and trend identification. Total ventricular area was normalized by total brain area (TBA), derived from brain-mask segmentation using the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) from FSL (FMRIB’s Software Library) [50], to obtain a brain-normalized ventricular fraction. As a sensitivity analysis, ventricular area was additionally normalized by total skull-mask area (obtained via morphological head-mask segmentation) to account for the potential confound of age-related brain atrophy artificially inflating brain-normalized ratios. Age-related ventricular enlargement was modeled using second-order polynomial regression to capture non-linear trajectories across the lifespan; trend lines were fitted over the 18–59 year age range to avoid instability from the sparse 60 + year stratum.

Statistical comparisons between MS and HC groups employed independent samples t-tests, justified by both the large sample sizes in each group (n > 50, such that the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) guarantees approximate normality of sample means) and the coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean < 1) of ventricular measurements, indicating that the mean is a meaningful summary statistic. Shapiro-Wilk tests were additionally applied for descriptive reporting of distributional characteristics. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d.

Total lesion area analysis

WMH area was similarly computed as summed 2D pixel counts across all brain-covering axial slices (see Sect. "Ventricular area analysis" for rationale). WMH area was normalized by TBA (brain-mask area) as the primary measure, with skull-mask normalization applied as a sensitivity analysis as described in Sect. "Ventricular area analysis". Age-related lesion area was modeled using polynomial regression with the same age-range restriction.

Test selection for HC vs. MS group comparisons followed an adaptive two-criterion procedure: (1) if both groups had n ≥ 50 and CV was below 1.0 in both groups, an independent t-test was applied (CLT applies and the mean is a meaningful central tendency); (2) if CV ≥ 1.0 in either group — indicating a severely right-skewed, near-exponential distribution for which the mean is not a meaningful summary — Mann-Whitney U tests were applied regardless of sample size. Shapiro-Wilk tests were run for descriptive reporting but did not serve as the test-selection gate at large sample sizes, where they detect trivially small deviations from normality with near-certainty. WMH area and ratio measures, which have CV ≥ 1 due to their highly right-skewed distributions (many patients with near-zero area, few with very high area), were analyzed with Mann-Whitney U tests; rank-biserial correlation (r ≈ Z/√N) was used as the effect size measure. Measures, which have CV < 1, were analyzed with independent t-tests; Cohen’s d was used as the effect size measure.

Lesion subgroup analysis

Proportional analysis of PEWMH, DWMH, and JCWMH contributions examined gender-specific distribution patterns in both absolute areas and lesion-proportion ratios. The same two-criterion test-selection procedure (Sect. "Total lesion area analysis") was applied for gender comparisons within MS patients: normality was assessed via Shapiro-Wilk; for PEWMH, where both male and female distributions were confirmed as normal, an independent t-test was used with Cohen’s d as the effect size; for DWMH and JCWMH, where at least one group showed non-normal distribution, Mann-Whitney U tests were applied with rank-biserial correlation (r) as the effect size. Bonferroni correction was applied for three simultaneous subtype comparisons (α = 0.017).

Advanced statistical methods

Correlation matrices were computed for both HC and MS groups across seven variables: ventricular ratio, WMH ratio, periventricular WMH ratio, deep WMH ratio, juxtacortical WMH ratio, patient age, and patient sex. Spearman correlations were used throughout, as confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk normality assessment. Multiple comparison corrections used Bonferroni adjustment: α = 0.01 for age-variable comparisons (five pairs) and α = 0.0024 for the full correlation matrix (21 pairs). Significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Effect sizes were interpreted according to established conventions: small (Cohen’s d = 0.2; rank-biserial r = 0.1), medium (Cohen’s d = 0.5; r = 0.3), and large (Cohen’s d = 0.8; r = 0.5) effects.

Implementation details

All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU (12GB VRAM), Intel Core i7-7700 K (8 cores), 64GB DDR4 workstation using TensorFlow 2.11 with CUDA 11.8. Training averaged 40–45 s per epoch and inference requiring 5 milliseconds per image. Essential libraries included tensorflow, nibabel, scikit-learn, scipy, scikit-image, and opencv. Post-processing employed morphological operations with 4-pixel minimum object threshold and disk-shaped kernel (radius = 1). Reproducibility was ensured through fixed random seeds and JSON-logged experimental configurations with version control. Complete implementation is available at https://github.com/Mahdi-Bashiri/MS-DeepBrain-Study.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study comprised 1,381 participants, including 1000 HC and 381 MS patients recruited between 2021 and 2024. The overall cohort demonstrated a female predominance (68.6%, n = 946) compared to males (31.4%, n = 435) across both study groups (Table 1). Gender distribution analysis revealed similar patterns between groups, with HC participants showing 67.3% female representation (n = 673) versus 32.7% male (n = 327), while MS patients exhibited 71.7% female (n = 273) versus 28.3% male (n = 108) distribution, consistent with the known female predominance in MS epidemiology. Chi-square analysis indicated no statistically significant difference in gender proportions between HC and MS groups (χ²=2.23, p = 0.136).

HC participants exhibited mean ages of 34.8 ± 10.8 years for males and 34.7 ± 9.8 years for females. The overlapping age distributions across all demographic subgroups ensured adequate age-matching for subsequent neuroimaging analyses, with median ages ranging from 35.0 to 37.0 years across groups.

Four deep learning architectures were evaluated on the independent test set using 4-fold cross-validation for automated neuroanatomical segmentation. Table 2 presents performance metrics for ventricular and white matter hyperintensity segmentation across all models.

All architectures demonstrated higher accuracy for ventricular segmentation compared to WMH segmentation (DSC ranges: 90.1–93.0% vs. 77.6–84.7%), reflecting the heterogeneous nature of MS lesion morphology.

Ventricular area analysis

Age-stratified ventricular analysis revealed distinct patterns between HC and MS groups across five age cohorts (Fig. 3). Absolute ventricular area measurements demonstrated progressive age-related enlargement in both groups, with HC participants showing mean areas ranging from 2,356.0 mm² (males: 2,562.8 mm²; females: 2,245.0 mm²) in the 18–29 years cohort to 2,857.7 mm² (males: 3,501.4 mm²; females: 2,535.8 mm²) in the 50–59 years cohort, while MS patients exhibited substantially higher values, ranging from 3070.4 mm² (males: 3,440.0 mm²; females: 2,894.8 mm²) to 4,873.6 mm² (males: 5,135.1 mm²; females: 4,778.6 mm²) across corresponding age groups. Gender stratification revealed males contributing proportionally more to combined ventricular area in all age groups, particularly evident in the 50–59 years cohort. Brain-mask normalized ventricular ratio analysis controlled for individual brain size variations (Fig. 3, second column). HC ratios ranged from 1.06% (males: 1.07%; females: 1.06%) in the 18–29 years cohort to 1.32% (males: 1.51%; females: 1.23%) in the 50–59 years cohort. MS patients showed consistently higher ratios, ranging from 1.39% (males: 1.47%; females: 1.36%) to 2.37% (males: 2.37%; females: 2.37%) across age groups, representing a 1.7-fold age-related increase. As a sensitivity analysis addressing the potential confound of age-related brain atrophy artificially inflating brain-normalized ratios, ventricular areas were additionally normalized by total skull mask area (Fig. 3, third column). HC skull-normalized ratios ranged from 0.58% (males: 0.59%; females: 0.57%) in the 18–29 years cohort to 0.69% (males: 0.79%; females: 0.64%) in the 50–59 years cohort. MS ratios under this normalization ranged from 0.74% (males: 0.77%; females: 0.72%) to 1.19% (males: 1.20%; females: 1.19%) across age groups. The directional pattern of group differences was consistent between both normalization approaches, supporting the robustness of the observed ventricular area differences independent of the denominator choice.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study presents deep learning-based neuroanatomical profiling of multiple sclerosis in 1,381 subjects from a Middle Eastern population. Comparative evaluation of four deep learning architectures across four-fold cross-validation identified U-Net as the best-performing model for automated segmentation, demonstrating the highest overall WMH lesion-level detection accuracy (L-F1 = 77.4 ± 1.4%) alongside competitive boundary agreement (DSC = 88.8 ± 0.5%, HD95 = 2.8 ± 0.0 mm), and was therefore selected for population-level analysis.

MS patients showed substantially elevated WMH area compared to healthy controls, with MS age-group mean WMH ratios ranging from 0.315% (18–29 years) to 0.749% (50–59 years), representing an approximately 2.4-fold age-related increase within the MS cohort; sensitivity analyses using skull-mask normalization confirmed this pattern is not attributable to age-related brain atrophy artificially deflating the denominator. Between-group testing confirmed highly significant differences (Mann-Whitney U = 11,963.0, p < 10⁻⁶, rank-biserial correlation r = 0.310), indicating a moderate effect magnitude. Normalized lesion ratios in HC remained consistently near 0.02% across all age strata, underscoring the pathological nature of lesion accumulation in MS. These findings provide normative baselines that may support therapeutic monitoring in Middle Eastern populations.

Periventricular white matter hyperintensities constituted 53.91 ± 20.62% of total lesion area, with age-group means increasing from 49.15% (18–29 years) to 63.49% (50–59 years), consistent with the periventricular predominance criterion of the McDonald 2017 diagnostic framework [53]. Gender stratification revealed no statistically significant differences in lesion subtype distribution for any anatomical category after Bonferroni correction (PEWMH: t = 0.524, p = 0.600, Cohen’s d = 0.06; DWMH: U = 15,273, p = 0.584; JCWMH: U = 13,515.5, p = 0.205). These results indicate that periventricular predominance is a consistent feature of MS pathology in this cohort, independent of sex. It should be noted that juxtacortical lesion proportions (6.71 ± 9.61%) are likely underestimated in this study due to partial volume effects associated with the 5–6 mm slice thickness, which disproportionately affects detection of small lesions at the gray–white matter junction; these findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Comparative analysis and population context

The female predominance in our MS cohort (71.7%, ratio 2.53:1) is consistent with global epidemiological patterns reporting female-to-male ratios of approximately 2–3:1 in MS [27]. The mean age (37.4 ± 10.2 years) corresponds with typical disease presentation, while our extended age range to 68 years enabled examination of long-term lesion accumulation patterns.

Lesion area

Our MS cohort demonstrated brain-normalized WMH ratios progressing from 0.315% to 0.749% across age strata (18–29 to 50–59 years), representing a 2.4-fold increase with age and a mean absolute lesion area of 1,234.9 ± 1,490.8 mm² across all MS patients. While direct numerical comparison with volumetric Western datasets is methodologically constrained by measurement dimensionality differences, contextual reference points are informative. In a North American RRMS cohort (mean age 32.5 years, disease duration-matched), adult T2 lesion volume was reported at approximately 10.0 ± 3.4 cm³ [22]. European early-MS cohorts similarly report mean periventricular lesion volumes around 2,287 µl per patient at disease onset [38]. The substantially higher mean absolute lesion area in our older age strata is consistent with cumulative disease progression and is in line with evidence that lesion accumulation is age-dependent in MS [22], though the earlier mean age of disease onset in the Iranian population — approximately 4–5 years earlier than Western estimates — likely contributes to elevated cumulative area at equivalent age strata [58]. Importantly, HC WMH ratios in our cohort remained near 0.01% across all age groups, consistent with the established near-zero WMH area expected in healthy young-to-middle-aged adults without vascular risk factors, confirming the pathological rather than vascular origin of WMH in the MS group.

Periventricular predominance

Periventricular white matter hyperintensities constituted 53.91 ± 20.62% of total lesion area in our cohort, with age-group means rising from 49.15% (18–29 years) to 63.49% (50–59 years). This periventricular predominance is consistent with patterns reported in Western early MS cohorts, where periventricular lesion volume consistently exceeds that of other topographic regions despite subcortical lesions being more numerous by count [4, 38]. The progressive age-related increase in periventricular proportion observed in our cohort — a 1.3-fold rise across age strata —and the concomitant proportional decline in deep contribution (from 41.67% to 30.78%) suggest a topographic redistribution of lesion area toward periventricular regions with advancing age. This parallels the inverse age correlation with new periventricular lesion formation reported in European longitudinal MS data, suggesting that once established, periventricular lesions accumulate and coalesce preferentially with advancing age across different populations [38]. Gender stratification in our cohort revealed no significant differences in lesion subtype distribution (PEWMH: p = 0.600; DWMH: p = 0.584; JCWMH: p`=0.205), consistent with Western studies reporting sex-independent anatomical distribution patterns in MS lesion topography [20]. Lesion subtype proportions are dimensionless ratios of subtype area to total lesion area, and therefore cancel out absolute head size differences between sexes; systematic gender differences in head size do not confound these comparisons

Ventricular area

Ventricular area in our MS patients was significantly higher than in HC (3,812.7 ± 1,791.1 mm² vs. 2,559.6 ± 946.9 mm²; Cohen’s d = 1.012), with brain-normalized ratios 1.7-fold higher in MS across age strata. This large effect size aligns with established evidence from European and North American cohorts that ventricular enlargement represents a robust marker of brain atrophy in RRMS, and that the degree of ventricular expansion scales with disease duration and disability accumulation [19, 36]. In healthy controls, longitudinal studies in Western populations report lateral ventricle volume increase of approximately 3% per year with normal aging [26], while our HC brain-normalized ventricular ratios showed a more modest age-related increase (1.06% to 1.32% from the youngest to oldest age strata), consistent with expected physiological aging. The weaker age-correlation in HC (r = 0.207) versus MS (r = 0.403) is in agreement with reports that ventricular enlargement is accelerated and disease-driven in MS beyond the trajectory of normal aging [19].

Age-related progression patterns

The stronger age-related correlations observed in MS patients versus HC across both ventricular (r = 0.403 vs. 0.207) and WMH measures (r = 0.266 vs. 0.085) confirm disease-specific acceleration of neuroanatomical change. The negative age–deep WMH correlation in MS (r = − 0.193) alongside the positive age–periventricular correlation (r = + 0.243) is consistent with the topographic redistribution pattern described above, and may reflect progressive enlargement of periventricular lesions displacing the proportional contribution of deep white matter lesions. A qualitatively distinct finding is the strong positive ventricular–WMH coupling in MS (r = + 0.551) versus the weak negative relationship in HC (r = − 0.124), indicating that ventricular enlargement and lesion accumulation represent coupled pathological processes in MS rather than independent phenomena. This coupling has been reported in Western MS cohorts in the context of periventricular tissue loss and atrophic expansion of the ventricular system driven by adjacent white matter damage [16, 38]. The weak negative ventricular–WMH relationship in HC (r = − 0.124) is likely attributable to partial volume effects at narrow ventricular margins in FLAIR imaging: in subjects with less prominent ventricular boundaries, suppressed CSF signal is replaced by a hyperintense rim that may be classified as WMH, creating a spurious inverse association in subjects with near-zero true lesion burden. The stronger Peri–Deep WMH inter-subtype correlation in MS (r = − 0.886 vs. HC r = − 0.290) further reflects the wider dynamic range of lesion topographic distribution in MS, amplifying the compositional constraint inherent to proportional subtype measures.

Population-specific context

While the patterns described above are broadly consistent with Western MS neuroimaging literature, the specific numerical normative values established in this study — WMH ratios of 0.315%–0.749% and brain-normalized ventricular ratios of 1.39%–2.37% across age strata in an Iranian MS cohort — are not directly derivable from existing Western datasets. Given the earlier mean onset age, the distinct HLA-DRB1 immunogenetic background, and the specific environmental factors characterizing this population [30, 58], population-specific normative databases are necessary to avoid systematic under- or over-estimation of disease burden in clinical practice.

Technical performance and methodological considerations

Comparative evaluation of four architectures (U-Net, Attention U-Net, Trans-U-Net, DeepLabV3Plus) via four-fold cross-validation identified U-Net as the best-performing model. Although more architecturally sophisticated models — particularly Trans-U-Net and Attention U-Net, which incorporate transformer self-attention and spatial attention mechanisms respectively — achieved competitive pixel-level DSC values (87.8% and 86.9%), U-Net demonstrated superior lesion-level detection accuracy and lower cross-fold variance. This outcome is consistent with the well-documented phenomenon that additional architectural complexity does not always confer advantages when training data volume is limited. Thereby, our training set may be insufficient to realize the full representational capacity of transformer-based architectures, which are known to require substantially larger datasets to outperform convolutional baselines [15]. The 6 mm axial slice thickness and 2D acquisition also limit the spatial context available to architectures designed for volumetric inputs, further favoring the simpler U-Net design. Performance is comparable to established MS segmentation tools, with the multi-center training dataset (local cohort plus MSSEG) enhancing model generalizability across acquisition protocols.

Study limitations

The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of individual disease trajectories. The 5–6 mm axial slice thickness represents the primary imaging limitation of this study. This protocol reflects standard clinical practice at 1.5T centers in the region, where acquisition time and patient throughput constraints preclude thinner-slice research-grade protocols. The thick slice acquisition precludes reliable 3D volumetric reconstruction, which is why area-based measurements were adopted throughout. This choice is methodologically appropriate for population-level trend identification but limits absolute volumetric precision and may lead to partial volume effects, particularly for smaller juxtacortical lesions. Detection sensitivity for this subtype may therefore be underestimated. The imaging protocol does not conform to MAGNIMS or NAIMS guideline recommendations for MS research (typically 1–3 mm slice thickness), and normative values derived here should be interpreted within the context of this acquisition constraint. Integration of T1-weighted sequences, which could provide additional atrophy and black hole characterization, was precluded by inter-sequence registration challenges inherent to the retrospective acquisition protocol with differing spatial parameters across sequence types. Regarding generalizability to higher-resolution data: as the models operate slice-by-slice in 2D, they are architecturally compatible with thinner-slice acquisitions; however, fine-tuning on higher-resolution data would be required before deployment in such settings. The test set used for deep learning model evaluation was drawn from the same data pool as the training set, with independence maintained at the patient level through stratified splitting. While this prevents data leakage, including independent external multi-center datasets can improve generalizability of the segmentation pipeline.

Infratentorial lesions may be underrepresented in FLAIR-based analysis, as cerebellar lesions are better visualized on T2 sequences. This represents a constraint in comprehensive MS lesion coverage given infratentorial locations’ diagnostic importance in McDonald 2017 criteria. The absence of clinical disability measures (EDSS, disease duration) limits direct clinical correlation analysis.

Future directions

Prospective validation using higher-resolution MRI protocols (≤ 3 mm slice thickness, isotropic acquisition) would enable volumetric rather than area-based analysis and strengthen the generalizability of the normative values established here. The normative WMH and ventricular ratio ranges can support MS monitoring in Iranian patients by providing age- and gender-stratified reference thresholds not derivable from existing Western datasets — the first such normative reference for this population. These thresholds are most actionable when evaluating treatment response, flagging atypical lesion accumulation in longitudinal follow-up, or calibrating disease severity in regional trials. Regarding segmentation readiness, the U-Net pipeline achieves research-grade performance (DSC = 88.8%, L-F1 = 77.4%); however, prospective validation — integrating local imaging workflows, radiologist review, and multi-site testing — is necessary before routine deployment [52]. Longitudinal studies incorporating disability measures (EDSS, disease duration) represent the essential next step to validate these thresholds prospectively, enable clinical correlation analysis, and support outcome prediction models for this population. Prospective data collection with spatially co-registered, isotropic multi-modal protocols will enable formal integration of T1-weighted sequences for cortical atrophy quantification and black hole identification, extending the neuroanatomical profiling framework established in this study.

Conclusion

This study presents deep learning-based neuroanatomical characterization of multiple sclerosis in a large Middle Eastern population, addressing gaps in global MS research demographics. Comparative evaluation of four architectures identified U-Net as optimal for automated lesion and ventricle segmentation in 1,381 subjects, leading to reveal population-specific patterns of brain changes. The findings provide preliminary population-specific reference ranges that may support therapeutic monitoring, demonstrating substantial lesion area disparities between MS patients and controls with periventricular predominance and gender-specific anatomical distributions. The 1.7- and 2.4-fold elevation in ventricular and lesion loads across age strata, coupled with age-related progression patterns, provides benchmarks for disease assessment in this underrepresented demographic. The automated analytical approach offers scalable solutions for large-scale neuroimaging studies. These population-specific biomarker profiles may contribute to future genetic-neuroimaging association studies and support precision medicine approaches in MS management. This work provides a methodological framework for similar investigations in other underrepresented populations, supporting more inclusive global MS research efforts.
