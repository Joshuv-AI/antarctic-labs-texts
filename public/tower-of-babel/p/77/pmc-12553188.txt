Anatomical–biochemical discordance and prognostic role of bone metastases in advanced MTC treated with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE
Authors: S. Bilgic, A. Nazari, M. S. Sağer, K. Sonmezoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare malignancy with limited treatment options in the metastatic setting. While peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE has been well established in gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, its clinical utility in MTC remains under investigation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 18 patients with advanced, somatostatin receptor–positive MTC treated with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE. Treatment decisions—including in patients with low somatostatin receptor (SSTR) expression or first-line PRRT indication—were made by a multidisciplinary tumor board. Anatomical response was assessed using RECIST 1.1 on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT after two cycles. Biochemical and clinical outcomes were also recorded. Progression-free survival (PFS) was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression.ResultsMedian PFS was 37.0 months. Radiologic disease control -defined as partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD) -was achieved in 78%, and biochemical response (≥ 50% decrease in calcitonin) in 44% of patients. Patients with bone metastases had significantly shorter PFS (24.6 vs. 47.0 months, p = 0.027). Longer PFS was also observed in those receiving higher cumulative dose, those with larger solitary tumors, and those treated in the first-line setting, although these trends did not reach statistical significance. Discordance between anatomical and biochemical response was observed in 50% of cases, highlighting limitations of calcitonin-based monitoring.ConclusionPRRT with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE is a well-tolerated treatment option for selected patients with advanced MTC, including those receiving first-line therapy and those with low SSTR expression. In our limited cohort, bone metastases were associated with shorter PFS, and discordant imaging/biochemical responses are common. Functional imaging–guided selection and individualized response assessment are essential for optimal management.

Introduction

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare neuroendocrine tumor originating from parafollicular C-cells and accounts for approximately 1–2% of all thyroid malignancies [1]. Despite its relatively indolent course in localized stages, advanced MTC often exhibits an aggressive clinical behavior, particularly in patients with distant metastases [2]. Current treatment options for metastatic MTC remain limited, and while multikinase inhibitors such as vandetanib and cabozantinib have demonstrated progression-free survival benefits, their use is frequently accompanied by significant toxicity [3]. Notably, grade 3–4 adverse events have been reported in up to 69% of patients treated with cabozantinib and 35% with vandetanib, highlighting the need for safer therapeutic alternatives [4–6].

Increasing interest has emerged in peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with radiolabeled somatostatin analogs, particularly [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, which has demonstrated efficacy in well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors [7]. Although MTC expresses somatostatin receptors (SSTR), the clinical evidence supporting the use of PRRT in this subgroup is still evolving, with heterogeneous response rates and limited prospective data [8]. In selected patients with progressive disease and high SSTR expression on functional imaging, [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE may offer a viable therapeutic alternative, particularly when standard options are exhausted.

Although radiological and biochemical responses to PRRT in MTC have been reported in earlier studies, median progression-free survival has frequently remained undefined, reflecting the rarity of the disease and limited longitudinal data [9, 10]. Biochemical tumor markers such as serum calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) are routinely used to monitor treatment response; however, their correlation with survival endpoints—particularly progression-free survival (PFS)—has been inconsistent. Furthermore, the impact of metastatic distribution, particularly bone involvement, on treatment outcomes in [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE–treated MTC has not been systematically evaluated.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the prognostic impact of bone metastases in [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE–treated MTC. In addition, we report the rate and clinical implications of anatomical–biochemical discordance using dual-tracer PET/CT, and provide real-world outcome data from patients with low SSTR expression (Krenning score 1) and those treated with PRRT as first-line systemic therapy.

In this study, we aimed to identify clinical and biochemical prognostic factors associated with survival in patients with advanced MTC treated with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE. We specifically investigated the impact of bone metastasis and assessed whether radiopharmaceutical dose intensity or biochemical response predicted progression-free survival.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This retrospective cohort included patients with advanced medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) who received their first cycle of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE therapy between 2012 and 2018. All patients were treated at a single tertiary care center and had either locally advanced or metastatic disease, corresponding to Stage IVB or IVC according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system [11]. None of the patients were candidates for curative intent therapies at the time of PRRT initiation. Primary thyroid surgeries had been performed between 2001 and 2016.

All included patients had histologically confirmed metastatic MTC, based on either excisional or core needle biopsy. Eligibility for PRRT was based on somatostatin receptor positivity on functional imaging, with all patients demonstrating a Krenning score of 1 to 4 on baseline SSTR imaging [12]. Eligibility required somatostatin receptor expression on functional imaging, with a Krenning score of ≥ 1 on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT. The distribution of Krenning scores was: score 1 in 7 patients (39%), score 2 in 3 patients (17%), score 3 in 4 patients (22%), and score 4 in 4 patients (22%). No patients were excluded due to insufficient uptake. Patients were followed until disease progression or last clinical contact.

Patients with a Krenning score of 1 were included only if [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT showed focal uptake and concurrent [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT revealed low or absent metabolic activity, suggesting an indolent tumor phenotype suitable for PRRT.

Treatment protocol

All patients received [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE therapy in a dedicated nuclear medicine unit following a standardized protocol. The radiopharmaceutical was administered intravenously over 20–30 min, with individual cycle activities typically ranging between 3.7 and 7.4 GBq (100–200 mCi), depending on institutional availability and patient characteristics. Patient-specific renal dosimetry was not routinely performed. Administered activity was guided by institutional practice, patient characteristics, and tolerability. A renal-protective amino acid solution containing lysine and arginine was co-infused, beginning 30 min prior to radiotracer injection and maintained for a total duration of 4 to 6 h.

The number of administered cycles varied between patients based on clinical indication and imaging response, with most patients receiving 2 to 4 cycles at intervals of approximately 8 to 12 weeks. After the initial two cycles, all patients underwent interim [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT for anatomical response assessment. In the absence of progressive disease, treatment was continued with additional cycles following the same protocol, typically administered in pairs. A maximum of 8 cycles was allowed. Patients were evaluated prior to each cycle for treatment eligibility using hematological, renal, and liver function tests. Concomitant medications such as antiemetics (e.g., ondansetron) and corticosteroids were administered as needed.

Post-treatment whole-body scintigraphy or SPECT/CT imaging was performed at 24 h after each administration to confirm adequate tracer biodistribution and exclude significant off-target uptake. In this study, [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE was administered with tumor control intent, aiming both to delay progression and to alleviate symptoms such as pain or fatigue where present. Treatment decisions—including continuation and cycle number—were guided by interim imaging and clinical evaluation.

Post-treatment evaluation

Imaging was performed using a Discovery PET/CT 710 scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT studies were acquired approximately 45–60 min after intravenous injection. Low-dose, non-contrast CT scans were obtained for attenuation correction and anatomical localization using the following parameters: tube voltage 130 kVp, current 48–76 mAs, 4.0 mm slice thickness, gantry rotation time 0.6 s, and collimator width 6 × 3 mm². Whole-body images were acquired from the vertex to the mid-thigh with 2 min per bed position. Image reconstruction was performed using an ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with 4 iterations and 8 subsets. All images were analyzed on a dedicated Advantage Workstation (Version 4.7, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA).

Treatment response was evaluated using anatomical and biochemical criteria. Anatomical response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, using [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT performed after the every second PRRT cycle [13], which has also been successfully applied in prior studies evaluating PRRT response in neuroendocrine tumors [14–17]. While all patients underwent both [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT imaging at baseline for disease characterization, only [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT was used for post-treatment response assessment. [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT served as a prognostic imaging modality and was not employed to evaluate treatment response.

Biochemical response was evaluated based on the percentage change in serum calcitonin levels from baseline to post-treatment values. Responses were categorized into three groups: Partial Response (PR) was defined as a ≥ 50% decrease from baseline; Stable Disease (SD) as a change between > − 50% and < + 25%; and Progressive Disease (PD) as a ≥ 25% increase compared to baseline levels [18]. All responses were confirmed by repeat measurements at least four weeks apart.

All three response domains (anatomical, metabolic, and biochemical) were reviewed independently, and progression-free survival was calculated based on radiologic criteria. In patients with new or worsening symptoms (e.g., pain, compressive complaints, or ECOG performance status decline), restaging [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT was performed earlier than scheduled. Radiologic progression, as defined by RECIST 1.1 criteria, was required for progression-free survival analysis in all cases [19]. Patients were considered evaluable if they had at least one measurable lesion on the low-dose CT component of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT. Target lesions were selected based on both anatomical dimensions and functional uptake characteristics. All patients included in the study had at least one target lesion, and RECIST 1.1 measurements were performed accordingly. For non-target lesions, progression was defined as unequivocal worsening or the appearance of new lesions. We acknowledge that non-contrast low-dose CT has inherent limitations in detecting liver or soft-tissue progression; However, this modality was chosen for its routine clinical use, favorable tumor detection rate [20].

Biochemical progression (e.g., isolated calcitonin increase) was noted in some patients but was not used as a sole criterion for defining disease progression, nor did it lead to treatment discontinuation in the absence of radiologic progression.

Radiologic progression was determined by two independent board-certified nuclear medicine physicians who were blinded to clinical history, treatment cycle, and biochemical markers. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Treatment decisions were made by the attending physicians based on the entirety of clinical and imaging data.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline clinical and demographic characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

For group comparisons between patients receiving high-dose or frequent PRRT cycles versus those receiving low-dose or wide-interval therapy, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the first cycle of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE to documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. PFS was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with log-rank tests used to compare survival curves between predefined subgroups (e.g., presence vs. absence of bone metastasis; high vs. low dose group). Median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate the association of clinical, biochemical, and imaging parameters with PFS. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and Wald p-values were reported.

Calcitonin response was analyzed both as a continuous percentage change and as a categorical variable, using predefined thresholds adapted from prior literature (≥ 50% decrease for partial response, ≥ 50% increase for progression). Associations between calcitonin dynamics and survival outcomes were assessed using non-parametric tests and survival modeling.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.5.0), and graphics were generated using the ggplot2 and survminer packages [21].

Results

The majority had Stage IVC disease (n = 14, 78%), while 4 patients (22%) had Stage IVB disease. Median time from initial diagnosis to PRRT was 44.5 months (IQR: 21.2–106.2). The most common sites of metastasis included bone (n = 12, 67%), cervical lymph nodes (n = 14, 78%), mediastinal lymph nodes (n = 5, 28%), liver (n = 4, 22%), and lung (n = 2, 11%). At baseline, 13 patients (72%) presented with clinical complaints such as pain or fatigue, while 5 patients (28%) were asymptomatic. Mean lesion count was 9.8 ± 7.2, and the mean baseline FDG SUVmax was 7.0 ± 4.3. The median size of the largest lesion per patient was 15 mm (IQR: 10.3–38.8), with a mean value of 25.4 ± 21.6 mm.

Six patients (33%) had received prior local therapies, and 4 patients (22%) had received systemic treatment before PRRT. Only one patient (6%) had a confirmed genetic mutation (MEN2A). The median follow-up duration was 34 months from the date of first PRRT administration.

Treatment exposure

Most patients received between 2 and 4 cycles of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE (median: 4), with 4 patients receiving 6 cycles and one patient receiving 8 cycles.

A total of 18 patients received a mean of 3.4 ± 1.8 cycles of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, with a cumulative administered activity of 22.2 ± 12.5 GBq (598.1 ± 337.2 mCi). The average dose per cycle was 6.6 ± 1.2 GBq (176.8 ± 32.0 mCi). The median treatment duration was 8.2 months (IQR: 3.4–13.0).

Toxicity

Treatment was well tolerated, with no CTCAE Grade ≥ 3 toxicities observed. CTCAE-based toxicity was observed in 11 patients (61%), and all events were Grade 1 or 2. Hepatotoxicity occurred in 7 patients (39%), thrombocytopenia in 5 (28%), leucopenia in 3 (17%), and anemia in 1 patient (6%). In terms of clinical adverse events, 14 patients (78%) reported no complaint. Hair loss was reported in 3 patients (16%) and jaw pain in 1 patient (6%). Response

Radiological response

Following therapy, radiologic response assessment revealed stable disease in 12 patients (67%), partial response in 2 patients (11%), and progressive disease in 4 patients (22%). Anatomical treatment response was assessed using RECIST 1.1 criteria on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT performed after the second PRRT cycle. In all patients, post-therapeutic [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE SPECT/CT was also acquired, allowing for qualitative assessment of radiopharmaceutical distribution and lesion targeting. Biochemical response

Biochemical response was evaluated based on percentage change in serum calcitonin from baseline. Partial biochemical response (≥ 50% decrease) was observed in 8 patients (44%), while 6 patients (33%) demonstrated stable levels (change between − 50% and + 25%). Progressive biochemical disease (≥ 25% increase) was seen in 4 patients (22%).

Log-transformed calcitonin changes revealed distinct patterns between treatment strategies. Patients receiving low-dose or wide-interval [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE therapy showed a median increase in calcitonin levels, whereas those treated with high-dose or more frequent administration experienced a notable decrease (Fig. 2A).

When stratified by bone metastasis status, patients without bone involvement exhibited greater reductions in calcitonin levels compared to those with skeletal metastases, who more frequently demonstrated biochemical progression or limited response (Fig. 2B).Fig. 2Biochemical response assessed by percent change in serum calcitonin. Symptom control

Clinically, 9 patients (50%) reported improvement in symptoms, 5 (28%) remained asymptomatic, and 4 (22%) experienced persistent complaints.

Prognostic factors

Kaplan-Meier subgroup analyses

The overall median PFS in this cohort was 37.0 months. A total of 9 patients experienced radiologic progression, and 3 of these subsequently died during follow-up (14.9–40.0 months after progression). No patient died without prior documented progression; therefore, all PFS events were defined by radiologic progression, in line with standard PFS definitions.

In univariate Kaplan-Meier analysis, the presence of bone metastasis was associated with significantly shorter progression-free survival (median PFS: 24.6 vs. 47.0 months, p = 0.027). Patients with a higher lesion number also showed inferior PFS compared to those with lower burden (median PFS: 26.0 vs. 47.0 months, p = 0.047).

Other variables exhibited non-significant but potentially meaningful trends. Patients with Stage 4 C disease had shorter median PFS compared to those with Stage 4B (26.0 vs. 47.0 months, p = 0.084). Similarly, patients who received higher total administered activity tended to have longer PFS than those in the low-dose group (47.0 vs. 26.0 months, p = 0.109). Larger lesion size was also associated with a numerically longer PFS (47.0 vs. 26.0 months, p = 0.164), although this finding did not reach statistical significance.

No significant differences in PFS were observed when patients were stratified by age group (< 55 vs. ≥55 years; 42.0 vs. 23.3 months, p = 0.375), sex (Female vs. Male; 37.0 months in both groups, p = 0.776), or presence of liver metastasis (37.0 months in both groups, p = 0.961). Likewise, FDG avidity (FDG SUVmax: Low vs. High; 44.3 vs. 37.0 months, p = 0.680) and history of locoregional recurrence (Absent vs. Present; 26.0 vs. 37.0 months, p = 0.655) were not significantly associated with PFS outcomes.

Patients who received PRRT as first-line systemic therapy (n = 14) demonstrated a longer median progression-free survival (47.0 months) compared to those who had received prior systemic therapy (31.5 months), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.136; Fig. 3E).Fig. 3Kaplan-Meier curves for progression-free survival (PFS) by clinical and treatment-related parameters. Univariate analysis summary

In univariate Cox regression analysis, bone metastasis was associated with a markedly higher risk of progression (HR: 7.609, 95% CI: 0.932–62.132, p = 0.058). Additionally, average dose per cycle demonstrated borderline significance (p = 0.058, respectively), suggesting a potential relationship with progression-free survival. Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of advanced medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), we demonstrate that [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) provides durable disease control with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 37.0 months. Radiological stabilization or response was achieved in 78% of patients, and biochemical response—defined by a ≥ 50% decline in serum calcitonin—was observed in 44%. PRRT was well tolerated, with no grade ≥ 3 toxicities reported. These findings suggest that PRRT may offer clinical benefit in selected patients with metastatic MTC in patients with progressive disease.

Among the clinical variables evaluated, the presence of bone metastases was the most significant predictor of progression-free survival (PFS). As demonstrated in Fig. 3A, patients without bone metastases had a markedly better PFS profile, with a median that was not reached during follow-up, while those with bone involvement experienced substantially earlier progression. This difference reached statistical significance (p = 0.027), suggesting that osseous disease may reflect a more aggressive biological behavior in advanced MTC. Similarly, a recent multicenter analysis by Liu et al. identified bone metastases as an independent adverse prognostic factor for overall survival in MTC patients treated with PRRT (HR 2.7, p = 0.017) [22]. Although their endpoint differed from ours, this finding reinforces the adverse prognostic impact of osseous disease and supports the consistency of our results with other contemporary cohorts.

Lesion number showed a trend-level association with progression-free survival, as demonstrated in Fig. 3B (p = 0.047). Patients with higher lesion counts appeared to experience longer PFS compared to those with fewer detectable lesions. This counterintuitive trend likely reflects selection bias—where patients with widespread but indolent, somatostatin receptor–positive disease were more often referred for PRRT—rather than a true prognostic relationship. Alternatively, lesion number alone may be a poor surrogate for total tumor burden, and factors such as metabolic activity or anatomical distribution may better reflect disease behavior. Given the small number of events and the resulting risk of statistical instability, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Disease stage demonstrated a modest trend toward prognostic significance in predicting PFS. As shown in Fig. 3C, patients with stage 4 C disease appeared to experience more frequent and earlier progression compared to those with stage 4B, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.084). While exploratory in nature, this trend may hold clinical relevance, as stage 4 C typically reflects extra-thoracic or disseminated disease and may be associated with greater tumor heterogeneity or resistance. The observed separation in survival curves suggests that AJCC staging could potentially aid in risk stratification for PRRT outcomes.

Total administered dose also demonstrated a trend toward improved PFS, as illustrated in Fig. 3D. Patients who received higher cumulative doses of [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE exhibited longer progression-free survival compared to those in the lower dose group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.11). This pattern may reflect the benefit of sustained treatment exposure in patients who tolerate PRRT well and can complete multiple cycles. Conversely, patients in the low-dose group may have experienced early progression or discontinued treatment prematurely due to clinical deterioration.

Discordance between anatomical and biochemical response

Interestingly, a notable discordance was observed between anatomical response assessed by [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE imaging and biochemical response based on calcitonin levels. As detailed in Table 4, 9 out of 18 patients (50%) demonstrated discordant findings between anatomical and biochemical response classifications. This included patients with radiologic disease control but concurrent biochemical progression, as well as cases with biochemical response in the absence of radiologic improvement. Importantly, this discordance was observed between [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT and calcitonin levels. This discordance highlights the limitations of calcitonin dynamics as a surrogate marker for clinical benefit in MTC patients undergoing PRRT. Similarly, Dadgar et al. reported no significant correlation between tumor marker expression (calcitonin and CEA) and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT positivity, number of detected lesions, or SUVmax [23]. Such patterns have also been reported in patients receiving systemic therapies, where transient elevations in calcitonin levels—despite radiologic stability—may reflect a flare phenomenon or non-progression-related fluctuation rather than true disease progression [24]. These findings support the need for multi-dimensional response assessment and caution against relying solely on calcitonin trends to evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings compare favorably with the retrospective series by Beukhof et al., in which 10 patients with metastatic MTC received ¹⁷⁷Lu-octreotate. In their cohort, only 4 patients (40%) achieved stable disease and the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 8.4 months, markedly shorter than the 37.0 months observed in our study [25]. Although both cohorts included patients with stage 4 disease and frequent bone metastases (67% vs. 60%), important differences may account for the disparity in outcomes. Notably, Beukhof’s cohort included patients with poor performance status (WHO 3 in 30%) and several patients received PRRT for symptom palliation. Furthermore, patient selection in their study was based on SSTR2A immunohistochemistry rather than functional uptake on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE imaging, which may have limited the predictive accuracy for treatment response.

When stratified by baseline functional imaging, patients with low Krenning scores (score 1) in our cohort demonstrated a numerically longer median PFS compared to those with higher scores (47.0 vs. 26.0 months, p = 0.125), although this did not reach statistical significance. This finding contrasts with the findings of de Vries et al., who reported that low SSTR2A expression on immunohistochemistry was associated with shorter survival in MTC [26]. The discrepancy may reflect fundamental differences between static tissue-based receptor quantification and in vivo functional imaging, as well as the biological heterogeneity of MTC, where low-intensity uptake on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT does not necessarily preclude clinically meaningful benefit from PRRT. Taken together, these methodological and biological differences in treatment indication, selection criteria, and patient characteristics may have contributed to the more favorable PFS and higher radiologic disease control rate observed in our cohort. Our findings also compare favorably with those of Parghane et al., who reported on 43 patients with metastatic MTC treated with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE [27]. Their cohort demonstrated a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 24 months (95% CI: 15.1–32.9), notably shorter than the 37.0 months observed in our study. Several key differences in baseline disease characteristics may account for this disparity. Parghane et al. included only symptomatic patients, with extensive disease burden (≥ 2 organ involvement in 79%), high FDG uptake, and markedly elevated tumor markers (mean baseline calcitonin ~ 13,000 pg/mL). In contrast, only 72% of our patients were symptomatic, and the mean baseline calcitonin level was considerably lower at 2162 ± 1512 pg/mL, suggesting a potentially less aggressive tumor biology. Moreover, while Parghane et al. found that patients with calcitonin doubling time >24 months had superior outcomes, our study did not stratify by doubling time but showed a lower rate of biochemical progression (22% vs. 49%). Importantly, both studies underscore the imperfect concordance between anatomical and biochemical response, and highlight the prognostic relevance of FDG uptake and calcitonin kinetics in guiding patient selection and monitoring during PRRT. Similarly, Dadgar et al. reported the feasibility of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga/[¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE in MTC and also noted the frequent discordance between imaging findings and biomarker trends, supporting our observation that calcitonin dynamics alone may not reliably reflect treatment response [23].

A small series by Satapathy et al. explored the use of concomitant [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE and low-dose capecitabine in eight patients with advanced medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) [28]. Radiologic response was assessed using RECIST 1.1, with 6 of 7 evaluable patients (86%) showing stable disease and none demonstrating partial or complete response. In contrast, our study employing PRRT monotherapy achieved a radiologic disease control rate of 78%, including 4 patients (22%) with partial response. Biochemical response was also limited in Satapathy’s series, with only 1 patient achieving a >50% decline in calcitonin, compared to 44% of our cohort meeting this threshold. Although their study reported a median follow-up of 34 months and several patients remained progression-free at last contact, progression-free survival (PFS) was not formally reported, precluding a direct comparison.

In our institution, the decision to initiate PRRT as first-line systemic therapy was made by a multidisciplinary tumor board based on individualized imaging characteristics and clinical status. Patients with somatostatin receptor–positive disease, even when receptor density was low (e.g., Krenning score 1), were considered suitable candidates if FDG uptake was absent or minimal, suggesting indolent tumor biology. PRRT was preferred over tyrosine kinase inhibitors in patients with comorbidities, advanced age, or concerns regarding long-term tolerability.

The ZETA trial conducted by Wells et al. represents the largest randomized phase III study in advanced MTC to date, evaluating vandetanib versus placebo in 331 patients [4]. Using RECIST v1.0 for centralized response assessment, vandetanib modest prolonged median progression-free survival (PFS) to an estimated 30.5 months, compared to 19.3 months with placebo (HR, 0.46; p < 0.001). While our study reported a comparable median PFS of 37.0 months with [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, key differences between patient populations limit direct comparisons. Notably, our cohort had a higher rate of bone metastases (67% vs. 34%) and a lower proportion of pretreated patients (22% vs. 39%). Importantly, the favorable safety profile of PRRT, with no grade ≥ 3 toxicities in our cohort, may offer an advantage over kinase inhibitor therapy, where discontinuation due to adverse events reached 12%.

Notably, a key methodological distinction of our study is the use of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT for treatment response assessment in all patients, interpreted using RECIST 1.1 criteria. In our institution, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE PET/CT has been routinely used in the evaluation of thyroid cancers since 2011, providing consistency and clinical familiarity in interpreting imaging-based outcomes [29–31]. While many previous reports have relied on conventional imaging, biochemical trends, or mixed response definitions, our approach integrates functional and anatomical imaging to enable standardized and reproducible evaluation. This may partly explain the higher rate of objective response observed in our cohort and supports the broader implementation of somatostatin receptor–based imaging in PRRT monitoring for MTC.

In contrast to the toxicity profile commonly observed with tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as Vandetanib, [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE treatment in our cohort was exceptionally well tolerated. In a meta-analysis by Trimboli et al., adverse events associated with Vandetanib included gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, nausea), dermatologic reactions (rash, acne), hypertension, and fatigue—often requiring dose reduction or treatment interruption [5]. Notably, treatment discontinuation due to disease progression or adverse events occurred in up to 30% of patients. In comparison, none of the patients in our series experienced grade ≥ 3 toxicity, and only mild side effects such as hair loss and jaw pain were reported in a small minority. Given the chronic administration required for Vandetanib and the potential long-term impact on quality of life, the tolerability of PRRT—delivered in a cyclic and time-limited fashion—may represents a clinical advantage in selected patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and relatively small sample size (n = 18) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although all patients were consecutively treated and data collection was uniform, the absence of a control group precludes direct comparisons with other therapeutic modalities. Additionally, key prognostic biomarkers such as RET mutation status, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and calcitonin doubling time were not available in any patient, precluding their inclusion in the analysis. This limitation restricted our ability to apply established molecular risk models or assess dual biomarker dynamics. Furthermore, our inclusion criteria allowed for PRRT in patients with low-level somatostatin receptor expression, including those with a Krenning score of 1, which may not reflect standard eligibility thresholds used in prospective trials. However, low Krenning score patients in our cohort exhibited low FDG uptake, suggesting non-aggressive biology, and were therefore considered appropriate candidates for PRRT. Finally, the limited number of progression events precluded the use of multivariable modeling, restricting our analysis to univariable associations. Further prospective, randomized, and multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, [¹⁷⁷Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE peptide receptor radionuclide therapy may represent a well-tolerated and potentially effective treatment option in patients in selected patients with advanced MTC, achieving durable radiologic disease control in our limited cohort. Based on our observations, the presence of bone metastases appeared to be associated with earlier progression, suggesting a potentially adverse impact on progression-free survival. The discordance observed between calcitonin dynamics and clinical outcomes further underscores the need for caution when interpreting tumor marker changes in isolation. These exploratory findings warrant further validation in larger, prospective studies and support the potential utility of functional imaging–based evaluation in this population.
