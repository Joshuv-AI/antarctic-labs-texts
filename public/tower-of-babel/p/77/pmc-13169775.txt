Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists reduce major adverse cardiovascular events and worsening heart failure in patients with heart failure: an umbrella meta-analysis
Authors: Changxin Sun, Chuchuan Meng, Yongfang Yuan, Lanqing Hu, Xiaoya Li, Min Wu, Longtao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), initially developed for type 2 diabetes, have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits. However, evidence regarding their efficacy in heart failure (HF) remains conflicting due to inconsistent results from individual studies and meta-analyses.MethodsWe conducted an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. PubMed, Embase, and the Web of Science were searched from inception until August 2025. Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using AMSTAR-2 and ROBIS. Evidence certainty was evaluated using GRADE. Dual analytical approaches (a least redundant set and an all meta-analyses approach) were employed to address primary study overlap.ResultsTwelve systematic reviews (encompassing 29 unique randomized controlled trials) were included. GLP-1RAs treatment significantly reduced the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (HR = 0.86, 95%CI: 0.66–0.98) and worsening HF (HR = 0.56, 95%CI: 0.41–0.77). It also significantly improved functional capacity as measured by the 6-minute walking test (mean difference = 14.23 m, 95%CI: 6.19 to 22.27). However, no significant benefits were observed for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, hospitalization HF, or cardiac structural parameters including left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular end-diastolic volume, and left ventricular end-systolic volume. Considerable overlap existed among the included reviews (Corrected Covered Area = 20.20%). The overall quality of evidence for outcomes ranged from low to moderate.ConclusionThis umbrella review suggests that GLP-1RAs may reduce MACE and worsening HF, and improve functional capacity in patients with HF. However, no benefits were observed for mortality or HF hospitalization. The positive findings were primarily driven by studies in HF with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction (HFmrEF/HFpEF), while evidence in HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) was limited and showed no benefit. Given the low-to-moderate certainty of evidence and the absence of benefit on hard clinical outcomes, these findings do not support a definitive change in clinical practice but rather highlight the urgent need for dedicated, phenotype-specific HF trials.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02246-6.Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, Heart failure, Umbrella meta-analysis, Major adverse cardiovascular events, 6-minute walk test

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and heart failure (HF) represent two closely related global health challenges, imposing a substantial clinical and economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Individuals with T2D face a two- to five-fold increased risk of developing HF, independent of conventional risk factors such as coronary artery disease and hypertension [2]. It is estimated that approximately 22% of patients with diabetes have HF, while over 60% of individuals with HF exhibit insulin resistance and more than 40% are diagnosed with diabetes [3]. This strong association is driven by a complex interplay of hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and subsequent myocardial metabolic dysfunction and fibrosis [4]. Consequently, HF has emerged as one of the most prevalent and severe complications of T2D, significantly impairing quality of life and survival. Notably, in high-income countries, the annual medical cost for a hospitalized HF patient exceeds $80,000, with one-year all-cause mortality ranging from 15% to 30%, and a five-year all-cause mortality rate reaching up to 75% [5, 6]. Furthermore, low- and middle-income populations bear a disproportionately high healthcare burden, as HF prevalence is greater among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [6, 7]. These realities underscore the urgent need for effective strategies for HF management.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), initially developed as glucose-lowering agents for T2D, have demonstrated significant cardiovascular benefits beyond glycemic control [8]. Landmark trials including LEADER (liraglutide) and SUSTAIN-6 (semaglutide) revealed substantial reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among diabetic patients with established cardiovascular disease [9, 10]. These benefits are likely mediated by improved metabolic parameters, weight reduction, and potential direct vascular protection [11–13]. However, evidence regarding GLP-1RAs in HF remains controversial, with several meta-analyses reporting no significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality or HF exacerbation [14–17]. Similarly, evaluations of MACE incidence in dedicated HF cohorts have yielded conflicting outcomes [18, 19]. Furthermore, the efficacy of GLP-1RAs on the 6-minute walking test (6-MWT) remains a subject of debate [19, 20]. Despite the proliferation of systematic reviews, their methodological quality is inconsistent, resulting in differences in the certainty of their conclusions. Given these conflicting findings and the lack of consensus in the current evidence base, an umbrella review is essential to comprehensively synthesize and evaluate the accumulating data from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, thereby providing clearer insights into the efficacy of GLP-1RAs in HF. Defining this efficacy is clinically vital, as GLP-1RAs could simplify the management of multimorbidity in HF patients by concurrently addressing several cardiometabolic conditions. Our study utilized dual methodological approaches—including a least-redundant set—to account for primary study overlap. The primary objective was to synthesize the effects of GLP-1RAs on MACE, mortality, and functional capacity, while identifying key gaps for future randomized controlled trials in HF populations.

Methods

Study design and registration

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the pre-specified protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251119728) and follows the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR). The objective was to synthesize evidence from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating the efficacy of GLP-1RAs in patients with HF, with or without T2D.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, and the Web of Science from inception until August 2025, without language restrictions. The search strategy was developed in consultation with clinicians, cardiologists, and methodologists, and included keywords related to: (1) GLP-1RAs (e.g., liraglutide, semaglutide, exenatide); (2) HF (including all types and phenotypes, such as left-sided, right-sided, and HF with reduced, mildly reduced, or preserved ejection fraction, in accordance with contemporary ESC [21] and AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines [22]); (3) systematic reviews or meta-analyses. The outcomes of this study were not strictly predefined and included, but were not limited to, cardiovascular mortality, hospitalization, and functional capacity. The complete search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

Study selection

Eligible studies were systematic reviews or meta-analyses that: (1) included adult patients with HF (any phenotype); (2) evaluated GLP-1RAs as the intervention; (3) compared against placebo or usual care; (4) reported quantitative synthesis (e.g., pooled hazard ratio [HR], or mean difference [MD]) for at least one predefined outcome (e.g., MACE, all-cause mortality, hospitalization HF, left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], 6-MWT). Exclusion criteria included reviews focusing on non-HF populations or non-GLP-1RAs interventions. Two reviewers (C.S. and C.M.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (Y.Y.).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted items included: first author, year of publication, types of included primary studies, number of studies and participants, intervention and comparator details, outcome definitions and effect measures, methodological quality assessment tools used, heterogeneity and publication bias assessments, missing data were requested from corresponding authors where necessary.

Outcome definitions

For the outcome “worsening HF,” we accepted the definitions used in the included systematic reviews. Upon review of the primary sources cited in these meta-analyses, worsening HF was typically defined as a composite endpoint that included any of the following: intensification of diuretic therapy, urgent care visits for HF, or hospitalization for HF. However, minor variability in the exact components of this composite across reviews may exist (Supplementary Table S2).

Quality assessment

The degree of overlap among primary studies across the included systematic reviews was quantitatively assessed using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) index, a standardized metric for evaluating redundancy and non-independence in evidence synthesis [23]. The CCA was calculated using the formula: CCA = (N − r) ⁄ [(r × c) − r], where N represents the total number of study inclusions, r denotes the number of unique primary studies, and c corresponds to the number of systematic reviews. Based on established interpretation criteria, a CCA value of < 5% was interpreted as slight overlap, 5%–15% as moderate, and ≥ 15% as high overlap. The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews was evaluated using the A MeaSurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2) checklist, which allows for a critical appraisal of the design, conduct, and analysis of systematic reviews [24]. Additionally, the risk of bias in the systematic reviews was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Systematic (ROBIS) reviews tool [25, 26]. The overall confidence in the results of each review was classified accordingly. The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) framework [27]. The reporting completeness of the included systematic reviews was assessed using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [28]. Evidence was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low based on considerations of risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias. All quality assessments were performed by two independent reviewers (C.S. and C.M.). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus under the supervision of a third reviewer (Y.Y.).

Data synthesis

All meta-analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 and Review Manager version 5.4. Given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies (e.g., HF phenotypes, GLP-1RA types, and follow-up durations), all pooled analyses were conducted using a random-effects model to account for within- and between-study variability and provide a more conservative estimate. Where data permitted, we performed exploratory subgroup analyses by HF phenotype and drug formulation to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. To mitigate potential bias arising from the inclusion of overlapping primary studies across multiple systematic reviews, an inherent challenge in umbrella reviews, we conducted two complementary sets of analyses for key outcomes: a “least redundant set,” which was designed to maximize the comprehensiveness of the underlying evidence base while strategically minimizing redundancy. To address primary study overlap, we employed two complementary approaches: (1) a least redundant set, which minimized redundancy by selecting the highest-quality meta-analysis containing each primary study (AMSTAR-2-based) to represent it in the pooled analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 2); and (2) an all meta-analyses approach, incorporating all available meta-analyses regardless of overlap. Exploratory subgroup analyses by HF phenotype and drug formulation were conducted where data permitted. Effect measures are presented as HR with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for time-to-event outcomes (e.g., cardiovascular mortality, hospitalization HF) and MD or standardized MD (SMD) with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes (e.g., change in LVEF, 6-minute walk test distance). To ensure the accuracy of the analysis results, the relative risk / odds ratio reported in the original study were not combined with the hazard ratio (HR). The relevant data have been included in Supplementary Table S3. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test (with a significance level of P < 0.10) and the I² statistic. An I² value greater than 50% was considered to represent substantial heterogeneity. Potential publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots for each outcome. However, due to the limited number of studies (n < 10) included in each meta-analysis, formal statistical testing (e.g., Egger’s test) was not performed. The stability and reliability of the pooled results were evaluated through sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the impact of each study on the overall effect estimate. In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted within the least-redundant set to explore potential sources of clinical heterogeneity. Subgrouping was performed according to HF phenotype, categorizing studies as “HF with unspecified type”, “HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)” and “HF with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction (HFmrEF/HFpEF),” based on the classifications reported in the original systematic reviews. For analyses involving left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), further subgroup analyses were performed according to the mode of GLP-1–based therapy, including oral GLP-1 receptor agonists, oral GLP-1 receptor agonists combined with intravenous GLP administration, and intravenous GLP-1 infusion alone. These subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and were undertaken to facilitate interpretation of heterogeneity rather than to provide definitive comparative estimates.

Results

Search results

Following the predefined search strategy, a total of 697 potentially relevant records were identified. After removing duplicates, 466 studies remained. Following a screening of titles and abstracts, 387 additional records were excluded. The remaining 79 articles underwent full-text assessment. Of these, 66 were excluded due to reasons such as unavailability of full texts, lack of a confirmed HF history population, or insufficient outcome data. Ultimately, 12 systematic reviews met the eligibility criteria and were included in this umbrella review. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Detailed reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews was evaluated using the AMSTAR-2 tool. Among the 12 studies, four were rated as high quality, one as moderate, four as low, and three as very low quality. Key items (Q2, Q4, Q7, Q9, Q11, Q13, Q15) were particularly critical in determining overall ratings. Among these, Q2 (prior protocol registration and justification of deviations) and Q15 (investigation of publication bias) were the most frequently unmet criteria, each lacking in three studies (25.00%). Items Q7 (list of excluded studies with justifications), Q9 (risk of bias assessment technique), and Q13 (consideration of risk of bias in interpretation) were each not fulfilled in two studies (16.67%). Regarding non-critical items, Q6 (duplicate data extraction) and Q12 (impact of risk of bias on synthesis) were the most commonly omitted, each absent in three studies (25.00%), followed by Q10 (funding reporting), Q14 (heterogeneity discussion), and Q16 (conflict of interest statement), which were each missing in two studies (16.67%). Risk of bias assessment

The methodological risk of bias of the included systematic reviews was evaluated using the ROBIS tool. As detailed in Supplementary Table S6, four reviews (33.3%) were judged to be at low risk of bias, while the remaining eight (66.7%) were rated as high risk. The predominant concern arose from Domain 2 (identification and selection of studies), which was flawed in all eight high-risk reviews, indicating potential deficiencies in the search strategy or study selection process. Additionally, two reviews exhibited further limitations in Domain 3 (data collection and study appraisal). No significant issues were identified in Domain 1 (study eligibility criteria) or Domain 4 (synthesis and findings).

Reporting completeness assessment

The assessment of reporting completeness using the PRISMA checklist revealed consistent reporting across reviews for basic items such as the title, abstract, and rationale. However, critical methodological reporting was frequently inadequate. Key deficiencies were observed in the description of the search strategy (Item 7, fully reported in 33.3% of reviews), the specification of all data items (Item 10b, 16.7%), and the assessment of the certainty of evidence (Item 15, 16.7%). These reporting gaps limit the transparency, reproducibility, and overall reliability of the included systematic reviews. The detailed item-by-item assessment for each study is provided in Supplementary Table S7.

MACE-related outcomes indicators

To address the inherent overlap of primary studies across systematic reviews, two complementary analytical approaches were employed for key outcomes: a “least redundant set” and an “all meta-analyses” approach. The results of both approaches are summarized below and presented visually in Fig. 2. In the least redundant set analysis, GLP-1RAs treatment was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the risk of MACE (HR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.66–0.98, P = 0.03) and worsening HF (HR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.41–0.77, P < 0.001). Conversely, no significant benefits were observed for hospitalization HF (HR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.86–1.10, P = 0.63), cardiovascular death (HR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.81–1.06, P = 0.26), all-cause death (HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.82–1.10, P = 0.48), or stroke (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.74–1.34, P = 0.96). The all meta-analyses approach yielded similar trends but with narrower confidence intervals due to the increased sample size. Significant benefits were again observed for MACE (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.80–0.89, P < 0.001) and worsening HF (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.48–0.73, P < 0.001). There remained no significant effect on hospitalization HF (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.93–1.09, P = 0.82) or cardiovascular death (HR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.84–1.05, P = 0.24). Data for all-cause death and stroke were not available in this model. Overall, both analytical methods consistently demonstrated that GLP-1RAs therapy significantly reduces the risk of MACE and worsening HF events, while showing no significant effect on mortality or hospitalization for HF. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Figure S1 and S2.

Cardiac structural/functional parameters

Figure 3 presents forest plots summarizing the MD for continuous outcomes based on the least redundant set analysis. Notably, the “all meta-analyses” approach was not applicable for these endpoints due to complete overlap in the primary studies included across the meta-analyses; thus, only the least redundant set is reported. Treatment with GLP-1RAs demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the 6-MWT distance (MD = 14.23 m, 95% CI: 6.19 to 22.27, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were found in LVEF (MD = 0.23%, 95% CI: − 0.37 to 0.83, P = 0.46), left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) (MD = − 0.24 mL, 95% CI: − 3.46 to 2.99, P = 0.89), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) (MD = 0.56 mL, 95% CI: − 1.60 to 2.73, P = 0.61), or heart rate (MD = 3.07 bpm, 95% CI: − 1.04 to 7.19, P = 0.14). These findings suggest that GLP-1RAs therapy significantly enhances functional exercise capacity, as evidenced by the 6-MWT, but does not substantially impact cardiac structural parameters or heart rate in patients with HF. Subgroup analysis

Based on HF subtypes, the population was stratified into three subgroups: HFrEF, HFmrEF/HFpEF, and HF with unspecified type. Subgroup analysis suggested a potential differential effect of GLP-1RAs on MACE across subtypes. A significant reduction in MACE was observed in both the unspecified type subgroup (HR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.80–0.94, P < 0.001) and the HFmrEF/HFpEF subgroup (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.60–0.86, P < 0.001), although the between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.06). For cardiovascular death, GLP-1RAs did not show significant benefit in either subgroup, with no notable difference between them. Subgroup analysis by HF type did not reveal significant changes in LVEF; however, analysis by drug formulation indicated that intravenous GLP-1RAs administration was associated with a significant increase in LVEF (P < 0.05), whereas oral formulations showed no significant effect. Outcomes including all-cause death, stroke, LVEDV, and LVESV were each based on only two studies (one per subgroup) and were not statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were not performed for worsening HF (all studies involved HFmrEF/HFpEF) or for hospitalization HF and heart rate (all studies involved unspecified HF type).

Sensitivity analysis and bias assessment

Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. For the outcomes of all-cause death and stroke, the results were particularly influenced by the study conducted by Zhao et al. [29], indicating a notable impact on the overall effect size. The findings for all other outcomes remained consistent and robust throughout the sensitivity analysis. Detailed results of the sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Figure S4. Additionally, funnel plots were visually inspected to assess potential bias for all outcomes reported in Section “Evidence quality assessment” and “Reporting completeness assessment”. No substantial asymmetry was observed in any of the funnel plots, suggesting no obvious publication bias across the results (Supplementary Figure S5).

Discussion

This umbrella review systematically evaluated the current body of evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses regarding the efficacy of GLP-1RAs in patients with HF. This study employed a dual analytical strategy to ensure a robust and nuanced synthesis of literature. This strategy comprised a “least redundant set” analysis to minimize study overlap and an “all meta-analyses” approach to maximize inclusiveness. The main findings indicate that GLP-1RAs therapy is associated with a significant reduction in the risk of MACE and worsening HF events. However, no statistically significant benefits were observed for all-cause death, cardiovascular mortality, hospitalization HF, or stroke. Furthermore, among continuous functional and structural outcomes, only the 6-MWT showed significant improvement, while cardiac structural parameters including LVEF, LVEDV, and LVESV remained unchanged.

The pathophysiological interplay between HF and diabetes mellitus, particularly T2D, is increasingly recognized as a bidirectional relationship mediated through multiple overlapping mechanisms [34]. Insulin resistance, a hallmark of T2D, not only contributes to impaired systemic metabolic homeostasis but also plays a critical role in the development and progression of HF by inducing myocardial metabolic dysfunction. This includes a shift in cardiac substrate utilization from glucose toward fatty acid oxidation, resulting in reduced energy efficiency, lipotoxicity, and mitochondrial impairment [35, 36]. Furthermore, chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance promote systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, which collectively contribute to reduced vascular compliance and enhanced myocardial fibrosis [35]. These mechanisms are especially pertinent in obese patients with HFpEF, who frequently exhibit a pronounced inflammatory metabolic phenotype [37]. Additionally, insulin resistance is associated with sympathetic nervous system overactivation and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system dysregulation, both of which contribute to fluid retention, hypertension, and adverse cardiac remodeling [38]. GLP-1RAs may counter these mechanisms not only through improved glycemic control and weight reduction but also via direct anti-inflammatory, diuresis and vasodilatory effects, thereby ameliorating endothelial function and reducing afterload [8]. Therefore, targeting these shared pathological pathways through GLP-1RAs represents a mechanistically plausible strategy for intervening in the progression of HF.

The significant reduction in MACE and worsening HF observed in our analysis is consistent with findings from major cardiovascular outcome trials involving GLP-1RAs, such as the LEADER and SUSTAIN-6 trials [39, 40], which established their role in reducing cardiovascular events in patients with T2D and high cardiovascular risk. Our results extend these benefits specifically to the HF population, reinforcing the potential cardioprotective effects of this drug class. The improvement in 6-minute walking test distance (14.23 m) was statistically significant, suggesting a beneficial effect on functional capacity. However, this improvement falls below the established minimal clinically important difference for HF patients (typically 15–35 m), rendering its clinical relevance uncertain [41]. Notably, this functional gain occurred without concomitant improvements in cardiac structural parameters (LVEF, LVEDV, LVESV), suggesting that the benefits of GLP-1RAs may be mediated through peripheral mechanisms—such as weight loss, reduced afterload, or improved endothelial function—rather than direct myocardial reverse remodeling.

When compared to other antihyperglycemic agents, GLP-1RAs demonstrate distinct advantages. In HFpEF patients, semaglutide and tirzepatide significantly reduced HF hospitalization or all-cause death versus DPP-4 inhibitors [42]. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, while offering dual metabolic-hemodynamic benefits in HFrEF and diabetic kidney disease, are particularly effective at reducing hospitalization and mortality in those populations [43]. In contrast, GLP-1RAs show more pronounced benefits in HFpEF, particularly among patients with obesity, where they markedly lower HF events (RR = 0.40) and improve quality of life [44, 45]. Additionally, GLP-1RAs may reduce MACE risk more than other non-insulin therapies in T2D patients with cardiovascular disease [46]. Mechanistically, GLP-1RAs exert cardioprotective effects through improvements in endothelial function, reduction in vascular inflammation and oxidative stress, and favorable modulation of plaque composition [47], which may not be as prominently observed with other classes like DPP-4 inhibitors or insulin [48]. This multifaceted action, incorporating metabolic improvement, weight reduction, and direct vascular benefits, uniquely positions GLP-1RAs in the management of cardiometabolic HF.

The absence of significant changes in LVEF, LVEDV, and LVESV suggests that GLP-1RAs may not induce substantial reverse cardiac remodeling within the observed treatment periods. This may be partly attributable to the ongoing controversy regarding the extent of GLP-1 receptor expression in human cardiomyocytes, which remains incompletely elucidated [47]. Recent evidence indicates that GLP-1RAs exert promising anti-inflammatory effects in HF with preserved ejection fraction, particularly in obese patients, by reducing systemic inflammation and improving clinical outcomes without significant structural cardiac changes [49]. In contrast, Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors have consistently demonstrated significant structural improvements in cardiac reverse remodeling across multiple HF trials [50, 51]. The dissociation between clinical benefits and the absence of structural changes reinforces the concept that the therapeutic effects of GLP-1RAs are likely mediated through alternative mechanisms targeting HF pathophysiology. Evidence suggests that GLP-1RAs exert beneficial effects on weight reduction in individuals with obesity or overweight, mediated through paracrine and endocrine signaling pathways involving the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and central nervous system. These mechanisms include delayed gastric emptying, reduced appetite, enhanced insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, and suppression of glucagon release [11]. Furthermore, GLP-1RAs have been shown to promote osmotic diuresis, improve renal function via renal vasodilation and increased renal blood flow, reduce angiotensin II levels, lower blood pressure, and consequently decrease cardiac workload [12]. Therefore, the cardioprotective mechanisms of GLP-1RAs appear to operate primarily through metabolic modulation and functional improvement rather than structural reverse remodeling.

Our subgroup analysis indicates differential treatment effects across HF phenotypes. Although the interaction term was not statistically significant (P = 0.06), the numerically greater reduction in MACE observed in the HFmrEF/HFpEF subgroup (HR = 0.72) compared to the overall population (HR = 0.87) and the unspecified HF subgroup is mechanistically plausible. This finding aligns with the pathophysiological concept of HFpEF, particularly in obesity, as a “metabolic-inflammatory” phenotype in which insulin resistance and chronic inflammation serve as central drivers [37]. The robust metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1RAs may therefore preferentially target core pathophysiological mechanisms in this subgroup [11, 49], consistent with recent evidence demonstrating substantial benefit in obese HFpEF patients [44]. The treatment effect observed in the unspecified HF subgroup, while suggestive of a potential efficacy gradient correlated with metabolic phenotype, should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and non-significant interaction term; these hypothesis-generating findings require confirmation in dedicated phenotype-stratified RCTs.

The disparity between the reduced risk of worsening HF and the neutral effect on hospitalization HF may stem from the distinct mechanisms of GLP-1RAs. Worsening HF is a composite endpoint sensitive to symptomatic and functional improvement [52], as evidenced by the significant gain in 6-MWT distance with GLP-1RAs. This suggests the drugs effectively alleviate the symptom burden that drives this endpoint. In contrast, hospitalization HF typically reflects severe decompensation from advanced myocardial dysfunction and remodeling. The consistent lack of improvement in LVEF, LVEDV, and LVESV indicates GLP-1RAs do not substantially reverse adverse cardiac remodeling in the short-to-medium term [14]. Therefore, the clinical benefit profile, characterized by symptomatic and functional improvement without significant structural reversal, explains why GLP-1RAs reduce the risk of symptomatic deterioration but not hospitalization for severe decompensation. This aligns with their proposed primary mechanisms of action, which are largely peripheral, including weight loss, natriuresis, and afterload reduction, rather than direct myocardial repair [8, 53].

The substantial overlap among included systematic reviews (CCA = 20.20%) reflects the limited pool of primary RCTs in this population. Despite our dual analytical approach to mitigate this issue, the pooled estimates may still be disproportionately influenced by the same set of trials, potentially overestimating the precision and generalizability of the findings. Notably, the consistency of results between the least redundant set and all meta-analyses approaches strengthens the credibility of our primary conclusions. However, the ROBIS assessment revealed that eight of the 12 systematic reviews (66.7%) were rated as high risk of bias, primarily due to deficiencies in the identification and selection of studies. This, coupled with frequent methodological limitations identified by the AMSTAR-2 tool—particularly in protocol registration, assessment of publication bias, and handling of heterogeneity—necessitates cautious interpretation of the evidence. The overall quality of evidence as assessed by the GRADE framework ranged primarily from low to moderate, with risk of bias and imprecision being the most frequent reasons for downgrading. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the pooled estimates for all-cause mortality and stroke were particularly influenced by a single study, suggesting limited robustness for these outcomes and highlighting the need for further research to definitively assess these endpoints. Conversely, results for MACE and worsening HF remained stable across all sensitivity analyses. No significant publication bias was detected for any outcome based on visual inspection of funnel plots.

A key strength of this umbrella review is its comprehensive methodological rigor. The study implemented a dual analytical strategy, including the “least redundant set” and “all meta-analyses” approaches, to minimize overlap while enhancing inclusiveness and methodological robustness. Additionally, multiple standardized tools were applied for critical appraisal, including AMSTAR-2 for methodological quality assessment, ROBIS for risk of bias evaluation, GRADE for evidence certainty grading, PRISMA for reporting completeness, and the CCA index for quantifying overlap among included systematic reviews. This multifaceted evaluation framework strengthens the validity and interpretability of the findings. Several limitations warrant consideration. Several limitations should be considered. The limited number of primary RCTs and clinical heterogeneity across populations, follow-up durations, and GLP-1RA types may limit generalizability. Key subgroups (HFrEF, HFpEF) were underpowered. Variability in endpoint definitions, particularly for worsening HF, limits comparability across studies. The predominance of high ROBIS risk and low-to-moderate AMSTAR-2/GRADE ratings underscores evidence fragility. The absence of mortality, hospitalization, or structural benefits, and the modest 6-minute walk test improvement below the minimal clinically important difference, further temper clinical implications. Finally, as an umbrella review of aggregate data, we could not adjust for confounders such as baseline metabolic status or background HF therapies. Dedicated, well-phenotyped RCTs with standardized outcomes and longer follow-up are urgently needed.

Conclusion

This umbrella review demonstrates that GLP-1RA therapy is associated with significant reductions in MACE and worsening HF, along with improved functional capacity, but without significant benefits for mortality, HF hospitalization, or cardiac structural parameters. These findings are primarily driven by studies in HFmrEF/HFpEF, while evidence in HFrEF remains limited and shows no benefit. Given the low-to-moderate certainty of the evidence and the absence of benefit on hard clinical outcomes, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Dedicated, phenotype-specific RCTs with standardized endpoints are urgently needed to confirm the role of GLP-1RAs across the full spectrum of heart failure.
