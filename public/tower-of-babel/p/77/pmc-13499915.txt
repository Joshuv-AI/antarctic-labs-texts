Relation of fluid neurodegeneration biomarkers with white matter integrity in Alzheimer's disease patients in Latin America
Authors: Thamires NC Magalhães, Raphael F Casseb, Adriel S Moraes, Camila VL Teixeira, Ana M Carletti, Thiago JR De Rezende, Helena PG Joaquim, Leda L Talib, Orestes Forlenza, Fernando Cendes, Marcio LF Balthazar, Charlotte E Teunissen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer's disease (AD) is increasingly prevalent in Latin America. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are promising biomarkers of neurodegeneration, but their relationship with white matter (WM) integrity remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate associations between fluid neurodegeneration biomarkers and WM microstructure in a Brazilian cohort of individuals across the AD continuum and cognitively healthy controls.MethodsNinety-one participants were included: 27 cognitively healthy controls (mean age = 68.3 ± 5.2 years) and 64 amyloid-positive individuals with mild cognitive impairment or AD dementia (mean age = 70.6 ± 6.9 years). AD participants were characterized by low cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ42 concentrations (<540 pg/mL) and altered Aβ42/p-Tau and Aβ42/t-Tau ratios. Serum and CSF concentrations of NfL and GFAP were measured using single-molecule array technology and examined in relation to diffusion tensor imaging metrics, including fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, radial diffusivity, and axial diffusivity (AxD).ResultsWithin the clinical AD group, higher serum NfL levels were associated with lower AxD in the left cingulum tract (r = -0.372, p = 0.007). In cognitively healthy controls, serum NfL showed positive correlations with AxD and mean diffusivity in the right cingulum (r = 0.650, p = 0.001 and r = 0.607, p = 0.003, respectively). No significant associations were observed between serum or CSF GFAP concentrations and diffusion tensor imaging metrics.ConclusionsSerum NfL was associated with anatomically specific WM microstructural changes, with differing patterns across clinical groups.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, biomarkers, diffusion tensor imaging, glial fibrillary acidic protein, neurofilament light chain, neuroimaging

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a major global health concern, with extra challenges in Latin American (LatAm) countries. 1 The prevalence of dementia in this region is high and rising, affecting between 7.1% and 11.5% of individuals aged 65 and older, compared to lower, stable, or declining rates in Europe and the United States.1,2 LatAm countries confront various pertinent risk factors, including substantial genetic diversity, stemming from historical events such as colonization, migration, and the blending of indigenous populations with Europeans, Africans, and Asians. 2 This diversity can impact disease susceptibility, treatment response, and overall health outcomes. Furthermore, social determinants of health also have a significant impact on LatAm, disease risk factors such as poverty, inequality, education, and access to healthcare play an important role in health risks and outcomes. 2

Access to biomarker procedures in LatAm is often hindered by high costs, as they are not typically covered by public healthcare insurance. Despite these obstacles, the use of biomarkers remains crucial in clinical practice and the recruitment of participants for AD trials in order to establish the etiology of cognitive dysfunction.3,4 Recent technological advancements have transformed the investigation of AD biomarkers, ensuring precise measurement across various biological materials.5–7 The introduction of single-molecule array technology (Simoa) 3 has enabled highly sensitive quantification of biomarkers, particularly in peripheral blood, thereby advancing research in AD.4,5 This emphasis on blood biomarkers offers a more accessible and cost-effective approach for both research and clinical practice. Utilizing information from multimodal biomarkers, including molecular and neuroimaging components, can greatly improve the accuracy of predicting AD-type dementia.8,9 These approaches offer valuable insights into the underlying pathological processes and can assist in the early detection and monitoring of the disease.

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) have emerged as promising indicators of downstream neurodegenerative processes in AD.8,9 NfL, a neuro-specific biomarker detectable in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood, presents challenges in interpretation due to its age-related increase.10,11 Nonetheless, it serves as a universal measure for neuroaxonal injury or degeneration in clinical research and practice.12–15 GFAP, a key component of the astrocyte cytoskeleton, is implicated in reactive astrocytosis, potentially driving, or resulting from pathological changes in AD.16–19 A population-based neuropathological study demonstrated increased GFAP expression and protein concentrations around dense amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, correlating with tau accumulation. 17

Studies indicate that serum GFAP could function as a predictive non-amyloid blood biomarker for risk evaluation in cognitively healthy individuals, while serum NfL may be more beneficial for monitoring disease progression.20,21 Elevated baseline GFAP levels are strongly associated with a higher risk of dementia progression. 20 Moreover, higher GFAP levels correlate with accelerated cognitive decline.20,22 These findings suggest that plasma GFAP could serve as a clinical biomarker of Aβ-related astrocyte reactivity, reflecting both cognitive decline and brain tissue degeneration. 22 In contrast, serum NfL levels increase more rapidly in individuals who develop dementia compared to those who remain dementia-free. 20

While the cellular origins of NfL and GFAP are well-established, their exact roles in the neuropathological processes of AD remain elusive. A promising strategy to explore these connections is by analyzing the relationship between these biomarkers and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics. DTI parameters, such as diffusivity and fractional anisotropy (FA), offer detailed insights into the microstructural integrity of white matter (WM) in the brain.23–25 Detectable changes in WM through DTI are instrumental for early AD diagnosis and tracking disease progression, as they reflect WM integrity alterations due to axonal damage, inflammation, myelin loss, and brain lesions.26–31

Research has consistently shown that increased mean diffusivity (MD) is prevalent in areas affected by neurofibrillary pathology, including the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, temporoparietal association cortex, and posterior cingulate gyrus. 31 For instance, a study found that elevated levels of Aβ42 and t-tau correlated with decreased FA and increased MD in generalized WM networks in individuals with subjective cognitive complaints, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD. 32 Given these associations, it is plausible that NfL and GFAP levels would also correlate with DTI measurements.

Recent studies have begun to explore these connections, with Nabizadeh et al. (2022) finding that blood NfL levels were associated with decreased FA, 29 and Bettcher et al. (2021) demonstrating that higher GFAP levels were linked to lower WM integrity in regions vulnerable to AD pathology. 33 However, there is a significant gap in the literature, particularly regarding studies focused on Latin American populations.

This study aimed to assess GFAP and NfL levels in the blood and CSF of Latin American clinical AD patients and to investigate potential correlations between these biomarkers and DTI parameters (FA, MD, axial diffusivity (AxD), and radial diffusivity (RD)) to understand their relationship with WM integrity changes better. As the first study in LatAm to correlate these markers with DTI parameters, we leveraged Simoa technology to quantify these biomarkers in Brazilian biological samples, establishing a critical foundation for future research in this region.

Methods

Subjects’ collection

Ninety-one individuals participated in this study, including 27 cognitively healthy participants (mean age 68.3 ± 5.2 years) and 64 clinical AD patients (mean age 70.6 ± 6.9 years; 49 with MCI and 15 with AD dementia). Detailed demographics are provided in Table 1.

Patients were recruited from the outpatient service of the University of Campinas (UNICAMP) Clinic Hospital. Neuropsychological evaluations were conducted by an experienced neuropsychologist, as described in previous studies. 34 All patients met the NIA/AA core criteria for MCI and AD, 35 with a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 or 1.0, and a mandatory memory score of 0.5. 36 To further substantiate the diagnosis, all patients were confirmed to be amyloid-positive based on CSF Aβ42 levels (<540 pg/mL, 37 INNOTEST® kits, Fujirebio) and supported by abnormal Aβ42/phosphorylated-Tau (p-Tau) and Aβ42/total-Tau (t-Tau) ratios (12.0 ± 9.9 pg/mL; 6.7 ± 3.4 pg/mL, respectively). 38

For the purposes of statistical analyses, particularly those involving molecular biomarkers, patients with MCI and AD dementia were pooled into a single group defined as clinical AD. This decision was made to avoid underpowered subgroup analyses, as the sample sizes of each clinical group (particularly AD dementia) were modest. Grouping patients under the umbrella of the clinical AD ensured sufficient statistical power while remaining conceptually consistent with prior literature, which considers amyloid-positive individuals across clinical stages as part of the AD spectrum.

Cognitively healthy participants (control group) were recruited through research outreach efforts or were patients’ companions, such as spouses or caregivers. These participants completed an extensive neuropsychological battery and had no neurological or psychiatric disorders, nor did they require psychoactive medications. They scored within normal ranges on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), adjusted for age and education, 39 and showed no imaging abnormalities. Control group participants had no memory complaints or neurological deficits, with a CDR score of zero. CSF collection was not conducted for these participants.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded from the study participants with other neurological or psychiatric disorders, a history of head trauma leading to loss of consciousness, a history of drug or alcohol addiction, recent use of sedative drugs within 24 h before the neuropsychological evaluation, prior chronic exposure to neurotoxic substances, and those with a Hachinski ischemic score > 4. 40 Pre-diagnostic procedures also included laboratory tests for vitamin B12, folate, syphilis serology, and thyroid hormones. Regarding vascular effects, all individuals in the study exhibited normal age-appropriate white matter hyperintensities on MRI, with a Fazekas scale of 0 or 1. 41

The study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of UNICAMP Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study began. For participants unable to provide consent, it was obtained from their responsible caretakers, following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Biological material collection

Blood serum samples were obtained from all participants, while CSF, which was collected via lumbar puncture, was only obtained from the patients. The biological material was collected at the University of Campinas (UNICAMP) Clinic Hospital. Following collection, the blood samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to separate serum components. Similarly, CSF samples were centrifuged at 700 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant samples were transferred into 1-mL Eppendorf microtubes and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Molecular biomarkers quantification

As detailed in previous studies, 42 the measurement of Aβ42, t-Tau, and p-Tau levels was carried out using amyloid-β INNOTEST® kits (1–42), h-TAU INNOTEST® Ag, and INNOTEST® Phospho-tau (181P), by Fujirebio. The analysis was performed at the Medical Research Laboratory (LIM-27) located at the University of Sao Paulo (USP).

For analyses involving GFAP and NfL, our CSF and serum samples were sent to the Neurochemistry Laboratory of the Amsterdam University Medical Center (AUMC) for quantification of the NfL and GFAP biomarkers. Before analysis, the samples underwent a brief thawing at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g to prevent any potential interference from sample debris. GFAP and NfL levels in both serum and CSF were assessed using the Simoa GFAP Discovery Kit and the Simoa NF-Light Advantage Kit (Quanterix, Lexington, MA), 43 following the manufacturer's instructions on the Simoa HD-X Analyzer. Each serum sample was analyzed in duplicate for GFAP, while NfL was analyzed in a single run.

MRI acquisition

All MRI scans were conducted using a Philips® Achieva 3.0 T scanner, scheduled within one week following the neuropsychological assessment and on a separate day from blood collection and/or lumbar puncture exam. Each subject underwent the following protocol: a) A sagittal high-resolution T1-weighted sequence was employed (with isotropic voxels of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, TR/TE = 7/3.2 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm, comprising 180 slices); b) A standard DTI protocol was employed (with an acquired voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, reconstructed to 1 × 1 × 2 mm3, no gap, TR = 8.5 s, TE = 61 ms, 32 diffusion directions with b = 1000 s/mm2, acquisition matrix = 128 × 128 reconstructed to 256 × 256).

To evaluate white-matter vascular hyperintensities, we obtained coronal and axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) T2-weighted images anatomically aligned to the hippocampus (reconstructed voxel size = 0.45 × 0.45 × 4.00 mm3, TR/TE/TI = 12000/140/2850 ms, FOV = 220 × 206 mm, with a 1 mm gap).

DTI processing and automated segmentation

All image processing steps were conducted in the native space using MRICloud, a publicly accessible web-based platform (accessible at MRICloud.org) designed for segmenting and quantifying multi-contrast imaging data 44 with high reproducibility. 45 To derive diffusion indices, we employed an automated segmentation approach based on multiple labeled atlases.46,47 DTI-weighted images underwent correction for eddy currents and motion using a 12-parameter affine transform. Subsequently, a multi-contrast algorithm registered all atlases to the images and performed parcellation using the Diffeomorphic Likelihood Fusion Algorithm (DLFA).48,49

Imaging analysis

The DTI image analysis produced a range of metrics, including FA, AxD, RD, and MD, specifically computed for 64 WM tracts. We concentrate our analysis on regions particularly vulnerable to AD-related pathological changes.23,50 These include the bilateral cingulum, cingulum-hippocampal, fornix stria terminalis, and fornix tracts, all essential mesial temporal lobe structure components.

In line with this interpretation, we performed additional exploratory analyses including white-matter tracts not typically considered highly vulnerable to AD pathology as control regions. These analyses did not reveal significant associations between serum or CSF biomarkers and DTI metrics in control tracts, further supporting the anatomical specificity of the observed relationships within AD-relevant pathways. While these null findings are not shown, they reinforce our decision to focus the primary analyses on tracts with established relevance to AD-related neurodegeneration.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyzes were performed using the SPSS software package (version 29, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Group comparisons for demographic data, DTI parameters, and blood molecular biomarkers were conducted using independent-samples t-tests, while categorical variables (e.g., sex) were analyzed using chi-square tests. Years of education were included as a covariate in relevant analyzes to account for group differences.

The distribution of molecular biomarker values ​​was formally assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated deviations from normality for several measures. Consequently, we employed Spearman rank correlation analyzes to examine associations between GFAP and NfL (in serum and CSF) and imaging biomarkers (DTI parameters). The same approach was used to evaluate correlations between GFAP/NfL and CSF Aβ42, t-Tau, and p-Tau. Spearman correlations were selected due to their robustness to outliers and appropriateness for non-normally distributed biological data.

Age and education were examined as potential confounders, and the main findings remained consistent after accounting for these variables. Multiple comparisons were controlled for using the Bonferroni correction, with the adjusted significance threshold set at p < 0.008.

Results

Demographics and fluid biomarkers

Significant differences in years of education were observed between clinical AD group and the control group (Table 1). However, no significant differences were found in serum GFAP and NfL levels between groups. Importantly, CSF biomarkers were only available for the clinical AD group, confirming their amyloid-positive status.

DTI parameters

Compared with controls, patients in the clinical AD group showed lower FA values ​​and higher diffusivity parameters, with significant differences in the left cingulate, cingulum-hippocampus, and bilateral fornix tracts (Table 2 and Supplemental Table 1). These findings indicate more pronounced alterations in white matter integrity among patients on the clinical AD group.

Correlations analysis

DTI parameters with GFAP and NfL

As illustrated in Figure 1, the associations between serum NfL and cingulum microstructure showed opposing patterns across groups. Within the clinical AD, higher serum NfL was associated with lower AxD in the left cingulum (r = −0.372, p = 0.007). In contrast, in cognitively healthy controls, serum NfL levels showed significant positive correlations with AxD and MD in the right cingulum (r = 0.650, p = 0.001; r = 0.607, p = 0.003, respectively). These spatially distinct and directionally divergent effects are depicted in the figure, where significant tract-wise correlations are overlaid on anatomical templates and color-coded according to Spearman coefficients. To enhance transparency, we provide the corresponding individual-level scatterplots for each significant association in the Supplemental Figure 2, showing participant-level variability, linear trend lines, and the exact Spearman rho and p-values. No significant relationships were observed between serum or CSF GFAP concentrations and any DTI metric.

For each significant association, the corresponding tract segment is rendered on the anatomical template, with colors representing the direction and magnitude of the Spearman correlation coefficient (from negative in blue to positive in red/yellow). White asterisks indicate tract nodes where the correlation survived correction for multiple comparisons. In cognitively healthy controls (A, B), higher serum NfL levels were associated with greater AxD and MD in the right cingulum, reflected by warm-colored clusters extending along the trajectory of the tract. The figure highlights both the hemispheric specificity and the directional divergence of NfL–DTI relationships between controls and clinical AD individuals.

CSF proteins (p-tau, t-tau, and Aβ42) versus GFAP and NfL

We next studied the correlation of serum and CSF GFAP and NfL levels, CSF Aβ42, Tau, and p-Tau levels. We found a significant positive correlation between CSF GFAP levels (r = 0.575, p = 0.003) and CSF t-Tau protein. We found no significant correlation with Aβ42 or p-Tau.

Discussion

Our primary objective was to evaluate GFAP and NfL levels in the blood and CSF of LatAm-Brazilians patients and correlate these markers with DTI parameters to examine their relationship with changes in WM integrity, to understand the pathological substrates of these fluid biomarkers. We did not observe significant differences in these markers between AD patients and controls. However, we found interesting relationships between NfL and DTI parameters. We observed a negative correlation between serum NfL levels and AxD in the left cingulum of clinical AD patients, and we also observed positive correlations between serum NfL and AxD in the right cingulum in control subjects. This contrasting pattern suggests that the relationship between serum NfL and white matter microstructure may vary according to clinical status and underlying neurobiological processes.

In the clinical AD group, elevated serum NfL levels might indicate more severe neuroaxonal damage, possibly due to a higher risk of disease progression. The negative correlation observed between serum NfL and AxD in the left cingulum suggests that higher NfL levels are associated with decreased AxD, which may seem counterintuitive as increased AxD usually signifies WM damage. However, research has shown inconsistent results regarding the sensitivity of NfL levels in CSF and blood to microstructural changes in WM detected by DTI.51,52 Severe axonal degeneration can disrupt the structural integrity of WM tracts, significantly altering diffusion properties.53,54 Studies have also highlighted differences in NfL levels between serum and CSF, with NfL in CSF appearing to be a more reliable biomarker of neurodegeneration in early-stage sporadic AD compared to NfL in blood.55–58 Conversely, in the control group, the positive correlation between serum NfL and AxD in the right cingulum may reflect a different neurobiological process. In cognitively healthy older adults, serum NfL variations could be due to minor neuroaxonal renewal, where small increases in NfL reflect slight elevations in AxD as part of a non-pathological aging process. 59 However, since our control group includes older individuals, and we lack CSF protein data, such as Aβ and Tau, it is possible that some of these individuals could be at risk of progressing towards AD. The lack of a difference in serum NfL and GFAP between AD and controls is in support of this conclusion.

The presence of these contrasting correlations in different brain hemispheres and groups may also reflect lateralized vulnerabilities and patterns of disease progression. Furthermore, disruption of white matter integrity in specific tracts, such as those observed in our study, may contribute to the clinical manifestations of AD, including cognitive decline and functional impairments. The left cingulum, crucial for functions such as memory and language, 60 is more severely affected in AD,61,62 showing a stronger and more direct relationship between NfL and disruption of WM integrity.9,59 Conversely, the right cingulum may be less affected in the early stages of the disease, or in cognitively healthy individuals, may present different neurobiological dynamics that are more related to maintenance and small adaptive changes than to extensive neurodegeneration. 63 The different correlations of serum NfL with AD in the left and right cingulum between AD patients and controls highlight the complexity of neurodegenerative and neuroadaptive processes. These findings further support the relationship between serum NfL and WM microstructure, while suggesting that the direction and spatial distribution of these associations may differ across clinical groups. Together, these results emphasize the importance of considering hemispheric and disease-stage-related differences when interpreting NfL-related changes in neurodegenerative research.

In our study, we did not observe significant correlations between serum or CSF GFAP levels and DTI measures. This may be due to GFAP being a marker of astrocytic activation, reflecting a glial response to neurodegeneration, 64 rather than a direct measure of the structural integrity of WM tracts. Additionally, few studies to date have explored these correlations.33,65 GFAP and DTI measurements may capture different aspects of the neurodegenerative process, the temporal dynamics of glial activation and WM damage may be different. GFAP levels may be elevated in response to acute or chronic astrocytic activation, 66 while changes in DTI measurements may occur at a different stage of disease progression. 53 Additionally, the sensitivity of DTI to detect subtle changes in WM integrity may vary depending on the specific tracts being analyzed. Lastly, variability in individual responses to neurodegeneration, including genetic, environmental, and disease severity factors, may also contribute to the lack of correlation observed in our sample.

The significant positive correlation between GFAP levels in the CSF and t-Tau protein suggests a specific relationship between glial activation and neurodegeneration in our study population. GFAP is a marker of astrocytic activation, and elevated levels indicate increased astrocytic response to neuronal damage. 66 This finding is consistent with the understanding that astrocytes become reactive in response to neuronal injury, releasing GFAP into the CSF. 64 The elevated t-Tau levels further confirm ongoing neurodegeneration, as tau protein is released into the extracellular space upon axonal damage. 67 The significant correlation between these markers suggests a synchronized response of astrocytic activation to the underlying neuronal damage in AD. 64 However, the absence of a significant correlation between GFAP and Aβ42 or p-Tau may indicate that the glial response, as measured by GFAP, is more closely linked to general neurodegenerative processes than to specific amyloid pathology or hyperphosphorylation of the tau process, in addition to that the absence of a significant correlation in our case could be reflecting a differential time course or regional specificity of these pathological processes.

Although our study successfully identified associations between DTI parameters and molecular markers, it is crucial to acknowledge its limitations. These include a relatively small sample size and the absence of CSF markers in our control group. The lack of CSF markers limits our ability to fully assess whether any pathological processes are occurring in these individuals. It is also important to note that we analyzed the MCI and AD dementia participants as a single group. Although subgroup analyses can sometimes provide additional insights, the sample sizes—particularly in the AD dementia subgroup (n = 15)—were insufficient to yield stable or interpretable correlation estimates, especially for biomarkers with high biological variability such as GFAP and NfL. Conducting separate analyses under these constraints would substantially reduce statistical power, increase the likelihood of spurious findings, and limit the robustness of conclusions. Therefore, consistent with prior literature that considers amyloid-positive individuals across clinical stages as part of a unified clinical AD group, we maintained the combined analytical approach. We acknowledge this as a methodological consideration and highlight that larger samples would allow future studies to evaluate potential stage-specific associations.

Expanding the study to include additional parameters of WM integrity and employing a longitudinal approach to follow patients with MCI who convert to AD would be beneficial. Furthermore, the unexpected correlations in our results suggest complex interactions between different biomarkers in disease progression, emphasizing the need for further investigation into their underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

In summary, our study sheds light on the relationship between NfL and DTI measures of white matter integrity in Latin American patients across clinical AD patients. The observed negative correlation between serum NfL and AxD in the left cingulum suggests a potential link between NfL and white matter changes, warranting further study. Additionally, the positive correlation between CSF GFAP and t-Tau indicates an association between glial activation and neurodegeneration in this population. Despite limitations, our findings highlight the complexity of biomarker interactions in AD and emphasize the need for larger, longitudinal studies in diverse populations. This research adds to the growing AD biomarker literature and supports future efforts to enhance diagnostics and therapies.
