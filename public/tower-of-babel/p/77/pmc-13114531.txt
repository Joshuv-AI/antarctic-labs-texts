Metal‐Free Submicron‐Hollow‐Fiber Conjugated Polymer Sponges for Efficient Pollutant Removal and Thermal Insulation
Authors: Songah Jeong, Hyungwoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInspired by naturally occurring tubular structures, hollow fibers offer unique advantages for a wide range of contemporary applications, such as a high surface‐area‐to‐volume ratio, a low density, and directional mass transport. Although polymeric materials, including conjugated polymer networks, have shown promise for the fabrication of these architectures, their synthesis typically lacks design modularity and often requires sacrificial templates or metal catalysts, limiting their overall sustainability. In this study, we present a new molecular design strategy for metal‐free conjugated hollow fibers that can self‐assemble into a spongy monolith. Selected monomers undergo rapid Chichibabin‐type condensation to form fully conjugated aromatic networks, which simultaneously self‐assemble into uniform submicron hollow fibers that, in turn, become entangled to form monoliths. This transformation occurs via single‐step, one‐pot synthesis without the need for metals, templates, or post‐processing. The designed sponge exhibits efficient light absorption, a high surface area, and excellent mass transfer, allowing it to be employed for multiple functions, including oil absorption, the photocatalytic decolorization of dyes, the capture of gas‐phase iodine, and thermal insulation. This bottom‐up design represents a versatile and sustainable platform for the engineering of hollow‐fiber‐based materials with broad utility for energy, environmental, and biomedical applications.

Introduction

Hollow fibers, defined as a tubular structure with an internal hollow core extending along the axial direction, are abundant in nature and function as conduits or structural support, such as in plant vasculature, human blood vessels, and marine Porifera. This geometry has various beneficial features, such as a high surface‐area‐to‐volume ratio, a low density, high surface accessibility, and efficient transport behavior. For this reason, hollow fibers have been employed in many contemporary applications, including separation technology, biomedical devices, drug delivery, thermal insulation, and textile engineering. Because the hollow fibers employed for these functions require structural and dimensional versatility and tailored functionality, rational fiber design strategies have been devised at the molecular level to achieve the necessary structural hierarchy [1, 2].

In macromolecular design, conventional hollow‐fiber polymer architectures can be molecularly engineered using various techniques, including spinning (e.g., dry/wet, coaxial, melt, blow, or electrospinning), coating (e.g., dip coating or sintering), printing, gas forming, and self‐crimping [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] However, a template is still required for most of these methods [9, 10, 11], providing physicochemical stability and surface compatibility for the formation of the outer layer [12, 13], after which it is removed via solvent dissolution or thermal/photonic treatment [14, 15] to create the hollow core. Various organic and inorganic materials can be used as this template, including polyacrylonitrile, polyethylene oxide, polyvinylpyrrolidone, and silica [16, 17], which are compatible with the materials most commonly used for hollow fibers (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane, polyethersulfone, polyimide, or polysaccharide). Despite their accessibility and cost‐effectiveness, these fabrication methods still face intrinsic limitations in terms of design expandability, morphological control, scalability, and discontinuous processing due to their reliance on top‐down template‐based fabrication. Of the various macromolecules used to fabricate hollow fibers, conjugated polymer network‐based materials, such as conjugated microporous polymers (CMPs), are particularly attractive due to their intrinsic π‐conjugated skeleton, high structural rigidity, broad design versatility with various building blocks, and physicochemical stability. They can also form high‐dimensional global morphologies via self‐assembly during the cross‐linking process without the need for a template [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Although various hollow fibers have been designed using these materials and fabricated in a bottom‐up manner with tailored functions [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29], their polymerization reactions (e.g., Sonogashira–Hagihara, Suzuki–Miyaura, and Yamamoto reactions) typically rely on transition metal catalysts [30, 31, 32]. However, removing the residual metal impurities from the resulting network fibers is difficult, leading to concerns regarding toxicity and high costs [33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Metal‐free reactions (such as aldol condensation, Knoevenagel condensation, pyrrole–aldehyde coupling, and quinone–amine reactions) [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43] have been employed as energy‐efficient and environmentally benign synthetic routes [44] for the construction of conjugated assembled structures [45, 46]. Nevertheless, structures fabricated in this manner are generally spherical or solid‐fibrous particles, with few reports of the development of metal‐free hollow fibers [47]. In this context, it remains necessary to devise novel metal‐free design strategies and processing methods for conjugated hollow fibers to improve their practical applicability, which would contribute to the engineering fundamentals underlying hollow‐fiber materials.

In this paper, we present a macromolecular design for the single‐step, one‐pot, and metal‐free synthesis of hollow fibers and their in situ assembly into a spongy monolith. To achieve this, aromatic acetyl and aldehyde monomers were subject to Chichibabin‐type condensation polymerization, which involves pyridine ring formation under acidic conditions without the use of a metal catalyst. Due to the rapid reaction kinetics and favorable thermodynamics, fully conjugated pyridinic networks readily formed during this reaction, and these networks spontaneously self‐assembled into submicron‐sized hollow fibers during cross‐linking. These fibers subsequently became entangled into a spongy monolith without the need for any post‐polymerization processing. The resulting conjugated hollow‐fiber spongy monoliths were thus made up of conjugated pyridinic frameworks at a molecular level while exhibiting robustness, reusability, and a hollow morphology on a macroscopic scale. Meanwhile, the synergetic combination of these structural features led to improved light absorption, a high surface area, efficient adsorption, and improved mass transport. Based on these properties, we demonstrated the applicability of the designed sponges in oil absorption, photocatalytic dye degradation, iodine capture, and thermal insulation. We envisage that the proposed hollow‐fiber sponge could be developed into a versatile platform by incorporating exogenous components, such as MXenes, metal–organic frameworks, or carbon materials, to increase its mechanical integrity or establish additional functionalities for the fiber networks [48, 49, 50, 51]. These composites could then be extended to a wide range of environmental, energy‐related, and biomedical applications, including separation, electromagnetic shielding, and piezoelectric systems.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1 presents the proposed modular design of pyridinic networks fabricated using select monomers via metal‐free condensation and the formation of self‐assembled structures. In the primary route, a triacetyl quarterphenyl core (tQP) and a dialdehyde linker terephthalaldehyde (TPA) were employed at a molar ratio of 2:1 to produce a fully conjugated pyridinic network (tQP‐TPA) through Chichibabin cyclo‐condensation at 0.24 M in acetic acid (AcOH) in the presence of ammonium acetate, which generated a pyridine ring between the monomers. Owing to the synergistic contribution of favorable molecular geometry, reaction kinetics, and local assembly environment, tQP‐TPA readily self‐assembled into relatively uniform smooth hollow fibers immediately after the mixing of the two monomers, despite its intrinsic network rigidity, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Figure 1). The cross‐section of these hollow fibers was investigated using a cooling cross‐section polisher (CCP), revealing an overall diameter of ∼0.54 µm and a wall thickness of ∼0.18 µm; the wide‐area cross‐sectional view is shown in Figure S1a. Because this concomitant assembly process during cross‐linking is highly sensitive to the reaction conditions [18, 24, 52, 53], changes to the monomer concentration (e.g., 0.08 m) and solvent composition (e.g., 5:3 AcOH/DMF) led to the formation of micron‐sized particles or defective fibers, instead of the well‐formed hollow fibers observed above (Figure S1b,c). The subsequent entanglement of the optimal hollow fibers produced a spongy monolith within 12 h at 120 °C. This tQP‐TPA sponge was lightweight, with a density of 0.87 g cm−3, and was able to rest on the head of a dandelion without collapsing it. It is denser than typical lightweight aerogels but remains lighter than porous ceramics or bulk polymers, placing it within the density range commonly reported for porous polymer foams, which reflects a balance between structural integrity and porosity. Meanwhile, because it contained sp2‐hybridized hollow fibers embedded with pyridine units, this sponge had the potential for use in a number of applications, including solvent uptake, the photodegradation of organic dyes, gas‐phase iodine capture, and thermal insulation.

We also tested this molecular design strategy with alternative acetyl and aldehyde monomers (Figure 1). For example, a triacetyl phenyl core (tP) combined with TPA led to spherical particles (tP‐TPA) with a diameter of approximately 2.5 µm, similar to those observed previously [38, 39]. In contrast, wrinkled or cauliflower‐like particles were obtained when using diacetyl biphenyl (dBP) or phenyl (dP) cores, respectively, under the same conditions. We conjecture that the tP core could not properly self‐assemble into fibers due to its smaller aromatic structure compared to tQP, which weakened the aromatic stacking and/or network ordering during polymerization [24]. In addition, the use of difunctional dBP and dP cores resulted in the formation of particles with rough surfaces, which could be attributed to unfavorable changes in the connectivity or cross‐linking density within the pyridinic networks. Furthermore, these control particulate samples could not form a monolith due to the absence of fiber entanglement. Collectively, these results highlight the importance of the core geometry and the positioning of acetyl functionalities in directing anisotropic self‐assembly, for which tQP was the most suitable core monomer. Meanwhile, the condensation polymerization of tQP in conjunction with isophthalaldehyde (IPA) or 4,4′‐biphenyldicarboxaldehyde (BPDA) yielded self‐assembled monoliths that are yet composed of solid rather than hollow fibers, in contrast to tQP‐TPA. So, as shown in the SEM images in Figure 1, the bent IPA linker produced a solid‐fiber monolith (tQP‐IPA) with a thick diameter of approximately 2.4 µm, which was similar to the solid fibers generated from the long, linear BPDA linker (tQP‐BPDA) (∼1.1 µm), presumably due to their inherent length, steric hindrance, and/or distorted configuration. On this basis, tQP‐TPA, tQP‐IPA, and tQP‐BPDA were chosen as representative monolithic systems sharing a common tQP‐based pyridinic framework formed under identical polymerization–assembly conditions. Despite differences in internal fiber architecture, this comparative set was deliberately used to examine monomer‐induced morphological evolution and its influence on monolithic performance, rather than to claim fully isolated structure–property relationships.

The formation of the assembled hollow fibers is likely governed by a complex interplay of multiple factors rather than a single dominant driving force. However, early‐stage time‐resolved SEM images taken during tQP‐TPA synthesis indicate that the fibrous structures possess a hollow interior from the onset of growth, implying that hollow‐fiber nucleation precedes subsequent anisotropic growth and that the internal void is preserved during the elongation (Figure S2). This growth behavior is further supported by the changes in aspect ratio and diameter analysis estimated from SEM images, which reveal a pronounced increase in aspect ratio with only a marginal change in fiber diameter during polymerization (Figure 2a,b). Such axial growth could be facilitated by the formation of reversible imine intermediates during Chichibabin reaction, together with the increasing planarity of the developing pyridinic network.

The presence of hollow fibers in the tQP‐TPA sample was confirmed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), revealing an outer diameter of 540 ± 36 nm, an inner diameter of 200 ± 25 nm, and a wall thickness of 180 ± 35 nm (Figure 2c, top left). Due to its π‐conjugated backbone, tQP‐TPA absorbed light up to the UVA and blue regions, with maximum absorption observed at 350 nm, subsequently emitting green fluorescence with maximum emission at 490 nm under excitation at 430 nm (Figure S3). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was thus used to visualize the fluorescent individual fibers under 405 nm laser irradiation (Figure 2c, top right). The distributions of the fiber diameters observed within the monoliths were found to be dependent on the monomer composition (Figure 2c, bottom). A relatively uniform distribution was observed for the fibers in tQP‐TPA (150 nm–1.2 µm), which was attributable to the rapid formation of hollow fibers following the mixing of the components. In contrast, tQP‐BPDA, which consisted of solid fibers, exhibited a broad distribution of fiber diameters with an average of 1.1 ± 0.8 µm, which increased to 2.4 ± 1.1 µm for the thickest solid fibers observed for tQP‐IPA. These increases are likely to originate from their inefficient network packing due to structural distortion, weaker aromatic stacking, and limited crosslinking density, as compared to tQP‐TPA.

The specific surface area and pore size distribution of the fibrous networks were assessed using nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements (Figure 2d). All of the samples exhibited similar Type II Langmuir isotherms, indicating that capillary condensation occurred mainly via slit‐shaped macropores, while their surface areas were calculated to be <10 m2 g−1 using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Nonetheless, subsequent pore size distribution analysis based on non‐local density functional theory (NLDFT) revealed that tQP‐TPA contained some micropores with an average diameter of ∼0.5 nm, whereas tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA had mesopores with average diameters of ∼13 and ∼15 nm, respectively, because of the different monomer composition. In addition, when evaluated using X‐ray diffraction (XRD), the crystalline structure of the assembled monoliths slightly differed (Figure 2e). For example, tQP‐TPA exhibited peaks at 2θ = 5.1°, 9.1°, 10.3°, 13.7°, and 15.6°, corresponding to the Miller indices of the (100), (110), (200), (210), and (300) facets, respectively, which was indicative of hexagonal packing with a lattice parameter of 19.5 Å. Although tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA exhibited XRD patterns that were also in close agreement with hexagonally packed structures, their lattice parameters increased slightly to 19.7 and 19.6 Å, respectively, due to changes in the packing density and structural order.

The mechanical properties of the fibrous monoliths were examined using compressive testing of uniformly sized cylindrical samples (diameter, 5 mm; height, 13 mm) (Figure 2f). During loading–unloading cycles of compressive strain of up to 60%, the compressive Young's modulus of tQP‐TPA was measured to be 1.17 MPa, which is similar to that of synthetic foams, silicone rubbers, and biological tissues [54, 55]. In contrast, the less‐rigid tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA had considerably lower modulus values of 0.48 and 0.58 MPa, respectively, illustrating the enhanced mechanical robustness of the hollow‐fiber‐based tQP‐TPA. However, all of the monoliths were able to recover their initial shape after compression (Figure 2f, inset), thus they were re‐measurable during the repeated tests (Figure S4a). For comparison, tensile testing for tQP‐TPA was performed; however, a reduced tensile modulus of 0.73 ± 0.01 MPa and a short elongation at break of ∼7% were found (Figure S4b), which reflects the nature of fiber entanglement within the monolith rather than the intrinsic mechanical properties of individual fibers. In addition, flexural fatigue tests over 1000 bending–recovery cycles and flexural creep tests at various temperatures showed stable mechanical behavior without noticeable degradation, supporting the high structural reliability of the cross‐linked network (Figure S5).

The elemental composition of the monoliths was investigated using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The deconvoluted C 1s peaks were observed at 284.48 eV (C atoms associated with pyridine rings) and 283.78 eV (other aromatic sp2 C atoms) for the samples, which, in conjunction with the N 1s peak at 397.68 eV associated with pyridinic N atoms, supports the formation of aromatic conjugated networks with pyridinic units (Figure 2g) [56]. The N/C ratio was estimated to be 0.018 for tQP‐TPA, higher than that for tQP‐IPA (0.007) and tQP‐BPDA (0.015), suggesting that pyridine moieties accounted for 10% of the total aromatic groups in the tQP‐TPA framework (Figure 2h). This value was close to the theoretical maximum, suggesting that the optimal combination of monomer geometry and functionality produced the high reactivity and structural compatibility required for efficient network formation and subsequent self‐assembly for tQP‐TPA.

The presence of conjugated pyridinic skeletons in the monoliths was confirmed using Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR) (Figure S6), with characteristic peaks for aromatic C═C and C═N stretching and C─N stretching observed at 1600 and 1274 cm−1, respectively [57] In addition, all of the samples were thermally stable up to ∼320 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere due to their cross‐linked structure, with thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) producing high char yields of ∼60% at 800 °C (Figure S7). After heating, the fibrous morphologies in all monoliths were even maintained despite slight dimensional changes (Figure S8), while the carbonized monoliths became fragile without compressibility.

The intrinsic fibrous structure and porosity of tQP‐TPA promoted efficient mass transfer, while its facile formation and compressible nature ensured its workability and reusability under various conditions. Taking advantage of these features, we analyzed the absorption capacity of tQP‐TPA with a range of organic solvents, including fuel oils (Figure 3). tQP‐TPA was able to readily absorb organic solvents with low viscosities (0.3–1.1 mPa·s at 25 °C) and viscous liquids such as automotive fuels (0.4–5.0 mPa·s) and silicone oil (9.7 mPa·s) [27], with equilibrium reached within 10 s for these absorbates (Figure 3a). The absorption capacity at equilibrium was generally proportional to the density of the absorbate, except for water (Figure 3b), confirming that the absorption behavior of the hollow channels was generally independent of the viscosity. However, the superhydrophobicity of tQP‐TPA expelled water, as evidenced by a measured water contact angle of 152°. Unlike tQP‐TPA, tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA exhibited an inferior absorption performance and also absorbed water, exhibiting a linear correlation between the absorption capacity and the density of the absorbate (Figure S9). The instant water contact angles for tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA were measured to be 121° and 135°, respectively, lower than that in tQP‐TPA. These could be attributed to the hollow fibers that provide greater hydrophobicity and a larger storage capacity than the control solid fibers, which relied only on inter‐fiber voids. The structural robustness of tQP‐TPA was further confirmed by compress testing after solvent adsorption using chloroform as a representative solvent, which revealed an identical stress–strain curve compared to that before the adsorption (Figure S10).

Given the structural characteristics of tQP‐TPA, we investigated its photocatalytic performance. Its optical bandgap, estimated using a Tauc plot (Figure 3c), was calculated to be 3.45 eV, which is sufficient to absorb UVA light. The apparent quantum yield (AQY) of tQP‐TPA was also determined to be 1.2% under excitation using a 150 W xenon lamp, comparable to or exceeding values reported for other metal‐free photocatalytic systems [58]. As a proof of concept, the photocatalytic decolorization of methylene blue (MB) was measured using UV–vis spectroscopy in the presence of tQP‐TPA under incandescent light from a halogen lamp (0.5 mW cm−2). The MB solution (4 ppm in a 4:1 water–methanol mixture) became clear after irradiation, with an MB degradation rate of > 95% after 9 h. In contrast, tQP‐TPA adsorbed ∼32% of the MB after 9 h without irradiation through physical adsorption, and negligible color change was observed under irradiation in the absence of tQP‐TPA (Figure 3d). The observed spectral changes during this degradation process are presented in Figure S11. Meanwhile, tQP‐TPA powder (tQP‐TPA‐p), formed at the low concentration of 0.08 m, exhibited an enhanced degradation rate and adsorption efficiency under the same conditions due to an increase in the exposed surface area, with > 96% degradation after 4 h under irradiation and ∼67% without irradiation (Figure S12). This suggested that dye degradation primarily occurred on the accessible surface during the dye adsorption process [59, 60]. Furthermore, we quantified H2O2, a representative reactive oxygen species (ROS), generated by tQP‐TPA using a Pierce quantitative peroxide assay kit and UV–vis spectrometry (Figure 3e) (spectra in Figure S13). The results indicate consistent H2O2 evolution under light irradiation, in contrast to the control cases without the catalyst or light, supporting the photocatalytic activity of tQP‐TPA. The dyes methyl orange (MO) and basic green 1 (BG1) were also photochemically degraded under the same conditions using tQP‐TPA (Figure 3e). Despite a slower degradation than that for MB, their degradation rates of > 95% were observed within 20 h after irradiation. The adsorption profiles in dark conditions are presented in Figure S14, revealing incomplete dye removal in the absence of light irradiation. In addition, although total organic carbon analysis was beyond the scope of this study, incorporating such measurements in future work would enable a more comprehensive assessment of the photocatalytic process by distinguishing dye decolorization from complete organic mineralization.

The photocatalytic activity of tQP‐TPA was also compared with that of the solid‐fiber‐based tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA samples. The optical bandgaps were 3.47 and 3.48 eV for tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA, respectively (Figure S15a), slightly higher than that of tQP‐TPA due to their lower effective conjugation length. tQP‐IPA achieved 94% MB degradation within 11 h, compared to 85% for tQP‐BPDA within 22 h. This was likely because the solid structure and higher bandgap of these samples hindered internal mass transfer and light absorption during the degradation process (Figure 3f). MB molecules also adsorbed onto the control samples at a lower rate after 9 h in the dark (<30%) than for tQP‐TPA (Figure S15b). The degradation rates for all three samples were estimated following the pseudo‐first‐order kinetic model. The rate constant (k) for tQP‐TPA was 0.45 h−1, 3.5 times higher than that of tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA (both k, 0.13 h−1) (Figure 3f; inset). In addition, tQP‐TPA could be readily recycled by simply washing it in methanol, retaining comparable catalytic activity over five consecutive degradation cycles (Figure S16). Considering the comparable optical bandgaps of three pyridinic monoliths, the enhanced photocatalytic activity of tQP‐TPA could be reasonably attributed to its hollow‐fiber architecture, which provides a higher accessible surface area and more efficient mass transport pathways. Although the overall fiber diameters are not identical among the samples, the presence of internal channels in tQP‐TPA is expected to offer additional active interfaces, thereby facilitating faster dye degradation kinetics.

The ability of tQP‐TPA to capture gas‐phase iodine, a volatile and hazardous radioactive species, was also tested. After exposure to iodine in a closed system at 80 °C, vapor‐phase adsorption occurred, with a cylindrical sample of tQP‐TPA (diameter, 5 mm; height, 5 mm) darkening in color. However, this sample could be fully restored to its original state through washing with methanol, illustrating its potential reusability for iodine capture (Figure 4a). Figure 4b presents the adsorption behavior of tQP‐TPA, showing that, following exposure, the iodine uptake of tQP‐TPA rapidly increased to 0.71 g g−1 within 24 h. This value was comparable to or exceeded that of activated carbons, zeolites, aerogels, and commercial polymer sorbents [61], and higher than that observed for the solid‐fiber networks fabricated in the present study (0.39 g g−1 for tQP‐IPA; 0.56 g g−1 for tQP‐BPDA). These results were attributed to the presence of hollow pyridinic conjugated channels in tQP‐TPA. In contrast, a commercial melamine foam exhibited a fairly limited uptake of iodine (0.1 g g−1), highlighting the significance of the microstructure in gas‐phase capture applications.

After iodine capture, the pyridinic monoliths were also investigated using TGA (Figure 4c). All used samples experienced an abrupt weight loss at around 110 °C due to the thermal desorption of iodine [62]. In particular, the tQP‐TPA sample, which achieved the highest iodine uptake, lost 22% of its weight, compared to 30% and 34% for tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA, respectively. This relatively low loss of weight for tQP‐TPA could arise from the strong capture of iodine within the hollow channels due to the stronger charge‐transfer interactions between iodine and the interior pyridinic wall, which were not readily dissociated by heating [63].

SEM and energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirmed a change in morphology for tQP‐TPA after iodine adsorption. The individual fibers became smoother, thicker, and more densely filled compared to their initial state, with an average fiber thickness of approximately 700 ± 130 nm (Figure 4d, top). This suggests that both the interior and exterior of the fibers contributed to iodine adsorption. The corresponding EDS maps of the carbon and iodine atoms provided clear evidence that gas‐phase iodine molecules were captured by the fibers (Figure 4d, bottom). The EDS signal for iodine increased significantly after adsorption (Figure S17a), with subsequent quantitative analysis revealing an iodine content of 7.24 wt.% (Figure S17b).

XPS analysis was employed to detail the chemical state of the bound iodine (Figure 4e). After iodine exposure, the C 1s spectrum for tQP‐TPA retained the two peaks at 284.38 and 283.78 eV. However, the N 1s peak shifted from 398.68 to 398.88 eV, indicating the formation of charge‐transfer complexes between the nitrogen and iodine atoms [64, 65, 66]. In addition, new I 3d signals appeared after iodine adsorption as two sets of doublets due to the dissociative adsorption of iodine and its spin‐orbit coupling: 630.68 and 619.18 eV for I2, representing I 3d3/2 and I 3d5/2, respectively, and 629.08 and 617.58 eV for I3 − [66, 67]. These peaks became more prominent in the full survey spectrum (Figure S18a), indicating the presence of iodine species. The calculated I/C atomic ratio increased significantly to 0.64 after iodine adsorption due to the high uptake efficiency of the material. The adsorbed iodine in tQP‐TPA was also analyzed using FT‐IR (Figure S18b). The characteristic C─N stretching in pyridine moieties, observed at 1273 cm−1, was notably reduced after adsorption, corroborating the presence of strongly bound iodine due to the pyridinic nitrogen, aligning with the high observed adsorption performance. Due to its hollow‐fiber structure, tQP‐TPA was reusable after iodine desorption when washed in a solvent (Figure 4f). In particular, after immersion in methanol for 3 h, the iodine‐loaded monolith was completely restored, achieving a comparable iodine uptake during five repeated adsorption–desorption cycles under the same conditions without a significant loss of performance.

In fact, tQP‐TPA was also able to capture iodine in solution, with an iodine uptake of 0.05 g g−1 when immersed in a 2 mM iodine solution in n‐hexane (Figure S19). Its uptake in solution was higher than that of tQP‐BPDA and tQP‐IPA (0.02 g g−1 for both) and that of melamine foam (0.01 g g−1), which could be reproducible following the washing process. The adsorption isotherm of tQP‐TPA was also measured and analyzed using both Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models [16], with the former providing a better fit to the experimental data, suggesting that iodine adsorption was dominated by monolayer chemisorption accompanied by physisorption (Figure 4g). The obtained parameters are summarized in Table S1. Nonetheless, it still remained too low for practical use due to the equilibrium for desorption in solution. Therefore, gas‐phase adsorption and solution‐based desorption cycles were the most appropriate practical use for tQP‐TPA. Based on kinetic analysis using a pseudo second order model, which is generally appropriate for chemisorption process, the adsorption rate constant (k 2) was determined to be 0.17 g g−1 h−1 (R 2, > 0.99) in gas phase, ∼4 times higher than that in the solution phase (k 2, 0.04 g g−1 h−1; R 2, 0.95). In addition, Raman spectroscopy further confirmed the presence of I5 − and I3 − anions, as evidenced by characteristic peaks at 169 and 109 cm−1, respectively, indicating that the adsorbed I2 molecules existed in an anion form owing to polarization or charge‐transfer interactions with the surface of tQP‐TPA (Figure 4h) [68]. Accordingly, the initially fluorescent monolith exhibiting a broad Raman peak centered at ∼1900 cm−1 became non‐fluorescent after adsorption (Figure 4i).

The potential use of the pyridinic monoliths as thermal insulation was explored due to their promising fiber‐based porous structure. Cylindrical monolith samples (diameter, 2 cm; height, 2 cm) of tQP‐TPA, tQP‐IPA, and tQP‐BPDA were prepared and placed on a hot plate at 250 °C, with changes in their temperature monitored using an infrared thermal camera (Figure 5a). Over time, the surface temperature of the samples gradually increased from the bottom, though tQP‐TPA exhibited the strongest resistance to a rise in temperature compared to the others. The temperature profiles for each monolith sample were systematically analyzed at three designated positions: h1 (0.1 cm from the bottom), h2 (1 cm), and h3 (2 cm) (Figure 5b). The temperature at each position reached thermal equilibrium within 10 min. At h1, tQP‐TPA, tQP‐IPA, and tQP‐BPDA reached the equilibriated temperatures of approximately 121 °C, 150 °C, and 147 °C, respectively, decreasing to 70 °C, 98 °C, and 90 °C at h2 and 44 °C, 68 °C, and 61 °C at h3, respectively, highlighting the superior thermal insulating properties of tQP‐TPA (Figure 5c). Meanwhile, tQP‐IPA and tQP‐BPDA showed no significant difference in thermal behavior, which is likely associated with the absence of internal channels despite differences in fiber diameter, underscoring the potential role of hollow architectures in air confinement and thermal insulation. Further, we quantitatively investigated the thermal insulating behavior of tQP‐TPA using the transient plane source (TPS) method, revealing intrinsic bulk thermal properties, including a thermal conductivity (λ) of 0.060 ± 0.001 W m−1 K−1, a thermal diffusivity (α) of 0.37 ± 0.01 mm2 s−1, and a volumetric heat capacity (ρCp) of 0.1629 ± 0.0065 MJ m−3 K−1, with minimal influence from external convection, surface radiation, or macroscopic sample geometry. These values are comparable to those of polymeric insulators, such as polymer foams, and remain lower than those of bulk polymers (λ, typically > 0.15 W m−1 K−1), supporting the effective insulation capability of the monolith.

The insulation performance of tQP‐TPA was also compared with other common materials. Cube‐shaped samples of wood, glass, and melamine foam similar in size to the tQP‐TPA sample were prepared and tested under the same conditions. These reference materials exhibited significantly higher surface temperatures than the pyridinic monolith during heating (Figure 5d). For example, after 20 min of heating, the surface temperature at h3 rapidly increased and exceeded 100 °C for glass, 90 °C for wood, and 80 °C for melamine foam, more than twice that observed for tQP‐TPA (Figure 5e). The temperature profiles at the other positions on the cube samples are presented in Figure S20, with temperatures that were markedly higher than those observed for tQP‐TPA.

The insulation capability of tQP‐TPA was subsequently assessed using a wax melting test to evaluate its practical potential. Polycaprolactone (PCL), which has a molecular weight (M n) of 2 kDa and a melting point (T m) of ∼50 °C, was thermally molded and cut into a star shape and placed on the top face of each sample, which was then heated on a 250 °C hot plate (Figure 5f). The PCL objects were dyed with MB when used with the melamine foam and tQP‐TPA for visual clarity. The PCL stars on the wood and glass samples completely melted and became transparent within 5 min of heating, while it formed a liquid droplet on the melamine foam due to the low wettability at the interface. These observations indicated that the top surface temperature exceeded 50°C. In contrast, the PCL stars on tQP‐TPA did not melt and retained their original shape even after 20 min on the plate due to its insulation properties. Furthermore, we prepared control tQP‐TPA monoliths with half the diameter and half the height. When placed on the hot plate, the top‐surface temperature reached 46 °C for the former and 66 °C for the latter, values comparable to the temperatures at h1 and h2 positions of the original monolith. These support that heat propagation within the monolith is primarily governed by the distance from the heat source rather than the shape. Consequently, the PCL objects on top of the control monolith with half the diameter did not melt, whereas those on top of the shorter monolith did (Figure S21).

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated a metal‐free molecular design strategy for the synthesis of conjugated hollow fibers and their in situ assembly into spongy monoliths via single‐step, Chichibabin‐type condensation polymerization. The use of specific triacetyl and dialdehyde monomers led to the rapid formation of a fully conjugated pyridinic network, which simultaneously self‐assembled into submicron hollow fibers that, in turn, spontaneously became entangled to form compressible monoliths without the need for a template or post‐treatment. The resulting sponges exhibited physicochemical stability, light absorption, a large surface area, and efficient mass transport, all of which were attributed to their hierarchical fibrous architecture and conjugated backbone. These properties enabled the optimal monolith to be employed in oil absorption, photocatalytic dye degradation, iodine capture, and thermal insulation. This work not only presents a scalable and sustainable route for the fabrication of metal‐free, hollow‐fiber materials but also advances the fundamental understanding of conjugated hollow‐fiber systems. We envision that the realization of rigorously isostructural monolithic systems in the future will allow a more precise and quantitative understanding of the intrinsic behavior of these conjugated monoliths. Also, with the incorporation of other functional groups and/or synergistic additives, this platform could be further extended to a broad scope of practical environmental, energy, and biomedical applications.
