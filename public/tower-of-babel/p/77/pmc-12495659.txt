An ADR pharmaceutical care for severe gastrointestinal bleeding of rivaroxaban in a patient with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation based on TDM and genetic testing: a case report
Authors: Youqi Huang, Hongjin Gao, Mingyu Chen, Yuze Lin, Huiting Liu, Min Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDue to its predictable pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics, stable blood concentration, and relatively short half-life, rivaroxaban is widely used in the prevention and treatment of thrombosis. It nevertheless exhibits a certain level of inter-individual variability, and its safety concerns, including bleeding, are also becoming more noteworthy.Case presentationThis paper describes an elderly patient with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation that was complicated with coronary heart disease, who is a homozygous mutation carrier of the ABCB1 allele (rs1045642 C > T, rs1128503 C > T, rs2032582 G > T). He was developed severe gastrointestinal bleeding during administration of oral rivaroxaban combined with aspirin. We investigated the possible causes of the bleeding, and any potential correlation with the ABCB1 gene polymorphism, combined with antiplatelet drugs and anemia.ConclusionIn the treatment of patients with atrial fibrillation, doctors should pay close attention to drug interactions with antiplatelet agents in high-risk groups and closely monitor various examination indexes, including hemoglobin. In this case, bleeding might be potentially influenced by homozygous mutations in ABCB1, but more clinical data are needed to clarify the association between ABCB1 polymorphism and rivaroxaban pharmacokinetics and bleeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02222-4.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common persistent arrhythmia, and it increases in prevalence with age and cardiovascular comorbidities [1]. It is characterized by continuous, rapid, and irregular atrial beats that may be asymptomatic or present with symptoms such as dyspnea and dizziness. It can also be associated with serious complications, including increased risk for stroke. Oral anticoagulants (OACs) are a cornerstone for the prevention of stroke and the treatment of systemic embolism in patients with AF. Multiple studies have shown that non-vitamin K antagonist OACs (NOACs) are more effective than warfarin for preventing thromboembolic events and reducing the risk of bleeding events in patients with nonvalvular AF [2, 3], as a result, NOAC prescriptions have increased rapidly. Rivaroxaban is the first NOAC for clinical use to directly inhibit Factor Xa. Although NOACs have predictable pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics, preserving stable blood concentration and having a relatively short half-life [4, 5], they still show a certain degree of inter-individual variability, and safety issues, such as bleeding have attracted increasing attention [6]. We report a 73-year-old AF patient treated with rivaroxaban who presented with severe anemia associated with gastrointestinal bleeding, in which an ABCB1 gene polymorphism and drug–drug interaction (DDI) may have been contributing factors.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old male patient with a body mass of 65 kg and body mass index of 21.2 kg·m−2 had heart palpitations with no obvious cause 8 years prior and was treated at our hospital with warfarin 3.75 mg daily anticoagulant after a 24 h Holter electrocardiogram examination revealed AF. The patient reported regular monitoring of the international normalized ratio, and was later adjusted to rivaroxaban tablet 15 mg daily anticoagulation treatment for 2 years; the palpitation symptoms continued to occur from time to time. On September 23, 2022, the patient was admitted to the hospital for a magnetic resonance examination of his head, and the results showed that the left pontine infarct was acute to subacute. Color Doppler examination of the arteries of both lower limbs showed that the intima for both lower limbs was thickened due to plaque formation, and there was a segmental occlusion of the left anterior tibial artery. The patient was treated with an aspirin enteric-coated tablet 100 mg daily. Five days before admission, he had experienced black stool (with no obvious cause), accompanied by general fatigue, dizziness, nausea, and acid reflux. On April 2, 2023, the patient went to the emergency department for treatment due to “dizziness, weakness, paroxysmal palpitations, and black stool” and was admitted to the emergency room with “severe anemia.” Upon admission, his body temperature was 36.5℃, pulse was 135 beats/min, respiration was 20 breaths/min, blood pressure was 136/68 mmHg, and there was severe anemia. The patient has a history of hypertension, diabetes, AF, cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease, and anxiety.

Laboratory tests indicated hemoglobin 51 g·L−1 (normal, 130–175 g/L), N-terminal type B natriuretic peptide precursor 1103.00 Pg·mL−1 (normal, 0–900 Pg/mL), white blood cells 10.7 × 109·L−1 (normal, 3.5–9.5 × 109/L), red blood cells 3.12 × 1012/L (normal, 4.30–5.80 × 1012/L), platelet count 344 × 109/L (normal, 100–300 g/L), prothrombin time 13.3 s (normal, 9.8–12.1 s). After the patient was admitted to the hospital on April 2, aspirin and amlodipine atorvastatin calcium tablets were immediately stopped, and 40 mg omeprazole sodium twice daily was given intravenously to suppress acid and protect the stomach. He was also treated with fluids, blood transfusion, blood pressure control, blood glucose lowering, parenteral nutrition support, and other treatment. On April 4, the patient reported black stool once, and test for fecal occult blood were positive. Considering the high risk of bleeding, the patient was reduced to a rivaroxaban tablet 10 mg once daily as an anticoagulant. On April 6, the patient’s fecal occult blood test was negative, and no symptoms of black stool appeared. Gastroscopy indicated cardia and chronic atrophic gastritis, and anticoagulation was resumed with a rivaroxaban tablet 15 mg once daily. On April 8, the patient did not have black stool or hematemesis, and the hemoglobin was more stable than before, so oral administration of rabeprazole was changed to inhibit acid and protect the stomach. On the 12th day after admission on April 13, the patient showed good recovery without any bleeding events. The hemoglobin level has stabilized. Main drug treatment during hospitalization

Clinical pharmacists continue to pay attention to changes in gastrointestinal bleeding, AF, and coronary heart disease in the patient, and they are highly alert to the recurrence of complications, including bleeding during anticoagulant treatment. The patient with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and other chronic diseases need to adhere to a variety of drugs for a long time. In refined pharmaceutical care, clinical pharmacists seek to understand the patient’s past disease history and drug history. After finding that the patient used to take a 15 mg rivaroxaban tablet on an empty stomach in the morning, it was recommended that he change to oral administration after meals to increase its bioavailability and reduce the risk for stroke. On the fourth day of hospitalization, the physician considered that the patient’s risk of bleeding was too high and reduced the dose to 10 mg once daily. However, some studies have shown that the risk of bleeding from a 10 mg dose of rivaroxaban is close to that of the standard dose [7, 8]. In Asian AF patients, low doses of rivaroxaban are associated with a significantly increased risk for myocardial infarction [7]. In the present case, the clinical pharmacist suggested that rivaroxaban 15 mg once daily be resumed, and the doctor accepted the suggestion. On the fifth day after admission, the patient had no signs of bleeding such as skin and mucous petechiae or ecchymosis, gingival bleeding, hematuria, black stool, or other sign. His condition was stable, and he was discharged 12 days after admission. The clinical pharmacist provided medication education and guidance to the patient and his family at discharge; he was asked to eat regularly and to take the medicine according to the doctor’s advice and to seek medical attention as soon as possible if any bleeding was noticed, including bleeding gums, skin ecchymosis, or blood in the stool. After rivaroxaban was taken in combination with aspirin, the patient showed repeated instances of decreased hemoglobin level and positive results in fecal occult blood test. Studies have shown that low doses of aspirin can increase anemia events in the elderly [9], and the patient was advised to regularly monitor hemoglobin.

Discussion

Rivaroxaban has been approved in many countries for the prevention and treatment of venous thrombo embolism events, as well as for the prevention of stroke in patients with AF. In particular as the population ages, increasing numbers of patients are being treated with rivaroxaban. However, clinically significant interindividual variability in drug interactions and treatment responses has been reported in both healthy volunteers and patients [10, 11]. In two previous studies, among patients receiving OAC therapy, the rate of hospitalization for gastrointestinal bleeding in the rivaroxaban group was the highest [12, 13]. The risk of bleeding caused by rivaroxaban should be kept in mind in clinical settings, in particular with respect to certain patients who are undergoing anticoagulation therapy; the risk-benefit ratio of its use should be carefully evaluated.

The half-life of rivaroxaban in elderly patients is 11–13 h, and liver metabolism and kidney clearance capacity decline with age, leading to an accumulation of rivaroxaban in the body, thus affecting its pharmacokinetics and potentially leading to toxic side effects [14]. In addition, elderly people who have cardiovascular diseases due to vascular sclerosis, poor elasticity, atrophy of the gastric mucosa, or other reasons may have insufficient blood supply to the stomach, resulting in peptic ulcers and bleeding. With rivaroxaban, the activities of factor X and factor II in the elderly are significantly reduced, resulting in a natural sensitivity in the elderly to the pharmacological activity of rivaroxaban [15].

Rivaroxaban is mainly metabolized by the liver enzymes CYP3A4 and CYP2J2 and transported by P-glycoprotein and breast cancer resistance protein, the P-gp transporter of which is encoded by the ABCB1 genotype [16, 17]. Overall, the risk of major bleeding appears to be associated with elevated plasma levels of direct OACs [18]. Pharmacogenomic studies have found that individual pharmacokinetic differences in rivaroxaban are related to the gene polymorphisms of transporters and metabolic enzymes [19, 20]. However, clinical studies of the effect of the ABCB1 polymorphism on the pharmacokinetics and safety of rivaroxaban have not reached a uniform conclusion. Many single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are found in the ABCB1 gene. Studies of bleeding events after having taken rivaroxaban have mainly focused on the exons of rs1128503, rs1045642, and rs2032582 of ABCB1. We examined three SNPS for ABCB1 in this patient. All three were homozygous mutations (rs1045642:TT, rs1128503:TT, rs2032582:TT). A previous study found that clinically relevant non-major bleeding occurred more frequently in TT carriers than rs1045642 (CC) carriers in a Caucasian population, but the ABCB1 rs1045642 polymorphism did not affect the pharmacokinetics of rivaroxaban in patients with non-valvular AF aged 80 years and older [21]. A retrospective analysis of Mongolian patients found that rs1128503 site variation was correlated with rivaroxaban blood concentration but not with bleeding events [22]. Another study reported a case of rivaroxaban-induced hemorrhage in a homozygous TT genotype of rs1045642 and rs2032582 [23]. The genotypes rs1045642 (TT) and rs2032582 (TT) may be associated with an increased plasma concentration of rivaroxaban [24]. We measured rivaroxaban plasma concentrations at admission and after admission using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry, and found that steady-state trough and peak plasma concentrations did not exceed the expected rivaroxaban levels that are recommended by the European Heart Rhythm Association (trough plasma concentrations 12–137 ng/mL; peak concentration 184–343 ng/mL) [25]. Therefore, the correlation between severe, rivaroxaban-induced, gastrointestinal bleeding and rivaroxaban-induced metabolism in this patient with nonvalvular AF needs further investigation.

DDI is an important factor in rivaroxaban-induced bleeding. The main drugs used in this case were rivaroxaban, combined with aspirin enteric-coated tablets and amlodipine atorvastatin calcium tablets. The use of antiplatelet drugs is a component of the HAS-BLED score, and aspirin combined with NOACs for the treatment of nonvalvular AF increases the risk of bleeding in patients who are susceptible to life-threatening bleeding [26]. According to the HAS-BLED score and relevant clinical evidence, concomitant use of rivaroxaban and aspirin is a well-established risk factor for major bleeding events in patients with atrial fibrillation, especially among elderly individuals with multiple comorbidities [27, 28]. In this case, given the patient’s advanced age, history of coronary artery disease, and concurrent aspirin use, the drug-drug interaction between rivaroxaban and aspirin most likely played a central role in precipitating the gastrointestinal bleeding episode. Although studies have found no association between statin use and increased risk of bleeding [29, 30], it was considered that atorvastatin calcium tablets may irritate the gastric mucosa, leading to side effects such as abdominal pain and bloating. After admission of our case, aspirin, amlodipine, and atorvastatin calcium tablets were discontinued to treat gastrointestinal bleeding.

In addition, decreased hemoglobin level is among the main risk factors for bleeding in AF patients following rivaroxaban use. Obayo et al.. found that anemia was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding and clinically relevant non-major bleeding in NOAC patients [31]. This may be due to the high binding rate between rivaroxaban and plasma protein in the human body. When the hemoglobin level is reduced, rivaroxaban binds to it to reduce the increase in free drug, which increases the drug concentration in the blood and thus the occurrence of bleeding.

This case suggests that for patients at high risk for bleeding, particularly elderly AF patients with anemia and on long-term therapy with drugs that affect coagulation function, such as aspirin, it is necessary to carefully evaluate patient indications, weigh the risk for thromboembolic events and bleeding before medication, and closely follow up patients during medication. This would include regular monitoring of blood routine examination, timely detection of abnormalities, and adjustment of drug use. In this case, although the patient carried homozygous ABCB1 polymorphisms, the measured rivaroxaban plasma concentrations remained within the recommended therapeutic range. Therefore, a direct causal relationship between these gene variants and the bleeding event cannot be conclusively established. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential impact of ABCB1 polymorphisms on rivaroxaban safety and pharmacokinetics. At present, most studies of the effective range of blood concentrations of rivaroxaban are from populations of European and American countries; the recommended range for patients with nonvalvular AF in the Asian population is still lacking. More large-scale, real-world studies are needed to support the establishment of a treatment window for rivaroxaban in the Asian population and realize individualized medication.

Conclusions

In this case, gastrointestinal bleeding in an elderly patient with atrial fibrillation was most likely due to the concomitant use of rivaroxaban and aspirin, rather than ABCB1 gene polymorphisms, as rivaroxaban plasma concentrations remained within the therapeutic range. While genetic factors may contribute to interindividual variability, their role remains uncertain without elevated drug levels or stronger clinical evidence. Careful risk assessment, particularly regarding drug interactions and patient comorbidities, is essential for safe anticoagulation therapy in the elderly.

Limitations

This is a single-case observation; thus, ABCB1 polymorphism effects cannot be generalized. TDM was performed post-bleeding, limiting causal inferences. Anemia and DDIs may confound genetic associations.
