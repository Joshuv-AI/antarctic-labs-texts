Multiobjective framework for hardware and quality aware approximate Gaussian filtering toward energy efficient ultrasound image denoising
Authors: Sawaira Sana, Arslan Shaukat, Sajid Gul Khawaja, Ayman Qahmash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-time denoising of ultrasound images is essential for clinical and healthcare workflows, yet remains constrained by the computational and energy cost and latency of convolution-based filtering on resource- and energy-constrained embedded platforms. This paper presents a unified, automated design space exploration (DSE) framework for hardware- and energy-efficient ultrasound image denoising using approximate Gaussian filtering. The framework integrates approximate computing with multi-objective and many-objective evolutionary optimization to jointly balance image quality, power, area, and critical-path delay. A pipelined two-dimensional Gaussian filter architecture is employed, in which approximate adders and multipliers are jointly selected from the Evolutionary Approximation 8-bit (EvoApprox8b) arithmetic library, enabling circuit-level exploration of accuracy–efficiency trade-offs. Four DSE schemes are evaluated with increasing decision-space complexity and objective dimensionality. Nondominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), Nondominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm III (NSGA-III), and Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO) are systematically compared against random search. A task-driven composite metric, the Quality–Area–Power Product (QUAP), is introduced to support quality-aware ranking of hardware-efficient solutions. Experiments on representative ultrasound images show that NSGA-II achieves the most favorable trade-offs in three-objective optimization, with up to 90.1% reduction in power–area product while maintaining high reconstruction quality (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) > 35 dB, Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) > 0.93). In many-objective settings with timing constraints, NSGA-III provides superior diversity and robustness. Overall, the proposed framework enables scalable synthesis of approximate Gaussian filtering accelerators for energy-efficient embedded imaging systems, supporting sustainable and accessible healthcare technologies.

Introduction

Ultrasound (US) image processing is commonly used in medical diagnosis because of its non-invasive, cost-effective, portable, and real-time imaging capabilities. Nevertheless, ultrasound images are often degraded by artifacts, low signal-to-noise ratios, and operator dependency1,2. One of the greatest challenges in ultrasound image processing is speckle noise, which is generated by the coherent reflection of ultrasound waves. This noise is characterized by a granular interference pattern that can sometimes obscure image features. Removing speckle noise is crucial in enhancing image quality while ensuring that important information is retained. Of the different techniques proposed for image denoising, the application of the Gaussian filter is perhaps the most popular technique because of its simplicity, ease of implementation, and efficacy in removing high-frequency noise3–5. A comparative study on image denoising techniques has shown that the application of the Gaussian filter is perhaps better than other traditional techniques when images are corrupted by Gaussian noise6. A combination of the Gaussian filter and the discrete cosine transform (DCT) has also been proposed as an effective technique in speckle noise removal7.

However, hardware implementation of Gaussian filters has been shown to be computationally expensive, especially in real-time and embedded imaging applications. The 2-D convolution process involves a considerable number of multiply-and-accumulate operations, which have been shown to be area-, power-, and latency-intensive. These issues have been shown to be critical in portable ultrasound imaging devices, where both energy efficiency and real-time performance are considered critical factors3. Traditional hardware optimization techniques, including pipelining, dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, and power gating, have been shown to be useful in improving efficiency, but with certain trade-offs in design complexity, performance, and flexibility.

Recently, various approximate arithmetic components like multiplication and addition have been proposed and classified according to different trade-offs between area, delay, power, and various error metrics like MSE, MAE, etc. The problem is how to select and integrate these components for a particular application to achieve optimal performance under various objectives. It is more challenging for image processing operations like Gaussian filters, where hardware efficiency needs to be enhanced without compromising image quality using various metrics like PSNR, SSIM, and QUAP.

Design space exploration (DSE) provides a systematic means of addressing this trade-off space. With respect to the application of digital filtering, various researchers have addressed the issue of developing efficient implementations for Gaussian and similar filters through the application of approximation methods. In this context, Cabello et al.8 investigated the application of fixed-point quantized kernel implementations for improving FPGA resource utilization. Ramezani et al.9 also presented a low-latency design for a Gaussian filter through the application of approximate carry-save adders and quantization. Gupta et al..4 presented a low-power LMS filter with dynamic precision, while Hsiao et al.5 presented a parameterizable approximated two-dimensional Gaussian smoothing filter for noisy images. Apart from these implementations, other researchers have also worked on the general DSE methodologies of approximate arithmetic systems. Oliveira et al.10 demonstrated the promise of evolving the filter from scratch to optimize the trade-offs in power quality, but the interpretability of the results was compromised. Pereira et al.11 evaluated the effect of precision scaling and memorization in the architecture of the filter, further reinforcing the notion of the benefits of inexactness in achieving cost savings while incurring negligible loss in output image quality. While these works are interesting, they do not leverage the combinatorial configuration space made possible when the results of several approximate adders and multipliers are combined in the same architecture. Several other more general approaches to approximate hardware automation have been reported. AutoAx12 was based on a pre-characterized library of approximate arithmetic units and utilized infeasible solutions pruned based on error metrics and predictor-guided local search. Sekanina et al.13 proposed a DSE approach based on extensive exploration of approximation possibilities using electrical and error metrics. Dongning et al.14 proposed a genetic algorithm-based approach for optimizing approximate arithmetic for signal processing applications.

Recently, E-IDEA15 and AxHLS16 have been proposed in this domain, based on code mutation and high-level synthesis exploration, respectively. In application-specific image processing applications, Manu Manuel et al.17 proposed ROI-based parameter optimization and model-based DSE approaches for application-specific image processing on FPGA platforms. A hybrid DSE approach was proposed by Kadiyala et al.18 for application-specific design of hearing aid FIR filters using pruning heuristics and NSGA-II. In another related work, Manu Manuel et al.19 explored the application of DSE for image color processing using sparse LUTs, precision scaling, and approximate arithmetic techniques, aided by fast quality prediction models. A multi-objective NSGA-II algorithm was used to arrive at the Pareto front for image color processing applications. Although these approaches have been successfully applied for the automation of approximate computing, they are mostly restricted to optimization problems of at most three objectives, or are heuristic in nature, or ignore the optimization of arithmetic units for a fixed Gaussian filter accelerator. These limitations are more significant when the dimensionality of the design space is high. If multiple configurable approximate units are considered simultaneously, the number of possible hardware configurations increases exponentially. At the same time, in practice, it is necessary to balance multiple objectives simultaneously when designing hardware. However, most of the current techniques are focused on low-dimensional optimization scenarios. Therefore, they are less appropriate for handling multi-objective optimization problems with four or more objectives. Only a few studies have focused on high-dimensional DSE problems, especially for Gaussian filter accelerators using approximate computing. In order to bridge this research gap, a multi-objective and many-objective evolutionary DSE framework for designing energy-efficient Gaussian filter accelerators based on approximate arithmetic circuits is presented in this paper. The framework uses a combination of approximate adders and multipliers from the EvoApprox8b library and a pipelined AxGaussian filter architecture, and uses evolutionary optimization algorithms such as NSGA-II, NSGA-III, and MOPSO to explore different combinations of these arithmetic circuits. Unlike previous approaches, the proposed framework allows for the joint selection and placement of approximate arithmetic circuits within a fixed datapath and explicitly considers trade-offs involving image quality and hardware efficiency metrics. Various schemes of exploration are used to evaluate the framework with increasing complexity in decision space and dimensionality of objectives. Image quality is measured using PSNR and SSIM, while hardware efficiency is measured using area, power, and critical path delay using RTL synthesis. Composite metrics are also used to enable quality-aware ranking of the Pareto optimal solutions. Finally, the solutions are validated using a MATLAB-based simulator on typical ultrasound images to show that significant hardware savings are possible without compromising image reconstruction quality.

Materials and methods

In this section, we present the dataset used in our experiments, the hardware architecture of the AxGaussian filter, and the design space exploration methodology employed to assess hardware performance. The hardware metrics considered in this work include area, power, and latency, which are obtained through RTL synthesis in Cadence Genus using a 45 nm technology library. These metrics provide the basis for hardware-aware design decisions. All evaluations are performed in the context of ultrasound image denoising, where real-time ultrasound images are passed through Gaussian filters to quantify the trade-offs between hardware efficiency and image quality.

Dataset of approximate arithmetic units

For this study, the arithmetic units are selected from the EvoApprox8b library20. The library offers 430 8-bit approximate adders and 471 8-bit approximate multipliers based on 13 common adder designs and 6 multiplier architectures. It also includes 43 exact adders and 28 exact multipliers generated using Cartesian Genetic Programming (CGP), which serve as optimized baselines for comparison.

All components are characterized in terms of hardware metrics, namely area, delay, and power, obtained through RTL synthesis in Cadence Genus using a 45 nm generic standard cell library (GSCL) as the target technology (see Fig. 2)21. In addition, error-related metrics, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE), are provided to support hardware trade-off analysis. Hardware architecture of the AxGaussian filter

The AxGaussian filter is implemented as a pipelined hardware accelerator to support efficient evaluation of approximate arithmetic units. The top-level architecture, shown in Fig. 2, includes a coefficient ROM, an image register unit, a sliding-window extraction block, and a convolution unit. All arithmetic units are implemented in an 8-bit fixed-point format (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{1.7}$$\end{document}). The input images are converted to 8-bit grayscale, while the multiplication outputs produce 16-bit values that are accumulated using cascaded adders. This choice of fixed-point precision provides a good balance between maintaining image fidelity in ultrasound denoising and reducing hardware resource usage.Fig. 2Top-level hardware architecture of the AxGaussian filter. Windowing subsystem

The windowing subsystem is implemented using a shift-register-based structure (Fig. 3), which generates 4 × 4 convolution windows while minimizing memory usage. This enables efficient evaluation of different combinations of approximate arithmetic units in a pipelined manner. The sliding-window extraction is designed for ultrasound image patches and helps preserve local image structure during denoising. As a result, the pixel correlations that are important for ultrasound noise reduction are maintained throughout the pipeline stages.Fig. 3Shift-register–based windowing subsystem. Cascaded adders

Approximate adders with a cascaded arrangement of 8-bit approximate adders produce a result of 16 bits, facilitating hardware-based exploration of area, power, and delay trade-offs without requiring additional exact adders as in Fig. 5. The cascaded approximate adders of 8 bits accumulate pixel intensities over the window for ultrasound images.

For dealing with 16-bit convolution output and 8-bit approximate adders, two-stage accumulation is performed for lower 8 bits and upper 8 bits, and area, power, and delay can be evaluated without additional full-width adders.Fig. 5Cascaded 8-bit approximate adders generating 16-bit results for convolution accumulation.

Cascaded 8-bit approximate adders generating 16-bit results for convolution accumulation.

In order to perform 16-bit convolution output using 8-bit approximate adders, two stages of accumulation are employed, one for lower 8 bits and the other for upper 8 bits. This approach allows us to perform area, power, and delay evaluation without adding extra width to the adders, thus keeping the hardware overhead to a minimum. The adder library has been divided into two different categories, namely, Group G1 and Group G2. Group G1 includes approximate adders, which introduce a certain amount of computational error, while Group G2 includes accurate adders, i.e., error-free adders. In the proposed architecture, lower 8-bit additions are carried out using Group G1, and upper 8-bit additions are carried out using Group G2.

Results and discussion

This section describes the results obtained by the design-space exploration (DSE) framework on the AxGaussian filter. All the results are directly linked to the approach presented in “Materials and methods”, which aims to evaluate the efficiency of the hardware on the quality of the images. The hardware metrics are obtained using Cadence RTL synthesis (GSCL 45 nm).

Experimental setup

All experiments have been conducted on a workstation with the following parameters: AMD Ryzen 9 7950X 16-Core Processor running at 4.50 GHz, and 128 GB RAM. The experiments have been conducted on a set of 100 images of ultrasound images downloaded from the Radiopaedia website and resized to a fixed image size of 512 × 512. The images have been acquired using different ultrasound systems and have different inherent characteristics of speckle noise. To be able to compare the efficiency of hardware approximation methods in an unbiased manner, all images have been artificially contaminated with synthetic speckle noise, thus normalizing the noise level over all images. This approach minimizes the impact of image characteristics on the overall denoising efficiency of the filters. Synthetic speckle noise was added to all images using MATLAB’s imnoise function with the ’speckle’ option, simulating multiplicative noise with the following model: J = I + nI, where n is zero-mean Gaussian noise with controllable variance. The variance was set to a fixed value of 0.04 for all experiments. It is worth noting that ultrasound speckle is intrinsically multiplicative, correlated, and non-Gaussian. However, within the scope of this work, a simplified noise model is adopted for the sake of convenience in benchmarking within hardware-aware design space exploration. Within this scope, a simplified noise model allows for a fair comparison of a large number of approximate hardware configurations. Moreover, after envelope detection and log compression, multiplicative speckle can be considered an additive process, and hence, Gaussian-based smoothing can be considered an appropriate baseline for comparative evaluation within the scope of hardware-aware embedded systems.

The results obtained from the approximate Gaussian filtering configurations were compared to those from an exact fixed-point implementation. Random Search was employed as a baseline to contextualize the performance gains achieved by the evolutionary multi-objective optimization methods. The evaluation emphasized hardware-aware image quality trends rather than clinical diagnostic accuracy, while the use of 100 representative ultrasound images provided a broader basis for assessing consistency and relative trade-offs across different approximate hardware configurations.

Approximate arithmetic units were selected from the EvoApprox8b library, and hardware metrics—area, power, and delay—were obtained through RTL synthesis in Cadence Genus using the 45 nm generic standard cell library (GSCL), consistent with the EvoApprox8b characterization methodology. Although absolute power values depend on the technology node, the proposed design space exploration framework focuses on relative trade-offs and Pareto dominance, which are expected to remain largely consistent across technologies. Four design schemes, spanning three-objective to many-objective optimization, were evaluated. All algorithms were executed on the complete set of 100 resized ultrasound images, with multiple runs performed to account for stochastic variability. Representative Pareto-optimal solutions are reported. All area, power, and timing values are pre-layout estimates derived at the RTL synthesis stage and do not include placement and routing, clock-tree synthesis, or IR-drop effects.

While this approach has been shown to effectively reduce high-frequency speckle noise, it remains a linear low-pass filter and, therefore, does not preserve edges. Discussion

Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 quantify the effects of approximation scope and optimization strategy on hardware efficiency and image quality. The representative ultrasound examples in Fig. 6 and 7, together with Tables 10 and 11, illustrate how these hardware trade-offs manifest in filtered image outputs.

For three-objective optimization schemes, i.e., Schemes I, II, and III, NSGA-II yields the smallest area and highest power savings among all optimization techniques. This clearly indicates that NSGA-II is more suitable for hardware-oriented area and power-dominated optimization problems. However, based on the updated results for all optimization techniques using QUAP, as depicted in Table 9, it can be clearly seen that NSGA-III yields better composite image quality-aware performance for Schemes II and III. In Scheme IV, where critical path delay is considered an additional objective, NSGA-III yields more balanced performance, thus clearly indicating its suitability for many-objective optimization problems. MOPSO yields moderate savings, though not as good as achieved using NSGA-II and NSGA-III.

From the power consumption trends in Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7, it can be seen that NSGA-II achieves less power consumption in low-dimensional design spaces. With an increase in the number of variables and objectives from Schemes II to IV, NSGA-III demonstrates competitive power and area reduction with respect to timing constraints. The results across Tables 3, 4, 5 and 6 indicate maximum area savings of 42.5% and power savings of 50.1% across all schemes. The image-quality metrics reported in Table 9 show that PSNR and SSIM remain consistently high, with comparable QUAP values observed for Schemes II and III. This suggests that reducing the design-space dimensionality does not significantly affect the quality–efficiency trade-off.

From a design perspective, NSGA-II is more suitable for low-dimensional, three-objective optimization for area and power reduction, while NSGA-III would be more suitable if other objectives like critical path delay are added. MOPSO can be considered a baseline approach, but it may not attain competitive composite performance.

Overall, the results demonstrate the effectiveness of the methodology presented in “Materials and methods”, which verifies the DSE approach presented in the paper and validates the effectiveness of the Pareto-optimal approximate arithmetic configurations in reducing hardware costs while maintaining good image quality in the resulting ultrasound images. Conclusion and future work

This study presents a design space exploration (DSE) framework for the hardware-aware approximation of Gaussian filtering in real-time ultrasound image denoising. The framework incorporates approximate arithmetic units from the EvoApprox8b library into a pipelined AxGaussian filter architecture and examines the resulting trade-offs among area, power, delay, and image quality. By considering a range of approximation settings, from multiplier-only implementations to hybrid and many-objective configurations, it enables a structured exploration of design spaces with different levels of complexity.

Results obtained from 100 ultrasound images show that the proposed approach can effectively identify Pareto-optimal hardware configurations while preserving good image quality. In the three-objective setting, NSGA-II achieves the strongest hardware-oriented performance, providing area and power savings of up to 42.5% and 50.1%, respectively, while maintaining PSNR above 38 dB and SSIM above 0.94. When delay is introduced as an additional objective in the many-objective setting, NSGA-III yields more balanced trade-offs, indicating that it is better suited for delay-aware approximate hardware design.

The use of composite metrics such as PAP and QUAP also makes it possible to evaluate hardware efficiency together with image-quality-related performance. The best configurations achieve PAP reductions of up to 90.1% relative to the exact baseline, while still maintaining strong visual and structural fidelity. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the proposed framework provides an effective link between algorithmic optimization and practical hardware and application-level requirements.

Although the present work is based on RTL-level synthesis using a 45 nm generic standard cell library (GSCL) and static ultrasound images, the framework itself is not limited to this specific setup. Future work will focus on full ASIC implementation, real-time data acquisition with streaming ultrasound workloads, and adaptive approximation strategies. While the absolute values of power, area, and timing will vary across technology nodes, the relative ordering of Pareto-optimal configurations is expected to remain broadly consistent because it is mainly governed by structural approximation effects rather than node-specific characteristics. Future studies will investigate technology-scaled synthesis in more advanced nodes to quantify these trends more precisely.
