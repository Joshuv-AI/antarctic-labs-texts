Accurate identification of medically important Aedes mosquitoes (Diptera: Culicidae) in Thailand through DNA barcoding, wing geometric morphometrics, and machine learning
Authors: Sedthapong Laojun, Tanasak Changbunjong, Morakot Kaewthamasorn, Pattaranatcha Charnwichai, Saowalak Kaewmee, Sineewanlaya Wichit, Rodolphe Hamel, Tanawat Chaiphongpachara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMosquito-borne diseases remain a significant public health concern, underscoring the need for accurate species-level identification of vector species, including Aedes mosquitoes. Identification based solely on morphology is often limited by interspecific overlap, environmentally induced phenotypic plasticity, and physical damage to field-collected specimens. This study evaluated nine Aedes species (Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, Ae. chrysolineatus, Ae. lineatopennis, Ae. macfarlanei, Ae. poicilius, Ae. vexans, Ae. vigilax, and Ae. vittatus) and a related taxon (Aedeomyia catasticta) in Thailand, using DNA barcoding, wing geometric morphometric (WGM) analysis, and the Random Forests (RF) machine learning algorithm. DNA barcoding of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene showed strong concordance with morphological classifications, confirming its reliability for species-level identification. Across all 10 species, sequence similarity with GenBank and the Barcode of Life Data System ranged from 96% to 100%, highlighting reliable identification when robust references are available. WGM analysis revealed significant wing shape differences among species (P < 0.05), with 91.05% classification accuracy. The Mahalanobis distance and RF algorithms, applied to newly field-collected specimens assigned as unknown species, demonstrated strong discriminatory power, both achieving 100% accuracy for seven species based on wing shape. Slightly lower accuracy was observed for three species, with Mahalanobis distance achieving 90% (one misclassified individual) and the RF algorithm 80% (two misclassified individuals). These findings present a practical guideline for identifying Aedes mosquitoes and a related taxon in Thailand by integrating approaches. Accurate species identification is essential for selecting targeted vector control strategies and enhancing the effectiveness of Aedes-borne disease surveillance and management.Keywords: Aedini, DNA barcoding, Geometric morphometrics, Random forests, Species identification, Vector surveillance

Introduction

The genus Aedes, belonging to the subfamily Culicinae and tribe Aedini, is the largest within the family Culicidae, comprising approximately 1000 species across 41 subgenera (Harbach, 2023). Aedes mosquitoes occur worldwide, from the tropics to the Arctic, although individual species exhibit distinct geographical ranges and breeding ecologies (Harbach, 2007, 2023). Many oviposit in transient natural water bodies, such as above-ground ponds, rock pools, tree holes, bamboo internodes, leaf axils, and swamps, whereas others have adapted to artificial containers created by human activity (Rattanarithikul et al., 2010). The genus includes several medically important vectors of arthropod-borne viruses, notably those causing yellow fever and dengue fever (Ogunlade et al., 2021). Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are important vectors of dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Zika viruses, which can cause severe, sometimes life-threatening illness in humans (Gómez et al., 2022; Abbasi, 2025). Another notable species, Aedes vexans, has a broad distribution spanning tropical Africa, parts of Central America and Southeast Asia, and temperate regions of the Nearctic and Palaearctic (Harbach, 2023). Adult females are persistent biters, posing a substantial nuisance to humans and animals and participating in transmission cycles of various arboviruses (Ndiaye et al., 2016; O’Donnell et al., 2017). Several Aedes species also act as vectors of helminths causing lymphatic filariasis. For example, Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, and Ae. vexans are recognized vectors of the filarial nematode Dirofilaria immitis in dogs and cats and are therefore of significant veterinary importance. Moreover, Aedes poicilius has been reported as a potential vector of Wuchereria bancrofti in the Philippines (Salazar et al., 2016; Meetham et al., 2023).

In Thailand, arboviral infections, particularly dengue, chikungunya, and Zika, remain major public health concerns, highlighting the need for accurate species-level identification of Aedes mosquitoes to facilitate surveillance, assess disease risks, and guide effective control strategies (Sumruayphol et al., 2016; Raksakoon and Potiwat, 2021). Identification based solely on morphology is often limited by interspecific morphological overlap, environmentally driven phenotypic plasticity, and physical damage to field-collected specimens (Sumruayphol et al., 2016; Laojun et al., 2023a, 2023b, 2024a). Consequently, conventional morphological methods may be inadequate for reliable species identification (Champakaew et al., 2021). Recent studies have shown that integrating modern species-identification tools with traditional taxonomy can improve the accuracy and efficiency of mosquito classification (Champakaew et al., 2021; Chaiphongpachara et al., 2022a; Bisaglia et al., 2024; Kittichai et al., 2024). Before these approaches are widely deployed, however, comprehensive preliminary evaluations are required to assess diagnostic performance and operational feasibility. Despite the recognized medical importance of many Aedes species, a substantial gap remains in applying modern approaches to achieve accurate species-level identification, owing to limited supporting data and validation in operational settings.

In recent years, three alternative techniques have been increasingly employed to identify mosquito vectors in several regions: DNA barcoding (Wang et al., 2012; Ortega-Morales et al., 2021; Chaiphongpachara et al., 2022b), wing geometric morphometrics (WGM) (Wilke et al., 2016; Champakaew et al., 2021), and machine learning (Bellin et al., 2021). DNA barcoding is a molecular method that analyzes a short fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene and compares it with a reference barcode library to identify species (Beebe, 2018; Laojun and Chaiphongpachara, 2024). Geometric morphometrics is a quantitative approach that uses Cartesian landmark coordinates to assess and compare shape variation, enabling species classification based on morphological geometry (Kaba et al., 2017; Changbunjong et al., 2025). Machine learning, such as Random Forests (RF), encompasses a suite of computational methods that develop predictive models from training data to generate accurate classifications (Charoenpanyakul et al., 2024; Adeleke et al., 2025). The RF algorithm operates by constructing multiple independent decision trees during the training process. Each tree is built from a random subset of both the data and the predictor variables, and the final classification is determined through majority voting among the trees (Sun et al., 2023). These techniques have become increasingly important for decision-making in biological data analysis, particularly for species identification and classification.

To address existing challenges in vector identification, this study aimed to evaluate the identification of Aedes spp. mosquitoes and a related taxon, Aedeomyia catasticta, in Thailand, through the combined use of DNA barcoding, WGM, and the RF algorithm. The findings are expected to provide practical guidelines for implementing these combined approaches to strengthen vector surveillance and support the development of effective, targeted mosquito control strategies.

Materials and methods

Mosquito collection

Mosquitoes were collected using BG-Pro traps configured in Sentinel style (Biogents, Regensburg, Germany) equipped with BG-Mozzibait cartridges (Biogents) and carbon dioxide (CO2) attractant. CO2 was supplied via containers filled with 1 kg of dry ice pellets, and the traps were operated daily from 6:00 to 20:00 h. Collection sites (shown in Table 1) were selected from various locations across Thailand, based on prior entomological survey reports (Rattanarithikul et al., 2010; Chaiphongpachara et al., 2022b). At each location, eight traps were deployed and operated for three consecutive days. Traps were placed on the ground approximately 10–50 m from human dwellings. Mosquito collections were conducted during the rainy season, from June to November 2024, when mosquito populations typically reach their peak. Each evening at approximately 20:00 h, mosquitoes captured in the BG-Pro traps were retrieved and immediately euthanized by freezing with dry ice. All specimens were transported by car in cool boxes to the laboratory of the College of Allied Health Sciences, Suan Sunandha Rajabhat University, Samut Songkhram Campus, Thailand, for subsequent identification.

Morphological identification

In the laboratory, adult mosquitoes were individually examined to identify Aedes species using the standard taxonomic keys for mosquitoes in Thailand (Rattanarithikul et al., 2006, 2010). Specimens were observed under a Nikon SMZ800N stereo microscope (Nikon Corp., Japan). Although BG traps are primarily designed to attract and capture host-seeking female mosquitoes, a small number of males were incidentally collected. As these male specimens were not relevant to the study objectives, they were excluded from further analysis. Afterwards, each identified female Aedes specimen was placed in an individual tube and stored at −20 °C for subsequent DNA barcoding and WGM analysis.

Sample preparation

Mosquito specimens that had been morphologically identified were selected based on their integrity, ensuring that only individuals with undamaged wings were used. For each selected specimen, the right wing was detached from the thorax, mounted on a microscope slide, fixed with Hoyer’s medium, and covered with a coverslip. Wing images were then captured using a digital camera attached to a Nikon SMZ800N stereomicroscope, producing an image series for subsequent WGM analysis. In addition, three individuals from each species were randomly chosen from these specimens to be confirmed by DNA barcoding; their legs were removed from the thorax and preserved for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and DNA barcoding

Genomic DNA was extracted from four legs of each mosquito specimen using the FavorPrep™ Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Ping Tung, Taiwan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was eluted to a final concentration of approximately 10 ng/μl and stored at −20 °C until further use. The cox1 gene (commonly referred to as the barcode) was amplified using the primer pair COI-F (5′-GGA TTT GGA AAT TGA TTA GTT CCT T-3′) and COI-R (5′-AAA AAT TTT AAT TCC AGT TGG AAC AGC-3′) (Kumar et al., 2007), generating an expected fragment of approximately 675 base pairs. In cases where amplification with this primer set was unsuccessful, an alternative forward primer, LCO1490 (5′-GGT CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G-3′), was used in combination with the same COI-R reverse primer.

PCR amplification was performed in a total reaction volume of 25 μl, containing 4 μl of DNA template, 1× PCR buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, 0.2 μM of each primer, and 1.5 units of Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), with nuclease-free distilled water added to reach the final volume. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 5 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at 45 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at 54 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. Negative controls containing nuclease-free distilled water were included in each PCR run to detect potential contamination, while DNA from a previously identified Aedes specimen served as a positive control (Chaiphongpachara et al., 2022b). PCR products were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with Midori Green Advance (Nippon Gene, Düren, Germany). Amplicons corresponding to the expected size (675 bp) were subsequently purified and bidirectionally Sanger sequenced by SolGent, Inc. (Daejeon, Korea).

The raw sequence chromatograms obtained from both forward and reverse reads were inspected and edited using BioEdit version 7.2.5 (Hall, 1999). Consensus sequences were generated by aligning the bidirectional reads, and ambiguous or low-quality regions were trimmed to ensure accuracy across samples. The resulting consensus sequences were queried against the NCBI GenBank database using the BLAST algorithm (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and the Barcode of Life Data Systems (BOLD) database (https://www.boldsystems.org) to confirm species identity based on similarity to reference cox1 gene sequences. All cox1 sequences generated in this study were deposited in the GenBank database under the accession numbers PV963241-PV963243.

Phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the ClustalW algorithm (Larkin et al., 2007), and a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree with 1000 bootstrap replicates was constructed in MEGA 11 (Tamura et al., 2021) to illustrate evolutionary relationships among the analyzed Aedes species and support species-level classification. The General Time Reversible model with gamma-distributed rate variation among sites (GTR+G) was identified as the best-fit model for the dataset based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) value. Anopheles wejchoochotei (GenBank: OK036973) was used as the outgroup.

Species delimitation

To refine species delimitation, three analytical methods were applied: Automated Barcode Gap Discovery (ABGD) (Puillandre et al., 2012), Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP) (Puillandre et al., 2021), and the multi-rate Poisson Tree Processes (mPTP) (Kapli et al., 2017). ABGD and ASAP are distance-based methods that detect a ‘barcode gap’ to distinguish within-species variation from between-species divergence, grouping cox1 sequence data into Molecular Operational Taxonomic Units (MOTUs) based on sequence similarity. ABGD uses recursive partitioning of pairwise genetic distances, while ASAP applies hierarchical clustering and ranks partitions based on barcode gap width and panmixia probability. ABGD analyses were performed on the ABGD webserver (https://bioinfo.mnhn.fr/abi/public/abgd/abgdweb.html) under default parameters using p-distance metrics. ASAP analyses were conducted via its dedicated interface on the same server (https://bioinfo.mnhn.fr/abi/public/asap/asapweb.html) with identical settings. In contrast, mPTP is a phylogeny-aware delimitation method that uses variation in mutation rates within a phylogenetic tree to distinguish interspecific from intraspecific diversity. For the mPTP analysis, a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was uploaded to the mPTP webserver (https://mptp.h-its.org/#/tree) for species delimitation.

Geometric morphometric analysis

Geometric morphometric analyses were conducted using XYOM software version 3 (Dujardin and Dujardin, 2019), which facilitated landmark digitization and subsequent analysis of wing size and shape. Eighteen anatomical landmarks were manually marked on the right wings of mosquito specimens (Fig. 1), positioned at homologous vein intersections and terminations to capture biologically relevant shape variation. To assess digitization precision, a repeatability test was conducted following the protocol of Arnqvist and Mårtensson (1998). For this test, 10 wing images per species were randomly selected and digitized twice by the same operator to calculate the repeatability index.

Eighteen sequentially numbered landmarks (1–18) positioned on the right wing, used for wing geometric morphometric analysis in this study.

Wing size was quantified using centroid size (CS), defined as the square root of the sum of squared distances from each landmark to the centroid of the configuration. A one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences in CS among 10 species groups, and a non-parametric permutation-based ANOVA (1000 permutations) was performed to provide pairwise P-values and to validate the results without assuming normality. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. For shape analysis, wing shape variables obtained through generalized Procrustes analysis were summarized using principal components analysis (PCA) to identify the main axes of shape variation. These principal components (14 PCs) were then used as shape descriptors in subsequent discriminant analysis (DA), with results visualized in a factor map. To evaluate shape differentiation among species, Mahalanobis distances were computed, and their statistical significance was assessed using a non-parametric test with 1000 permutations and Bonferroni adjustment at P-value < 0.05. Morphological relationships among species were visualized using a hierarchical clustering tree constructed with the unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA), based on Mahalanobis distances calculated from the mean shapes of each species group.

Finally, the classification performance of wing CS and wing shape was evaluated using a cross-validated classification test. For CS, maximum likelihood classification was applied, whereas for shape, a Mahalanobis distance-based validation was used. In both cases, each specimen was sequentially removed from the dataset, its CS (for size) or Mahalanobis distance (for shape) to the mean of each group calculated, and then assigned to the best-matching group, which was defined as the group with the highest likelihood for CS or the smallest Mahalanobis distance for shape. After each removal, the mean of the original group was updated to ensure proper validation and to provide an unbiased estimate of classification accuracy. In addition, to avoid overestimation due to chance agreement, the Adjusted Total Accuracy (TAU) was computed, which corrects the observed accuracy by accounting for classifications expected to occur randomly, thereby providing a more reliable measure of discriminatory performance, following the protocol of Kovarovic et al. (2011).

Identification of unknown specimens in the field using Mahalanobis distance

A total of 100 newly collected field specimens (10 per species) were collected from different geographical locations and treated as ‘unknown’ (Table 1). For each specimen, the right wing was excised, mounted on a microscope slide, and photographed using a digital camera. Eighteen anatomical landmarks, positioned in the same way as those defined for the reference species in this study, were digitized. The landmark data from all field specimens were then compared with the wing shape data of the reference species groups. Each specimen was identified individually by calculating its Mahalanobis distance to the mean shape of each reference group and assigning it to the best-matching group. All procedures were carried out within the Identification Section of the XYOM software.

Identification of unknown specimens in the field using Random Forests

Wing shape variables obtained through generalized Procrustes analysis were applied to the Random Forests (RF) machine learning algorithm to identify mosquito species. The RF algorithm, an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees during training, was used to evaluate its predictive performance for identifying field-collected mosquito specimens. In this classification approach, each decision tree generates a prediction (vote), and the class receiving the majority of votes is selected as the final prediction. In this study, wing shape variables from 10 mosquito species, obtained from reference specimens, served as the training dataset for the RF algorithm. The model learned the morphological patterns associated with each species and was then applied to identify unknown specimens. These unknown specimens were the same set of field-collected samples used in Section 2.8.

Each unknown specimen was analyzed individually using the RF model. For each prediction, the wing shape variables of the specimen were compared with those of the reference groups, and the model assigned the specimen to the most probable species based on the majority vote across all decision trees. In this process, each tree provided an independent classification, and the species receiving the highest number of votes was designated as the predicted species. The analysis was performed using the RF module of the XYOM software with default parameter settings, which included a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure reproducibility, a variable selection proportion of 0.9 from the available predictors, and a total of 1000 bootstrapped trees. Fourteen principal components (PCs) of shape were used as predictors.

Results

DNA barcoding

Ten mosquito species were collected from field sites in this study, including nine Aedes species (Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, Ae. chrysolineatus, Ae. lineatopennis, Ae. macfarlanei, Ae. poicilius, Ae. vexans, Ae. vigilax, and Ae. vittatus) and a related taxon (Aedeomyia catasticta) (Table 1). Species were initially identified based on detailed morphological characteristics. To increase identification accuracy and validate morphological classifications, three individuals per species were randomly selected for confirmation using DNA barcoding.

A total of 30 cox1 barcode sequences were generated and compared with public records in the GenBank and BOLD databases to assess species identity based on sequence similarity (Table 2). Specimens identified as Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, and Ae. macfarlanei exhibited exceptionally high similarity values (≥ 99%) to their respective reference sequences in both databases, confirming precise species-level identification. Aedes poicilius also showed consistently high similarity to GenBank entries (99.70–100%), whereas corresponding matches in BOLD were more variable (96.07–99.11%), potentially indicating intraspecific variation within that database. Similarly, Ae. lineatopennis, Ae. vittatus, and Ad. catasticta demonstrated high sequence similarity (97–100%) across both databases. In contrast, Ae. vexans and Ae. vigilax exhibited the widest similarity ranges, with GenBank values ranging from 95.70 to 99.70% and 96.96 to99.70%, respectively. BOLD similarity values were slightly narrower but still variable (96.00–99.57% for Ae. vexans; 96.96–99.85% for Ae. vigilax). Notably, Ae. chrysolineatus yielded the lowest similarity to GenBank references (96.90–97.21%) and lacked any corresponding records in the BOLD database. The low level of sequence similarity observed with the single available reference record suggests that molecular identification of this species using DNA barcoding is uncertain in this study. Despite this limitation, the overall high sequence similarity values confirm that all 10 mosquito species identified morphologically were reliably matched to their corresponding molecular identities through DNA barcoding.

Phylogenetic tree and species delimitation

The phylogenetic analysis demonstrated clear genetic divergence among the 10 mosquito species identified based on morphological characteristics. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using 30 cox1 barcode sequences generated in this study, along with 20 reference sequences retrieved from GenBank (Fig. 2). The resulting tree showed that all specimens clustered into well-supported monophyletic clades corresponding to their respective species, with high bootstrap support values ranging from 98 to 100%, thereby validating the initial morphological identifications. To further delineate species boundaries, three species delimitation methods were applied: ABGD, ASAP, and mPTP. All 10 species were consistently recognized as distinct units by ABGD and ASAP, reinforcing the reliability of molecular identification. However, the mPTP analysis (best score for multi-coalescent rate = 215.72) yielded slightly different results for Ae. chrysolineatus, which was subdivided into two distinct subgroups, suggesting genetic variation within this taxon.

Geometric morphometrics

Species classification based on wing size and shape

To evaluate the accuracy of wing size and shape in differentiating among 10 mosquito species, a cross-validated classification analysis was conducted, and the results are presented in Table 5. Classification based on CS yielded low overall accuracy, with only 21.4% (55 of 257) of specimens correctly assigned and a TAU of 12%. Among the 10 species, Ae. macfarlanei showed the highest reclassification accuracy at 72%, followed by Ae. vexans (53.13%) and Ae. lineatopennis (28%). In contrast, Ae. poicilius and Ae. vigilax showed the poorest performance, each with 0% accuracy, indicating that wing size alone offers limited discriminatory power for reliable species identification. Notably, Ae. chrysolineatus, Ae. macfarlanei, Ae. poicilius, Ae. vigilax, and Ad. catasticta achieved perfect reclassification scores of 100%. The remaining species also exhibited high levels of accuracy, ranging from 80 to 88%, showing the superior discriminatory power of wing shape variables over size metrics for mosquito species differentiation.

Discussion

Although Aedes spp. mosquitoes are medically important vectors that transmit life-threatening viruses to humans, accurate species identification remains a challenge (Sumruayphol et al., 2016). Morphological identification, while considered the standard method, depends greatly on the examiner’s expertise and the physical condition of the specimens (Rahola et al., 2022). In this study, the identification of nine Aedes species and one related taxon, Ad. catasticta, was successfully validated using DNA barcoding, WGM, and machine learning, highlighting the reliability of integrated approaches for precise species-level discrimination.

The species identification results based on DNA barcoding of the cox1 gene showed strong concordance with the morphological classifications of the mosquito specimens, thereby confirming the effectiveness of this molecular approach for species-level validation. Across all 10 mosquito species examined in this study, sequence similarities with reference entries in GenBank and BOLD ranged from 96 to 100%, highlighting the high accuracy of DNA barcoding in discriminating among closely related mosquito species when reliable reference sequences are available. These results are consistent with prior research that advocates DNA barcoding as a valuable supplementary tool for mosquito identification, particularly in situations where conventional morphological methods are limited by specimen damage or incompleteness (Noureldin et al., 2022; Lamichhane et al., 2024).

Some mosquito species exhibited broad ranges of sequence similarity when compared to public barcode databases. Notably, Ae. vexans showed similarity values within the ranges of 95.70–99.70% in GenBank and 96.00–99.57% in BOLD, while Ae. vigilax exhibited ranges of 96.96–99.70% in GenBank and 96.96–99.85% in BOLD. These wide intervals may indicate intraspecific genetic variation or reflect underlying geographical structure within these taxa. Such variation is likely attributable to comparisons with reference sequences derived from geographically distant populations, which may harbor region-specific genetic lineages. This interpretation is consistent with a previous study on Mansonia dives, which reported significant genetic differentiation between populations from two distinct regions in Thailand (Laojun et al., 2025). Similarly, high genetic variability in Ae. vexans has been documented across diverse geographical areas, with notable genetic divergence observed between northern and southern populations from Sweden (northern Europe), Hungary, and Serbia (central Europe) (Francuski et al., 2016). In the case of Ae. chrysolineatus, the observed sequence similarity with available GenBank records was relatively low (96.90–97.21%, based on a single sequence present in the database), and no corresponding entries were found in the BOLD system. This highlights a significant limitation due to the paucity of cox1 reference sequences in current DNA barcode databases. Such limitations may hinder accurate species identification, particularly for underrepresented or poorly documented taxa (Wilson et al., 2011; Chaiphongpachara et al., 2022b). Moreover, low sequence similarity values may also reflect potential misidentifications or taxonomic inaccuracies in sequences deposited in GenBank, as previous studies have reported occasional errors in publicly available barcode records (Cheng et al., 2023; Somboon et al., 2025). Therefore, careful verification of reference sequences and the continued expansion of curated barcode databases are essential to ensure the accuracy and reliability of molecular-based mosquito identification (Somboon et al., 2025).

While high sequence similarity supports species identification, DNA barcoding must also incorporate evidence from phylogenetic placement and species delimitation analyses. Sequence similarity alone is insufficient for definitive classification. In this study, the DNA barcoding results were fully consistent with both phylogenetic and species delimitation analyses, confirming clear genetic differentiation among the 10 mosquito species examined. The maximum likelihood tree constructed in this study revealed that all specimens clustered into well-supported monophyletic clades corresponding to their morphologically identified species, with bootstrap support values ranging from 98 to 100%. Notably, Ae. chrysolineatus was subdivided into two distinct subgroups by the mPTP analysis, a pattern not observed in the ABGD or ASAP results. This divergence may reflect underlying population structure potentially driven by geographical variation, as the two subgroups corresponded to specimens collected from Thailand (GenBank: PV963265-PV963267) and India (GenBank: EU259295), respectively. The mPTP method, which is highly sensitive to branch length variation in phylogenetic trees, can detect subtle genetic divergence and population-level differentiation. In contrast, ABGD and ASAP are based on genetic distance thresholds and may interpret such differences as intraspecific variation. These findings align with previous research on various arthropod taxa, which recommends employing multiple species delimitation methods to validate species boundaries, particularly in cases where genetic structure is present (Schwarzfeld and Sperling, 2015; Klimov et al., 2019).

In addition to molecular approaches, wing structures were analyzed using WGM to assess size and shape differences among Aedes species. Wings are widely recognized as key anatomical features for mosquito species identification using WGM, primarily because of their flat, two-dimensional structure, clearly defined vein intersections, and venation patterns that are conserved within species yet distinct across taxa (Lorenz et al., 2017). However, the most commonly used variable in WGM analyses is wing shape rather than wing size. This is because wing size is highly susceptible to environmental influences and often exhibits substantial variation within a species. Furthermore, wing size frequently overlaps among different mosquito species, such as Armigeres spp. (Laojun et al., 2024c) and Coquillettidia spp. (Laojun et al., 2023b). Our findings support this observation, as considerable overlap in wing size was detected among several Aedes species pairs, which is consistent with previous studies. When the classification accuracy of wing size was evaluated using maximum likelihood validation, the results showed very low accuracy (21.40%), indicating that wing size alone is not a reliable parameter for species identification.

Wing shape analysis revealed clear morphological differentiation among the 10 mosquito species examined. All pairwise comparisons of wing shape were statistically significant, indicating that shape effectively captures interspecific variation. Mahalanobis distance-based validation further demonstrated high classification accuracy for Aedes species (91.05%). This result is consistent with a previous study that evaluated wing contour, the first posterior cell, and the third posterior cell to differentiate three Aedes species in western Thailand, namely Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, and Ae. vexans, which reported similarly high classification accuracies of 90.29%, 93.20%, and 93.20%, respectively (Laojun et al., 2024b). However, this high classification accuracy may depend on the specific mosquito species included in the analysis. The species examined in the present study may exhibit pronounced differences in wing shape, thereby enhancing discriminatory performance. In contrast, when morphologically similar species are analyzed, overall accuracy tends to decline. For instance, a previous WGM study involving Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, and Ae. scutellaris reported a lower classification accuracy of 79.44%, which was attributed to the high degree of wing shape similarity between Ae. albopictus and Ae. scutellaris (Sumruayphol et al., 2016). Similarly, a WGM-based evaluation of Ae. crinifer, Ae. serratus, and Ae. terrens in São Paulo, Brazil, revealed relatively low classification accuracy (73–77%) due to morphological resemblance between Ae. crinifer and Ae. serratus (de Souza et al., 2020). Therefore, the application of geometric morphometrics in field settings should be preceded by species-specific validation studies to ensure reliable discrimination, particularly in the case of closely related or morphologically similar taxa.

In the present study, the number of analyzed specimens per species ranged from 15 to 32, limited by the short collection period, as specimens had to be collected across multiple sites. Nevertheless, both the raw classification accuracy and the Adjusted Total Accuracy (TAU) were reported. TAU corrects for ‘chance agreement’ that may arise from the number of groups and unequal sample sizes (Kovarovic et al., 2011). It is calculated by estimating the expected accuracy by chance, based on group distribution, and subtracting this value from the observed accuracy. This provides a performance measure that more accurately reflects the true discriminatory power of the model, rather than results influenced by random assignment. In this study, TAU analysis demonstrated that wing shape retained a high discriminatory efficiency of 90%, confirming that the observed accuracy was attributable to genuine morphological differences among species rather than random chance.

Wing shape analysis revealed that Ad. catasticta was markedly distinct from all other mosquito species examined, as demonstrated by several statistical measures, including the factor map of the first two discriminant functions, Mahalanobis distances, and the hierarchical clustering tree. This pronounced morphological divergence is consistent with the DNA barcoding results, which also clearly separated Ad. catasticta from the other taxa. Notably, Ad. catasticta belongs to a different genus than Aedes, but was included in the analysis due to its frequent misidentification as Aedes mosquitoes (Rattanarithikul et al., 2006, 2010). The source of this misidentification lies in morphological convergence, as Ad. catasticta exhibits features such as banded legs, comparable body size, and scaling patterns that resemble mosquitoes in the tribe Aedini (Harbach, 2023). Among the remaining species, Ae. macfarlanei exhibited the most pronounced morphological divergence, showing significant wing shape separation from other Aedes species. Historically, this species was transferred from the genus Aedes to Ochlerotatus based on morphological cladistics and evolutionary divergence. However, it was later reinstated in Aedes, as the excessive subdivision of subgenera rendered the taxonomic system of the tribe Aedini overly complex and unstable (Harbach, 2023). The pronounced shape differentiation observed in this study reflects the historical taxonomic confusion, while underscoring the distinct morphological characteristics of Ae. macfarlanei relative to other members of the genus Aedes. Additionally, the hierarchical clustering based on WGM showed that Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus were the most closely related species. This close association aligns with their shared taxonomic placement within the subgenus Stegomyia (Rattanarithikul et al., 2010). The phylogenetic tree derived from cox1 sequences also corroborated this relationship, with both species forming a well-supported monophyletic clade. Their proximity in both morphometric and molecular analyses highlights their strong evolutionary and phenotypic affinity as two globally important vectors of arboviral diseases. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the value of WGM as a powerful supplementary tool in mosquito taxonomy. By enabling assessments of wing shape, WGM enhances species identification and contributes to resolving taxonomic ambiguities, particularly among morphologically similar or closely related taxa.

To demonstrate the practical applicability of WGM in field settings, unknown field mosquito specimens were identified based on wing shape using Mahalanobis distances, compared against reference taxa established in this study. The identification yielded high accuracy: all species were correctly classified except for three, each with a single misidentified specimen. This result affirms the robustness of WGM as a reliable tool for mosquito identification. These findings align with previous studies, such as the field-based identification of morphologically similar Tabanus species (T. megalops, T. rubidus, and T. striatus) in Thailand, where WGM effectively discriminated among closely related taxa (Changbunjong et al., 2021).

The application of the RF algorithm to identify unknown field-collected mosquito specimens demonstrated strong discriminatory power based on wing shape variables. Seven mosquito species achieved perfect identification accuracy, with all specimens correctly classified, indicating a strong species-specific signal embedded in wing morphology and supporting the robustness of the RF approach. Slightly lower performance was observed for three species, each with 80% accuracy due to two misclassified specimens per group. When comparing the identification accuracy of unknown field specimens between the RF algorithm and Mahalanobis distance-based classification, the latter performed slightly better. This difference may be attributed to the RF’s requirement for a relatively large and representative training dataset to achieve optimal learning and prediction. This observation is consistent with previous research on mosquito species and age identification using mass spectrometric analysis, where Principal Components-Linear Discriminant Analysis (PC-LDA) and RF were evaluated. That study reported classification accuracy exceeding 97% with PC-LDA, whereas the RF approach achieved 84% (Wagner et al., 2023). Therefore, in practical applications, it may be necessary to train the RF model with larger datasets to improve predictive performance. Nevertheless, the overall high classification accuracy observed here underscores the potential of RF as a practical and reliable tool for species-level mosquito identification based on morphometric data.

Conclusions

Accurate identification is essential for selecting appropriate vector control strategies for specific mosquito species in a given area, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of surveillance and management of Aedes-borne diseases. This study demonstrates that DNA barcoding, WGM analysis, and the RF machine learning algorithm can each serve as effective supplementary tools for the identification of Aedes mosquitoes and a related taxon. However, the advantages and limitations of each approach should be carefully evaluated when selecting the most appropriate method for a given application. DNA barcoding provides high accuracy and is particularly effective for detecting cryptic species and resolving taxonomic ambiguities. Nevertheless, it requires access to a molecular laboratory, specialized equipment, and technical expertise. The reliability of species identification also depends heavily on the completeness and quality of reference sequences available in public databases such as GenBank and BOLD. Geometric morphometrics offers a cost-effective alternative that does not require advanced laboratory equipment. It distinguishes Aedes mosquito species based on measurable differences in wing shape and can be effectively applied in field settings where molecular tools are unavailable. However, the accuracy of WGM depends on the morphological divergence among the mosquito species present in a given area. In cases where species exhibit highly similar wing shapes, its discriminatory power may be reduced. Random Forests, a supervised machine learning algorithm, provides a robust and flexible classification framework using morphometric data. It can handle complex datasets and detect non-linear relationships among variables, often achieving high predictive accuracy. However, its performance relies on the availability of a sufficiently large and representative training dataset to achieve optimal results.
