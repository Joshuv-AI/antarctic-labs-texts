Association between wearable-derived dyskinesia time burden and longitudinal change in disability in people with Parkinson’s disease: a real-world treat-to-target cohort study
Authors: Yingchao Guan, Tong Wu, Yanan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWearable monitoring offers a way to quantify motor-state burden in Parkinson’s disease during routine care, but the clinical meaning of longitudinal change in dyskinesia burden remains uncertain. This secondary analysis used a publicly available real-world treat-to-target dataset from people with Parkinson’s disease managed with serial Personal KinetiGraph (PKG) monitoring. Participants with paired baseline and final total Movement Disorder Society-sponsored Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) scores were included in the paired clinical analyses. The primary adjusted regression used a complete-case sample restricted to participants with paired percent time in dyskinesia (PTD) values and complete covariate data. Change-score linear regression was used to examine whether longitudinal change in PTD was associated with change in total MDS-UPDRS score, adjusting for baseline total MDS-UPDRS, sex, disease duration, and baseline PTD. Among 30 participants with paired MDS-UPDRS data, total scores improved from 65.3 (SD 19.2) at baseline to 53.9 (SD 23.9) at final assessment (mean change − 11.4, 95% CI − 16.7 to − 6.0; p < 0.001). In the primary adjusted regression (n = 25), each 1-percentage-point increase in PTD corresponded to an estimated 0.25-point less favourable change in total MDS-UPDRS score (β = 0.25, SE 0.13, 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.52; p = 0.064). The estimate was in the expected direction, but the confidence interval crossed zero and the result was not conventionally statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses, including ANCOVA-type modelling and bootstrap resampling, were supportive only and did not materially change the overall interpretation. In this exploratory secondary analysis, increasing wearable-derived dyskinesia burden tended to accompany less favourable change in disability during routine treat-to-target care for Parkinson’s disease. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the small complete-case sample and the observational design. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease is characterised by progressive motor and non-motor impairment, and long-term treatment often requires balancing improvement in bradykinesia against the emergence of motor complications such as dyskinesia1. In routine care, this balance is not always easy to judge from clinic visits alone, because motor-state fluctuations vary across the day and may not be fully captured during a brief assessment2.

Wearable monitoring has become an increasingly useful adjunct in this setting3,4. Devices such as the Personal KinetiGraph (PKG) can provide continuous, objective summaries of motor-state burden during daily life, including indices related to bradykinesia and dyskinesia5,6. This has made wearable-derived measures potentially useful for treatment adjustment and longitudinal monitoring in Parkinson’s disease2–4,6. Even so, the clinical meaning of change in these measures over time remains incompletely understood, particularly when considered alongside changes in disability.

One question of practical relevance is whether increasing dyskinesia burden during treatment optimisation is associated with less favourable functional change over time. This issue is especially relevant in treat-to-target care pathways, where reducing bradykinesia may sometimes come at the cost of greater dyskinesia7. If worsening dyskinesia burden tracks with poorer disability outcomes, wearable-derived motor-state measures may help identify patients in whom treatment gains are offset by emerging motor complications8. At the same time, such relationships are unlikely to be simple, because disability in Parkinson’s disease reflects multiple interacting influences, including disease severity, medication response, and other aspects of clinical management.

The present study was therefore designed as a secondary analysis of a publicly available real-world treat-to-target dataset9. Using paired clinical assessments and serial PKG-derived measures, we examined whether longitudinal change in percent time in dyskinesia (PTD) was associated with longitudinal change in total Movement Disorder Society-sponsored Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) score. We hypothesised that greater increases in dyskinesia burden would be associated with less favourable change in disability over follow-up.

Methods

Data source and study design

This study was a secondary analysis of a previously published de-identified dataset deposited in the Dryad Digital Repository (10.5061/dryad.2ngf1vhj6)9. The source data came from a real-world treat-to-target pathway for people with Parkinson’s disease in which serial PKG monitoring was incorporated into routine clinical management7. In that pathway, PKG summaries were used to support treatment optimisation in practice, mainly through adjustment of antiparkinsonian therapy. The present study did not assign or modify treatment. Instead, it examined whether change in wearable-derived dyskinesia burden was associated with change in disability over time in this real-world care setting. Reporting followed STROBE and RECORD guidance10,11.

Participants and cohort derivation

The uploaded source dataset contained 103 participants with baseline PKG summary measures. Baseline total Movement Disorder Society-sponsored Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) scores were available for 100 participants. For the present analysis, paired clinical outcome analyses required both baseline and final total MDS-UPDRS scores. Thirty participants met this criterion and also had a documented final outcome of discharge. We used discharge as the clearest available end point of the routine treat-to-target episode in the source dataset, allowing a more consistent definition of the final clinical assessment across participants. However, this criterion may limit generalisability, as the analysed subgroup may not fully represent all patients entering the pathway.

The primary exposure analysis further required paired baseline and final percent time in dyskinesia (PTD) values. Of the 30 participants with paired total MDS-UPDRS data, 26 also had paired PTD values. The primary adjusted regression used a complete-case approach and additionally required complete data for the prespecified covariates: baseline total MDS-UPDRS, sex, disease duration, and baseline PTD. Outcome and wearable-derived measures

The primary clinical outcome was change in total MDS-UPDRS score from baseline to final assessment12. Secondary descriptive outcomes included change in MDS-UPDRS subscale scores, Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39), Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQuest), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and selected motor measures available in the uploaded dataset12–15.

The primary wearable-derived exposure was change in percent time in dyskinesia (ΔPTD). According to the source data dictionary, PTD was defined as percent time in dyskinesia and PTB as percent time in bradykinesia6. For descriptive purposes, we also report PKG summary measures BK50 and DK50, representing summary indices of bradykinesia and dyskinesia burden5,6. Baseline and follow-up PKG values were taken from the processed summary variables provided in the public dataset.

Because the uploaded dataset contained summary-level PKG outputs rather than raw epoch-level recordings, no additional reprocessing of day-level data was performed. Baseline and final PKG summaries were linked to the corresponding baseline and final clinical records using the nearest available time-matched records in the source dataset. As this was a secondary analysis of a publicly available de-identified dataset derived from a routine-care treat-to-target pathway, exact visit-level synchronisation between wearable monitoring windows and clinic-based assessments could not be guaranteed for every participant. Because only summary-level PKG outputs were available in the public dataset, the present analysis could not verify exact day-level correspondence between wearable summaries and clinic-based assessments for every participant.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation), and categorical variables as count (%). Baseline-to-final within-person changes were examined using paired t tests.

The primary inferential analysis used change-score linear regression to examine whether longitudinal change in dyskinesia burden tracked with longitudinal change in disability over follow-up. The dependent variable was change in total MDS-UPDRS score (final minus baseline), and the primary exposure was change in PTD (final minus baseline). The prespecified adjustment variables were baseline total MDS-UPDRS, sex, disease duration, and baseline PTD. These covariates were chosen a priori to account for baseline disability severity, demographic differences, disease chronicity, and initial dyskinesia burden.

Given the limited number of participants with complete longitudinal data, the adjusted model was treated as exploratory. We therefore used a complete-case approach for the primary regression and interpreted effect estimates with emphasis on confidence intervals and direction of effect rather than p values alone. To assess possible selection bias, baseline characteristics of participants included in the primary adjusted regression model were compared with those of participants excluded because of incomplete longitudinal outcome, wearable, or covariate data (Table S1).

Additional analyses included adjustment for baseline levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD)16 where available, winsorization of ΔPTD and ΔMDS-UPDRS total at the 5th and 95th percentiles, a non-parametric bootstrap with 5,000 resamples, and an ANCOVA-type sensitivity analysis. For the ANCOVA-type analysis, final total MDS-UPDRS score was used as the dependent variable, with ΔPTD, baseline total MDS-UPDRS, sex, disease duration, and baseline PTD entered as covariates.

All analyses were conducted in Python using pandas, scipy, and statsmodels. All tests were two-sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Borderline estimates were interpreted cautiously.

Improvements were also observed in MDS-UPDRS Part I, Part II, PDQ-39, and PKG BK50, whereas changes in MDS-UPDRS Part III, NMSQuest, and DK50 were not statistically significant after accounting for multiple comparisons. MDS-UPDRS Part IV and MoCA showed borderline change.

In the subgroup with paired PKG burden measures, PTB decreased markedly from 71.4% (SD 20.0) to 9.5% (SD 11.1), while PTD increased from 7.2% (SD 11.8) to 65.3% (SD 23.2). Both changes were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for each) (Table 2). Thus, the paired sample showed overall improvement in disability alongside a marked shift in wearable-derived motor-state burden, although the magnitude of these descriptive PKG changes should be interpreted cautiously in light of the selected paired subgroup and the structure of the available routine-care dataset.

Association between change in PTD and change in disability

In the primary adjusted change-score regression (n = 25), each 1-percentage-point increase in ΔPTD corresponded to an estimated 0.25-point less favourable change in total MDS-UPDRS score (β = 0.25, SE 0.13, 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.52, p = 0.064; R2 = 0.56) (Table 3; Fig. 2). The estimate was in the expected direction, but the confidence interval crossed zero and the result was not conventionally statistically significant; it should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were directionally consistent with the primary model and were interpreted as supportive only (Table 3). Additional adjustment for baseline LEDD in the subset with available medication data yielded a similar point estimate, although with wider uncertainty (β = 0.23, 95% CI − 0.18 to 0.63, p = 0.242; n = 17). Winsorization of ΔPTD and ΔMDS-UPDRS total at the 5th and 95th percentiles produced a somewhat attenuated but still positive estimate (β = 0.19, 95% CI 0.02–0.37, p = 0.031; n = 25). An ANCOVA-type sensitivity analysis using final total MDS-UPDRS score as the dependent variable yielded the same estimate as the primary model (β = 0.25, 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.52, p = 0.064; n = 25). In a 5,000-resample bootstrap of the primary adjusted model, the bootstrap mean coefficient for ΔPTD was 0.237, with a percentile 95% confidence interval of 0.003–0.490. These analyses did not resolve the limitations related to the small complete-case sample, possible selection bias, or the observational nature of the dataset.

Discussion

In this real-world secondary analysis, increasing wearable-derived dyskinesia burden tended to accompany less favourable change in disability during routine treat-to-target care for Parkinson’s disease. This pattern was in the expected direction, but the main adjusted estimate was borderline, based on a small complete-case sample, and should be interpreted with caution. The findings are therefore best viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The descriptive pattern of rising PTD together with falling PTB is clinically plausible in a treat-to-target setting, where attempts to reduce bradykinesia may in some patients be accompanied by greater dyskinesia7,8. That said, the magnitude of the observed changes in this study was striking and should be interpreted with particular caution. In this secondary analysis of a selected paired subgroup from a public routine-care dataset, these descriptive shifts may reflect sample selection, the structure of the available summary dataset, and imperfect alignment between wearable and clinical measurements, rather than a typical pattern expected across all patients managed within PKG-guided care6. These descriptive changes should therefore not be assumed to represent the usual magnitude of change in broader clinical populations.

Although the primary association was not conventionally statistically significant, the estimated effect size is still worth noting. Based on the point estimate, a 10-percentage-point increase in ΔPTD corresponded to an approximately 2.5-point less favourable change in total MDS-UPDRS score. This may be clinically noticeable at the individual level, but its practical importance remains uncertain because the confidence interval was wide and the present study was not designed to define a clinically important threshold.

These results do not support causal interpretation. This was a secondary analysis of observational routine-care data, and residual confounding is likely11. Medication changes, treatment responsiveness, baseline disease severity, and other aspects of clinical management may all have influenced both dyskinesia burden and disability over time.

From a rehabilitation perspective, the findings are of interest, but they do not show that PKG-guided scheduling or tailoring of therapy improves outcomes. At most, they suggest a hypothesis for future prospective study: namely, whether periods of higher dyskinesia burden interfere with mobility practice or functional training, and whether wearable-derived motor-state information can be used in a practical way to inform rehabilitation timing2–4.

The sensitivity analyses were supportive only and did not materially change the overall interpretation. The ANCOVA-type model produced the same estimate as the primary analysis, and bootstrap resampling supported a positive direction for the ΔPTD coefficient. Even so, these analyses do not overcome the limits imposed by the small complete-case sample, possible selection bias, imperfect time alignment, and the observational design.

This study has several limitations. The primary adjusted regression was based on a small complete-case sample (n = 25), limiting power and increasing the risk of model instability. The main association between ΔPTD and change in total MDS-UPDRS score was borderline and should be interpreted cautiously. Because this was a secondary analysis of observational routine-care data, residual confounding is likely, particularly from medication adjustment, treatment response, baseline disease severity, and unmeasured rehabilitation exposure. Only a subset of the original cohort contributed complete longitudinal data, so selection bias cannot be excluded. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the included complete-case sample tended to have lower baseline PTD, higher baseline PTB, lower Hoehn and Yahr stage, and somewhat better baseline quality-of-life scores than excluded participants, suggesting that the analysed subgroup may not fully represent the broader source cohort. In addition, PKG summaries and clinic-based assessments may not have been perfectly aligned at every time point, and the summary-level structure of the public dataset prevented verification of exact day-level correspondence for every participant. Finally, the large descriptive shifts in PTD and PTB require cautious interpretation and should be confirmed in larger prospective cohorts with prespecified wearable-data handling and follow-up schedules.

In conclusion, this exploratory secondary analysis found a directionally consistent but statistically borderline association between increasing wearable-derived dyskinesia burden and less favourable longitudinal change in disability in Parkinson’s disease. These findings support further prospective work on wearable-derived motor-state burden as a possible marker of longitudinal functional trajectory, but they are not sufficient to support causal inference, definition of clinically actionable thresholds, or immediate changes in routine practice.
