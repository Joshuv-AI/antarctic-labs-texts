Association of age at menarche with gestational hypertension and preeclampsia: A large prospective cohort in China
Authors: Hang An, Xiaojing Liu, Zhiwen Li, Le Zhang, Yali Zhang, Jianmeng Liu, Rongwei Ye, Nan Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored the potential association between age at menarche and the risks of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia in Chinese women. Data were sourced from the China‐US Collaborative Project for Neural Tube Defects Prevention, a large population‐based cohort study. Our study consisted of 209 411 women pre‐registered for pregnancy in two provinces in South China. Trained healthcare workers measured blood pressure at registration and recorded other pertinent health information. Using logistic regression, we assessed the correlations between age at menarche and the likelihood of developing gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, considering confounders such as maternal age, body mass index, ethnicity, parity, folic acid supplementation, education level, and occupation. The observed incidences for gestational hypertension and preeclampsia were 9.65% and 2.54%, respectively. The adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for gestational hypertension, based on age at menarche, were as follows: ≤13 years, 1.18 (95% confidence interval: 1.11–1.26); 14 years, 1.09 (1.04–1.15); 15 years, 1.11 (1.06–1.16); 16 years, 1.06 (1.01–1.12); and ≥17 years, 1.00 (reference; P for trend < .001). The correlation between age at menarche and preeclampsia varied across age groups, with the following respective ORs: 1.35 (1.20–1.52), 1.21 (1.09–1.34), 1.27 (1.15–1.39), 1.14 (1.03–1.26), and 1.00 (reference; P for trend < .001). This association appeared to be more pronounced in women with no folic acid supplementation and those with a lower education level. In conclusion, an earlier age at menarche seems to be linked to increased risks of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia.

INTRODUCTION

Menarche, the first menstrual cycle, signifies the commencement of puberty and the activation of the female reproductive endocrine system. 1 The age at menarche typically spans 7−16 years. 2 Recent trends depict a declining mean age at menarche. 3 , 4 This warrants increased scrutiny of the health implications of menarche, as an earlier onset might increase the risk of elevated blood pressure and cardiovascular diseases in later life. 5 , 6 Women who experience menarche at a younger age may also face hormonal imbalances 7 , 8 and heightened inflammatory immune responses, 9 both of which correlate with anomalous blood pressure levels during gestation.

Hypertensive conditions during pregnancy, including gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, pose major complications that can lead to unfavorable pregnancy outcomes and potential long‐term health repercussions. 10 , 11 , 12 A growing body of research underscores the pivotal role of augmented proinflammatory adipokine production and insulin resistance in precipitating maternal hypertension. 13 While these associations seem plausible, there exists a paucity of studies probing the link between age at menarche and hypertensive conditions during pregnancy. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Existing literature suggests that a later age at menarche might elevate the risk of preeclampsia. 15 , 16 , 17 , 19 However, many of these investigations were conducted as case–control studies that had limited sample sizes or yielded inconsistent findings. 14 , 18 Addressing age at menarche from a preventative perspective holds relevance, given the feasibility of intervention prior to conception. Moreover, while gestational hypertension and preeclampsia may exhibit distinct etiological factors, 20 no study has yet compared the age at menarche in relation to these conditions. Consequently, we used data from a comprehensive prospective population‐based cohort study to investigate the relationship between age at menarche and hypertensive pregnancy outcomes, and further assessed potential modulating factors.

METHODS

Background and original cohort

We used data from an extensive prospective cohort study, details of which have been documented previously. 21 , 22 Initiated in 1993, the Chinese Ministry of Health embarked on a health drive to curtail neural tube defects across 21 counties within two southern provinces (Zhejiang and Jiangsu) and one northern province (Hebei). As part of this initiative, all women residing within these research regions, who were either on the cusp of marriage or had conceived, were enlisted in a perinatal health care surveillance system. This system functioned as the primary repository for prenatal care, along with gynecological and demographic data. Concurrently, all the women were recommended to take a 400‐μg folic acid supplement daily, starting from the time of their registration with the pregnancy monitoring platform and persisting through the initial trimester of pregnancy. Those who agreed to folic acid supplementation were provided the pills at registration. Health professionals, at each month's end, cataloged menstrual period dates and tracked the remaining pill count (if any). To assess the impact of folic acid on neural tube defects, we identified women who registered between October 1993 and September 1995, gave birth by 31 December 1996, and whose offspring (whether live‐born, stillborn, or terminated due to prenatal birth defect diagnosis) were verifiable for the presence or absence of a neural tube defect. The cohort included 247 831 women.

The institutional review boards of both the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Peking University Health Science Center sanctioned the project. Given that the data were already gathered, the institutional review boards regarded secondary analyses as exempt from the need for informed consent.

Selection of study subjects

We focused on participants registered in the two southern provinces: Jiangsu and Zhejiang. These neighboring provinces maintained meticulous records regarding hypertensive disorders during pregnancy within their surveillance system. Out of 215 871 women from these provinces, we excluded patients for whom the age at menarche was absent (n = 5382); with a history of chronic hypertension before the 20‐week gestation mark; and with an ambiguous diagnosis related to hypertensive pregnancy disorders (n = 5711). Thus, the analysis included 209 411 participants, representing 97.01% of the initial cohort (Figure 1).

Flowchart of participants.

Age at menarche and covariates

Data were collected through the perinatal healthcare surveillance systems. Participants completed a detailed questionnaire, overseen by proficient health workers. Gynecologic data and prenatal healthcare particulars were first chronicled in a brochure by doctors. These data were digitized by personnel from maternal and child health hospitals, and ultimately relayed to the Peking University project center. Covariates amassed via this relatively uniform questionnaire included maternal age, body mass index (BMI; determined by dividing the weight at the initial prenatal visit by the square of the height), parity, ethnicity, folic acid supplementation, education level, and occupation.

Definition of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia

During every medical encounter, blood pressure cuffs were calibrated in line with the arm's circumference. Blood pressure measurements, using a mercury sphygmomanometer, were carried out on the right arm and were noted on at least two sequential instances separated by ≥6 h. Gestational hypertension was delineated as either a blood pressure reading of ≥140/90 mmHg after the 20‐week gestation mark or a spike of ≥30/15 mmHg beyond the 20‐week threshold compared to first trimester recordings. Preeclampsia (including eclampsia) was characterized by a blood pressure of ≥140/90 mmHg or a spike of 30/15 mmHg after 20 gestational weeks, concomitant with proteinuria (a singular random urine specimen displaying a minimum of 1+ protein on a dipstick assessment) after the 20‐week mark. 23

Statistical analysis

The study evaluated and contrasted maternal attributes (age, BMI, parity, ethnicity, folic acid supplementation, education level, and occupation) among women with gestational hypertension (excluding preeclampsia), those with preeclampsia, and those unaffected by gestational hypertension. Variance analysis was employed to assess quantitative variables, and the chi‐squared test was used to assess categorical variables. Using logistic regression, both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) concerning age at menarche (continuous or categorical) with gestational hypertension and preeclampsia were derived, following adjustment for confounders such as maternal age, BMI, parity, ethnicity, folic acid supplementation, education level, and occupation. Stratified analyses were performed to test the modification effects potentially introduced by variables like folic acid supplementation, education level, parity, BMI, and maternal age; interaction components were integrated into the multivariate logistic regression models to gauge the impacts. Interaction P‐values were derived from the cross‐products of folic acid supplementation or education level against the age of menarche. All data processing and evaluations were executed using SPSS for Windows software (ver. 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

We included 209 411 women in the analysis. Of these, 20 202 (9.65%) were diagnosed with gestational hypertension and 5309 (2.54%) with preeclampsia. The mean age at menarche was 15.13 years (median, 15.00 years). Women of advanced age, with a higher BMI, being primiparous, and consuming folic acid exhibited an increased likelihood of hypertension during pregnancy. Those affected by gestational hypertension predominantly engaged in factory occupations, whereas individuals with preeclampsia generally had higher academic qualifications. Table 3 presents the correlations between the age of menarche categories and pregnancy‐related hypertension. An earlier age of menarche corresponded with an escalated risk for both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. Factoring in maternal age, BMI, ethnicity, parity, folic acid supplementation, education level, and occupation, the adjusted ORs for gestational hypertension, segmented by age of menarche categories (≤13, 14, 15, 16, ≥17 years old), were 1.18 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.11−1.26), 1.09 (1.04−1.15), 1.11 (1.06−1.16), 1.06 (1.01−1.12), and 1.00 (reference; P for trend < .001), respectively. The ORs for preeclampsia were 1.35 (1.20−1.52), 1.21 (1.09−1.34), 1.27 (1.15−1.39), 1.14 (1.03−1.26), and 1.00 (reference; P for trend < .001), respectively. Women who experienced menarche at ≤13 years had the greatest risk for both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. We further compared the effect of menarche age as a continuous variable on hypertension during pregnancy. Women with a 1‐year decrease in the early onset of menarche were associated with a 3% (1.02−1.04) and 5% (1.03−1.08) increased risk of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia after the adjustment of covariates, respectively.

Next, we investigated the modified effects of significant interaction variables, notably folic acid supplementation and education level. When stratified by folic acid supplementation, a pronounced association between an earlier age at menarche and gestational hypertension was evident among those who did not take folic acid (P interaction < 0.001; Figure 2A). The interaction between age at menarche and folic acid supplementation was also significant for preeclampsia (P interaction < 0.001; Figure 2B). When stratified by education level, the detrimental effects of early menarche on gestational hypertension were predominantly observed in those with junior high school education or lower, or unknown education level (P interaction = 0.030; Figure 2C). Comparable findings emerged for the preeclampsia group, barring those who underwent menarche at age 15 (P interaction = 0.040; Figure 2D).

When stratified by parity, the correlations between earlier menarche and increased risks for gestational hypertension and preeclampsia were more evident in multiparous women (Figure 2E and 2F). Detailed data are available in Tables S2–4. Stratification based on BMI and maternal age might also reveal modifying effects. Specifically, the negative correlations were notably pronounced among women with elevated BMI and those of advanced maternal age (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

This comprehensive population‐based cohort study of 209 411 pregnant women revealed that an earlier age at menarche correlated with an increased risk of both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. Specifically, for every 1‐year decrease in age at menarche, the risk increased by 3% for gestational hypertension and 5% for preeclampsia. These associations were pronounced among women who did not take folic acid supplements and among those with a lower education level.

Several studies have investigated the relationship between age at menarche and hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 For instance, in a Finnish cohort, 19 women diagnosed with preeclampsia tended to experience menarche earlier compared to their counterparts (13.0 [9.0−17.0] years old vs. 13.0 [10.0‐18.0] years old). In a Swedish cohort study, 15 those who experienced menarche at ≤10 years had a 3‐times increased risk of preeclampsia when using menarche age of 13 years as reference. Another recent case‐control study also showed early age at menarche (10–15 years) significantly increased the risk of preeclampsia (adjusted OR 7.79, 95% CI 3.10–25.29). 24 In contrast, when compared with menarche age of 12 years, advanced age at menarche (≥15 years old) could prevent against the risk of preeclampsia (adjusted OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19–0.90). 17 These findings are in agreement with the present study. While both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia present as elevated blood pressure disorders during gestation, their underlying etiologies might diverge. 20 Yet, no studies to our knowledge have compared the effects of menarche age with these specific outcomes. Leveraging data from the Cambridge Baby Growth Study (comprising 438 participants), Clive et al. 18 suggested that a later age at menarche was associated with increased blood pressure; however, the findings did not correspond to an increased incidence of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. Similarly, the UK Biobank study did not corroborate such a connection. 14 The results from a Chinese cohort study indicated that early menarche age could significantly decrease the risk of hypertension among women aged >40 years. 25 One explanation for the inconsistent effects of menarche age on hypertension and HDP may lie in the different population, study design and genetic predispositions across previously published studies. Another possible explanation is that pregnant women are more sensitive to metabolism dysfunction and immunological changes, which usually accompany early age at menarche. 26 , 27

The present findings suggest that folic acid supplementation and maternal education level modulate the association between early menarche age and the onset of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. Within our cohort, folic acid supplementation in tandem with an early age of menarche was linked to a marginally diminished risk of gestational hypertension relative to those who abstained from folic acid supplementation. Earlier studies 28 , 29 have posited that folic acid supplementation may reduce the risks of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia by attenuating plasma homocysteine concentrations. Such insights may assist the development of preventive stratagems for gestational hypertension among women who experienced menarche early. Additionally, our analysis stratified by education level underscored that the detrimental impact of an early age of menarche on both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia predominantly manifested among women with lower education level. Women with greater education might be more attuned to their health, thereby counteracting the negative ramifications of an early menarche age. This accentuates the importance of health education before and during pregnancy.

The precise mechanisms bridging an earlier age of menarche with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy remain unclear. Recent research suggests the potential involvement of metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory responses. Excess caloric intake during childhood or specific endocrine influences could expedite sexual maturation, thereby advancing the onset of menarche. 30 , 31 Women who experienced menarche earlier were observed to have elevated levels of circulating C‐reactive protein, 32 a hallmark indicator of inflammation. Such heightened inflammatory processes might amplify the risks of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia via endothelial dysfunction and reduced placental perfusion. 33 , 34 An earlier age of menarche has also been associated with insulin resistance, 7 which might activate the sympathetic nervous system and promote renal sodium retention, setting the stage for hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. 35 , 36

Our study has several notable strengths. First, we based our research on a prospective cohort, wherein details regarding the age at menarche and other pertinent maternal characteristics were gathered upon enrollment, thereby minimizing the potential influence of the resultant outcomes. The precision of data acquisition and subsequent monitoring of hypertension during pregnancy was ensured through the meticulous perinatal health care surveillance system. Furthermore, the diagnoses of gestational hypertension were systematically carried out by adept health professionals following a standardized protocol. With a substantial sample size, we could discern both overarching and niche subgroup effects. The in‐depth data encompassing pregnancy complications and clinical records enhanced our capacity to assess associations spanning broader subtypes of gestational hypertension and granular categories of the age at menarche. Although the age at menarche is, to a degree, genetically determined, our cohort was predominantly (>99%) of Han ethnicity, offering a uniform genetic backdrop. Moreover, the geographical specificity of our cohort, originating from Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces, ensured comparable dietary and lifestyle practices. Finally, our analyses stratified by folic acid supplementation and education level revealed that both these factors might modulate the association between age at menarche and hypertension during pregnancy.

Nevertheless, the present study had several limitations. Foremost, the age at menarche was determined retrospectively via self‐reporting without supporting medical documentation, introducing potential recall bias. However, a previous study supports the generally high accuracy of self‐reported age at menarche. 37 Then this large historical cohort study was prospectively conducted 20 years ago, when sociodemographic characteristics, dietary patterns and lifestyle of the included population might be different from those in the contemporary period. Given our reliance on pre‐existing datasets, certain confounders, notably maternal smoking and alcohol consumption, remain unaddressed. However, during the timeframe of our study, instances of smoking or alcohol consumption were relatively infrequent, particularly among reproductive‐age women in more agrarian Chinese settings. 38 , 39 Moreover, relevant maternal data, like familial history of preeclampsia, current pregnancy status, dietary patterns and metabolic status, were also unavailable in the present study. Further studies are needed to collect the information to reduce potential biases. Another limitation was related to the geographical area of the initial cohort, which spanned two southern and a northern province in China; however, we excluded the northern province from our analysis owing to a dearth of exhaustive clinical records on hypertension. Finally, our reliance on menstrual dates to ascertain gestational age may have skewed the classification of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, potentially attenuating the perceived risk.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study, based on a large, meticulous prospective cohort, investigated the relationship between age at menarche and gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. When potential confounders were considered, our analyses revealed that an earlier age at menarche correlated with heightened risks of both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. The dynamics of this relationship were modulated folic acid supplementation and maternal education level. Our findings suggest that early biological maturation may be a risk factor of hypertension during pregnancy, emphasizing the need for preemptive interventions.
