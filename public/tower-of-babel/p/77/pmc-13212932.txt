Box A of HMGB1 plasmid reverses the age-related changes in the plasma proteomic profile of perimenopausal monkeys
Authors: Sasikarn Komkleow, Sukanya Jaroenporn, Daranee Chokchaichamnankit, Thanaporn Bubparam, Sakawdaurn Yasom, Theetat Ruangjaroon, Jisnuson Svasti, Teeranai Ittiudomrak, Polkit Sangvanich, Apiwat Mutirangura, Chantragan Srisomsap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging, a complex biological process, is intrinsically linked to the pathogenesis of numerous age-related diseases. A key factor in the aging process is the accumulation of DNA damage and the subsequent activation or failure of the DNA damage response. To mitigate this damage, DNA repair mechanisms often involve the formation of DNA gaps. This study investigates the potential role of the Box A domain of High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) in modulating age-related changes. We utilized a label-free quantitative proteomic technique to analyze the plasma proteome of three female adult and eight female perimenopausal cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis), with the perimenopausal group receiving an intravenous administration of the Box A plasmid. Proteomic analysis revealed differential expressions in proteins primarily associated with stress response, immune regulation, lipid transport, and cellular homeostasis following Box A plasmid intervention. Notably, the expression levels of key proteins, such as apolipoprotein E (APOE) and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), showed a reversal effect, restoring levels closer to those observed in the younger, adult monkeys. These findings highlight the potential of the Box A of HMGB1 plasmid as a therapeutic candidate to mitigate age-related proteomic alterations, offering a novel avenue for targeted interventions in aging and associated diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46747-9.

Introduction

Aging, defined as the progressive physiological functional decline over time, is intrinsically linked to the pathogenesis of numerous age-related diseases, including immune system disorders, musculoskeletal disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and neurological diseases1,2. A characteristic feature of aging is the accumulation of DNA damage, which plays a significant role in the deterioration of cellular function2,3. The sustained destruction of DNA and the subsequent activation or failure of the DNA-damage response (DDR) are pivotal in the aging process, often leading to detrimental cellular outcomes such as senescence, apoptosis, and telomere shortening3. Maintaining DNA integrity is crucial for cell viability. One mechanism employed by cells to ensure this integrity involves the dynamic regulation of DNA structures, often observed as DNA gaps, known as youth-DNA-gaps4,5. These gaps are believed to minimize mechanical stress and torsion forces within the DNA structure, thereby protecting it from damage4,5. Interestingly, the number of these physiological DNA gaps typically is reduced in yeast, rats, and human cells as they age, as well as in chemically-induced senescent cells6–9. The High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) protein has emerged as a key molecule involved in various biological processes highly relevant to aging, including inflammation, DNA repair, and cell senescence4–10. The Box A domain of HMGB1 is a highly conserved DNA-binding domain crucial for modulating HMGB1’s biological functions11. Box A is known to bind DNA and interact with other proteins, acting as a molecular regulator that influences the formation of DNA gaps to enhance DNA integrity and protection4,7,12. Growing evidence suggests that Box A-induced DNA gaps may reverse aging characteristics in vivo and in vitro13–17, having been shown to inhibit liver fibrosis and improve aging brain functions in aged rat models4. Furthermore, Box A can enhance stemness, suggesting a role in improving stem cell activity compromised by illness and aging10.

The circulatory system, particularly the blood plasma, serves as a rich source for studying systemic aging, as its protein composition reflects the body’s overall physiological condition18,19. Consequently, proteomics, the large-scale study of proteins, is a powerful tool for comprehensively unraveling the underlying molecular mechanisms and age-related alterations20–22. Label-free quantitative proteomics offers a robust approach for identifying potential biomarkers and accurately measuring protein expression levels23.

Despite vast research highlighting the role of HMGB1 and its Box A domain in aging models using cell cultures, rats, and mice4,24,25, the translational relevance of these findings to primates, including humans, remains uncertain. To address this critical gap, it is imperative to investigate the systemic role of the Box A domain in modulating age-related changes using a nonhuman primate model.

In this study, we present a novel investigation into the systemic role of the Box A domain of HMGB1 in aging. Utilizing a label-free quantitative proteomic approach, we analyzed the plasma proteome of cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) following the intravenous administration of the Box A plasmid. This approach allowed us to elucidate age-associated proteomic alterations and examine the modulatory role of the Box A domain in key aging-related pathways in a nonhuman primate model. Results

Label-free quantitative proteomic analysis of plasma from adult and perimenopausal monkeys

Label-free quantitative proteomics was performed to identify and quantify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the plasma of adult female (Y-PC) and perimenopausal (A-PC) cynomolgus macaques. A total of 265 proteins were identified in the Y-PC monkey group, and 261 proteins were identified in the A-PC group. A large proportion of the identified proteins, 230 proteins, were common to both groups (Fig. 2A). Only 35 proteins were uniquely present in the Y-PC group, and 31 proteins were unique to the A-PC group (Fig. 2A). This indicates that the majority of the plasma proteome remained consistent between the two age groups. Differential expression analysis, calculated using PEAKS Studio Xpro version 10.6, identified 114 proteins that met the significance criteria of ANOVA p-value ≤ 0.05 and absolute fold change greater than 1.2. Among these differentially expressed proteins, 43 proteins were up-regulated, and 71 proteins were down-regulated in the perimenopausal (A-PC) group when compared to the adult (Y-PC) group (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

The analysis revealed significant changes in proteins related to diverse biological processes. The protein exhibiting the greatest up-regulation (2.68-fold increase) in the A-PC group compared to the Y-PC group was Apolipoprotein E (APOE), a critical protein involved in lipid transport and neurological function. Conversely, the protein with the greatest down-regulation (3.25-fold decrease) was Keratinocyte Proline-Rich Protein (KPRP). Other notable proteins showing high fold-changes included several proteins associated with immune function and inflammation as well as structural and transport proteins. Proteins that were up-regulated (ranging from 2.03 to 2.28-fold increase in A-PC vs. Y-PC) included Immunoglobulin heavy variable 3–64 (IGHV3-64), Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein 1 (ORM1), Keratin type II cytoskeletal 73 (KRT73), and Keratin type II cytoskeletal 74 (KRT74). Conversely, key proteins that were significantly down-regulated (exhibiting a decrease ranging from 1.90 to 2.95-fold) included Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG), along with several immunoglobulins such as Immunoglobulin kappa variable 3D-7 (IGKV3D-7), Immunoglobulin heavy variable 4–28 (IGHV4-28), and Immunoglobulin heavy variable 3–72 (IGHV3-72). A volcano plot was constructed to visualize the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the groups, highlighting APOE, HV634, KPRP, and SHBG as the most significantly altered proteins of interest (Fig. 3). Additionally, the investigation of plasma proteins in Y-PC and A-PC groups was carried out for eight weeks (W8), although the findings indicated that there was the same pattern with slight changes in the proteins from the initial results (W0).

Functional and interaction analysis of plasma from adult and perimenopausal monkeys

The differentially expressed proteins identified between the adult (Y-PC) and perimenopausal (A-PC) groups (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional classification using the PANTHER Classification System. The analysis (Fig. 4) revealed that the majority of dysregulated proteins were primarily involved in several key biological processes. The most highly represented classifications included the response to stimulus (25.80%), immune system process (23.00%), metabolic process (17.20%), and biological regulation (12.90%), with other processes accounting for 21.10%. These findings highlight that aging in cynomolgus macaques is significantly associated with alterations in systemic stress response and immune function pathways.

The protein-protein interaction networks of 43 up-regulated proteins and 71 down-regulated proteins (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) were analyzed by the STRING database to predict functional associations and pathways (GO pathway). Thirty-three out of 43 up-regulated proteins demonstrated strong interaction (Fig. 5A). This network was clustered into four functionally distinct groups: response to stress (22 proteins), immune response (13 proteins), lipid transport (7 proteins), and keratinization (4 proteins). Some proteins were involved in many functions, such as keratin type I cytoskeletal 16 (KRT16), apolipoprotein E (APOE), apolipoprotein D (APOD), apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), and complement C4-A (C4A).

Thirty-nine of the 71 down-regulated proteins showed significant protein-protein interactions (Fig. 5B). Label-free quantitative proteomic analysis of plasma from perimenopausal monkeys treated with Box A plasmid

To evaluate the modulatory role of the Box A plasmid in age-related proteomic alterations, label-free quantitative proteomics was used to compare the plasma proteome of perimenopausal monkeys before (A-Box A W0) and one week after the final dosage (A-Box A W8) of Box A administration. The analysis identified 274 proteins at W0 (A-Box A W0) and 262 proteins at W8 (A-Box A W8), with 239 proteins common to both time points. There were 35 unique proteins at W0 and 23 unique proteins at W8 (Fig. 2B). Differential expression analysis using the cutoff criteria of ANOVA p-value ≤ 0.05 and absolute fold change greater than 1.4 identified 47 differentially expressed proteins between A-Box A W0 and A-Box A W8. Interestingly, the majority of these changes represented an increase in expression, with 40 proteins up-regulated and only 7 proteins down-regulated following the Box A intervention (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The most highly up-regulated protein (2.41-fold increase) was Hornerin (HRNR), a protein involved in keratinization. Conversely, the greatest decrease in expression (2.88-fold reduction) was observed in Immunoglobulin heavy constant gamma 2 (IGHG2).

Crucially, we observed a reversal phenomenon in the expression levels of several proteins known to be dysregulated during aging (Supplementary Table 5). Following Box A treatment, the expression of key proteins trended back toward the levels previously observed in the younger, adult (Y-PC) monkeys. Specifically, proteins that were up-regulated with age (e.g., Apolipoprotein E (APOE), Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein 1 (ORM1), Apolipoprotein C-I (APOC1), and Apolipoprotein C-II (APOC2) significantly decreased toward young levels. Conversely, proteins that were down-regulated with age, including Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) and several immunoglobulins (e.g., IGKV1D-3, IGKV1-33, IGHV3-49, IGHV4-28, and IGKV3D-7), along with Putative apolipoprotein(a)-like protein 2 (LPAL2), increased toward young levels. This finding strongly suggests that the Box A plasmid intervention mitigates the age-related plasma proteomic signature in perimenopausal macaques.

The protein-protein interactions of 40 upregulated proteins and 7 down-regulated proteins (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4) following Box A treatment were analyzed using the STRING database to predict functional associations and pathways (Fig. 6). Twenty-seven out of 47 proteins showed strong interaction, clustering into three primary functional groups: Response to Stress (14 proteins), Lipid Transport (7 proteins), and Intermediate Filament Organization/Keratinization (3 proteins). Key proteins acting as central hubs within this network, indicating their involvement in multiple functions, included Keratin type I cytoskeletal 16 (KRT16), Apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), and Lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP).

Validation of the identified proteins from LC-MS/MS

Validation of key differentially expressed proteins was performed using Western blot analysis for Apolipoprotein E (APOE) and Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG), utilizing both pooled and individual plasma samples (Fig. 7). The Western blot results from the pooled samples generally confirmed the reversal trends observed in the quantitative proteomics data following the Box A intervention (Fig. 7A). Specifically, Apolipoprotein E (APOE), which was up-regulated with age, showed a decrease in expression in the A-Box A W8 group, moving toward young levels. Conversely, SHBG, which was down-regulated with age, showed a corresponding increase in expression in the A-Box A W8 group, further supporting the mitigating effect of the Box A plasmid on age-related proteomic alterations.

Fig. 7Western blot verification of expression levels for selected dysregulated proteins in blood plasma. A Analysis using a pooled sample of blood plasma. B Analysis using individual samples of blood plasma.

Western blot verification of expression levels for selected dysregulated proteins in blood plasma. A Analysis using a pooled sample of blood plasma. B Analysis using individual samples of blood plasma.

The analysis of individual plasma samples revealed a consistent general pattern that mirrored the pooled sample results, confirming the reversal effect of Box A on APOE and SHBG levels. However, as expected in biological studies involving non-human primates, the results exhibited fluctuations due to the inherent biological variation among individual monkeys (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

Aging is a complex biological process characterized by a progressive decline in physiological function and an increased susceptibility to age-related diseases. One of the fundamental hallmarks of aging is the accumulation of DNA damage, which arises from endogenous and exogenous sources, leading to genomic instability and cellular dysfunction2,26,27. The HMGB1 Box A domain has emerged as a promising candidate for rejuvenation therapies due to its multifaceted roles in modulating cellular processes associated with aging, particularly those related to DNA integrity. Box A domain has been proposed to possess “molecular scissor-like” activity, which involves creating gaps in DNA4,7,12. This process enhances DNA stability and protects against DNA damage, suggesting that Box A has significant potential as a therapeutic tool to modulate the aging process and target age-related diseases5.

This study employed label-free quantitative proteomics to investigate the systemic effects of aging and Box A intervention in non-human primates. The initial comparison between adult (Y-PC) and perimenopausal (A-PC) monkeys revealed significant age-related changes, with 43 proteins up-regulated and 71 proteins down-regulated in the A-PC group. Functional analysis revealed that these dysregulated proteins clustered into key pathways critically linked to the hallmarks of aging: Response to Stress, Immunological Response, Lipid Transport, Regulation of Proteolysis, and Blood Coagulation. The up-regulation of proteins associated with stress and inflammation (e.g., ORM1) and the deregulation of coagulation factors reflect the state of chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging) and the heightened risk of cardiovascular events characteristic of advanced aging22,28,29.

The most compelling finding of this research is the reversal effect observed in the plasma proteomic profile following intravenous administration of the Box A plasmid in perimenopausal monkeys. The analysis of proteins before and after Box A treatment (A-Box A W0 vs. W8) showed significant alterations, with 40 proteins up-regulated and 7 proteins down-regulated. Critically, these changes translated into a restoration of expression levels for specific age-dysregulated proteins back toward the levels seen in younger monkeys. This reversal phenomenon suggests that Box A may act as a molecular modulator, restoring the expression balance in pathways related to Intermediate Filament Organization, Response to Stress, and Lipid Transport.

The observed reversal effect was particularly notable and validated via Western blot for two key markers: Apolipoprotein E (APOE) and Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG). In this study, APOE was up-regulated with age in perimenopausal monkeys, a condition implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous age-related diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, atherosclerosis, and metabolic disorders30–32. APOE is crucial for cholesterol transport33 but also plays roles in DNA synthesis, cell proliferation, and angiogenesis34. Numerous studies reported that overexpression of APOE contributes to the pathogenesis of a wide range of diseases. Studies on mice have revealed that overexpression of APOE causes hypertriglyceridemia35. Another research indicated that overexpression of APOE reduces fat accumulation while increasing cell proliferation in human adipocytes36. Our investigation found that HMGB1 Box A tends to reduce the high expression of APOE in perimenopausal monkeys, bringing it back to a level comparable to that of younger monkeys. This finding highlights the therapeutic potential of Box A in targeting age-related molecular alterations.

Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) is a carrier protein that is mostly produced in the liver and released into the bloodstream. It possesses a strong affinity for sex steroids and regulates the availability of these hormones37. Circulating SHBG is recognized to have a significant role in aging and age-related diseases, although the specific mechanism is not established. It has been demonstrated that decreased SHBG is associated with osteoporosis in elderly males38. Another study discovered a reduction in SHBG levels among older women diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease39. On the contrary, high expression of SHBG prevented body weight increase and accumulation of fat caused by a high-fat diet in mice. Furthermore, the high expression level of SHBG inhibited the increase in insulin, leptin, and resistin levels, along with the decrease in adiponectin caused by a high-fat meal40. Some studies have suggested a protective effect of high SHBG expression against certain cancers, including breast and prostate cancer. Elevated SHBG levels may lower cancer risk by reducing the bioavailability of sex hormones, which are known to promote tumor growth and progression41,42. Our research findings indicate that the administration of Box A to elderly monkeys resulted in a significant rise in their SHBG expression levels, reaching a level comparable to that seen in young monkeys. This finding supports the significance of Box A in enhancing molecular aging to approach a juvenile state.

Our study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying primate aging by identifying significant age-related proteomic signatures and demonstrating the mitigating effect of the HMGB1 Box A domain in a non-human primate model. The observed reversal of key age-dysregulated proteins (APOE and SHBG) positions Box A as a promising candidate for therapeutic intervention in age-related diseases. Future research is warranted to elucidate the specific pathways and mechanisms (e.g., epigenetic regulation, DNA repair machinery) through which Box A modulates protein expression and to evaluate its long-term efficacy and safety in clinical settings. This research contributes a crucial foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting healthy aging and improving the quality of life in the elderly population.

Methods

Animal

Three female adult and eight perimenopausal cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) were used in this study. The animals were selected based on chronological age and reproductive history, which were meticulously documented through daily menstrual cycle monitoring for at least one year prior to the study. The selected adult female monkeys (Y) met the criteria of (i) age 5–7 years old and (ii) a history of at least three consecutive regular menstrual cycles (28 ± 4 days). The perimenopausal group (A) met the criteria of (i) age 12–17 years and (ii) exhibiting irregular menstrual cycles—a clinical marker of reproductive senescence—for at least 12 months before the study onset.

To minimize confounding factors related to stress, all monkeys were maintained in a strictly controlled Animal Biosafety Level 1 (ABSL-1) at the National Primate Research Center of Thailand-Chulalongkorn University (NPRCT-CU), an AAALAC International-accredited facility (Accreditation No. 1752). The animals were housed in individual stainless-steel cages in climate-controlled rooms with a 12:12 h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, and a relative humidity of 60 ± 10%. Environmental enrichment was provided to promote natural behavior and psychological well-being. Monkeys had ad libitum access to hyperchlorinated (1 ppm) water and were fed twice daily with standard monkey chow (Perfect Companion Group Co., Ltd., Thailand) in the morning (09:00–11:00 h) and fresh seasonal fruits in the afternoon (14:30–15:30 h). All experimental protocols were strictly conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of NPRCT-CU (Protocol Review No. 2175015).

Preparation of Box A of HMGB1 plasmid

Box A of HMGB1 plasmid (designated Box A plasmid) and a plasmid control (PC), both engineered with a FLAG sequence (Invitrogen, USA), were prepared. The plasmids were coated with Calcium Phosphate nanoparticles for in vivo administration as previously described4.

Experimental design

The perimenopausal monkeys were randomly divided into two subgroups (n = 4 in each group): the control group (A-PC) and the treatment group (A-Box A). The adult monkeys served as the young control group (Y-PC, n = 3). The Y-PC and A-PC groups received intravenous injections of the Plasmid Control (PC), while the A-Box A group received intravenous injections of the Box A plasmid. The administration was performed at a dose of 50 µg/kg BW once per week for a total of eight consecutive weeks. Blood samples were collected at baseline (Week 0, W0) and one week after the final dosage administration (Week 8, W8) for subsequent proteomic analysis (Fig. 1).

Plasma protein extraction

Plasma samples (10 µg) were initially resuspended in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3), and protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Samples were reduced using 10 mM DTT at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by alkylation with 200 mM iodoacetamide (1/10 volume) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Proteins were then enzymatically digested overnight at 37 °C using trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a 1:50 enzyme/protein ratio. The reaction was stopped by adding formic acid to a final concentration of 1% and the resulting peptides were dried completely using a Speed Vacuum.

Label-free liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Dried peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid in H2O for label-free LC-MS/MS quantification. Separation was performed on a Nanoflow liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each sample was run in triplicate to ensure robust quantification. The peptide solution (2 µL containing 50 ng/µL) was injected onto a C18 Acclaim PepMap RSLC (75 μm i.d. × 150 mm) column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) maintained at 40 °C with a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The LC gradient was performed using 0.1% formic acid in 98% water/2%ACN (solution A) and 0.1% formic acid in 100% ACN (solution B) as follows: 2–40% B for 45 min, 45–85% B for 5 min, followed by 15 min with 98% B. Protein identifications were performed by PEAKS Studio Xpro version 10.6 build 20,201,221 (Bioinformatics Solutions, Waterloo, ON, Canada). The data was searched against the SwissProt database of Human. The following parameters were specified for protein identification: digested by trypsin, with two missed cleavages; parent mass error tolerance was 20.0 ppm; fragment mass error tolerance: 0.05 Da, minimum charge: 2, maximum charge: 3, carbamidomethylation, oxidation (M), and acetylation (N-term) were specified as variable modifications.

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed to validate the expression levels of key proteins identified by LC-MS/MS (e.g., Apolipoprotein E and Sex Hormone Binding Globulin). The amount of protein in plasma samples was quantified by the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Ten micrograms of protein samples were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE, and proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes (Pall Corp., Washington, NY, USA), which were blocked with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature with agitation. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, including apolipoprotein E (1: 2,000 dilution; cat.no. ab183597; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and sex hormone binding globulin (1: 1,000 dilution; cat.no. ab126617; Abcam) in TBST buffer containing 3%BSA at 4 °C with agitation overnight. After washing 3 times with TBST buffer, the membranes were incubated with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG and goat anti-rabbit IgG as secondary antibodies (1:5,000 dilution; Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature with agitation. Membranes were then washed 3 times in TBST buffer. The bands were visualized by using ECL Prime (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and detected by GE ImageQuant Las 4000 mini (GE Healthcare). Equal protein loading was verified by staining the membrane with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (CBB) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Bioinformatics

Differential protein expression across Y-PC, A-PC and A-Box A groups were compared using Venn diagram generator software (https://www.interactivenn.net/). Bioinformatics platforms such STRING (https://string-db.org/), a database for predicted signaling networks and protein interactions, were used to evaluate various proteins from the PEAKS Studio Xpro software with the highest confidence (0.9), then represented by a visual network. The UniProt database was utilized for ontological analysis to classify the roles of the dysregulated genes.
