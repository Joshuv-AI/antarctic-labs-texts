Development of a frailty screening tool using components from clinical parameters and frailty criteria for Thai community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Rujapope Sutiwisesak, Chawin Korsirikoon, Saliltip Khamrangsee, Peerapa Techaniyom, Pitaksin Chitta, Busadee Pratumvinit, Atchara Dawangpa, Wongsathit Wongloet, Witchayaporn Kaewboonruang, Prasert Assantachai, Weerasak Muangpaisan, Tanu-udom Maneesing, Orawan Supapueng, Jarupa Soongsathitanon, Tararaj Dharakul, Kobporn Boonnak, John J. Borkowski, Chanachai Sae-Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComprehensive frailty assessment is crucial for longitudinal care in older adults but is often impractical in community, rural, or understaffed settings. This study aimed to determine key identifiers of frailty from different assessment tools and use them to develop a screening tool for frailty status using data from community-dwelling older adults in Bangkok, Thailand.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 848 community-dwelling participants aged above 60 years during October 2022 and November 2023. Comprehensive data, including demographics, socioeconomic status, physical health, lifestyle, and laboratory results were collected. Frailty status was determined using Fried Frailty Phenotype (FFP), the FRAIL scale, Kihon Checklist (KCL), and Thai Frailty Index (TFI). A decision tree classification model was developed using an exhaustive CHAID algorithm, with variables selected via initial screening for multicollinearity and multinomial logistic regression. The dataset was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets for validation.ResultsFrailty status varied across assessment tools and showed some discrepancies. The final three-layer decision tree using five key predictors: 4-meter gait speed lower than 1 m/s, unintentional weight loss in one year more than 5%, experiencing difficulties in chewing hard/solid food, KCL1 (Do you go out by bus or train by yourself? ), and KCL25 (In the last 2 weeks, have you felt tired without a reason? ). Gait speed was identified as the most important predictor. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.751 on the training data and 0.776 on the testing data. It demonstrated strong performance in identifying robust individuals, with a correct classification rate (recall) of 1.000 in the testing dataset, and 0.603 and 0.731, for pre-frail and frail individuals respectively, in the testing dataset. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values were 0.896 for robust, 0.740 for pre-frail, and 0.813 for frail classifications. For practicality, the decision tree can be collapsed down to only 3 questions to identify “non-robust” individuals for comprehensive frailty assessment referral.ConclusionsOur study developed a practical and scalable data-driven decision tree for rapid community-based frailty identification. By combining a simple physical test (gait speed), percentage of weight loss, and a specific KCL item, this tool offers a cost-effective method to classify frailty status, particularly robust individuals. Its simplicity makes it suitable for use even by less experienced staff, supporting early identification and referral of at-risk older adults who would benefit from timely intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07411-z.

Introduction

Frailty is a multidimensional age-related syndrome primarily characterized by a set of conditions, including unintentional weight loss, reduced resilience to various stressors, decreased grip strength, reduced global physical activity, and slower walking speed [1]. The primary causes of frailty include, but are not limited to, advanced age [2], multiple underlying diseases, and unhealthy lifestyles such as lack of exercise, inadequate nutrition, smoking, and alcohol consumption [3]. Frailty often leads to progressive physical and cognitive decline, resulting in increased risk of disability, dependency, and a greater likelihood of hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality. This syndrome significantly impacts the quality of life of older adults and places a considerable burden on caregivers and healthcare systems [4]. However, the reported prevalence of frailty varies considerably depending on the diagnosis criteria applied and socioeconomic status [5], suggesting the heterogeneity not only across different population groups but also in the methods used for frailty assessment.

Although comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) has become the most effective method for both frailty detection and severity assessment, its implementation is often limited by its resource-intensive and time-consuming nature [6]. Among more practical tools, the Fried Frailty Phenotype (FFP) is a widely accepted and popular approach [1]. It incorporates both questionnaires and physical performance measures, evaluating five criteria: (1) unintentional or unexplained weight loss within a year, (2) self-reported exhaustion or fatigue, (3) low grip strength, (4) slow walking speed, and (5) low physical activity in daily life [1]. While this phenotype-based model is practical for clinical and research settings, components such as self-reported fatigue and physical activity rely heavily on subjective recall, which can introduce bias and reduce assessment consistency.

To address population-specific contexts, various tools have been developed. For instance, in Japan, the Kihon Checklist (KCL) is recommended in national guidelines as a standardized frailty screening instrument [7] covering overall bodily function, nutritional status, oral function, outdoor activity, cognitive function, and depressive mood. While these parameters enable a comprehensive assessment of frailty in daily life, the KCL also relies on 25 yes/no questions. Besides validity concerns, this might limit the depth of information gathered and may not fully reflect the individual’s true physical condition. Similarly, the FRAIL scale, comprising five simple items: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illnesses, and weight loss, offers a brief screening method with reasonable predictive validity [8]. It is quick to administer in various clinical and non-clinical settings but may lack the depth needed for nuanced risk stratification. In the Thai context, the Thai Frailty Index (TFI) includes variables related to health conditions, functional limitations, and psychosocial aspects, providing a culturally appropriate robust measure of frailty [9].

While these tools are effective for patient monitoring over time, many are best suited to hospital environments with well-trained staffs. Nevertheless, there remains a need for tools that are optimized for rapid, equipment-free use by non-specialized personnel in specific community settings. A hybrid model that strategically integrates key variables from validated tools like the FFP, KCL, FRAIL scale, and TFI, alongside objective physical performance tests such as the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), functional reach test, sit-and-reach test, and back scratch test, would offer a more comprehensive approach. Additionally, anthropometric and physiological markers such as blood pressure and calf circumference, which reflect cardiovascular and muscular health, could enhance predictive accuracy. Such an integrated approach could facilitate early detection and intervention, even by less experienced personnel, thereby improving outcomes and promoting healthy aging at the population level. A decision tree model is a structured, rule-based predictive tool that partitions data into sequential decision nodes to classify outcomes or estimate values. It is an easy-to-use for non-experts and offers practical interpretation [10], but is also prone to overfitting the noise with limited and uncleaned data [11].

Therefore, this study aims to develop a practical screening tool for frailty detection by integrating questionnaire-based assessments with clinical and physical performance tests. The study was conducted among Bangkok community-dwelling older adults, a population situated in a highly urbanized and demographically diverse setting. Older adults in Bangkok represent a wide range of socioeconomic, educational, and cultural backgrounds. This diversity is reflective of other metropolitan and urban communities in Thailand and across the region. Consequently, the findings obtained from this population are likely to be broadly applicable and support the development of a generalizable assessment tool. Here we show that a small set of key predictors in the decision tree model offers high classification accuracy, particularly for distinguishing robust individuals from those at risk. This integrative model is designed for ease of implementation in a wide range of clinical and community settings, including those with limited resources or non-specialist personnel. The ultimate goal is to support earlier frailty identification and timely geriatric interventions, potentially improving health outcomes and quality of life among older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the Bangkok Noi district, Bangkok, Thailand (Thai Clinical Trials Registry: TCTR20240626002). This cohort enrolled participants during October 2022 and November 2023. All research protocols strictly adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the reporting was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. Before enrollment, all participants provided written informed consent for the collection of blood samples, clinical data, and genetic analysis. The Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, granted ethical approval for this study (COA no. Si 213/2021). To develop a reliable decision tree model for frailty classification, the minimum sample size for each group was set at 60 to overcome the complexity of frailty status determination [12]. Given that the frail group is typically the smallest subgroup, reported as low as 8.3% among the community-dwelling older adults [13], the total required sample size was calculated to be 870 participants, including an additional 20% to account for potential dropout. All participants have lived in 26 communities in the Bangkok Noi district for at least 1 year and were enrolled using consecutive sampling during the community outreach program with a mobile health screening unit established to serve the community through coordination with community leaders. Eligible participants were required to be aged ≥ 60 years and community-dwelling. Twenty-two participants were excluded from the study due to physical disabilities which impaired their ability to participate in the required activities, specifically, any condition that limited the safe and accurate completion of the 4-meter gait speed or other physical performance assessments (e.g., recent major orthopedic surgery, severe stroke residuals), as depicted in Fig. 1.

Data collection

To compare the performance metrics with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of the developed decision tree and the available frailty screening tools for frail status, we used both the frailty phenotype paradigm (FFP and FRAIL scale) and the deficit accumulation paradigm (the KCL and TFI). Firstly, the FFP, which is widely used in epidemiological and clinical research, includes the percentage of unintentional weight loss (normal < 5%), exhaustion evaluated via two questions from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) (normal = 0/2 score) [14], the global physical activity questionnaire (GPAQ) (normal ≥ 600 metabolic equivalent-minutes/week for male and ≥ 368 metabolic equivalent-minutes/week for female) [15], 4-meter gait speed test (normal ≥ 1 m/sec) [16–18], and grip strength. Grip strength was measured by the best of two attempts with the preferred hand using Takei Tkk5401 digital hand grip gauge (Takei, Japan) (normal for females > 18 kg and for males > 28 kg) [19–23]. Secondly, KCL represents the deficit accumulation paradigm, integrating physical, cognitive, social, and psychological deficits by using 25 questions. It has been validated as a predictor of disability and long-term care needs in Asian populations [24, 25]. Thirdly, the FRAIL scale, a short questionnaire comprising five questions similar to the Cardiovascular Health Study frailty criteria and including a history of diagnosed illnesses, was used to assess participants’ frailty status. These questions were: (1) Are you fatigued? (2) Are you unable to walk up one flight of stairs? (3) Are you unable to walk one block? (4) Do you have more than five illnesses? and (5) Have you lost more than 5% of your body weight in the last six months [26, 27]? . This tool is a simplified phenotype-based instrument suitable for community and primary care use. Lastly, TFI is a culturally adapted deficit accumulation instrument based on the Rockwood model [9]. It reflects a broad picture of multidimensional health status relevant to the Thai population. The TFI, comprising 29 of the original 30 variables, excluding sleep quality due to lack of data collection, was employed to assess frailty. To maintain consistency in scoring and comparability with the original TFI scale, we assigned 1 point for the missing sleep quality item to all participants and calculated the total score by dividing the sum. This tool covers health status, comorbidities, medication use, physical activity, cognitive function, and mood [9].

For frailty status designation, participants were considered frail if they met more than three out of the five parameters of the FFP, scored more than 8 out of 25 on the KCL, scored 3–5 for the FRAIL scale, and scored greater than 0.25 for the TFI. Participants who met one or two FFP criteria, scored 4–7 on the KCL, had a FRAIL score of 1–2, and had a TFI score of 0.1–0.25 were classified as pre-frail. Those who scored below these thresholds were considered robust. The frailty status of each participant was classified according to all of the stated criteria. Therefore, each individual was assigned 4 frailty statuses based on 4 criteria. However, for the final process of algorithm generation, frailty status was considered using FFP as the gold standard.

Additional data collected included demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related information, such as blood pressure, clinical laboratory results, and anthropometric measurements. Cognitive function was also assessed by the Thai Mental State Examination (TMSE) [28], with a score of less than 23 indicating cognitive impairment. Screening for major depression was conducted using the Thai Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS-6), a 6-question questionnaire assessing subjects’ mood status, with a score of 2 or more considered positive for depression [29].

Subsequently, physical performance was evaluated using the SPPB, a timed physical performance test comprising a 4-meter casual gait speed, a five-chair stand test, and a standing balance test. SPPB scores are categorized as very low (0–3), low (4–6), moderate (7–9), and high (10–12) physical performance [30]. This assessment was followed by a physical mobility test, which included the functional reach test, sit-and-reach test, and back scratch test. These tests were conducted by trained assessors following standardized operating procedures, and they remained blinded to participant group assignments.

Statistical analyses

Demographic variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous data, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical data. Comparisons among frailty statuses were performed using analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

For decision tree development, all 156 demographic, anthropometric, and laboratory variables were initially screened. Variables with less than 5% data completeness were excluded, resulting in 122 variables. Subsequently, each screened variable was subjected to multinomial logistic regression to identify potential predictor variables for frailty status classification. Variables exhibiting a p-value greater than 0.2 were excluded, resulting in 107 potential variables. This conservative p-value was chosen to minimize the risk of excluding potentially relevant variables whose non-linear interactions would only be captured by the subsequent decision tree algorithm [31–33]. From these 107 variables, 35 were further excluded due to having a variance inflation factor greater than 5 and exhibiting significant multicollinearity [34, 35], yielding a final set of 72 potential variables. These 72 variables were then included in the decision tree development.

The full dataset, comprising 848 participants, was randomly partitioned into two subsets: a training set (n = 678, 80%) for model construction, and a testing set (n = 170, 20%) to evaluate the model’s generalizability and predictive performance. The tree growing method employed was exhaustive chi-squared automatic interaction detector with a maximum depth of 3. The sample size was deemed sufficient for training and validating a parsimonious decision tree model. This adequacy is supported by the final model’s reliance on only five predictors and its shallow depth (three layers), which minimizes the risk of instability and overfitting given the sample size. FFP was used as the dependent variable. The minimum number of cases required for a parent node to split was set at 50, and the minimum number of cases for a child node was set at 25.

Finally, the discriminative ability of the decision tree model for each frailty status was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and the corresponding area under the curve values. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Results

Demographics and prevalence of frailty in Thai community-dwelling older adults

848 participants (266 males and 582 females) were categorized into 336 as robust, 388 as pre-frail, and 124 as frail, according to the FFP (Table 1). Females (15.1%) exhibited a higher prevalence of frailty compared to males (13.5%). In contrast, 39.2% of females and 40.6% of males were classified as robust. Unsurprisingly, the frail group was significantly older and more affected by chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus and arthritis than the pre-frail and robust groups. They perceived their health status and walking stability significantly worse and depended on a caregiver heavier than other groups.

Moreover, 848 participants underwent other frailty assessments: the FRAIL scale, the KCL, and the TFI. When compared to FFP, the FRAIL scale demonstrated concordant classifications for 275 robust participants (82.6%), 178 pre-frail (46.1%), and 30 frail (24.2%). The KCL showed higher agreement, with matched classifications for 282 robust (83.9%), 208 pre-frail (53.6%), and 99 frail individuals (79.8%). In contrast, the TFI showed lower agreement, classifying 193 participants (57.4%) as robust, 231 (59.5%) as pre-frail, and 56 (45.2%) as frail, relative to FFP (Supplementary Table S1).

Development of the predictive decision tree model for frailty classification

The final 72 screened potential variables (Supplementary Table S2) were included in the decision tree development. The list included variables from all the categories collected initially, such as frailty phenotypes, clinical data, assessments of physical performance, neurocognitive and mental evaluations, and laboratory results. Certain laboratory markers (e.g., creatinine, triglyceride, red cell distribution width) were excluded due to either failed initial variable screening or exhibiting high multicollinearity with other clinical or hematologic parameters. Although five lab results (glucose, eGFR, direct LDL cholesterol, albumin, and absolute basophil) passed initial screening and were available for the decision tree algorithm, they were not selected. The final decision tree model, as presented in Fig. 2, consisted of three layers included 13 nodes, with 7 terminal nodes, and ultimately utilized five significant predictors for its splits: 4-meter gait speed less than 1 m/s, weight loss more than 5% in one year, experiencing difficulties in chewing hard/solid food, KCL1 (Do you go out by bus or train by yourself? ), and KCL25 (In the last 2 weeks, have you felt tired without a reason? ).

To facilitate interpretation, the seven end-of-branch nodes identified in the decision tree (Fig. 2) are mapped into a three-tier frailty status. Individuals in Node 7 are categorized as robust (Green), requiring only annual re-screening. The remaining Nodes are categorized as either pre-frail (Orange) or frail (Red). The visual flowchart of this decision tree, along with user’s instructions for its application, are available in Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Material S1, respectively.

Overall classification performance of the developed decision tree

The classification performance of the developed decision tree model was evaluated against the FFP criteria using the testing dataset (N = 170). As shown in the Table 2, The three-tier model demonstrated an overall accuracy of 77.1% (95% CI: 70.7–83.3%). The agreement between the decision tree classifications and the FFP criteria was further assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.619 (95% CI: 0.514–0.724), indicating substantial agreement according to standard clinical benchmarks. Notably, the model achieved high precision in identifying robust individuals, with no frail individuals misclassified as robust. Discriminative power

The discriminative performance of the decision tree model for each frailty status is presented, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AU-ROC) values reported along with their 95% CI and asymptotic significance. For the robust classification, the model demonstrated good discriminative performance with an AU-ROC of 89.6% (95% CI: 87.5–91.8%, p < 0.001). The AU-ROCs for the pre-frail and frail classifications were 74.0% (95% CI: 70.5–77.5%, p < 0.001) and 81.3% (95% CI: 76.5–86.2%, p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 3).

Simplified decision tree for generalized usage

Despite better performance in categorizing frailty status of our decision tree model, we acknowledged that there is a design limitation from analyzing a cross-sectional data from a single exposure. Therefore, it would work best for point-of-care determination of the status, rather than provide a judgement for downstream clinical pathway or information for follow-up visits. Since the model has ability to recall robust individuals without those from other frailty statuses if the screening resulted in Node 7 (Fig. 2), we decided to group the remaining end-of-branch nodes as “non-robust” individuals (Fig. 4A). The result of the new grouping suggested that 4 end-of-branch nodes (Node 9–12) were originated from a common Node 2. If the decision tree was collapsed down to make Node 2 as end-of-branch node for another non-robust group, the model contained simple 3 pass/fail parameters, including 4-metre gait speed, weight loss and feeling of unexplained fatigue (Fig. 4B). This simplified model indicated “robust” if all of them were passed, while failing any of them indicated “non-robust”.

To further evaluate the clinical utility of the tool across different screening contexts, the decision tree outputs were collapsed into two binary (2 × 2) classification models: (1) a robust vs. non-robust model for community-based triage and (2) a frail vs. not-frail model for clinical diagnostic screening (Table 3). The robust vs. non-robust model, designed to identify all individuals potentially at risk (pre-frail or frail), demonstrated high screening performance with a sensitivity of 87.5% (95% CI: 81.1–93.9%) and a specificity of 89.4% (95% CI: 81.9–96.9%). This model yielded a Youden’s Index of 0.77 and an AU-ROC of 88.5% (95% CI: 81.5–95.5%). In contrast, the not-frail vs. frail model focused on the specific identification of clinically frail individuals. While this model exhibited a lower sensitivity of 46.2% (95% CI: 28.8–64.5%), it demonstrated an exceptionally high specificity of 96.5% (95% CI: 92.1–98.5%) and a low false-positive rate of 3.5%. This model achieved a Youden’s Index of 0.43 and an AU-ROC of 71.4% (95% CI: 61.6–81.0%). These results suggest that while the tool is conservative in its specific diagnosis of frailty, it serves as a highly reliable rule-out mechanism, ensuring that robust and pre-frail individuals are not unnecessarily included for specialized geriatric resources.

Discussion

In this study, we identified the factors that are associated with frailty classification using a multiple logistic regression model and decision tree algorithms. To simplify the frailty screening tool for use in community settings, we identified the five most influential and parsimonious variables for robust classification. The final decision tree model leverages these predictors: weight loss greater than 5% in 1 year, a critical indicator of physiological decline [36] typically ascertained through measurements obtained from a standard commercialized weighing scale; 4-meter walking speed slower than 1 m/sec, which emerged as the root node and thus the most important predictor in the tree, representing a single, objective, and readily measurable physical performance test requiring only a timer and marked space [37]; experiencing difficulties in chewing hard/solid food, combining the important aspects of both muscle strength and nutritional status, which is often linked to frailty progression [38, 39]; and two highly discriminating items from the KCL, namely, KCL1 (“Do you go out by bus or train by yourself?”, which is a proxy for complex Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), reflecting independence in mobility and social participation [40]) and KCL25 (“In the last 2 weeks, have you felt tired without a reason?”, which captures the core frailty criterion of subjective exhaustion/fatigue [25]). Further simplification of the model resulted in only 3 important parameters: gait speed, weight loss and feeling of fatigue. Gait speed is recognized as a “sixth vital sign” and an integrated measure of physiologic reserve, reflecting combined function across the musculoskeletal and both central and peripheral nervous systems [41]. Its dominance as the initial split reinforces its primacy as the most powerful single predictor of frailty and related adverse outcomes.

While the classification tree is not intended to replace the CGA, the tool developed in this study offers a practical alternative for identifying frailty status, particularly in community-based or resource-limited settings where CGA may not be feasible. Some parameters within CGA require the expertise of medical personnel for evaluation or rely on extensive questionnaires and specialized physical performance tests that demand dedicated equipment and spaces. This can affect the categorization of elderly individuals, particularly in non-specialized or community settings. Our approach began with a comprehensive pool of 156 demographic, anthropometric, physical, and laboratory variables. Through a rigorous, multi-stage statistical refinement process, including initial data completeness checks, multinomial logistic regression for predictor identification [42], and subsequent multicollinearity analysis, we narrowed down to 72 potential variables for the decision tree development. This algorithmic selection process ensures that the chosen variables are not only clinically relevant (drawn from established measures like KCL and core frailty criteria) but also empirically validated for their predictive power and efficiency when combined in the decision tree’s structure. The TFI was developed through a standardized translation and validation process [9], while physical performance measures was not incorporated. This may limit its ability to fully capture functional aspects of frailty [43], despite it demonstrated strong psychometric properties. By relying on a rigorously selected, minimal set of highly predictive variables, our tool enables targeted referral for more comprehensive assessment (using established validated tools or CGA) and timely initiation of tailored interventions of individual at risk. Furthermore, the model’s performance exhibited substantial agreement with the FFP. Compared to the FRAIL scale, the KCL, and the TFI, our tool demonstrated the higher overall classification accuracy, outperforming other tests.

Even though our model outperformed other frailty screening, it suffered from design limitations where the initial data was collected from a single exposure leading to the lack of longitudinal perspective and, by the nature of the decision made from the model, less appropriate for follow-up assessment. We, then, further simplified the model down to 3 pass/fail indicators, including gait speed, weight loss and feeling of fatigue. Passing all indicators categorized individual as firmly “robust”, while failing any of them put the individual at-risk for “non-robust” status, which warrant further comprehensive assessment from the geriatricians. Since the nature of the simplified model was simple pass/fail, they can be used as a key indicator for promoting public awareness of their own frailty status. Development of test sites for gait speed measurement and a system for recording body weight would enable community-dwelling older adults to monitor their simple frailty status, reducing the workload of medical personnel, as only those with “non-robust” status would enter the geriatric care system for detailed assessment whether they are pre-frail or frail status.

Taken together, the primary utility of our model lies in its function as a rapid community-based triage mechanism, rather than a definitive diagnostic tool. We acknowledge that the model demonstrates a relatively low sensitivity (46.2%) when identify frail individuals, which warrants careful consideration in clinical usage. However, in large-scale community screenings, the burden of false positives can overwhelm specialized geriatric services. Our tool addresses this through its exceptionally high specificity for frailty (96.5%) and its 100% recall for robust individuals. This indicates a highly reliable rule-out mechanism; it ensures that nearly all of robust older adults are correctly identified and kept out of the medicalized system, thereby preserving specialized resources for those who require them. Furthermore, while isolated frailty sensitivity is modest, the tool’s sensitivity for detecting non-robust status (pre-frail and frail combined) is significantly higher (87.5%). This represents a strategic trade-off where the model sacrifices some diagnostic sensitivity for isolated frailty in exchange for the high triage efficiency required to manage large populations. This is critically important because the pre-frail stage is widely recognized as a dynamic state [44] where timely interventions, such as dietary or exercise programs, can effectively prevent progression to full frailty [45] or even facilitate reversal to a robust state [46–48]. By identifying the broader non-robust spectrum with high accuracy, our decision tree ensures that individuals who could benefit most from early intervention are identified for further assessment, ultimately reducing future healthcare burdens and improving long-term outcomes.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the few to use four different validated frailty assessment tools: FFP, the FRAIL scale, the KCL, and the TFI. Using these tools allowed us to capture a more comprehensive and multidimensional picture of frailty from various clinical and functional perspectives. Despite its strengths, our tool has certain limitations. It was specifically designed for rapid screening in community settings and is not intended to replace CGA. As such, while it offers practical value for early identification of at-risk individuals, it should be complemented by thorough clinical assessments for diagnostic confirmation and care planning. Additionally, the implementation of this tool requires a minimum of 4 m of unobstructed space to conduct the gait speed assessment accurately, which may limit its use in locations with space constraints. Moreover, the study’s sample consisted primarily of older adults residing in Bangkok, the capital and a central urban area of Thailand. This urban setting is characterized by comparatively greater access to healthcare services, specialized geriatric care, and health promotion programs. As a result, the health status and healthcare-seeking behaviors of participants in this region may differ significantly from those in rural or less-developed provinces, where resources, accessibility, and health system infrastructure may be more limited. This geographical context should be taken into account when interpreting the generalizability of the findings to the broader older Thai population. However, future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the instrument’s predictive validity for adverse outcomes such as hospitalization, disability, and mortality. These follow-up investigations will enable us to determine whether the decision tree can accurately identify individuals at heightened risk and track changes in frailty status over time. In addition, incorporating repeated assessments will allow us to examine the tool’s sensitivity to change, responsiveness to interventions, and stability across different clinical and community settings.

Conclusion

Increasing frailty prevalence demands accessible, rapid, self-monitored screening. Our data-driven decision tree offers a practical and scalable solution for community-based frailty identification. By rigorously selecting five, later reduced to three, key predictors, including measured weight loss, gait speed, and specific KCL items, our tool efficiently flags individuals at risk. It demonstrates good agreement with the FFP and is particularly effective in identifying pre-frail individuals, a crucial stage at which timely intervention can prevent further decline. Beyond its statistical performance, the tool offers practical advantages that support rapid and efficient risk identification in real-world community settings. This streamlined approach has the potential to facilitate more proactive and targeted geriatric care.
