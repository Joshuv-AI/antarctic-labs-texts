Effect of ultrasound on mass transfer during vacuum impregnation and selected quality parameters of products: A case study of carrots
Authors: Elżbieta Radziejewska-Kubzdela, Justyna Szadzińska, Róża Biegańska-Marecik, Tomasz Spiżewski, Dominik Mierzwa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Ascorbic acid, Daucus carota, Polyphenol, Carotenoid, Antioxidant activity, Structure change

Introduction

Vacuum impregnation (VI) is a well-established technique in the food industry. It is used to modify the composition of food products, leading to a change in their properties, for example, the texture, flavor, and nutritional value of products (e.g., during the production of functional foods) [1]. Impregnation is also used to introduce compounds that ease sequent processing, for example, the introduction of cryoprotectants prior to freezing.

The impregnation process consists of three stages: vacuum, aeration, and relaxation. The vacuum stage involves creating a negative, relative pressure (absolute pressure from 5 to 10 kPa) above the solution containing the samples, resulting in the escape of native fluids from within the tissue of the sample into the solution [2]. A slight deformation of the capillaries and pores also occurs in the tissue. Subsequently, in the aeration (restoration) stage, the pressure above the solution is restored to the ambient pressure. The authors’ previous studies [3] indicate that a major impregnation event may occur during this period, but this depends on the conditions and material (data not published). After ambient pressure is achieved, the samples remain in the solution for the relaxation stage (at atmospheric pressure). This is done to fully fill the capillaries and pores with the solution. Fito et al. [4] determined that mass exchange during VI occurs through two mechanisms, hydrodynamic (HDM) and deformation-relaxation (DRP). Both phenomena may be influenced by ultrasound (US).

The efficiency of the impregnation process depends both on the process’s parameters (pressure, time) and the properties of the raw material itself (composition, porosity, structure) [1]. Low absolute pressure or high tissue porosity allows for relatively easy impregnation [5]; the challenge is to process nonporous (<10% porosity) tissue under moderate or high absolute pressures over a reasonable time period. Implementation of processes under the lowest possible vacuum is important primarily from the economic point of view as it means lower energy consumption. The application of ultrasound may also increase the impregnation of low porous materials at high absolute pressures. The positive effect of these mechanical waves on mass (and heat) transport processes is well documented in the literature in relation to osmotic dehydration [6], [7], [8], [9], drying [9], [10], [11], [12], and other unit operations [13].

Ultrasound-assisted vacuum impregnation (UVI) has not been extensively studied. The application of ultrasound has mainly been studied at atmospheric pressure as a pretreatment before VI or during the relaxation stage. The use of ultrasound during the pretreatment process aims to modify the structure of the raw material (increasing the porosity and loosening the tissue) to facilitate the introduction of the impregnating solution. The application of ultrasound during the relaxation stage results in an intensification of the mass transfer. Comandini et al. [14] used ultrasound at the relaxation stage for apple samples (cv. Golden Delicious) in order to impregnate them with an aroma. However, the effect they recorded was no better than the result for vacuum impregnation without ultrasound enhancement. Ultrasound was applied at the pre-treatment stage by Mashkour et al. [15] to fortify potatoes with iron; by Zheng and Moreira [16] to impregnate potato samples with magnesium ions; and by Batista de Medeiros et al. [17] to infuse mangoes with grape-residue polyphenols. A positive effect was noted for ultrasonic pretreatment before VI in the case of the potatoes, however, this effect was not found in the mangoes.

Single reports concerning research on using ultrasound during the whole vacuum impregnation process can be found. These works concern products of both animal [18], [19] and plant origin. In Yılmaz and Bilek’s work [20], apple tissue was fortified with calcium and black carrot phenolics through VI with ultrasound enhancement (96–198 W, 35 kHz). It was found that the application of ultrasound during impregnation led to increases in calcium, total phenolics, flavonoid, and anthocyanin content as well as in the antioxidant capacity of the apple discs (compared to unsupported vacuum impregnation). Ultrasound-assisted vacuum impregnation was also used in the production of phenolic-enriched mushroom powder [21]. The authors found that samples obtained from ultrasonically assisted processes exhibited the highest total phenolic and total flavonoid content, along with the highest antioxidant capacity values (compared to regular VI and raw materials). Moreover, UVI-processed samples dried much faster, and achieved a higher effective diffusion coefficient and lower activation energy. This confirms that ultrasound has a positive impact not only on the impregnation process but also on the internal structure of the material. A work by Dinçer [22] concerned the enrichment of apples (cv. Granny Smith) with hibiscus anthocyanins. Although, the paper focuses mainly on the mathematical modelling of the process, experimental research confirmed that ultrasound (40 kHz, 31 W/dm3) had a positive influence on the concentration of anthocyanins from hibiscus extract. The qualitative effects depended both on the pressure and length of time of impregnation. The highest flavonoid content value was observed for the longest (30 min) US-assisted processing time at the lowest (10 kPa) pressure.

Among the above-mentioned works, only Yılmaz and Bilek [20] investigated the effects of using US at different stages of the vacuum impregnation (at the vacuum and aeration stages). They noted the beneficial effect of using US in both steps. Research on the application of ultrasound at various stages of VI (vacuum, aeration, and relaxation) was also conducted by our research group. These works concerned the impregnation of cranberries and potatoes with ascorbic acid. In the case of the cranberries, the best effect was obtained using US at the relaxation stage, while for potato it was found to be during whole process [3], [23].

The aim of this study was to analyze the influence of ultrasound on the effectiveness of vacuum impregnation at a relatively low vacuum (30 kPa) as applied to a raw material (carrot: porosity, ca. 4%) that is difficult to impregnate. The research hypothesis is as follows: ultrasound positively influences the effectiveness of vacuum impregnation, but the stage (period) at which it is applied influences the quantitative effects. Ascorbic acid was used as the bioactive compound to be introduced into the tissue. The impregnation was carried out in a ternary solution of ascorbic acid (effectiveness marker), citric acid (oxidation stabilizer), and sucrose (isotonic conditions marker). The effectiveness of the process (ascorbic acid gain and impregnation degree) and selected quality parameters, such as color, ion leakage, pH value, soluble solid, carotenoid, and polyphenol content, were analyzed as well as antioxidant activity.

Study methodology

Material

The research was carried out on carrot (Daucus carota), cv. Baltimore F1. The carrot roots were supplied by the Department of Vegetable Crops, Poznan University of Life Sciences in Poznań (Poland, 52°40′85.5″N 16°85′91.8″E). The Baltimore F1 is an early-cropping variety of the Berlicum type, intended for both direct consumption and industrial use. After harvesting, the roots were stored in cooled conditions (temperature 2 °C, humidity 95%). Before processing, the carrot roots were peeled and cut into slices 15 mm thick using an RG100 industrial cutter produced by Hällde (Kista, Sweden). Each impregnation process was performed on 400 g of raw material.

Impregnation procedure

The vacuum impregnation processes were carried out in an IS-PP apparatus (INTERSONIC, Olsztyn, Poland), whose scheme is presented in Fig. 1.

Schematic of the impregnation apparatus [24]. 1 & 4 - valves, 2 - PT100 temperature probe, 3 - vacuum gauge, 5 - lid, 6 - vacuum chamber, 7 - water jacket, 8 - vacuum pump, 9 - ultrasonic transducers.

Impregnation was carried out in an aqueous ternary solution composed of analytically pure ascorbic acid (0.5% w/w), citric acid (0.5% w/w), and sucrose (7% w/w) (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland). The effective concentration of the solution was 8% (w/w), which was about equal to the carrots’ soluble solid content (SSC) as measured using a PAL-α digital refractometer (ATAGO, Saitama, Japan). During each process, 3 kg of solution was used, so the ratio of the weight of the raw material to the solution was 1:7.5. The temperature of the solution was measured using a PT100 sensor (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), and kept constant at approximately 25 °C using a water jacket (7).

Schematic presentation of the impregnation processes that were conducted.

The carrot slices were immersed in the impregnating solution, and then a preset vacuum was created in the chamber. The set pressure was maintained for 30 min (vacuum period), and then the chamber was gradually aerated. The aeration time was the same in each case and amounted to about 10 min. After the pressures (inside and outside the chamber) had equalized, the slices were left in the solution for a 30-minute relaxation period. After this time, the samples were removed, drained, and divided into two parts. The first part was used in the measurement of color, texture, ion leakage, pH value, and soluble solid content, while the second one was frozen at −50 °C for further chemical analysis.

To analyze the influence of ultrasound on the effectiveness of the impregnation, several test variations were carried out in which ultrasound was applied to individual stages of the vacuum impregnation (vacuum, aeration, and relaxation), and also throughout the whole process (continuously). Mechanical waves at a frequency of 35 kHz and an effective power of 240 W (determined calorimetrically [25]), were generated by transducers attached to the bottom of the impregnation chamber and transmitted directly into the solution holding the samples. A schematic of the conducted trials is presented in Fig. 2. In the figure, the ultrasonic enhancement of a given impregnation phase is marked by the blue color (as shown in the legend).

Determination of ascorbic acid content (AAC) and impregnation degree (ID)

The effectiveness of the vacuum impregnation was assessed on the basis of ascorbic acid content (AAC) and impregnation degree (ID). The ascorbic acid extraction was carried out using 1 % m-HPO3 [26]. The samples were analyzed using an LC Agilent Technologies 1200 Rapid Resolution system (Waldbronn, Germany), equipped with a Zorbax SB-C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm; 5 µm) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mobile phases used a methanol solution (phase A) and a 0.005 mol/L KH2PO4 solution (phase B). To detect the ascorbic acid, a gradient of methanol (solvent A) (Sigma Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) per 0.005 mol/L KH2PO4 and pH 2.6 (solvent B) (Sigma Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan) was used according to the following program: linear increment starting with solvent A at 5% to 22% in 6 min, then a return to the initial conditions within the next 9 min, with a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min [27]. The eluate was detected using a UV–Vis detector set to 245 nm. The ascorbic acid was identified by comparing the retention time with that of an ascorbic acid standard (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Determination of the color change

The color of both the raw and impregnated samples was measured using a Konica Minolta CR400 colorimeter (Tokyo, Japan) and expressed in the CIELAB color space. The CR400 device was equipped with a CIE D65 illuminant and CIE 2° standard observer. Before measurement, 10 slices were ground in an IKA A11 Basic (Staufen, Germany) laboratory mill. Measurement of the pH and soluble solid content

An HI 221 pH-meter (Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA), calibrated with pH buffer 4.0 and 7.0 solutions, was used to make the pH measurements. Carrot slices were homogenized before measurement using an IKA-Werke homogenizer (Staufen im Breisgau, Germany). The assay was performed by directly immersing the electrode in the sample. The total soluble solid content was determined at 20 °C using an HI 96801 digital refractometer (Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA) by placing a few drops of liquid squeezed from the homogenized sample onto the prism of the refractometer.

Texture profile analysis

Texture parameters, such as the hardness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness of the fresh and processed carrot, were examined using a model TA‐TXplus texture meter equipped with a 50 kg load-cell (Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK). A “two bite” test procedure, known as texture profile analysis (TPA), was performed [28], in which samples were compressed to 20% of their original height. Pre-test, test, and post-test speeds were set at 2 mm/s, and the trigger force was 5.0 g. The tests were performed on a minimum of 15 samples.

Determination of ion leakage (IL)

Ion leakage was investigated using the conductometric method presented by Schössler et al. [29], with minor modifications. Deionized water (100 g) was poured into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask in which a single carrot slice was then placed. The flask was closed and left for one hour. After this time, the conductivity of the water was measured using a conductometer CC-401 produced by Elmetron (Zabrze, Poland). The conductivity was measured continuously for 120 s at a sampling frequency of 1 Hz. This measurement was carried out for fresh (untreated), impregnated, and boiled (for one hour) samples. Determination of structure forming compounds

The neutral detergent fiber (NDF) content, consisting of acid detergent fiber (ADF) and acid detergent lignin (ADL), was determined using the detergent method described by Dziedzic et al. [30], and van Soest and Wine [31]. Hemicellulose content was calculated as the difference between NDF and ADF, while the cellulose content was calculated as the difference between ADF and ADL. Analyses were conducted using a Fibertec System M 1,020,229 (Foss, Sweden).

Determination of carotenoid content

The determination of carotenoid content using high performance liquid column chromatography (HPLC) was based on a method described by de Sá and Rodriguez-Amaya [32]. To extract the carotenoids from the carrot, the sample was subjected to three extractions using acetone. This was done by homogenizing and shaking the homogenates in the dark using a mechanical shaker. After each extraction, the samples were centrifuged (5,200 rpm, 15 min, MPW-351RW, Poland), and the supernatants combined. The obtained extracts were evaporated in a vacuum evaporator. Carotenoid content was determined using the same equipment as used to determine the ascorbic acid content. The mobile phase constituted acetonitrile containing 0.05% triethylamine (solvent A) and a solution of methanol and ethyl acetate mixed at a ratio of 275:225 (solvent B). The following solvent gradient was used: 95% A for 0–20 min, 60% A for 20–55 min. The separation was carried out at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min for 55 min at room temperature. The injection volume per column was 10 μL. The detection was carried out within the range of 400 to 600 nm, reading at 445 nm. The results were calculated based on a β-carotene external standard. The results are given in μg/g DM of sample.

Phenolic compound determination

Polyphenol extraction was conducted according to a procedure described by Vallejo et al. [33]. The phenolic profile was determined by HPLC using an LC Agilent Technologies 1200 Rapid Resolution system, equipped with a UV–Vis detector (DAD 1260) (Agilent Technologies 1200 Rapid Resolution, Germany) and a Zorbax SB-C18 column, with a grain size of 5 μm and dimensions of 4.6 × 150 mm (Agilent Technology Inc., USA). Separation was performed in a reverse phase system using gradient elution. The mobile phase constituted 60 g/L acetic acid in 2 mmol sodium acetate solution (solvent A) and 100% acetonitrile (solvent B) [34]. The system was run with the following gradient program: 0–15% B for 15 min, 15–30% B for 10 min, 30–50% B for 5 min, and 50–100% B for 5 min, pre-run to the initial composition for 5 min. The flow rate was 1 mL/min and the total run time was 40 min. The phenols were quantified at 320 nm, and chlorogenic acid was used as the standard. The results were expressed in mg/100 g DM.

Antioxidant activity determination

The 2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) (ABTS) radical monocation scavenging capacity was determined following a procedure described by Re et al. [35]. ABTS. + was produced by reacting 0.007 mol/L ABTS water solution with 0.002 mol/L potassium persulphate. Phenolic extract (50 μL) was mixed with 5 mL diluted ABTS. + solution and it absorption was measured at 734 nm after 6 min at 30 °C. Trolox (Sigma Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) was used as the standard and the capacity for free radical scavenging was expressed as micromoles of Trolox per 1 g DM.

Statistical analysis

The data presented mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc mean comparisons and Pearson’s correlation were performed. Statistically significant differences at a level of p < 0.05 are marked with different letters. All calculations were performed using Statistica ver. 12 software produced by StatSoft (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results and discussion

Effectiveness of the process

The effectiveness of the process was assessed on the basis of the increase in the marker compound (ascorbic acid) and the degree of impregnation (ID), calculated according to equation (2). In Fig. 3 are presented the values for both parameters for each particular impregnation process.

Ascorbic acid gain (a) and impregnation degree - ID (b). The different letters above the bars indicate a significant difference at p < 0.05 for the ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test mean comparisons.

The use of ultrasound during vacuum impregnation caused a significant increase in the carrot tissue’s ascorbic acid content. The gain, calculated in comparison to impregnation without US assistance, varied, and depended on the stage in which the US was applied. It can be observed that the key factor influencing the effectiveness of ultrasound is the pressure. For the process in which the US support was applied at atmospheric pressure (UVI–2), the marker’s levels were higher than in the case where ultrasound was used in a vacuum (UVI–1): 22% and 9% gain, respectively. This may be related to the occurrence of various types of cavitation. When ultrasound was applied under reduced pressure (UVI–1), the surface-tension forces holding the vapor–gas bubbles were strong enough to prevent them from imploding. As a result, the so called stable cavitation (without the implosion of vapor–gas bubbles) was dominant and the impact of the ultrasound on mass transfer was smaller. When ultrasound was applied at atmospheric pressure (UVI–2), the increase in pressure above the solution meant that the surface-tension forces were not strong enough to maintain the vapor–gas bubbles permanently, and implosion occurred. This is the second type of cavitation, called transient cavitation, which is accompanied by much greater energy effects and leads to a much greater intensification of mass exchange [36]. This assumption is confirmed by results obtained by Miano et al. [37], who found a 27% reduction in the cavitation level when ultrasound was used for celery processing in a vacuum compared to the process conducted at atmospheric pressure. The hypothesis concerning different types of cavitation may also confirm the results obtained in the UVI–4 process, in which ultrasound was used during aeration (pressure equalization). The pressure during the aeration stage changes over time, therefore, the nature of the cavitation may also change. At the beginning, stable cavitation may have dominated, but as the pressure increased, it gradually transformed into transient cavitation. Thus, the increase in ascorbic acid content (ca. 17%) was higher than in the UVI–1 process but lower than in UVI–2, even though both of these processes were statistically similar.

Exposure time also influenced the effectiveness of ultrasonic assistance. In the UVI–3 process, ultrasound was used for the entire duration, which resulted in a greater than 60% increase in ascorbic acid content in the tissue. In this process, the impact of ultrasound was much more complex because both types of cavitation occurred. As a result, a synergistic ultrasound effect could be observed. The sum of the gains for the UVI–1, 2, and 4 processes was lower than the value observed for UVI–3. This is a relatively rare and difficult to explain phenomenon. Yilmaz and Bilek [20] also noted higher tissue impregnation efficiency for apple with black carrot juice concentrate using US during the entire process compared to US applied only at the vacuum or aeration stages.

Impregnation degree (ID) is a parameter that describes the relative increase in tissue mass during the impregnation process. It usually correlates with the content of the introduced component. The higher the mass gain after impregnation (ID), the higher the content of the compound that was introduced into the tissue using the solution. For the UVI–1, UVI–2, and UVI–4 processes, such a relationship can actually be observed (Fig. 3b). Only the UVI–3 process deviates from this relationship. This may be due to the long-term impact of ultrasound on the plant’s tissue and the leaching of its components. It may also result from more destructive changes occurring, for example, imploding vapor–gas bubbles that increase surface erosion and the fragmentation of the tissue’s surface. This effect was indicated by Awad et al. [38] when using US during the extraction of biologically active compounds. The use of US during VI also intensifies the mass exchange, causing not only more effective impregnation, but also the outflow of compounds from the tissue into the solution, as reported by Yimaz and Bilek [20].

Influence of ultrasound on the product’s properties

The pH values and soluble solid content in the material are presented in Fig. 4.

pH value (a) and soluble solid content - SSC (b). The different letters above the bars indicate a significant difference at p<0.05 for the ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test mean comparisons.

A significant decrease in pH compared to the raw material was found in all the tested samples. The application of ultrasound during vacuum impregnation resulted in a significant decrease in pH compared to VI only (Fig. 4a). Only in the case of UVI–4 was this effect not observed. The citric acid present in the impregnating solution was the main reason for the decrease in the pH value. In the case of citric acid, however, the impregnation may have been lower than for ascorbic acid due to this compound’s higher molecular weight. This may be one of the factors responsible for cases where penetration is difficult, especially where the duration of ultrasound use is short (10 min), as in the UVI–4 process. The relationship between the molecular weight of the compounds used in the impregnating solution and tissue penetration rate was discussed by Zhao and Xie [2].

Ultrasound had no significant influence on soluble solid content compared to VI. The lack of change in the SSC may have been related to the use of an impregnating solution that was close to isotonic with the tissue. Martínez-Monzó et al. [39] indicated that the use of such a solution allows the cells’ turgor to remain unchanged. A significant decrease in SSC was only found in the case of UVI–3. This may imply that there was some structural damage during the lengthy application of ultrasound during vacuum impregnation, which may have resulted in leakage of the soluble solid. This is in close agreement with the low ID observed (Fig. 3b).

Ion leakage (IL) and color change (dE) for the material processed in each impregnation procedure are presented in Fig. 5.

Ion leakage - IL (a) and relative color change - dE (b). The different letters above the bars indicate a significant difference at p < 0.05 for the ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test mean comparisons.

The IL value is related to the number of ions leached from the tissue of the material after the impregnation process. This value often correlates with structural changes and is used as a structure health predictor [29]. A high IL value indicates significant structural changes, for instance, resulting from an increase in the permeability of cell membranes or damage to cells. This type of effect due to ultrasound use on plant tissue was indicated by Kaderides et al. [40].

As can be seen, the use of ultrasound caused a slight increase in this parameter’s value compared to the unassisted process (VI); however, only two changes are statistically significant. The highest IL value was noted for the UVI–3 process, which confirms the hypothesis that significant structural changes can be caused by long-term exposure to ultrasound. This observation also correlates with an increase in ID (Fig. 3b) and a decrease in SSC (Fig. 4b). A statistically significant change (compared to VI) was also observed for the UVI–4 process. Taking into account the short exposure time to the ultrasound and the variable pressure (occurrence of stable and transient cavitation), it should be assumed that the high IL value resulted from intensive surface saturation of the tissues rather than from structural changes. The solution present in the surface layers was easily washed out during the conductivity study and gave a high concentration of ions.

Regarding the color of the material (Fig. 5b), it can be stated that impregnation did not significantly change the color of the material. For all processes, dE was below 4, which is considered to be the threshold value for regular observers [41]. The high dE value for UVI–3 may have been associated with disruption to the carrot’s tissue.

The texture of the material is an important parameter in determining the physical properties of the product. Many processing methods cause textural changes, which are sometimes negative. The presented results (Table1) confirm that ultrasound does not significantly affect the texture of the impregnated product. Statistically significant differences were only observed in the case of cohesiveness, where the lowest measurement was recorded for the UVI–3 process, which may also confirm the postulated hypothesis concerning structural changes. Literature data show that the use of ultrasound for the treatment of plant tissue can lead to an increase in the permeability of cell membranes or damage to cells. These changes are related to the sponge effect and the cavitation phenomenon that occur during tissue sonication [40]. According to Lorimer and Mason [42], ultrasound can also degrade the polysaccharides responsible for cell cohesion in the tissue. Changes in the structure of celery tissue during vacuum impregnation using US only, was studied by Miano et al. [37]. However, they did not report any changes in the tissue’s structure as a result of this process. This may have resulted from the short processing time (5 min) used in this study.

Comparing the content of structure-forming compounds in the raw material and the tested samples, it was found that vacuum impregnation caused a significant decrease in hemicellulose and pectin content. The use of ultrasound during the vacuum process also resulted in a significant decrease in the cellulose content in those samples treated with ultrasound during the relaxation stage (UVI–2), aeration stage (UVI–4), and during the entire process (UVI–3). Among the above-mentioned samples, the highest cellulose content was recorded for material obtained from the process with the longest duration of ultrasonic application (UVI–3). This may have been due to the overlapping of the degrading effect and the better extractability. On the other hand, for hemicellulose, only the degrading effect was noted in the material obtained from UVI–3. In the case of pectins, a significant decrease in content was found in the sample obtained from UVI–1. The use of ultrasound had no significant effect on the lignin content.

The degrading effect was visible in hemicellulose and cellulose during the stages where transient cavitation was dominant (aeration, relaxation). The decrease in pectin content, on the other hand, was related more to the application of ultrasound during the vacuum stage, where stable cavitation mainly seemed to occur. To explain the mechanism behind these changes will require further research. Ultrasound’s possible degrading effect on polysaccharides is indicated in studies by Lorimer and Mason [42], among others. They found that, as a result of the application of ultrasound, free radicals can form, which, through their further recombination and termination reactions, can cause the cleavage of the main and side polysaccharide chains.

Hemicelluloses and pectins are responsible for the plasticity and stretching in tissue, in contrast to cellulose, which gives it rigidity [43]. Thus, a reduction in hemicellulose and pectin content could be associated with a decrease in cohesiveness as a result of unassisted vacuum impregnation, and with an increase in this effect in the UVI–3 process (a significant decrease in hemicellulose content). The loss of cohesion in plant tissue as a result of the cavitation or sponge effect was also stated in studies by Nowacka et al. [44].

The influence of the applied treatment on the content of selected bioactive compounds was also determined for the tested samples. It was found that vacuum impregnation, both unassisted and assisted by ultrasound, caused about a 40% to 50% loss in carotenoid content compared to the raw material (Table3). These losses may have been caused by the partial loosening of the tissue, which may be indicated by the results of the tests for cohesiveness and content of structure-forming compounds. This loosening can lead to the oxidation or leakage of the tested compounds into the solution. In this study, however, no effect was found on the total carotenoid content due to the use of ultrasound. The influence of ultrasound was noted only in the case of lutein. A significant decrease in its content was found in samples obtained as a result of UVI–1 and UVI–3. Research showed that the degrading effect may be the result of free-radical reactions being initiated. Zhao et al. [45] and Sony et al. [46], in studying the effect of ultrasound on the extraction of astaxanthin and lutein (respectively), found that using too much power or a time period that was too long could lead to the degradation of these compounds. In the case of astaxanthin, colorless compounds may be formed, while for lutein, it was noted that isomerization or epoxidation occurred. In the case of the UVI–3 process, free radicals may have been generated in the tested samples by transient cavitation. However, for the UVI–1 process, the changes may have been associated with the significant degradation of pectins, which might lead to the oxidation or leakage of lutein.

The total phenolic compound content in fresh carrot was 280 mg/100 g DM. The process of vacuum impregnating the tissue caused an increase in the phenolic compound content by about 12%, but this change was not statistically significant. Supporting the vacuum impregnation with ultrasound significantly increased the phenolic content in the carrot samples only in the case where US was used during the entire impregnation process (UVI–3). The total content of compounds in the UVI–3 samples increased by 46% in relation to the VI sample and was related to the de novo synthesis of phenolic compounds. However, in comparison to the VI sample, no significant differences were noted when using US during the separate stages: vacuum (UVI–1), relaxation (UVI–2), and aeration (UVI–4). Similar results were obtained by Yılmaz and Bilek [20] during the ultrasound-assisted vacuum impregnation of apple tissue, in which the use of US support during the entire impregnation process resulted in a higher total phenolic content and total flavonoid content compared to the use of US only during the vacuum or restoration stage.

In the case of chlorogenic acid, which constituted 59% of the phenol content in the raw material, it was noted that there was a decrease in content for the UVI–1 and UVI–4 samples in comparison to the raw material and VI, while, conversely, an increase and the highest level of synthesized phenols was noted for the UVI–2 and UVI–3 samples. Many authors have characterized the carrot tissue’s physiological response to abiotic stress and the related synthesis of phenolic compounds [47], [48], [49]. Moreover, many studies have confirmed that the synthesis of phenolic compounds in carrot tissue is mainly associated with an increase in hydroxycinnamic acid content, which is consistent with the obtained results [47], [50], [51]. In the case of the remaining compounds, the phenol content in the UVI–2 and UVI–4 samples did not differ statistically from each other, but they were significantly lower compared to UVI–3. In addition, the content of isocoumarin and neochlorogenic acid was higher in the UVI–2 samples compared to those from UVI–1. This indicates a that US has a differential effect depending on the pressure.

In relation to ultrasonic-assisted processes, the phenomenon of cavitation plays an important role. The resulting gas bubbles and their subsequent implosion, generate increases in local cutting forces, pressure, and temperature. Local pressure alterations have the greatest impact on changes occurring in biological material and may induce de novo synthesis of phenolic compounds [36], [52]. The formation, growth, and implosion of cavitation bubbles may create microchannels that increase the effective porosity of the carrot tissue and thus allow greater impregnation of the tissue with the ascorbic acid solution [53]. In the case of the UVI–3 process, the highest content of ascorbic acid was also observed, which was introduced through impregnation into the tissue. Therefore, for the products obtained in the above process, the effect of de novo synthesis of phenolic compounds could overlap with the antioxidant effect of ascorbic acid.

The antioxidant activity (AA) of the carrot samples ranged from 18 µmol Trolox/g DM in the raw carrot to 44 µmol Trolox/g DM in the UVI–3 samples (Fig. 6). The use of tissue vacuum impregnation increased AA by 50%, which indicates that the increase in ascorbic acid content had a significant impact on the antioxidant activity of samples. There was a high correlation between the AA of the carrot samples and both the ascorbic acid content (r = 0.84) and the phenolic compound content (r = 0.91). The use of ultrasonic-assisted vacuum impregnation caused a slight decrease in AA in the case of the UVI–1 sample compared to VI, and an increase in the UVI–2 and UVI–4 samples. However, a significantly higher value for the UVI–3 sample, using ultrasonic support for the entire impregnation process, was recorded in comparison to VI. The same effect was achieved by Yılmaz and Bilek [20] during the ultrasound-assisted impregnation of apple tissue, although in this case the DPPH method was used for the determination, and the activity was expressed in mg of ascorbic acid. The use of ultrasound induced the de novo synthesis of phenolic compounds through the cavitation process and improved ascorbic acid tissue impregnation, which may stabilize phenolic compounds by preventing their oxidation. The above factors contributed to a significant increase in AA, especially in the UVI–3 samples.

Conclusions

The application of ultrasound (US) during vacuum impregnation caused an increase in ascorbic acid content through the intensification of mass exchange. Quantitative effects depend on the process conditions, i.e., pressure and exposure time. US applied under reduced pressure causes stable cavitation (without the implosion of gas–vapor bubbles) along with the visible effect of the strengthened mass transfer as evidenced by the ca. 10% increase in ascorbic acid content in relation to the unsupported process. The intensification of the mass transfer is greater when using ultrasound at atmospheric pressure (20% ascorbic acid gain), which may result from the dominance of transient cavitation (with implosion). Long-term exposure to ultrasound – i.e., applying it during the entire impregnation process – causes a very high intensification in mass exchange, resulting in a ca. 60% increase in ascorbic acid content compared to the unsupported process. An increase in ion leakage and a decrease in cohesion (TPA), soluble solid substance, and hemicellulose content confirm the hypothesis that structural changes in the material are responsible for the high increase in mass transfer efficiency in this process. Prolonged exposure to ultrasound may also lead to an increase in polyphenolic compound content and antioxidant activity, which may be the result of de novo synthesis. The application of ultrasound during the individual stages of impregnation caused small or statistically insignificant changes in the assessed quality parameters compared to the process without enhancement.
