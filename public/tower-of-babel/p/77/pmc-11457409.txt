Field-based physical fitness assessment in preschool children in China
Authors: Ruiyuan Li, Ruiqin Li, Meng Liu, Huan Zhao, Pei Deng, Jiefeng Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical fitness in the preschool years is considered a marker of current and future health. Therefore, understanding physical fitness levels of preschool children is necessary. Furthermore, the use of field-based methods to measure physical fitness is essential for assessing children in authentic venues such as schools. Hence, the main purpose of this study was to describe the physical fitness of Chinese preschool children across field-based methods considering a statement of confidence intervals for reference in physical fitness in each age and genders. MethodsA total of 619 children (296 boys and 323 girls) aged 3–6 years old from China participated in the present study. Physical fitness was measured using the Chinese National Physical Fitness Measurement (CNPFM-Pre) battery, which included the tennis ball throw (upper-limb muscular strength), standing long jump (lower-limb muscular strength), shuttle run test (speed-agility), double-leg timed hop (coordination), sit-and-reach (flexibility), and balance beam test (dynamic balance).ResultsThe results revealed significant differences in gender for standing long jump (SLJ; η2 = 0.012, p = 0.007), tennis ball throw (TT; η2 = 0.081, p < 0.001), sit-and-reach (SR; η2 = 0.114, p < 0.001), and one-legged stance test (OST; η2 = 0.037, p < 0.001). Age had positive effects on all physical fitness performance tests, as the shuttle run test (η2 = 0.672, p < 0.001), SLJ (η2 = 0.513, p < 0.001), TT (η2 = 0.394, p < 0.001), double-legged timed hop test (DTH; η2 = 0.445, p < 0.001), SR (η2 = 0.069, p < 0.001), balance beam test (BBT; η2 = 0.367, p < 0.001), and OST (η2 = 0.336, p < 0.001). Older preschool children performed better than their younger counterparts in all the tests. No significant interactions between age groups and gender were found.ConclusionsThis study provided age- and gender-specific physical fitness performance data in Chinese preschool children. Gender differences in physical fitness tests performance already exists at the preschool age. All physical fitness component results increased with age in this cohort. These findings can help healthcare, physical education, and pediatric professionals understand the physical fitness development of preschool children at different ages and help design sports tasks for preschool boys and girls.Keywords: Physical fitness, Motor skills, Preschool children

Background

Physical fitness is the body’s ability to efficiently perform daily activities or motions [1, 2], and it is a strong marker of early childhood health [3–5], including both health- and motor-related fitness (i.e., cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, speed-agility, balance, coordination, and flexibility) [6]. High physical fitness levels or motor skills is not only associated with a low risk of cardiovascular disease, obesity, mental illness, and skeletal injury [7, 8], it also influences preschoolers’ physical activity participation [9–11] and affects their health status later in adolescence and adulthood, as well [12–14]. Research on children’s motor skills has also shown the importance of developing motor skills and physical fitness in early childhood [15, 16]. If motor skills and physical fitness development is impeded in the early years of life, it may hinder a child’s ability to master complicated motor skills and lead to lifetime motor skill problems [4]. Therefore, it is necessary to accurately assess the physical fitness of preschool children and better understand the current level of physical fitness development of preschool children.

Although the early childhood period is a key time to promote the development of motor skills and physical fitness [4], there is a notable dearth of literature incorporating national or international sample data on preschool children. Many countries have adopted a battery of national fitness tests, such as EUROFIT [17], BOUGE [18], PFAAT [19], AAHPER [20], PROESP-BR [21], and ALPHA [7, 22], but all these tests have been proposed for children and adolescents. China released a national physical fitness test manual for preschool children, i.e., the Chinese National Physical Fitness Measurement (CNPFM-Pre) battery, in 2000 [23]. The CNPFM-Pre can identify preschoolers’ physical fitness by testing their motor performance. And it showed acceptable reliability and sensitivity [24, 25]. Moreover, the CNPFM-Pre is a field-based test, which is more conducive for examining the actual physical fitness level of preschool children. Despite the frequent use of the CNPFM-Pre for assessing physical fitness in Chinese preschool children [3, 26, 27], there are few reports of large sample studies in scientific research. Consequently, the age and gender-related characteristics of multidimensional physical fitness development in this population remain inadequately understood.

Therefore, the main objective of this study was to report physical fitness performance characteristics of Chinese preschool children of different genders and ages by comprehensively assessing the physical fitness of Chinese preschool children aged 3–6 years old using the CNPFM-Pre. It is hypothesized that boys and girls have gender-specific differences in different components of physical fitness and physical fitness performance increases with age.

Methods

Participants

This is a cross-sectional study. The sample size (N = 225) was determined using GPower (version 3.1.9.7; Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany) for two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), using α = 0.05; power = 0.8; effect size (f) = 0.25. Three public kindergartens in Beijing, China were selected using a convenient whole-group sampling method from which a stratified random sample of 619 preschool children (296 boys and 323 girls; aged 3 to 6 years), without known pathologies, was selected (Table 1). The physical development of the participants was typical, with no major illnesses, no recent history of trauma that may affect their participation in physical activity, and no physical discomfort such as a cold or fever at the time of testing. Their participation in the physical fitness tests was voluntary. The classification of age groups was based on the principles and descriptions of the CNPFM-Pre [23]. The 3-year-old group included children between 3 and 3.5 years (3 ≤ x < 3.5); the 3.5-year-old group included children between 3.5 and 4 years (3.5 ≤ x < 4); the 4-year-old group included children between 4 and 4.5 years (4 ≤ x < 4.5); the 4.5-year-old group included children between 4.5 and 5 years (4.5 ≤ x < 5); the 5-year-old group included children between 5 and 5.5 years (5 ≤ x < 5.5); and the 5.5-year-old group had children between 5.5 and 6 years (5.5 ≤ x < 6). Before conducting the tests, a detailed explanation was provided to the parents regarding the aims and risks associated with the investigation, with assistance from the kindergarten management, and informed consent was obtained from the parents. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University Institutional Research Commission (Approval number: 2022155H), and the study procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Descriptive characteristics of the participants

Procedures

Before conducting the physical fitness tests, body weight (kg) and height (cm) of the participants were measured without their shoes and coats using a balance scale (V-BODY HBF-371, Omron, Japan) and a stadiometer (Ningbo Finer Medical Instruments Co., Limited, Zhejiang, China), respectively. Thereafter, their body mass index was calculated (weight/height squared [kg/m2]). All the tests were conducted on a wide playground in the morning. Seven test sites are set up and the children were tested in small groups (4 children per group). The order of the seven tests was randomized, and they were conducted on the same day. The children performed a moderate warm-up exercise that primarily involved jogging and aerobic exercises before participating in the tests [28, 29]. Before the start of each test, the examiner carefully explained the test procedures to the participants, specified the test requirements, and demonstrated how to perform this test, ensuring they understood the details of the test. After this, the children were familiarized with this test and were allowed two practice attempts on the test. If a child did not understand the task to perform it appropriately, the testing procedures were explained again and the demonstration was repeated. Children complete one test for the next. Two formal tests were conducted for each test item. A test taker was responsible for recording the children’s test scores on a list immediately after the children completed the test.

Physical fitness test

In the present study physical fitness in preschool children was assessed using CNPFM-Pre. It is consists of six test items (i.e., 2 × 10 m shuttle run test, SRT; standing long jump, SLJ; tennis ball throw, TT; double-leg timed hop, DTH; sit-and-reach, SR; and balance beam test, BBT). All CNPFM-Pre test items proved to have shown acceptable reliability [24, 25]. In addition to performing all the test items of the CNPFM-Pre, we also assessed static balance (SB) using the one-legged stance test (OST). Which has shown good test–retest reliability in typically developing children [29, 30]. The details of the CNPFM-Pre test are described as follows.

2 × 10 m shuttle run test

The 2 × 10 m SRT assessed speed-agility. A number of 10-m long and 1.22-m wide straight runway was set up and a marker for the hand was set at the return line. Children began the test by standing in front of the starting line. With the “start” signal, the participants ran toward the turnaround point with maximum speed, and after touching the marker, they turned around and run back to the starting line as fast as possible. The test was executed twice, and the time spent (accurate to 0.01 s) to complete the test was recorded. A shorter time reflected better speed-agility. The best attempt was evaluated/or recorded for further evaluation.

Standing long jump

Lower-limb muscular strength was assessed using the SLJ test. The children stood behind the starting line, with their feet separated at shoulder width and swung their arms while jumping forward as far as possible, stomping hard with their feet on the ground and landing with their upright. The distance from the starting line to the location of the foot mark closest to the starting line was measured. The test was executed twice, and the best result was recorded (1.0 cm).

Tennis ball throw

Upper-limb muscular strength was assessed using the TT test. During the TT test, participants stood in front of the throwing line with their feet spread back and forth and the body facing the throwing direction. They held the tennis ball over the head with one hand and threw it forward as far as possible. The distance from the throwing line to the first landing point of the ball was recorded. The test was executed twice, and the longer distance values were used for data analysis (0.5 m). The best attempt was evaluated/or recorded for further evaluation.

Double-leg timed hop test

Coordination was assessed using the DTH test. A horizontal line was drawn every 0.5 m on a flat ground, 10 lines in total, and a soft square bag (10 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 5 cm high) was placed across each line. A starting line was set 20 cm away from the first soft square bag. During the test, the children stood behind the starting line with their feet together. Upon hearing the “start” command, they jumped with both feet at the same time and jumped over 10 square bags in a row with both legs at the fastest speed. The test was executed twice, and the shorter time values were recorded, accurate to 0.01 s. A shorter time reflected better coordination. The best attempt was evaluated/or recorded for further evaluation.

Sit and reach

Flexibility was assessed using the sit-and-reach (SR) test with participants seated on a forward bend tester (Reliable Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Before the test, the children sat on the floor with their bare feet together and legs stretched forward, and the soles of both feet were attached to the measurement plate with both arms and fingers straight and naturally placed on the measurement table, which was considered as zero points. The children were then asked to slowly bend the upper body forward and use the middle fingertips of both hands to push the moveable marker smoothly forward until no further movement is possible. The distance from the starting point to the final position of the moveable marker was recorded. The test was executed twice, and the best result was recorded (0.1 cm).

Balance beam test

Dynamic balance (DB) was assessed using the BBT. A participant was required to walk on a 10-cm wide by 30-cm tall by 3-m long wooden balance beam. During the BBT, children stood on the platform, facing the balance beam with their arms extended straight to their respective sides and raised to their shoulder level. Upon hearing the word “start,” a participant began to walk forward. The testers started the stop watch immediately the participant started to move and stopped it when the participant’s toes touched the finish line. The test was performed twice, and the shorter time values were used for data analysis (0.01 s). A shorter time reflected a better DB. The examiner specified that the participants could retake the test if they fell from the balance beam in the middle of the test, and two attempts were allowed thereafter. The best attempt was evaluated/or recorded for further evaluation.

One-legged stance test

In addition to performing all the test items of the CNPFM-Pre, we also assessed static balance (SB) using the one-legged stance test (OST). The timed one-leg standing measurement is used as an SB measure and has shown good test–retest reliability in typically developing children [30, 31]. During the OST, the participants were asked to stand on one leg as long as possible, standing naturally with both eyes looking forward, both arms raised laterally to the shoulder level in a "T" position, one foot raised, and the support leg straightened, the leg is raised with the knee flexed and the knee joint parallel to the support leg. The stopwatch is started as soon as one foot is lifted and stopped when the child loses balance or the child’s raised leg touches the floor. During the test, when the children lift their leg to lean on or wrap it around the support leg, their arms are not raised sideways and are not flat, and their feet is not raised high enough, they are re-instructed, and the test is restarted. The test was performed twice, and the duration for the test was recorded (in seconds, accurate to 0.01 s). The longer duration value was used for data analysis. The best attempt was evaluated/or recorded for further evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were processed using IBM SPSS statistical software package (version 26.0, Chicago, IL, USA). All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). A Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (with Lilliefors correction) was used to test the normality of the data and Levene's test was used to test for homogeneity of variance. Firstly, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to determine any significant (p ≤ 0.05) main effects of age or gender or age × gender interactions on the CNPFM-Pre test results. When a significant interaction was observed, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference post-hoc correction was performed to identify the specific interaction. Secondly, one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences in the results among the age groups (3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, and 5.5 years). Subsequently, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed by employing the Games-Howell approach. Partial η 2 was used to determine the effect size (ES) when significance was observed, with its strength being interpreted as follows: < 0.06, small; < 0.14, moderate; and ≥ 0.14, large [32]. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Boys performed better than girls in lower-limb muscular strength (SLJ) and upper-limb muscular strength (TT). Girls performed better than boys in flexibility (SR) and SB (OST). Older preschool children performed better than the younger ones in all fitness tests, except for the SR test, in which the 4.5-year-old group performed better than the other age groups.

Firstly, the two-way ANOVA models revealed no significant interaction between age and gender on the SRT, SLJ, TT, DTH, SR, BBT, and OST. Further, it was found a significant main effect of age on physical fitness performance in terms of SRT (F = 248.36, p < 0.001), SLJ (F = 131.18, p < 0.001), TT (F = 78.828, p < 0.001), DTH (F = 97.58, p < 0.001), SR (F = 9.047, p < 0.001), BBT (F = 70.443, p < 0.001), and OST (F = 62.433, p < 0.001).

Secondly, one-way ANOVA models showed that the SRT, TT, and OST results differed significantly among the 3.5-, 4-, and 5-year-olds groups (p < 0.01). Regarding the SLJ, a significant difference was observed in the 3.5-, 4-, 4.5-, and 5.5-year-old groups (p < 0.05). Regarding the DTH, the 4-, 5- and 5.5-year-old groups differed significantly (p < 0.05). Regarding the BBT, the 3.5-, 4.5-, and 5-year-old groups differed significantly from the 3-, 4-, and 4.5-year-old groups, respectively (p < 0.001). Furthermore, a significant difference in the SR distance of the preschoolers at the age of 4 years (p = 0.008) was observed (see Fig. 1).

Further comparison of the physical fitness performance of boys and girls of each age group on the motor tests was performed. Boys performed significantly better than girls in the SLJ (4 years [p = 0.049] and 4.5 years [p = 0.004]), and TT (3 years [p = 0.023], 3.5 years [p = 0.027], 4.5 years [p < 0.001], 5 years [p = 0.023], and 5.5 years [p < 0.001]; see Fig. 2). Girls performed significantly better than boys in the SR (3 years [p = 0.005], 3.5 years [p = 0.001], 4 years [p = 0.006], 4.5 years [p < 0.001], 5 years [p < 0.001], and 5.5 years [p < 0.001]) and OST (4.5 years [p = 0.018], 5 years [p = 0.019], and 5.5 years [p < 0.001]) tests (see Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study provides age and gender developmental characteristics of the main fitness components (i.e., muscle strength, speed-agility, balance, coordination, and flexibility) in preschool children using field-based methods. Accodring to the hypotheses, the results showed that boys performed better than girls in lower- and upper-limb muscular strength, whereas girls performed better than boys in flexibility and SB. Physical fitness performance increased with age: older preschoolers performed better in most of the fitness tests than younger preschoolers. These findings can provide physical fitness indicators of early childhood development for health care, physical education, and pediatrics professionals to understand the current status of physical fitness development in preschool children and better promote their healthy behaviors. The findings can also help stakeholders to design appropriate movement tasks for boys and girls of different ages, as well as to quantify improvements in physical fitness after physical activity-based interventions in different age groups.

Physical fitness is widely considered as an important component of health and an important reflection of physical activity outcome indicators [33]. The preschool age is a key time to promote the development of physical fitness and motor ability [4, 16]. We observed that physical fitness develops progressively throughout early childhood, and older children performed better in most of the physical fitness tests (i.e., muscle strength, agility-speed, balance, and coordination), except for the flexibility test, and this finding is consistent with those of previous studies [34, 35]. During the preschool period, preschoolers experience significant physical development and body structure changes. As their bodies develop, their nervous systems, motor control systems, and motor patterns develop significantly [36], and consequently, their motor abilities change. This might explain the existence of a relationship between age and motor-related physical fitness performance.

Regarding gender differences, from as early as the preschool age, the differences in physical fitness levels between boys and girls are evident [37, 38]. In this study, boys showed better physical fitness performances in most tests compared with that shown by girls (i.e., muscle strength, speed-agility, and coordination). However, for the SRT and DTH tasks, this difference was not statistically significant (see Fig. 2). In contrast, girls performed better in the tasks involving balance and flexibility. These differences may be explained by gender-specific development and growth. The results of present study are consistent with recent studies that have analyzed motor abilities in early childhood [34, 35, 38]. Karolina et al. [35] found that boys showed higher levels of strength, speed, and agility than those shown by girls; however, in relation to flexibility, girls performed better in the SR test compared with the performance of boys. Moreover, there were observed similar patterns not only in children with ages similar to those in our study [34, 35] but also in older children up to the age of 11 years [39–41]. For example, the study by Zaqout et al. [41] showed that boys had better lower- and upper-limb muscular strength than that had by girls, while girls had better flexibility and balance. This suggests that gender dimorphism in physical fitness is already present in preschoolers.

Muscle fitness/strength is one of the most researched topics in physical fitness [42, 43]. Some studies have shown that muscle strength is negatively associated with cardiovascular disease and metabolic risk factors [5], and both upper and lower body muscular strength levels are considered important [44]. Our research found that both upper-limb and lower-limb muscular strength improved with increasing participant age, and boys had higher muscular strength qualities than those had by girls of the same age. Our results are consistent with those of Castetbon et al. [38] who similarly observed gender-differences in preschool children aged 4–6 years old. Such studies suggest that boys outperform girls in activities such as throwing, catching, or SLJ [45–47]. In all these studies, as with our findings, boys and older preschool children had higher outcomes than girls and younger preschool children, respectively.

Speed-agility is not only associated with improved performance in motor and locomotor skills for children [48], but it is also an important indicator of physical fitness, especially in relation to bone mass accumulation and bone mineral density later in life [5]. There is a wide variety of tests aimed at assessing speed-agility, often based on running patterns and changes in direction, such as the 4 × 10 m SRT and 4 × 5 m SRT, which have been used several times to assess speed-agility in preschool children. Overall, the present study found no gender difference in speed-agility, with older children performing better than younger children. Findings are in agreement with those of Karolina et al. [35] who used a 4 × 5 m SRT and confirmed that the values for boys and girls were the same, with improvement in agility as age increases. Cadenas-Sanchez et al. [34] also reported similar results after Spanish preschool children aged 3–6 years performed the 4 × 10 m SRT. They found no difference in speed-agility between boys and girls. This suggests that speed-agility abilities are not yet sexually dimorphic in the preschool years. It also suggests that an SRT based on running patterns can effectively assess speed-agility fitness in preschoolers.

Given the differences in applied methodology in previous studies, our results on fitness components cannot be compared with those of all previous study on preschool children. Regarding coordination, it was observed that boys performed similarly to girls in the DTH task, and the gender difference was not statistically significant. However, the present study found that compared to other physical fitness components in preschool children, coordination has received less attention. This may have happened because coordination is not well developed in the early years, although the findings may also be related to the difficulty of administering the test and the relative complexity of the methodology for young children.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to provide both SB and DB tests reference values for preschoolers. Only one previous study provided an SB reference value for Spanish preschoolers [34]. Francisco et al. [6] performed a systematic review of physical fitness testing systems for preschoolers and concluded that the assessment of balance in preschool years is critical, suggesting the addition of the OST to the physical fitness testing system to assess balance. Therefore, in addition to assessing all the test items of the CNPFM-Pre, this study added the OST to enable the assessment of both SB and DB in preschool children simultaneously. It was observed that the level of balance (both SB and DB) increased with the increasing age of the respondents. In addition, the study also found that girls had better SB compared to boys, while no difference in DB ability between boys and girls was observed. The results of the present study were found to be similar to those found in Italian children aged 6 to 11 years old [40] and European children aged 6 to 9 years old [7] who showed the same sex-differences in SB. However, regarding DB, no gender differences between boys and girls were found. Therefore, present data are not fully comparable with other studies due to the differences in age groups and testing methods. The gender differences observed may be attributed to the different types of balance tests [49]. This further suggests that balance should be measured categorically in preschool children.

An important strength of the current study is its holistic examination of preschool children’s physical fitness. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to provide age- and gender-specific reference date for a complete set of physical fitness components (i.e., muscular strength, speed-agility, flexibility, coordination, and balance) for preschool children and supplement the health-related data for SB to provide a comprehensive reference for the assessment of physical fitness in preschoolers. Furthermore, this is the first study to assess both SB and DB and provide reference data for preschool children. Another strength of our study is the use of field-based methods to assess preschoolers’ physical fitness, all of which can be easily implemented in schools without the need of specific instruments. These methods are important for large-scale testing of preschool children.

Nevertheless, although the present study is the first to report on the age and gender-related characteristics of multidimensional physical fitness development in Chinese preschool children, including SB elements, some limitations remain. Firstly, this study used a cross-sectional design to measure the physical fitness of developing children. Future longitudinal investigations are needed to observe the changes in physical fitness by gender and age in the same cohort of preschool children over a period and further confirm the results of this study. Secondly, the participants in this study were from one geographical region of China. Future studies could include a larger sample and taking into account a randomization in selection of the sample.

Conclusions

This study provides physical fitness development status in Chinese preschool children aged 3–6 years old, discriminating performance by gender and age. One of the main findings of this study is that physical fitness increases with age in the preschool years. Likewise, studies have found gender dimorphism in physical fitness among preschoolers. Overall, boys showed better performance than girls in upper- and lower-limb muscular strength while girls performed better than boys in flexibility and SB components. These findings can help health care, physical education, and pediatrics professionals to design appropriate movement tasks for boys and girls of different ages and help to promote healthy behaviors, as well as quantify improvements in physical fitness after physical activity-based interventions for different age groups.
