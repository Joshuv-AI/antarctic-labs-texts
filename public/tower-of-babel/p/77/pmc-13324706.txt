Triglyceride-glucose index as a marker of metabolic and inflammatory risk across different thyroid function states
Authors: Ümran Karabulut Doğan, Sefer Aslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index has recently gained attention as a cost-effective and easily calculable surrogate marker reflecting insulin resistance–related metabolic risk. Given the close interplay between thyroid hormones, lipid metabolism, glucose regulation, and inflammation, evaluating the association between thyroid dysfunction and the TyG index remains clinically relevant. This study aimed to investigate variation in the TyG index across different thyroid function states—overt hypothyroidism (OH), subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH), euthyroidism (EH), and healthy controls—and to assess its associations with lipid parameters and inflammatory markers, including the CRP/albumin ratio. In this cross-sectional observational study, 222 adults were categorized according to thyroid status based on TSH and free T4 levels. The TyG index was calculated using fasting glucose and triglyceride values. Associations with lipid and inflammatory markers were evaluated, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the ability of the TyG index to discriminate individuals with elevated TyG-defined metabolic risk. No statistically significant differences in TyG index levels were observed across thyroid function groups (p = 0.152). However, the TyG index showed significant positive correlations with triglyceride levels (r = 0.419, p < 0.001), CRP/albumin ratio (r = 0.342, p < 0.001), and LDL-C (r = 0.204, p = 0.009). ROC analysis demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.731; 95% CI: 0.652–0.809), identifying individuals with higher TyG levels associated with increased metabolic risk. Although TyG index levels did not differ significantly across thyroid function states, their associations with lipid and inflammatory markers suggest that the TyG index reflects metabolic and inflammatory risk profiles independent of thyroid status, rather than serving as a direct diagnostic measure of insulin resistance.

Introduction

Insulin resistance (IR) represents a central pathophysiological mechanism contributing to a range of metabolic and endocrine disorders, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). It is defined by a diminished cellular response to insulin-mediated glucose uptake, resulting in compensatory hyperinsulinemia and subsequent metabolic dysregulation. With the global prevalence of metabolic disorders rising steadily, the early identification of IR is crucial for mitigating long-term morbidity and mortality.

In recent years, the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index has gained increasing attention not only as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance but also as a broader indicator of cardiometabolic risk. Large population-based studies published after 2020 have demonstrated that elevated TyG levels are independently associated with incident type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality, even in individuals without overt metabolic disease. These findings suggest that the TyG index may capture early metabolic dysregulation before the development of clinically apparent insulin resistance or diabetes1.

Emerging evidence has also highlighted a complex interaction between thyroid function and TyG index–related metabolic risk. Recent observational studies have reported that subtle variations in thyroid hormones, even within the reference range, may influence glucose–lipid interactions and insulin sensitivity, thereby affecting TyG levels. In particular, subclinical hypothyroidism has been associated with higher TyG index values and unfavorable lipid profiles, supporting the hypothesis that thyroid dysfunction may modulate TyG-defined metabolic risk rather than directly determining insulin resistance2.

Inflammation has been proposed as a key mechanistic link between thyroid dysfunction, insulin resistance, and the TyG index. Contemporary studies have shown that TyG index correlates with systemic inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and composite inflammation indices. These associations reinforce the concept that TyG reflects a combined metabolic–inflammatory phenotype rather than a purely glycemic construct3.

Importantly, recent methodological studies have emphasized that while the TyG index correlates well with direct measures of insulin resistance, such as the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp or HOMA-IR, it should not be interpreted as a diagnostic substitute in the absence of an independent reference standard. Instead, current consensus suggests that TyG-derived cut-off values primarily discriminate individuals with higher metabolic risk profiles, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation in cross-sectional analyses4.

Despite this expanding literature, data examining the TyG index specifically across different thyroid function states remain limited and inconsistent. Most recent studies have focused on general or diabetic populations, with relatively few addressing euthyroid, subclinical hypothyroid, and overt hypothyroid individuals simultaneously. Furthermore, the relationship between TyG index and integrated inflammatory markers, such as the CRP/albumin ratio, has not been sufficiently explored in the context of thyroid dysfunction. These gaps highlight the need for focused investigations to clarify how thyroid status may influence TyG-defined metabolic and inflammatory risk5.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, derived from fasting triglyceride and glucose levels, has emerged as a straightforward, low-cost, and reliable surrogate marker for IR in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals6–8. Its strong correlation with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp—the gold standard for assessing insulin sensitivity—has led to its increasing adoption in clinical and epidemiological research due to its practicality and diagnostic value.

Several non-invasive indices have been proposed for insulin resistance, including the TyG-BMI index and the TG/HDL-C ratio. We selected the TyG index as our primary marker because it is simple, widely validated across diverse populations, and depends only on fasting triglyceride and glucose—two routinely measured parameters—making it practical for real-world clinical settings. While TyG-BMI may improve discrimination by incorporating adiposity, BMI is not always uniformly available and may introduce additional confounding when thyroid dysfunction influences weight and body composition. Similarly, TG/HDL-C is a useful dyslipidemia-related proxy but does not directly integrate glycemic status. Therefore, we prioritized TyG as a parsimonious and broadly comparable indicator, while acknowledging that complementary indices may be informative in future studies.

Thyroid hormones—specifically triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4)—are key regulators of metabolic equilibrium, exerting effects on basal metabolic rate, lipolysis, glucose transport, hepatic gluconeogenesis, and mitochondrial function. Both overt and subclinical hypothyroidism are frequently associated with metabolic disturbances such as dyslipidemia, weight gain, and impaired insulin sensitivity9–11. While overt hypothyroidism has well-established metabolic implications, the extent to which subclinical thyroid dysfunction influences IR and related markers, such as the TyG index, remains a matter of ongoing investigation.

In addition, low-grade chronic inflammation has been increasingly recognized as a common underlying factor in both IR and thyroid dysfunction. The C-reactive protein (CRP)/albumin ratio has gained attention as a sensitive biomarker of systemic inflammation, simultaneously reflecting inflammatory burden and nutritional status. Elevated CRP/albumin ratios have been observed in individuals with both hypothyroidism and insulin resistance, suggesting a potential inflammatory link between the two conditions12,13.

Despite the growing interest in the TyG index as a comprehensive cardiometabolic biomarker, limited research has addressed its variability across different thyroid function statuses, particularly in individuals without overt metabolic disease. Moreover, the interplay between TyG index, lipid parameters, and systemic inflammation in this context remains poorly defined.

Therefore, the present study aims to (1) compare TyG index levels across four distinct thyroid function groups—overt hypothyroidism (OH), subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH), euthyroid patients (EH), and healthy controls; and (2) examine the correlations between the TyG index and selected biochemical markers, including lipid fractions and CRP/albumin ratio. In addition, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of the TyG index in identifying insulin resistance within this population.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted between January and December 2024 at the Departments of Endocrinology and Internal Medicine at Adıyaman University.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Adıyaman University (Institutional Review Board, IRB No: XYZ2024-001), and written informed consent was secured from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.”

A total of 222 adult individuals, aged between 18 and 65 years, were recruited and categorized into four groups based on their thyroid function status:

Anthropometric and behavioral variables

Anthropometric measurements, including body mass index (BMI), were obtained at the time of blood sampling using standardized clinical procedures. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Due to the retrospective nature of data collection, detailed behavioral variables such as dietary intake, physical activity level, and alcohol consumption could not be systematically assessed. Smoking status was recorded where available and included in multivariable analyses when applicable.

Power subsection

Sample size and power calculation: The minimum required sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and four independent groups, the required total sample size was N = 180. To account for potential exclusions/missing data, we aimed to recruit ≥ 200; ultimately, 222 participants were included.

Multivariable regression analyses

In subgroup-specific multivariable linear regression analyses, the determinants of the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index differed across thyroid function categories.

In the overt hypothyroidism group, TyG index was independently associated with age (β = 0.003), triglyceride levels (β = 0.003), fasting glucose (β = 0.004), and CRP/albumin ratio (β = 0.123), while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) showed an inverse association (β = −0.002). The final regression model was statistically significant and explained a large proportion of variance in TyG index values (R² = 0.933, p < 0.001).

In the subclinical hypothyroidism group, independent predictors of TyG index included total cholesterol (β = 0.002), triglycerides (β = 0.001), fasting glucose (β = 0.003), and HDL-C (β = −0.005). The overall model was statistically significant, accounting for 84.5% of the variance in TyG index (R² = 0.845, p < 0.001).

Among euthyroid individuals, triglycerides (β = 0.004) and fasting glucose (β = 0.004) emerged as the primary independent determinants of the TyG index. Although creatinine remained in the final model, its association did not reach statistical significance. The regression model demonstrated strong explanatory power (R² = 0.954, p < 0.001).

In healthy controls, total cholesterol (β = 0.001), triglycerides (β = 0.003), and fasting glucose (β = 0.005) were independently associated with TyG index values. The final model was statistically significant and explained 89.2% of the variance in TyG index (R² = 0.892, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study provides valuable insight into the relationship between thyroid function and the TyG index, a validated surrogate for insulin resistance. Although no statistically significant differences in TyG values were observed across different thyroid states, the observed trends suggest underlying metabolic alterations even in subclinical dysfunction. Participants with overt or subclinical hypothyroidism tended to have slightly elevated TyG indices compared to euthyroid individuals and healthy controls, aligning with literature that implicates hypothyroid states in lipid metabolism disruption and reduced insulin sensitivity9–11.

In the present study, the TyG index was employed as a surrogate marker associated with insulin resistance risk rather than a direct measure of insulin resistance itself. Although TyG has been shown to correlate with gold-standard methods such as the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp and HOMA-IR in prior research, the absence of an independent reference standard in our study necessitates cautious interpretation. Accordingly, the observed associations should be interpreted as relationships between thyroid function, metabolic parameters, and TyG-defined insulin resistance risk, rather than insulin resistance per se. Similarly, the ROC-derived cut-off value identified in this analysis reflects the ability of the TyG index to discriminate individuals with higher TyG levels and increased metabolic risk, not to establish a diagnostic threshold for insulin resistance. This distinction is particularly important in cross-sectional designs, where causal and diagnostic inferences are inherently limited.

As expected, TyG index correlated with triglyceride levels (r = 0.419, p < 0.001), reflecting the mathematical structure of the index; therefore, this association was not interpreted as an independent biological relationship. The primary outcome—the TyG index—showed no statistically significant difference across thyroid function groups (p = 0.152); however, numerically higher TyG values were observed in the overt and subclinical hypothyroidism groups.

Recent studies published after 2020 further support the interpretation of the TyG index as a marker of metabolic risk rather than a direct measure of insulin resistance. Large population-based analyses have shown that elevated TyG levels are associated with future development of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular events, and mortality, even in metabolically asymptomatic individuals, suggesting that TyG may reflect early metabolic vulnerability1. In parallel, emerging evidence indicates that subtle alterations in thyroid hormone levels, including subclinical hypothyroidism, may influence TyG values through their effects on lipid handling and glucose metabolism, thereby modulating TyG-defined cardiometabolic risk2,5. Inflammatory pathways appear to play a contributory role in this association, as TyG has been consistently linked with systemic inflammatory markers, supporting the concept of a combined metabolic–inflammatory phenotype3. Importantly, recent methodological work emphasizes that TyG-derived cut-off values should be interpreted cautiously, as they discriminate higher-risk metabolic profiles rather than providing a validated diagnosis of insulin resistance in the absence of gold-standard measurements4. Taken together, these findings align with our results and support the role of the TyG index as an integrative, non-invasive indicator of metabolic and inflammatory risk across different thyroid function states.

Importantly, the TyG index demonstrated significant associations with both triglycerides and CRP/albumin ratio, reinforcing its role as an integrative metabolic-inflammatory marker. The strong correlation with triglycerides (r = 0.419) underscores the index’s biochemical foundation and its sensitivity to hepatic lipid handling and VLDL overproduction. Furthermore, the moderate association with CRP/albumin ratio (r = 0.342) supports the emerging hypothesis that insulin resistance and low-grade inflammation are pathophysiologically linked12–16. These findings align with current understanding of metabolic syndrome, in which inflammatory mediators such as CRP have been shown to exacerbate insulin signaling pathways, further fueling atherogenic processes.

The correlation between TyG and LDL-C, although weaker (r = 0.204), suggests that the index may reflect broader lipid derangements relevant to atherogenesis. This finding complements prior studies reporting elevated LDL and dyslipidemia in both insulin-resistant and hypothyroid individuals9,11. Elevated LDL-C is a known risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity, and its association with TyG—even at a modest level—supports the utility of TyG as a marker that extends beyond glycemic control alone.

Interestingly, the lack of significant associations with HDL-C and total cholesterol implies that the TyG index may better reflect specific lipid fractions involved in cardiometabolic risk, rather than overall lipid burden. This specificity could prove clinically useful, as total cholesterol levels often mask the presence of more subtle, yet clinically relevant, lipid imbalances17,18.

The ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.731, confirming moderate discriminatory power of the TyG index in detecting presumed insulin resistance. This is in line with previous population-based studies and further emphasizes the index’s practicality, especially in clinical settings where insulin assays are inaccessible19,20. The identified cut-off of 4.71 offers a feasible benchmark for risk stratification in non-diabetic populations, and may be particularly valuable in primary care or screening programs where simplicity and cost-effectiveness are critical.

From a clinical perspective, the TyG index offers a pragmatic, low-cost surrogate of insulin resistance derived from routinely obtained laboratory parameters. Although we did not observe statistically significant intergroup differences in TyG across thyroid function categories, the observed associations with inflammatory burden (CRP/albumin ratio) and atherogenic lipids (LDL-C) suggest that TyG may capture a broader cardiometabolic risk phenotype that is not fully explained by thyroid status alone. In outpatient practice, TyG may therefore serve as an adjunct marker to identify individuals who may benefit from closer metabolic risk assessment, especially when formal insulin resistance testing is not feasible.

Taken together, these findings position the TyG index as a valuable, non-invasive tool for assessing metabolic and inflammatory risk, irrespective of overt thyroid dysfunction. Its utility lies in its simplicity, affordability, and ability to capture early metabolic derangements that may not yet manifest through traditional markers. In this regard, it bridges the gap between clinical endocrinology and preventive cardiology, offering clinicians a pragmatic approach to early intervention.

Future longitudinal studies incorporating direct measures of insulin sensitivity and broader inflammatory markers are warranted to clarify causal relationships and expand upon these cross-sectional findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, despite the absence of significant differences in TyG index levels across thyroid function categories, the TyG index is consistently associated with lipid abnormalities and systemic inflammatory burden. These findings indicate that the TyG index reflects insulin resistance–related metabolic and inflammatory risk rather than insulin resistance itself. Given its simplicity and reliance on routinely available laboratory parameters, the TyG index may serve as a practical, non-invasive indicator for metabolic risk stratification in non-diabetic populations, particularly in research and screening settings where direct insulin resistance measurements are not available. Nevertheless, TyG-derived cut-off values should be interpreted cautiously, and future longitudinal studies incorporating validated measures of insulin sensitivity are required before clinical diagnostic application.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between thyroid function and the TyG index. Second, direct measures of insulin resistance, such as HOMA-IR or hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp techniques, were not available. As a result, the TyG index was used both as a surrogate indicator of insulin resistance–related metabolic risk and as the primary outcome variable in association and ROC analyses, which limits the diagnostic and clinical interpretability of the findings. Accordingly, TyG-derived cut-off values should be interpreted as markers of increased metabolic risk rather than as validated diagnostic thresholds for insulin resistance.

In addition, a pooled multivariable regression model including thyroid function group as an independent predictor of the TyG index was not performed; instead, regression analyses were conducted separately within each thyroid function category. While this approach allowed identification of group-specific determinants of the TyG index, it may limit direct between-group comparisons. Furthermore, although the overall sample size was adequate for the primary analyses, subgroup analyses may have been underpowered for more detailed stratification by sex or body mass index.

Behavioral factors such as dietary intake, physical activity, and alcohol consumption could not be systematically assessed due to the retrospective nature of data collection. Thyroid disease duration and medication status were also not fully captured, and undiagnosed dysglycemia could not be entirely excluded despite the exclusion of individuals with known diabetes. Finally, systemic inflammation was assessed using the CRP/albumin ratio; additional inflammatory biomarkers, such as interleukin-6 or tumor necrosis factor-α, were not measured and may have provided a more comprehensive characterization of inflammatory status.

Future studies incorporating longitudinal designs, direct measures of insulin sensitivity, and broader panels of metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers are warranted to validate and extend these findings and to clarify the clinical utility of the TyG index across different thyroid function states.
