Real-world data analysis of cranial nerve III, IV, and VI palsies: demographics, association factors, and comparative all-cause mortality
Authors: Chia-An Hsu, Min-Huei Hsu, Septi Melisa, Ju-Chuan Yen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBinocular diplopia is a distressing clinical condition that prompts clinicians to investigate underlying etiologies, particularly cranial nerve (CN) III, IV, or VI palsies. These may arise from microvascular ischemia, inflammation, trauma, compression, or neuromuscular junction disorders. This study aims to explore the association factors of these three ocular motor cranial nerve palsies and to compare their respective all-cause mortality rates using the real-world TriNetX Clinical Research Database (TriNetX CRD).Materials and methodsTriNetX is a global federated administrative database with real-time updates of electronic medical records (EMRs). We used the US Collaborative Network within the TriNetX platform to establish the patient cohorts. This network contains electronic health record data from more than 100 million patients across 68 US healthcare organizations (HCOs). This study utilized TriNetX platform to analyze the demographics and associated factors of ocular motor cranial nerve palsies, including diabetes, hypertension, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), overweight status, blood glucose and lipid profiles, body mass index (BMI), and history of brain aneurysm surgery, through intergroup comparisons using paired t-tests. All-cause mortality was assessed using Cox proportional hazards modeling and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsThe average age at presentation was 63, 57, and 60 years for CN III, IV, and VI palsies, respectively, with a slight male predominance. CN VI palsy was the most common, followed by CN IV and CN III palsies. CN III and CN VI palsy cohorts were more commonly associated with diabetes, hypertension, AMI, overweight, and brain aneurysm surgery, suggesting a microvascular or compressive etiology. In contrast, the CN IV palsy cohort was younger and more similar to the general population in clinical and laboratory characteristics. Regarding all-cause mortality, the CN III palsy cohort had the poorest survival, followed closely by the CN VI group, while the CN IV group exhibited the most favorable survival outcome.DiscussionThis study confirmed that CN VI palsy is the most frequent cause of ocular motor nerve palsy leading to binocular diplopia. Notably, CN III and VI palsies shared similar vascular and compressive association factors, while CN IV palsy appeared to be more frequently linked to congenital or traumatic origins. These differences were reflected in the mortality analysis, where CN III palsy showed the worst prognosis, CN VI a slightly better but comparable pattern, and CN IV the best survival outcome.ConclusionsUsing the TriNetX CRD, this study delineated the demographic profiles, associated clinical factors, and survival outcomes of patients with ocular motor cranial nerve palsies. The typical demographic was males in their late 50s to early 60s, with CN VI being the most frequently affected nerve. CN III and VI palsies were more often associated with microvascular and compressive conditions, which correlated with higher mortality. Conversely, CN IV palsy was associated with a younger population and more benign clinical profiles, reflected in better survival outcomes.Keywords: Ocular motor nerve palsy, Ischemia, Brain aneurysm surgery, Overweight, Diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Diplopia, specifically, binocular diplopia, is a troubling clinical symptom that prompts urgent action from clinicians, particularly ophthalmologists and neurologists [1]. It necessitates immediate evaluation to exclude emergent conditions such as impending aneurysmal rupture or brain tumors, although more commonly, the underlying cause is microvascular angiopathy associated with diabetes mellitus and/or hypertension [2, 3].

From an anatomical perspective, binocular diplopia may result from third, fourth, or sixth cranial nerve palsies [4]. And physiological etiologies include ischemia [5], compression, trauma, inflammation, and neuromuscular junction disorders. In adults, ischemic microangiopathy is the most common cause, whereas other etiologies are less frequent [6]. In contrast, in pediatric and adolescent populations, the distribution differs significantly [7, 8]. Central nervous system neoplasms account for approximately 23% of cases and are the most common cause of acquired ocular motor neuropathies in this age group. In Park’s study, inflammation was identified as the second most common etiology among children and adolescents with third, fourth, or sixth cranial nerve involvement [7].

Clinically, patients presenting with diplopia often experience considerable anxiety, particularly elderly individuals with acute onset, due to the associated risk of falls [9]. Additionally, diplopia can impair daily activities such as driving or walking, often requiring patients to occlude one eye or avoid certain tasks altogether. The cranial nerves responsible for extraocular movements include the oculomotor (CN III), trochlear (CN IV), and abducent (CN VI) nerves [10]. Their respective nuclei are located in the brainstem: CN III in the superior midbrain, CN IV in the lower midbrain, and CN VI in the pons. These nerve palsies manifest variably depending on the underlying cause [11]. For instance, trochlear nerve palsy is more frequently congenital, oculomotor nerve palsy is often associated with posterior communicating artery aneurysms, and abducent nerve palsy is more commonly linked to increased intracranial pressure, such as that caused by brain tumors [12, 13]. Moreover, associated neurological signs, such as Horner’s syndrome in cases of abducent nerve palsy, can assist in localizing lesions, particularly to the cavernous sinus [5, 14].

Given these differences, we sought to investigate the clinical association factors for third, fourth, and sixth cranial nerve palsies, including diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), overweight status, and aneurysms. Our primary research questions included: What are the demographic characteristics of each palsy group? What similarities and differences exist among them? What is the impact of these palsies on all-cause mortality? To address these questions, we utilized the TriNetX Clinical Research Database (TriNetX CRD), a large real-world dataset based in the United States that comprises information on over 100 million patients from more than 60 healthcare organizations. The database includes diagnostic codes, medication data, laboratory values, body mass index (BMI), and more.

Materials and methods

This study is a retrospective cohort observational analysis (Fig. 1) using TriNetX CRD to identify cases of cranial nerve III, IV, and VI palsies via ICD-10 diagnostic codes, and ICD-10 codes as H49.0 for CN III palsy, H49.1 for CN IV palsy H49.2 for CN VI palsy. We analyzed demographic variables including mean age with standard deviation, sex distribution, and ethnicity. We also examined associated clinical factors such as comorbidities (DM, hypertension, overweight status, AMI, brain aneurysm), laboratory parameters (blood glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], C-reactive protein [CRP]), and body mass index (BMI).

Flow chart of cohort identification and clinical parameter analysis in cranial nerve III, IV, and VI palsy research

In addition to overall cohort analysis, intergroup comparisons were conducted between the three cranial nerve palsy groups: CN III vs. CN IV, CN III vs. CN VI, and CN IV vs. CN VI. We also created a control subgroup comprising patients without involvement of the oculomotor, trochlear, or abducent nerves, serving as a reference group for comparison, we did not exclude other neurological or ophthalmological conditions in the “no palsy” control group. Furthermore, we assessed all-cause mortality using Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Propensity score matching which included age and comorbidities was applied for comparisons between the CN III and VI, CN III and IV, and CN IV and VI palsy groups to reduce confounding and ensure balanced comparison.

Results

Demographics

As summarized in Table 1, the mean age (± standard deviation) was 63.5 ± 20.6 years in the CN III palsy group, 57.6 ± 25.1 years in the CN IV group, 60 ± 24 years in the CN VI group, and 46 ± 25 years in the non-palsy cohort. The sex distribution showed a slight male predominance in the palsy groups (male-to-female ratios: CN III, 51:47; CN IV, 54:44; CN VI, 49:48) compared with a higher proportion of females in the non-palsy group (45:52). Ethnic distribution also varied, with White patients comprising the majority in the CN III (64%), CN IV (72%), and CN VI (66%) cohorts, whereas the non-palsy group demonstrated greater diversity, with lower White representation (54.55%) and higher proportions of African American (21.18%) and Asian (4.09%) patients.

Subgroup comparisons

As shown in Table 3, subgroup analyses demonstrated statistically significant differences in age, sex, ethnicity, comorbidities, and most laboratory parameters among the CN III, IV, and VI cohorts. Comparisons between CN III and CN IV, as well as CN IV and CN VI, revealed consistent differences (p < 0.0001) in comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, acute myocardial infarction, and aneurysm surgery, as well as in laboratory markers such as blood glucose, HbA1c, ESR, CRP, LDL, and HDL. By contrast, the CN III and CN VI cohorts showed greater similarity, with overlapping rates of overweight status (26% in both groups), comparable triglyceride levels (143 vs. 142 mg/dL), and only small but statistically significant differences in CRP (30.4 vs. 28.7 mg/dL; p = 0.0186) and BMI (28.6 vs. 28.4; p = 0.005). These results indicate that although statistical significance was reached in certain measures, the differences between CN III and CN VI cohorts were modest and likely of limited clinical relevance, even though. BMI was indeed greater in CN III cohorts than CN VI cohorts as it reflected that these two cohorts shared similar clinical presentations towards overweight as compared to the CN IV cohorts.

All-cause mortality comparisons between groups

All-cause mortality after the three types of ocular motor nerve palsies was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (see Figs. 2, 3 and 4; Tables 4 and 5). The results demonstrated that the cranial nerve III (oculomotor) palsy group had the worst survival outcome, followed by the cranial nerve VI (abducent) palsy group. The cranial nerve IV (trochlear) palsy group showed the best overall survival.

Although the difference in survival between the CN III and CN VI groups was statistically significant (p = 0.0012), it was relatively small. In contrast, comparisons involving the CN IV group showed more pronounced differences, with p-values < 0.0001 for both the CN IV vs. CN III and CN IV vs. CN VI comparisons. Survival analyses further highlighted prognostic differences among the ocular motor nerve palsy cohorts. Compared with CN III palsy, CN IV palsy was associated with a markedly reduced risk of mortality (HR = 0.42), and a similar reduction was observed when comparing CN IV with CN VI (HR = 0.419). By contrast, the comparison between CN III and CN VI palsy revealed no meaningful difference in mortality risk (HR = 1.074).

These results indicate that patients with trochlear nerve palsy had significantly better survival compared to those with either oculomotor or abducent nerve palsy, while survival between the CN III and CN VI groups was more comparable.

Discussions

In our study, among the three ocular motor cranial nerves, the most commonly affected nerve leading to diplopia was the abducent nerve (CN VI) with 54,008 cases, followed by the trochlear nerve (CN IV) with 34,963 cases, and the oculomotor nerve (CN III) with 34,530 cases. The numbers for CN IV and CN III were comparable. The mean ages (± standard deviation) for CN III, IV, and VI palsies were 63 ± 21, 57 ± 25, and 60 ± 24 years, respectively, which closely match the findings from Tamhankar’s 2014 multicenter study reporting a mean age of 65.6 ± 9.3 years, mainly presumed to be of microvascular origin (86.5%). Ethnicity distribution in our dataset compared reasonably with Tamhankar’s study [6]: for CN III palsy, our cohort had 62.4% White, 13.9% African American, 4.3% Asian, and 4.7% Other; CN IV palsy had 72% White, and CN VI 66% White.

Although we could not assess diplopia duration (e.g., 14 ± 7.8 days as noted by Tamhankar) [6], our real-world data reflected clinical patterns suggestive of ischemic origins in adult cranial nerve palsies. This is evidenced by the high rates of diabetes mellitus (16–35%) and hypertension (41–62%) in CN III and VI groups, in contrast to only 8% and 17% respectively in the non-palsy group. The trochlear nerve palsy cohort stood out with a younger age profile and lower comorbidity, resembling the no-palsy group [5].

AMI rates were higher in CN III (8%) and CN VI (7%) groups than in the no-palsy group (1%), again suggesting an ischemic component (Please refer to Table 4). Though hyperlipidemia wasn’t prominent in our dataset, possibly due to widespread lipid-lowering treatment, and so it should be extrapolated cautiously toward this aspect of not prominent hyperlipidemia” HDL and triglyceride levels revealed that the CN IV cohort had a more favorable lipid profile, potentially protective against ischemic events. This supports the notion that CN IV palsy often stems from congenital (38%) or traumatic (29%) causes, rather than ischemia. BMI data further reinforced this: CN III and VI patients had mean BMIs around 28, compared to around 27 in CN IV and non-palsy groups. Overweight rates were 26% in CN III and VI, 21% in CN IV, and only 9% in the no-palsy group, reflecting a broader obesity trend with implications for microvascular disease.

In terms of laboratory values, blood glucose and HbA1c levels were highest in the CN III group, followed by CN VI, and lowest in CN IV and non-palsy groups. This underscores the link between prediabetes/metabolic syndrome and cranial nerve III and VI palsies, while CN IV again presented as a clinically distinct group. Inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) followed similar patterns, elevated in CN III and VI groups, but not in CN IV, but these markers are non-specific and the difference may be driven by general systemic health rather than the palsies themselves, therefore, it should be interpreted cautiously. While autoimmune etiologies like giant cell arteritis are historically important, our data did not support a high burden of inflammatory or autoimmune origins. The marginally higher CRP in CN III vs. CN VI (p = 0.0186) was statistically significant but not strongly suggestive of major clinical difference.

All-cause mortality

Cox proportional hazards modeling and Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated that the CN III palsy cohort had the poorest overall survival, followed by CN VI, whereas CN IV was associated with the most favorable outcomes. The difference in survival between CN III and CN VI was statistically significant but modest (p = 0.0012; HR = 1.074), while differences between CN IV and both CN III (HR = 0.42) and CN VI (HR = 0.419) were highly significant (p < 0.0001). These findings likely reflect the higher comorbidity burden and more severe etiologies, such as aneurysmal disease, in CN III palsy, in contrast to the younger, less comorbid, and often congenital or traumatic nature of CN IV palsy [15].

Brain imaging considerations

With the widespread availability of MRI and CTA, clinicians now have better tools to evaluate patients with diplopia. Prior studies showed variable yields for detecting structural causes: 1%–16.5% in different cohorts [16, 17]. In our study, the CN III and CN VI palsy cohorts, there were77% received either Brain CT or MRI neuroimaging, whereas CN IV palsy cohorts, there were only 66% received above neuroimaging. Regarding the aspect of aneurysm issue, CN III IV VI palsy cohorts there were 11%, 3%, 5% unruptured aneurysm respectively In our study, brain aneurysm surgery was most common in CN III palsy (2%), followed by CN VI (1%), and none in CN IV. This aligns with the known risk of posterior communicating artery aneurysm in CN III palsy and justifies advanced imaging in these patients, even in the absence of pupil involvement [18].

Previous studies have reported variable approaches to neuroimaging in patients with ocular motor nerve palsies [19, 20]. Hesselink et al. noted that all neurologists recommended MRI for CN III palsy, whereas most ophthalmologists favored triaging based on clinical risk factors [21]. In a large South Korean cohort, Lyu et al. found that women younger than 65 years with cranial nerve palsy had a significantly increased risk of malignant brain tumors within one year, supporting early imaging in this subgroup [22]. Similarly, an Indian case series by Chodvadiya et al. demonstrated that brain imaging identified diverse pathologies, including trauma, hemorrhage, cerebral venous thrombosis, and intracranial tumors [23]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of individualized risk assessment in guiding decisions on MRI and vascular imaging (MRA/CTA) in patients presenting with diplopia [24].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective observational design precludes causal inference, and reliance on claims and administrative codes introduces the potential for misclassification. Moreover, the inherent limitations of using administrative data might include potential of diagnostic inaccuracies and the lack of specific clinical details like pupil involvement in CN III palsy. In addition, the absence of clinical records and imaging data prevents confirmation of diagnoses or underlying etiologies. Nevertheless, the large sample size and inclusion of patients from more than 60 healthcare organizations within the TriNetX CRD enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

Using the TriNetX CRD, we characterized the epidemiology and outcomes of cranial nerve III, IV, and VI palsies, finding the sixth nerve most commonly affected, typically in patients in their 60s with a slight male predominance. CN III and VI palsies were strongly associated with microvascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, myocardial infarction, overweight status, and elevated glucose and BMI, reflecting a more proinflammatory profile, whereas CN IV palsy represented a younger, less comorbid, and often non-ischemic group, frequently related to congenital or traumatic causes. All-cause mortality was highest among CN III patients, followed by CN VI, and lowest in CN IV. These findings highlight the distinct clinical landscapes of ocular motor nerve palsies and may inform diagnostic evaluation, imaging strategies, and risk stratification in patients presenting with binocular diplopia.
