Depression, ovarian cysts and infertility in women: A mediated Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Dongling Yuan, Yi Zhang, Yuting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
According to the perspective of psychosomatic medicine, infertility is the consequence of the interaction of individual psychological and physiological factors. Specifically, depression and ovarian cysts are strongly associated with infertility in women. However, the causality and mechanisms are still unclear. This study was conducted to investigate the causal relationships between depression, ovarian cysts and infertility based on large-scale genome-wide association studies pooled statistical Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. In the primary inverse-variance weighted analysis, there was a genetically predicted causal effect of depression on both female infertility and ovarian cysts, as well as a direct causal association observed between ovarian cysts and female infertility. Multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis revealed that ovarian cysts mediate the causal relationship between depression and infertility. The findings demonstrate from a genetic perspective that depression confers susceptibility to infertility via increasing the risk of ovarian cysts in females. It provides robust evidence for the potential biological link between psychological factors and reproductive health.

1. Introduction

Infertility is defined as the inability to pregnancy after regular and unprotected sexual intercourse following 1 year or more.[1] Recently, the incidence of infertility has gradually increased globally.[2] The data estimates that about 10% to 12% of couples worldwide suffer from infertility,[3] while the rate is as high as 31.1% in low- and middle-income countries.[4] Moreover, epidemiological studies showed that the prevalence of infertility is 6.7 to 25% in women of reproductive age, who need fertility.[5,6] Infertility has become a noteworthy public health issue globally, and has profound effects on individuals (i.e., psychological distress), families (i.e., marital discord), and society (i.e., economic burden) as a whole.

Since the 1940s, researchers have studied infertility from a psychosomatic medical perspective. Numerous pieces of evidence support the hypothesis that psychological factors play an important role in the occurrence and development of infertility.[7–9] For instance, negative emotions are a potential risk factor for infertility.[10] It has been shown that the infertility rate in women with a history of depression is twice as high as that of women without depression, and the prevalence of depressive disorders among individuals with infertility ranges from 17 to 38%.[11] Inversely, the lower the depression levels, the higher the pregnancy rate is.[12] Additionally, depressed patients are often accompanied by disruptions in neurohormone levels, which may contribute to decreased fertility and pregnancy rates.[13] Although there are well evidences that depression play an important role in infertility, the causal relationship is not yet clear.

Whereas in terms of physiological factors, numerous studies reveal there is a significant effect of ovarian cysts on female infertility.[14] The ovarian cyst is a common gynecological disease that can occur at any age, more in the reproductive age of women.[15] It has been found that ovarian cysts can induce dysregulation of the endocrine system, which negatively impacts fertility.[14] For instance, patients with ovarian cysts had much lower levels of E2 and AMH than healthy women.[14] Ovarian cysts are associated with reduced ovarian volume and decreased density of adjacent cortical follicles.[14] In addition, individuals with ovarian cysts have a persistent state of pro-inflammation, which may also impair normal ovarian function, subsequently dysregulating the synthesis and release of sex hormones as well as follicular maturation and ovulation, which may increase difficulties in pregnancy.[16] Despite these, it remains difficultly to determine the direct impact of cysts on subsequent fertility.[14]

Additionally, research revealed that ovarian cysts are associated with psychological disorders.[17] For instance, Depressed patients have been found to have a dysregulation of endocrine and inflammation.[17] Previous studies have shown that early depression can induce late inflammation in individuals.[18–20] While inflammation in turn predisposes to ovarian cysts[16] and even directly affects ovarian function, oocyte quality and endometrial receptivity.[21] These findings suggest that depression may also be a risk factor for ovarian cysts as well as infertility.

Previous studies have predominantly employed observational longitudinal designs to examine the associations or potential causal effects between depression, ovarian cysts, and infertility. Liang et al conducted a longitudinal cohort study to investigate the link between a history of depression and adverse outcomes such as infertility.[22] However, there remains a lack of evidence of a causal relationship between depression, ovarian cysts and infertility, due to confounding factors. Recently, Mendelian randomization (MR) has been widely used to estimate causal effects by using genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV). Because genetic variants for exposure and outcome are randomly assigned and relatively independent of environmental factors, MR is not affected by confounding factors and reverse causality.[23] The multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis is an extension of MR and allows the detection of causal effects of multiple risk factors and the potential mediating effects.[24] Researchers advocated for the use of methods such as MR analysis to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, thereby guiding effective treatments of ovarian cysts and infertility.[25]

Thus, the present study utilized multivariable MR analysis to explore the potential causal relationships and mechanisms between depression, ovarian cysts, and infertility. The findings will provide insights for the comorbidity research and clinical treatment of female infertility.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

All the summary single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–phenotype association data were obtained on the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). Specifically, the genetic associations for depression (including symptoms of depressed mood or loss of interest, most of the time for at least 2 weeks, that interfere with daily activities)were obtained from the psychiatric genomics consortium including 170,756 cases and 329,443 healthy individuals of European ancestry.[26] Participants were diagnosed using structured diagnostic tools, which included assessments conducted by trained interviewers, clinician-administered checklists, or medical record reviews, and were required to meet the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV), or International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10).[27] Genetic association estimates for ovarian cysts diagnosed by ICD-10 were obtained from a GWAS (http://www.mrbase.org/) of 79,817 European individuals. And female infertility genetic associations were identified from a GWAS of 6481 cases and 68,969healthy individuals of European ancestry. All MR analyses were conducted using the R packages of MR and Two Sample MR (github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR). Forest plots of the results were generated using code from the “ggplot2” package in R.

2.2. Genetic instruments

In this study, genetic instruments were selected as SNPs associated with the exposure at the genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10⁻⁶) from each genome-wide association study (GWAS), with all these SNPs being independent of each other as indicated by pairwise linkage disequilibrium (r2 <0.001 and distance >10,000 kb).[28] Furthermore, the strength of selected SNPs was evaluated using F-statistics, with SNPs exhibiting F-statistics < 10 being excluded to mitigate weak instrument bias in the MR analysis,[29] and it is calculated as follows:

2.3. Mendelian randomization analysis

Firstly, the univariable MR analyses were performed to estimate each causal relation between depression and infertility, ovarian cyst and infertility, depression and ovarian cyst respectively. While the multivariable MR analysis was used to evaluate the direct effects of depression on infertility in women and the mediated effect of ovarian cysts on the causal relationship of depression and infertility. Inverse-variance weighted method (IVW) was used as the main MR analysis.[30] The IVW method estimates the causal effect of exposure X on outcome Y by computing a weighted average of the effect of each IV on Y through X. In the absence of directional pleiotropy, it provides reliable causal estimates. Several other established methods that are robust to horizontal pleiotropy—such as MR-Egger, the weighted median, and MR-PRESSO—were used for comparison with the IVW results, as these methods may be biased when genetic variants exhibit horizontal pleiotropy.[31]

Then, the indirect effects of the mediating variables are assessed by the production method.[32] Specifically, we used the IVW method as the primary approach to estimate the causal effect of depression on ovarian cysts (β₁). We then applied MVMR[29] to estimate the causal effect of ovarian cysts on infertility risk (β₂), adjusting for the genetic effects of depression. The mediated effect of depression on the outcome through ovarian cysts was calculated as the product of β₁ and β₂ (β₁ × β₂). The direct effect (β₃) represents the estimated effect of depression on the outcome after adjusting for the mediator, while the total effect is the sum of the direct and indirect effects (β₃ + β₁ × β₂). Accordingly, the proportion of the effect mediated by ovarian cysts was estimated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect: (β₁ × β₂)/ (β₃ + β₁ × β₂).

Moreover, the 2-tails P < .05 was considered indicative of significance. The thresholds were further adjusted by Bonferroni correction for the number of exposed phenotypes. Thus, the threshold of statistical significance for the 2 exposures was set at P < .05/2 = 0.025.

2.4. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis has been pivotal in MR studies to assess the robustness of the results. Specifically, the IVW and MR-Egger approaches were used to evaluate the heterogeneity. The directional pleiotropy was estimated by MR-PRESSO[33] and the intercept was obtained from the MR-Egger regression.[31] Moreover, the leave-one-out analysis was conducted to assess whether MR estimates were driven or biased by a single SNP.[34] The P < .05 of indicators for sensitivity analysis was considered acceptable.

3. Results

3.1. Genetic instruments

There were 181 and 17 independent SNPs identified as genetic IVs for depression and ovarian cysts, respectively (Appendix S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q727). Six SNPs related to depression and 1 SNP associated with ovarian cysts were excluded due to intermediate allele frequencies. Ultimately, 175 and 16 independent SNPs were used as genetic IVs for depression and ovarian cysts, respectively. The F-statistics for all individual SNPs were >10, with mean F-statistics of 27.862 for depression and 25.675 for ovarian cysts. These values indicated that the causal associations are unlikely to be biased by weak instruments.

3.2. Univariable and multivariable Mendelian randomization

Univariable MR analysis showed that there were genetically predicted causal effects of depression on both female infertility (OR = 1.196; 95% CI: 1.053–1.359; P = .005) and ovarian cysts (OR = 1.327; 95% CI: 1.197–1.470; P < .001) with IVW analyses (Appendix S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q727). Meantime, a direct causal association of ovarian cysts with female infertility was also observed with the IVW method (OR = 1.386; 95% CI: 1.232–1.559; P < .001; Fig. 1). These estimates were similar to those from the weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses (Table 1 and Appendix S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q727).

4. Discussion

In this study, the GWAS data were used to explore the effects of genetically predicted effects of depression and ovarian cysts on female infertility via MR analysis. The results provided genetic evidence to support causal relationships between depression, ovarian cysts female infertility. Moreover, there was a mediated effect of ovarian cysts on the causal relationship between depression and infertility.

Firstly, there was a causal relationship between depression and female infertility. Specifically, women with depression have a 1.2 times higher risk of infertility than those without depression. This supported the previous findings that women with a history of depression not only affect reproductive function, but also have poorer infertility treatment efficacy.[35] Studies have also found that dysfunction of the endocrine system, inflammation, stressful environments and genetics are the main causes of ovarian cysts in women.[16,36] While depressed individuals are more susceptible to stress and have significant pro-inflammatory and endocrine system dysfunction.[19] In addition, depression, as a negative emotion, can greatly reduce energy and libido levels, which diminish the motivation of individuals to live and rear children.[37] These suggested that in the clinical setting, women should simultaneously receive treatment for depression to alleviate the dysfunction of endocrine and immunity systems, and increase their energy and motivation levels, thereby enhancing their willingness and ability for fertility.

Moreover, both univariable and multivariable MR analysis showed that there was a strong association between ovarian cysts and infertility, which suggested that ovarian cysts directly increase the risk of infertility in women. Furthermore, in MVMR analysis, there was no significant causal association between depression and female infertility, adjusting for the effect of ovarian cyst. These suggest that ovarian cysts play a major role on the causal association of depression with female fertility. As a common gynecological disease, ovarian cysts could seriously affect the ovarian reserve, manifested by the disturbance of reproductive hormone levels, reduction of ovarian volume and follicle density, all of which are causes for infertility.[14,38,39] In addition, according to the perspective of psychosomatic medicine,[40] infertility is the consequence of the interaction of individual psychological and physiological factors. It has been shown that depression is positively correlated with the level of inflammation, and depressed individuals have significant pro-inflammatory as well as immune system dysfunction,[17] which can induce ovarian cysts and then lead to infertility. These results provided genetic evidence that infertility may be a psychosomatic disorder and emotional disorders (such as depression) may play a significant role in the occurrence, progression, and treatment response of female infertility.

There are some limitations. First, MR analysis is inherently an epidemiological approach, thus other factors such as the influence of acute stressful events are also important and need attention in future studies. Second, this study used pooled GWAS data, which could not assess the nonlinear relationship between exposure and outcome. It also could not be stratified by factors such as age because of the lack of individual data. In addition, GWAS data for the ovarian cyst covered multiple types, but lacked detailed classification information for cyst pathological subtypes or clinical stages. Future research could further improve the stratified analysis of ovarian cysts by integrating multi-center clinical data. Next but not least, although this study conducted a pleiotropy test through the MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO, pleiotropy could cause the assumptions of independence and exclusivity to be invalid, which in turn affects the robustness of MR.

5. Conclusions

From a genetic perspective, the findings demonstrate that depression confers susceptibility to infertility via increasing the risk of ovarian cysts in females. It provides robust evidence for the potential biological link between psychological factors and reproductive health.
