Meta‐Analysis: Mortality Trends and Risk Factors in Severe Alcohol‐Associated Hepatitis
Authors: Mushfiqur R. Siddique, Muzzafar Haque, Francisco Idalsoaga, Luis Antonio Diaz, Gene Im, Ashwani K. Singal, Stephen Hoang, Mohammad Qasim Khan, Juan Pablo Arab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSevere alcohol‐associated hepatitis (sAH) is a life‐threatening condition. Despite advances in clinical management, prognosis remains poor and long‐term effectiveness of available therapies is uncertain. We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis to evaluate short‐term mortality (28, 60, and 90‐day) trends in sAH over the past five decades.MethodsWe searched PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus from database inception to February 2024 for studies reporting 28, 60, and 90‐day mortality in patients with sAH. Pooled mortality estimates were calculated using a random‐effects meta‐regression model. We assessed heterogeneity using the I2 statistic and explored sources of heterogeneity through subgroup and meta‐regression analyses. Separate Bayesian mixed‐effects binomial models were used to estimate the posterior distribution of mortality probability, updated sequentially across calendar time.Results34 studies comprising 1586 patients with sAH were included. Pooled mortality rates were 26.8% (95% CI: 21.0%–33.5%) at 28 days, 35.1% (95% CI: 28.3%–42.5%) at 60 days, and 43.7% (95% CI: 34.6%–53.3%) at 90 days. Mortality increased steadily with follow‐up time. Substantial heterogeneity was observed, as expected in pooled proportion meta‐analysis (I2 > 80%). Although cumulative Bayesian analysis showed that average 28‐day mortality declined from over 50% in the 1970s to ~25% after 2000, a formal decade‐based analysis indicated no statistically credible improvement in short‐term mortality was detected overall in the past four decades. In multivariable models adjusting for follow‐up time, the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score was significantly associated with mortality.ConclusionsShort‐term mortality in sAH remains high and has not improved in recent decades. These findings highlight the urgent need for effective therapies, improved patient selection for early liver transplantation, and better prognostic tools to guide clinical decision‐making.

Introduction

Alcohol‐associated hepatitis (AH) is an acute liver condition associated with the sudden onset of jaundice, malaise, decompensated liver disease, and coagulopathy [1, 2]. Prior evidence has shown that patients with severe AH (sAH) have a mortality of up to 50% at 3 months [3, 4, 5], with acute kidney injury (AKI), infections, and multi‐organ failure being the most relevant complications associated with a high mortality [2]. Due to the high mortality observed in sAH, the prediction of mortality is critical to guide decision‐making [6] To date, severity is commonly assessed using Maddrey's Discriminant Function (mDF) [7] or the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores [8]. In several cohorts, the MELD score has demonstrated greater performance in predicting short‐term mortality [8, 9, 10] Thus, a MELD ≥ 21 is typically used as the cutpoint to define an sAH episode [6].

Despite advances in clinical care, mortality rates have remained persistently high over the past four decades, primarily due to the limited availability of effective treatment options. Supportive treatment remains the cornerstone of AH management, including nutritional support, prevention of AKI and infections, as well as alcohol cessation [11, 12]. Although corticosteroids have demonstrated short‐term benefits, particularly in patients with MELD scores between 25 and 39 [13], Additionally, early liver transplantation has become a viable option for selected patients with sAH who fail to respond to medical therapy [12]. Despite the clinical significance of AH and its high mortality, there have been no targeted studies in recent decades evaluating short‐ and medium‐term mortality or mortality trends, making it difficult to design clinical trials and assess the efficacy of novel therapies for this disease. Consequently, understanding its natural history and the factors associated with mortality can inform and facilitate the clinical trial design in AH. In this systematic review, we aimed to evaluate mortality trends and identify the main risk factors for mortality at 28, 60, and 90 days in sAH.

Methods

Search Strategy

The systematic review and meta‐analysis followed a predefined protocol specifying inclusion and exclusion criteria, outcomes, and methodology. Reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14]. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to February 1, 2024, without restrictions on language, publication year, or status (Table 1 andS1). To identify additional eligible studies, abstracts from Digestive Disease Week, United European Gastroenterology Week, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases, and the European Association for the Study of the Liver were manually searched.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and other prospective clinical trials, such as double‐blinded, open‐label, multicenter, and noninferiority trials. Studies included adult patients (≥ 18 years) with sAH, defined as mDF ≥ 32 or MELD ≥ 21, diagnosed by liver biopsy or, when biopsy was not feasible, according to the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) criteria.

Studies published as full manuscripts or conference abstracts were included, with no restrictions on language, publication year, or status. Excluded studies comprised those involving paediatric patients, case reports, case series, review articles, preclinical studies, and RCT protocols. Additionally, studies involving patients with moderate AH or other causes of liver injury, including viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, or Wilson's disease—even when associated with chronic alcohol consumption—were not considered.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed were mortality rates at 28, 60, and 90 days following diagnosis or hospital admission for sAH. Secondary outcomes included publication year, use of corticosteroids, use of N‐acetylcysteine (NAC), use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF), gender ratio, mean age, liver transplantation access, MELD and mDF score, development of sepsis, HRS, or AKI.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Duplicate records were removed before screening titles and abstracts. Two authors (MH and MS) independently screened all identified records in duplicate, first by title and abstract and then by full text, applying the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion, and if consensus was not achieved, FI settled the final decision. Literature screening and PRISMA flow diagram generation were conducted using Covidence (systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by MH and MS using a standardised extraction form. Discrepancies were initially discussed by the two reviewers to identify whether differences arose from interpretation of the data, extraction errors, or ambiguity in the source article. When disagreement persisted after discussion, FI reviewed the original article and the extracted information and made the final decision.

Estimation of Pooled Proportions (Frequentist Framework)

A random‐effects meta‐regression model (i.e., a mixed‐effects model with a random study intercept and time as a fixed moderator) was used to estimate the overall mortality rate in the placebo or standard‐care group, where time from diagnosis or admission was a fixed effect and study was modelled as a random effect. This model was fit using the metafor R package (v4.8–0) [48]. Effect sizes were logit‐transformed proportions, weighted by the inverse of their within‐study sampling variances. Point estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed on the logit scale and converted to proportions. In addition, 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated following the metafor defaults to describe the expected range of effects in new studies. Wald‐type two‐sided p‐values were obtained for the moderator coefficients in the random‐effects meta‐regression model. A result was considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. The heterogeneity τ2, or between‐study variance, was estimated with REML. The I2 statistic was computed to describe the proportion of total variability attributable to between‐study heterogeneity, recognising that in single‐arm pooled proportion meta‐analyses, true heterogeneity is expected and I 2 values tend to be high. As such, I 2 was reported descriptively, and no categorical thresholds were applied for interpretation [49].

Meta‐Regressions With Additional Covariates (Frequentist Framework)

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we fitted an exhaustive set of meta‐regression models that incorporated the following study‐level covariates into the baseline model described above: publication year (centered), corticosteroid status, NAC status, and G‐CSF status. All subsets without interaction terms were evaluated with the glmulti R package (v1.0.8) [50]. Models were ranked by the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) computed by glmulti. Coefficients with Wald two‐sided p‐values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Note that models incorporating MELD and mDF were fitted separately due to the extent of missing data for these covariates.

Time‐Trend and Cumulative Analysis (Bayesian Framework)

A Bayesian mixed‐effects binomial model was also fit separately to data at 28 and 90 days post‐diagnosis—the day‐60 outcome was excluded from this analysis due to the limited number of observations: deathsi∼Binomialnipi logitpi=α+β×yeari+ui ui∼N0τ2 Here, pi and ni represent the mortality probability and sample size, respectively, in study i. The α term is the intercept, and β captures the changes in the log‐odds of death over calendar time (year was centered by subtracting the mean publication year). A logisticμ=0,s=1 prior was placed on α, corresponding to a uniform prior over the probability scale. A normalμ=0,s.d.=0.04 prior was placed on β. The standard deviation of this distribution corresponds to an approximate absolute range of ± 1.5% per year for baseline mortality risk levels. This reflects an informative and plausible prior based on the assumption that mortality risk changes gradually over time. Finally, a weakly informative exponentialλ=1 prior was applied to the between‐study standard deviation τ, providing weak regularisation of heterogeneity.

This model was used in two ways. First, studies were added sequentially in chronological order, so that at each step, the posterior from studies 1 through k‐1 became the prior when study k was incorporated, yielding a cumulative posterior distribution. Second, a separate model was fit to assess differences in mortality between decades, with publication decade modelled as a categorical variable. Estimates of mortality rates are reported as posterior means with 95% credible intervals (CrI). The Bayesian analyses were performed with R version 4.5.0 using the brms (v2.22.0) R package [51, 52].

Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence Assessment

We evaluated study‐level risk of bias using the 10‐ne of the most striking findings of studies developed by [53] Hoy et al. The tool was applied to all included studies, which were classified as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias (Table S2, Figure S1). Two authors (MH and MS) independently assessed the methodological quality of each study in duplicate. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third author (FI) was consulted when consensus could not be reached. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria were used to assess the overall certainty of the evidence (high, moderate, low, or very low) supporting the primary and secondary outcomes [54]. Given that the GRADE approach was originally designed for comparative intervention studies, in the absence of formal guidance for its application to prevalence studies, we followed pragmatic adaptations used in prior meta‐analyses and prognostic studies (PMID: 25775931). Thus, the GRADE level can be downgraded due to the risk of bias, indirectness of evidence, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias [54]. Outcomes with fewer than 35 events were reduced by two GRADE levels for imprecision, and outcomes with fewer than 300 events were reduced by one GRADE level for imprecision.

Results

Search Results

The systematic search identified a total of 72 manuscripts. These represented 44 distinct placebo or standard‐care mortality observations, derived from 34 unique primary studies conducted between 1971 and 2024, including 1586 patients (Figure S2). Several studies contributed multiple data points at distinct follow‐up intervals (28, 60, and/or 90 days), leading to a hierarchical data structure with repeated observations nested within studies.

The distribution of follow‐up intervals was notably skewed: 21 (61%) studies reported only 28‐day mortality, 4 (11%) reported only 90‐day outcomes, 6 (18%) included 28 and 90‐day outcomes, 1 (3%) reported 60 and 90‐day outcomes, 1 (3%) reported 28 and 60‐day outcomes, and just 1 (3%) reported all three timepoints. The evidence base was also temporally skewed, with 55% of mortality observations published after 2010.

Crude Mortality Estimates

We conducted random‐effects meta‐regression to determine pooled mortality rates across predefined intervals, focusing on placebo or standard‐care groups to reflect untreated sAH outcomes. At 28 days of follow‐up, the pooled mortality was estimated at 26.8% (95% CI: 21.0%–33.5%, 95% prediction interval 6.8%–64.6%). By 60 days, the estimate increased to 35.1% (95% CI: 28.3%–42.5%, 95% prediction interval: 9.8%–72.9%). By 90 days, the pooled mortality further rose to 43.7% (95% CI: 34.6%–53.3%, 95% prediction interval: 13.4%–79.7%) (Figure 1). The analysis, using a random‐effects meta‐regression model with follow‐up time as a fixed effect and study‐level random intercept, revealed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 83.3%; τ2 = 0.64), as expected for single‐arm pooled proportion meta‐analyses [49]. Inspection of logit mortality via bubble plots confirmed a positive correlation between follow‐up duration and mortality risk.

The cumulative Bayesian analysis yielded complementary insights. Posterior means for 28‐day mortality declined steadily from > 50% in the 1970s to about 25% after 2000, and corresponding 95% CrI contracted with accumulating information. By contrast, 90‐day posterior means converged toward approximately 40% in contemporary studies, although a wide credible interval persisted owing to sparse long‐term data (Figures 2, S3).

Exploration of Clinical Determinants

Systematic data limitations restricted covariate analyses: MELD at admission was not reported in 61% of studies, while mDF in 27%, and prevalence of HRS in 50%. Age and sex ratio were more consistently included, missing in only 20% and 18% of studies, respectively. As multiple‐imputation assumptions were not justifiable, each covariate was analysed separately in complete‐case subsets.

Continuous Covariates

Scatterplot matrices showed no consistent linear association between the log‐odds of mortality and MELD, mDF, HRS, age, sex ratio, or publication year across the full dataset. However, in the subset of studies reporting MELD (n = 12), a multivariable model adjusting for follow‐up duration identified a significant positive association between MELD and mortality (β = + 0.20 per MELD point; 95% CI: + 0.03 to + 0.38; p = 0.037) (Figure 3).

Model Selection

A grid search of 32 competing hierarchical models identified follow‐up duration alone as the most explanatory and parsimonious predictor of mortality. Adding publication year slightly improved model fit but was penalised for complexity, and additional covariates were either not significant or offered negligible improvements (ΔAICc > 2)

Discussion

AH is a condition associated with high short‐ and long‐term mortality [2]. However, it remains unclear how mortality has changed in recent decades in relation to advances in supportive care for these patients. The results of this systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrate the high mortality associated with severe AH despite advances in clinical management. Our pooled estimates show that mortality remains substantial, with rates rising from 26.8% at 28 days to 43.7% at 90 days, underscoring the urgency of improving treatment strategies for this patient population. These findings align with previous research indicating that severe AH continues to have a high burden on patients, healthcare systems, and society, and that current therapies have not significantly reduced mortality rates over the past several decades [1, 2, 4, 13].

One of the most striking findings of our analysis is the persistently high mortality in patients with severe AH. Notably, the principal determinant of mortality across studies was follow‐up duration rather than year of publication. In our hierarchical and meta‐regression models, each additional day of follow‐up increased the odds of death by approximately 1.4%, while calendar‐year effects were not significant. Our cumulative Bayesian analysis reinforces this observation: although short‐term survival has improved modestly, reflected by a decline in 28‐day mortality from over 50% in the 1970s to approximately 25% after 2000, 90‐day mortality has plateaued at around 40%–50%. A Bayesian model of pooled mortality by decade also shows this downward trend from the 1970s to the 1980s. The 95% CrIs in this analysis are non‐overlapping between the 1970s and the 2010s, suggesting a statistically credible improvement from the earliest part of the study period; however, the 95% CrIs between the 1980s and 2020s are all overlapping, suggesting little statistically credible improvement across this latter part of the study period (Figure S3). These findings highlight the biological inevitability of disease progression in untreated AH and challenge assumptions that modern supportive care alone has substantially improved long‐term outcomes [2, 6]. Similar to what has been published, even in patients with non‐severe AH [55].

Importantly, clinical severity influenced outcomes. Higher MELD scores at baseline were independently associated with greater mortality (β = + 0.20 per point; p = 0.025), confirming prior evidence supporting MELD's prognostic utility in AH [8, 9]. Multiple studies have evaluated and validated the use of MELD as the best score to define severity in severe AH, surpassing the mDF.8,10,118,11 When examining the association between mortality and other variables through continuous covariates, scatterplot matrices revealed no consistent linear relationship between mortality and HRS, mDF score, age, or sex ratio. However, mDF scores were often unreported, limiting their evaluative power. These omissions highlight a critical challenge in AH research: the heterogeneity and inconsistency in reporting disease severity across studies.

Multiple therapies have been studied in severe AH, with some demonstrating benefits, particularly in 28‐day mortality. The use of corticosteroids in patients with severe AH has shown a reduction in 28‐day mortality in several studies [7, 11, 23]. However, no evidence has been found for a benefit in mortality at longer time points, such as 90 or 180 days. Similarly, studies evaluating the efficacy of glucocorticoids combined with NAC demonstrated an increased 1‐month survival, but no benefit was observed for 6‐month survival [32]. Studies evaluating the effect of G‐CSF have shown mixed results, with some studies in Asia reporting a survival benefit, while others in Europe have failed to demonstrate any benefit [56]. However, in our analysis of supportive co‐interventions (corticosteroids, NAC, G‐CSF) in placebo or standard‐care arms, we failed to find consistent survival benefits. This likely reflects both methodological limitations and biological plausibility, given that these therapies were often used as post‐enrollment or rescue interventions. While prior trials support corticosteroids for short‐term mortality reduction in select patients, our pooled analyses caution against overinterpreting unstructured or heterogeneous treatment data.

Our review is subject to several limitations inherent in the available literature. First, the studies included in our analysis varied considerably in terms of study design, patient population, and therapeutic interventions, which could introduce bias and affect the generalisability of our findings. Although a random‐effects meta‐regression model was used to account for these differences, substantial heterogeneity is expected in pooled proportion meta‐analyses of this kind. The observed I2 (83.3%) reflects this inherent variability, rather than inconsistency, and the pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, we faced challenges related to missing or incomplete data, particularly concerning the reporting of key variables such as MELD and mDF scores. This missing data limited the depth of our analysis and reduced the robustness of our findings. Additionally, some studies used liver biopsy for diagnosis, while others relied on the NIAAA criteria. Moreover, our exploration of clinical determinants of mortality was hampered by the inconsistent reporting of several important factors, including age, sex, and co‐interventions like the use of corticosteroids or NAC. While some studies reported these variables consistently, others did not, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions about their impact on mortality. In particular, the lack of standardisation in reporting treatment regimens, including the use of corticosteroids, makes it difficult to assess the efficacy of pharmacological therapies in improving survival. Finally, day 60 mortality was reported by only three studies. Although these data were not included in the Bayesian time‐trend model, they were incorporated in the frequentist meta‐regression model, and the sparsity at this time point may have contributed to residual heterogeneity and reduced precision of estimates for time trends. Further data at intermediate time points would strengthen future analyses. Lastly, although we applied the GRADE framework to evaluate the certainty of evidence, we acknowledge the absence of formal guidance for its application in prevalence or single‐arm mortality studies, and the downgrading thresholds we used reflect pragmatic adaptations reported in prior literature.

The persistently high mortality observed in sAH, despite decades of clinical research, highlights an urgent need for more effective therapies and better‐designed clinical trials. Our findings emphasise the importance of incorporating MELD‐based risk stratification, standardised diagnostic criteria, and longer‐term outcomes, beyond the traditional 28‐day window, into future trial protocols. The limited and inconsistent benefits of current interventions, combined with substantial heterogeneity in study designs and reporting, underscore the necessity for harmonised methodologies and robust endpoint definitions. Moreover, the strong association between follow‐up duration and mortality suggests that trials must evaluate sustained efficacy and integrate pathways for early liver transplantation where appropriate. By addressing these gaps, future research can more accurately assess therapeutic impact and move the field toward improved survival and care for patients with sAH.

In conclusion, this systematic review illustrated that mortality in sAH remains alarmingly high, with pooled estimates of 26.8%, 35.1%, and 43.7% at 28, 60, and 90 days, respectively, despite advances in supportive care. No significant improvement in short‐term mortality was observed over the past five decades, underscoring the persistent lack of effective therapies. These findings highlight the urgent need for better risk stratification, timely referral for liver transplantation, and novel therapeutic strategies to improve survival in patients with sAH. Moreover, they underscore the importance of implementing preventive measures to address modifiable risk factors and reduce the incidence of sAH.
