Level of vitamin A deficiency and associated factors among pregnant women in Wolaita Sodo, Southern Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Eyerusalem Endrias Toma, Samson Kastro Dake, Dibora Teferi Haile, Abebe Bitew Zegeye, Teshome Assefa Gebeyehu, Asrat Yehualashet Aklile, Misganaw Andualem Tamire, Tadesse G/Giyorgis Takele, Kelemua Tesfaye Wolde, Elazar Tadesse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin A deficiency is a public health problem in Ethiopia. However, there is paucity of evidence in Vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women in Ethiopia. Thus, this study aimed to determine the level of vitamin A deficiency and identify associated factors among pregnant women.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Wolaita Sodo, Southern Ethiopia from May-July 2020. Four hundred twenty two pregnant women were selected by using simple random sampling. Serum retinol concentration was determined by using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography. Data were analyzed in RStudio and binary logistic regression analysis was done. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was reported.ResultsThe prevalence of vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women was 21.8% and the mean serum retinol level was 0.92 µmol/L. Women Aged 30 years or older (AOR = 7.80, 95% CI: 1.69, 36.01), did not attend ANC follow-up (AOR = 5.38, 95% CI: 1.55, 18.71), and did not consume green leafy vegetables (AOR = 2.70, 95% CI: 1.16, 6.29) were more likely to become vitamin A deficient. On the other hand, women from a household with middle (AOR = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.005, 0.31), richer (AOR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.31), and richest (AOR = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.003, 0.18) wealth index, and have a dietary diversity score of four or more (AOR, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.12) had a lower odds of being vitamin A deficient.ConclusionsThe prevalence of vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women in the study area was high. Food-based approaches that focus on dietary diversification like gardening of vegetables and poultry production should be promoted. Health extension workers should recommend pregnant women to attend ANC. We recommend further studies to perform serum alpha-1-acid glycoprotein and C - reactive protein analyses to adjust for inflammation.Keywords: Vitamin A deficiency, Pregnant women, Ethiopia

Background

Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) is a tissue concentration of vitamin A low enough to have adverse health consequences to the extent that physiological functions are impaired, even if there is no evidence of clinical deficiency [1]. VAD can be characterized as a severe, moderate or mild public health problem based on its prevalence level in a population [2]. Together with anemia and iodine deficiency, VAD has been identified as a global health priority in terms of micronutrient deficiencies [3]. It affects all age groups, yet, pregnant and breastfeeding women are most at risk, particularly those living in the poorer regions of the world [3].

Globally, VAD is estimated to affect around 19 million pregnant women, with 9.8 million affected by night blindness. About 7.8% of pregnant women in Africa have night blindness and 15.3% have low serum retinol concentrations [2]. Studies conducted in the southern and north-western part of Ethiopia reported higher prevalence ranging from 17% to 38% [4–6].

Low intake of vitamin A during nutritionally demanding periods in life such as pregnancy greatly raises the risk of health consequences of VAD [2].VAD during pregnancy might cause maternal anemia and lower hemoglobin level in newborns [7]. Maternal VAD may also decrease renal size of the infant at birth [1], increase risk of preterm delivery and maternal anemia [8], may lead to intrauterine growth retardation [9], ano-rectal malformation [10], high risk of preeclampsia [11], and vertical transmission of human immune-deficiency virus [11]. Though pregnant women are susceptible to VAD throughout the pregnancy, they become highly susceptible during the third trimester of pregnancy owing to accelerated fetal development and physiological increase in blood volume during this period [12].

Studies conducted over the decades consistently indicated the public-health significance of VAD in Ethiopia. However, the full extent of the problem in pregnant women is not known since most of the studies were carried out on preschool children. Furthermore, many studies have focused on women attending antenatal care services, thereby excluding pregnant women who do not utilize health facilities and potentially underestimating the true burden of vitamin A deficiency in the community. The community-based evidence on the prevalence of vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women is particularly lacking in the study area. Thus, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of vitamin A deficiency and associated factors among pregnant women in the study area.

Methods

Study setting

Wolaita Sodo town Administration is an administrative capital of Wolaita zone in south Ethiopia located at 380 km south from Addis Ababa. It is located at 6° 49’ N latitude and 39° 47’ E longitude with the altitude of about 1900 m, and with an area of 830 square kilometers. It has a census-projected population of 180,260; 2,838 were pregnant women. The town has three sub-cities and 24 kebeles (lower administrative unit). Five hospitals, 3 health centers, 11 health posts, and more than 20 private clinics deliver health services to the population in the town. The main staple foods of the population are teff and maize.

Study design and period

We did a community-based cross-sectional study involving pregnant women from May 15 through July 1 in 2020.

Source population and sampling

All pregnant women who reside in Wolaita Sodo town were the source population, and randomly selected pregnant women were studied. All pregnant women residing in the study area, irrespective of gestational age, who had lived in the area for at least six months were included in the study. Pregnant women who were critically ill with medical conditions that prevented them from participating in the interview or anthropometric assessment, those with severe mental illness that impaired their ability to provide reliable information or informed consent, and those who were unavailable after three repeated household visits were excluded from the study. No exclusions were made based on gestational age, parity, or obstetric characteristics to ensure that the study population represented the full spectrum of pregnant women in the community.

A sample size of 422 was calculated with the following assumptions; 95% confidence level, 80% power, 5% margin of error, expected proportion of 50%, and 10% non-response rate. The sampling frame was developed from the pregnancy registration of Health Extension Workers in each kebele. The HEWs used their routinely updated family folders and community registration records to identify households with pregnant women. The initial list was then verified through household visits by the data collectors to confirm pregnancy status, residence in the study area for at least six months, and to remove duplicate entries or women who were no longer eligible. After verification, each eligible pregnant woman was assigned a unique identification number, and the final list served as the sampling frame. The study participants were then selected by using simple random sampling technique.

Variables and measurements

Exposure variables and covariates

Socio-demographic and socio-economic: age, educational status, occupation, family size, marital status, religion, and wealth index.

Wealth index was used to measure the household socioeconomic status. Durable household assets and household possessions were used to develop a composite score for wealth index.

Diet: Dietary diversity, animal source foods consumption, and staple food. Dietary diversity was measured using dietary diversity score (DDS) from three non-consecutive days 24 h recalls. Based on Food and Agricultural Organization (FAO) recommendations, foods were categorized into 10 groups [13]. The responses were categorized as “Yes” if at least one food item was consumed and “No” when a food item in the group was not consumed. The results were summed and women who consumed < 4 food items were considered as having poor dietary diversity score [13].

Obstetric histories: number of childbirth, gestational age of the current pregnancy, birth interval, and antenatal care (ANC) visit.

Outcome

The outcome measure was vitamin A deficiency among pregnant women. Women with serum retinol concentration less than 0.7µmol/l were considered to have vitamin A deficiency [5].

Data collection instruments and procedure

Structured questionnaire was adopted from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) and Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations [13, 14]. Three data collectors were trained on the questionnaire, participants’ selection, and ethics. The questionnaire was pretested on 21 respondents which were not part of the main study.

Blood sample collection and analyses

Two experienced laboratory technologists were trained on blood samples collection. Approximately 5 ml of blood sample was collected via venipuncture from median cubital vein using sterile trace element-free evacuated blood collection tubes. Samples were transported in cold boxes containing frozen gel packs to Wolaita Sodo University comprehensive and teaching hospital laboratory facility promptly for serum separation and storage. The blood samples were allowed to clot for 20 min, centrifuged at 3000 rotations per minute for 10 min, and serum was separated. Serum was kept frozen at -20 °C until transported to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) laboratory for analyses. During transportation to EPHI, the serum was kept in an ice box protected from direct light, and kept frozen at -20 °C until analyses.

Serum retinol concentration was determined using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). The analysis was performed following standard procedures. Initially 5, 10, 20, 40 and 60 µg/dl of retinol standards was prepared. A suitable volume (100 µl) serum was transferred to a glass test tube. Internal standard (100 µl retinyl acetate solution) was added directly to the sample. An equal volume of ethanol was added to denature and precipitate proteins. The sample was mixed with a vortex for about 20 s. Then 1 ml of hexane was added and mixed with vortex for 45 s. After centrifugation at 4000 rates per minute for 10 min, the upper hexane layer was carefully removed using a micropipette and transferred into a second tube. Again another 1 ml of hexane was added to the first tube and the process of extraction is repeated. Finally, the hexane was evaporated with a stream of dry nitrogen and the residual is reconstituted in 200 µl methanol. The HPLC was calibrated using the standard and retinol was detected with an ultraviolet detector at 325 nm [15, 16].

Data analysis

Data were entered into Epi data software version 3.5.1 and extracted to excel 2016. The data were analyzed by using RStudio 2022.12.0 + 353. Descriptive analyses (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation) were computed where appropriate. Wealth index was computed by using principal component analysis. The generated wealth index value was divided into five categories: poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest. A binary logistic regression model predicting vitamin A deficiency was built in RStudio. Multivariate regression analysis was conducted to control for confounders. Initially, all relevant explanatory variables were included in the full multivariate regression model. Then, by using the ‘step’ command under RStudio, all possible alternative models were generated. The Akaike information criteria (AIC) was used to select the best model among the possible models and the regression model with the lowest AIC value was selected as the final model. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05 and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% confidence interval was reported.

Prevalence and factors associated with vitamin A deficiency

The mean serum retinol level was 0.92 µmol/L with standard deviation of 0.30. The overall prevalence of VAD among pregnant women was 21.8% (95% CI: 17.8, 26.1). The prevalence of VAD was 4.6%, 32.2%, and 63.2% among pregnant women in their first, second and third trimester respectively. The mean retinol level was higher for women who had a dietary diversity score (DDS) of four or more compared to those women who had less than four DDS (Fig. 1).

Comparison of serum retinol level with the dietary diversity score

In this study, pregnant women aged 30 years or older had 7.8 times higher odds of becoming vitamin A deficient compared to women below 20 years of age (AOR = 7.80, 95% CI: 1.69, 36.01). Women who did not attend ANC follow-up were 5.38 times more likely to have VAD compared to their counterparts (AOR = 5.38, 95% CI: 1.55, 18.71). Pregnant women who were from a household with middle, richer and richest wealth index had 3% (AOR = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.005, 0.31), 10% (AOR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.31), and 2% (AOR = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.003, 0.18) lower chance of becoming vitamin A deficient respectively. Women with a dietary diversity score of 4 or more are 4% less likely to become vitamin A deficient (AOR = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.12). On the other hand, women who did not consume green leafy vegetables the previous day have 2.7 times higher chance of becoming vitamin A deficient (AOR = 2.70, 95% CI: 1.16, 6.29) (Table 4).

Discussion

We employed a community-based cross-sectional study design to assess the prevalence of vitamin A deficiency and associated factors among pregnant women in Wolaita Sodo, Southern Ethiopia. According to this study, 21.8% of pregnant women were VAD, exceeding the estimated global prevalence among pregnant women of 15% or less [2]. Despite the ongoing maternal nutrition interventions in Ethiopia, pregnant women continue to experience VAD. Age of the women, ANC follow up history, wealth index, dietary diversity, and green vegetable consumption were significantly associated variables observed in this study.

The reported prevalence of vitamin A deficiency in China (5.3%) [17] and Nigeria (15.8%) [18] were lower than the results of the current study. A comparable prevalence was reported in Bangladesh (18.8%) [19], Egypt (23.5%) [7], and Northwest Ethiopia (18.4%) [6, 20]. On the other hand, a higher prevalence was reported by other studies conducted in Sidama (37.9%) [4], and Northwest Ethiopia (38.5%) [5]. These differences are likely multifactorial rather than attributable to a single factor. Countries such as China have experienced substantial improvements in maternal nutrition, dietary diversification, food fortification, and access to antenatal care, all of which contribute to better maternal micronutrient status. Differences between Ethiopian studies may reflect variations in rural–urban composition, food availability, socioeconomic status, and maternal healthcare utilization. The present study was conducted in an urban setting where women may have greater access to markets, diversified foods, and health services than women living in rural areas such as Sidama. This explanation is supported by evidence from the same area indicating that rural residence is associated with inadequate consumption of vitamin A-rich foods [21]. Moreover, a study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia used a higher cut-off point for serum retinol level.

The prevalence of VAD increased markedly with advancing gestational age, rising from 4.6% in the first trimester to 63.2% in the third trimester, indicating an inverse relationship between serum retinol concentration and gestational age. Although pregnant women are susceptible to vitamin A deficiency throughout gestation, susceptibility is at its highest during the third trimester of pregnancy due to accelerated fetal development and the physiological increase in blood volume during this period [22]. Similar findings were observed in studies conducted in Sidama [4], China [17], and Brazil [23], suggesting that this pattern is biologically plausible across different populations.

In the current study, older pregnant women were about eight times more likely to have VAD compared to their counterparts. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Sidama [4], Tigray [24], and rural Nepal [25]. Although increasing maternal age itself may not directly reduce serum retinol concentration, older women are more likely to have experienced repeated pregnancies and prolonged breastfeeding, which can gradually deplete vitamin A stores if dietary replenishment is inadequate. On the contrary, evidence from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in the United States reported relatively stable serum retinol concentrations among women of reproductive age [26]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in nutritional status, parity, dietary quality, food fortification practices, and healthcare systems between high-income and low-income settings, suggesting that the observed association in our study is likely explained by reproductive and socioeconomic factors rather than age alone.

Women who had not attended ANC were significantly more likely to have VAD than those who had ANC follow-up, consistent with findings from South Gondar and North Showa in Ethiopia [27, 28]. ANC attendance may improve maternal nutritional status through repeated dietary counseling, early identification of nutritional risks, treatment of infections that impair nutrient metabolism, and linkage to maternal nutrition interventions. ANC attendance may also increase health-seeking behavior. Therefore, the observed association might be because of the benefits of ANC services and the improved health awareness of women and access to healthcare.

Women from households with a middle or higher wealth status were less likely to become vitamin A deficient, consistently with several other studies [7, 17, 19, 26]. Higher socioeconomic status is often associated with improved dietary diversity, better food security, greater nutrition knowledge, and increased utilization of maternal health services. Conversely, women from poorer households may have limited consumption of animal-source foods, fruits, and vegetables, thereby increasing the risk of inadequate vitamin A intake.

Women who achieved the minimum dietary diversity score had a lower likelihood of VAD, supporting findings from Sidama [4]. This could be possibly because those who consume a diversified diet include vitamin A rich foods to their dietary intake. Similarly, women who did not consume green leafy vegetables were more likely to become vitamin A deficient. Green leafy vegetables contain β-carotenes that are the contributors of vitamin A to our body [29, 30].

This study applied a community-based design, which makes the findings less biased and generalizable. We have also included women who were not attending ANC follow-up. As the study was dependent on biomarkers, the measurements were objective and it exposed the full extent of the deficiencies including the subclinical dimensions.

This study applied a cross-sectional design and thus it is difficult to identify cause effect relationship. Some of the study subjects did not remember the exact date of their last normal menstrual period. The current study did not perform further laboratory analyses like alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP) and C - reactive protein (CRP) to adjust serum vitamin A level for inflammation. We have used stepwise regression with AIC for model selection, and there is a risk of over-fitting and instability of selected predictors.

Conclusions

The prevalence of vitamin A deficiency in the current study constitutes a severe public health problem according to the World Health Organization population-level classification. However, this classification should be interpreted within the context of the study population and design. The significant predictors of vitamin A deficiency were the age of the women, ANC follow-up, socioeconomic status, dietary diversity, and green vegetable consumption.

Food-based approaches that focus on dietary diversification like gardening of vegetables and poultry production should be promoted through full engagement of the agriculture and health sectors. Health extension workers should motivate pregnant women to attend ANC. Health workers should enhance nutrition counseling during ANC to improve women’s awareness regarding the importance of optimal nutrition, dietary diversity, vitamin A-rich food consumption, and additional intake of vitamin A riches foods when gestational age increases. We recommend further studies to perform AGP and CPR laboratory analyses to adjust for inflammation.
