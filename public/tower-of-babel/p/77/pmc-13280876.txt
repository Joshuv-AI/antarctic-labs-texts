Ultrasonic-Assisted Silanization of SiO2 Using TMSPM: Surface Functionalization, Hydrophobicity Enhancement, and Structural Analysis
Authors: Warlinda Eka Triastuti, Sumarno, Suprapto, Bramantyo Airlangga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the structural and chemical changes occurring upon ultrasonic-assisted silanization of SiO2 nanoparticles. While traditional silanization often faces challenges in grafting uniformity, the application of ultrasonication in this work facilitates enhanced interfacial contact and rapid functionalization with 3-(trimethoxysilyl)­propyl methacrylate (TMSPM). The concentration of TMSPM was varied to evaluate correlation between TMSPM concentration gradients, grafting density, and particle size of silanized SiO2 under ultrasonic conditions. FTIR analysis confirmed the successful chemical bonding of TMSPM, evidenced by the intensified Si–O–Si absorption bands and the distinct appearance of methacrylate carbonyl groups. The results indicated that 10% TMSPM can be considered the most effective concentration for SiO2 silanization, as indicated by the substantial increase in carbon content of 31% via EDS analysis and a maximum water contact angle of 128°. This significant shift from hydrophilic to hydrophobic behavior demonstrates the effectiveness of the ultrasonic-assisted approach in producing high-quality, silanized silica fillers. Furthermore, XRD analysis revealed that while the fundamental amorphous structure of SiO2 is preserved, the modification process introduces subtle changes in the short-range structural order of the nanoparticles.

Introduction

Silicon dioxide nanoparticles are widely used in acrylic-based dental resin composites due to their unique properties, including high surface area, chemical stability, cost-effectiveness, and ability to improve the mechanical and physicochemical properties of the matrix. , The effectiveness of silica nanoparticles in increasing stiffness is closely related to their size and surface area. Smaller nanoparticles provide a higher surface area per unit mass, which enhances the interaction with polymer chains. , The incorporation of SiO2 as a filler improves the strength, wear resistance, and overall performance of dental restoration materials. , Despite such benefits, the surface chemistry of silica consists of high amounts of silanol groups (Si–OH), making the silica particles extremely hydrophilic and susceptible to forming strong hydrogen bonds. Consequently, unfunctionalized silica nanoparticles readily form agglomerates and show low affinity toward hydrophobic substrates, thus limiting uniform dispersion within the composite matrix and hindering effective stress transfer across the interface. This problem becomes even more significant in epoxy resins since the presence of hydrophilic silica causes an increase in moisture absorption into the composite, thus reducing the mechanical properties of the material.

To overcome these limitations, surface modification through silanization has become one of the most widely adopted strategies for improving the interfacial properties of silica-based fillers. Silane coupling agents can react with hydroxyl groups on the silica surface through hydrolysis and condensation reactions, leading to the formation of siloxane bonds (Si–O–Si), forming a covalent bond. , Silanization involves the chemical modification of SiO2 surfaces using silane coupling agents, which can significantly improve the interfacial adhesion between the filler and the resin matrix. Trimethoxysilylpropyl methacrylate (TMSPM) is a commonly used silane in dental applications due to its methacrylate functional group, which can covalently bond with the resin. − Although the silanization of silica (SiO2) has been well-known to improve the compatibility between inorganic fillers and polymer matrices, conventional methods that employ long-time magnetic stirring invariably lead to nonuniform grafting and particle agglomeration. ,

Silanization involves the attachment of silane molecules to the SiO2 surface. This process can be optimized using ultrasonic assistance, which allows for better control over the density of introduced functional groups, thereby improving process efficiency and uniformity. Surface processing techniques have been utilized to improve the material properties of various alloys. Ultrasonic treatment can increase surface roughness and modify surface free energy, which is crucial for applications requiring improved adhesion and wettability. In addition, silanization, especially involving methyl groups, is highly effective for increasing surface hydrophobicity of silica through the conversion of hydrophilic silanol groups into hydrophobic Si-CH3 groups. For instance, ultrasonic nanocrystal surface modification (UNSM) of silicon wafers has been shown to reduce reflectivity and improve wear resistance by 23% due to surface roughening and strengthening. − Nano TiC powder dispersions have been improved through ultrasonic treatment, facilitated by the presence of Tween 80. Therefore, the lipophilic and hydrophobic behavior of CaCO3 can be further enhanced through ultrasonic treatment. Previous studies by several authors have demonstrated that ultrasonication can enhance the surface modification of inorganic particles by improving dispersion and promoting more effective interaction between coupling agents and particle surfaces, supporting its application in the present silanization process.

Although several studies have reported the general advantages of silane-treated silica and the application of ultrasonic energy in facilitating silanization was previously reported in the literature, most of the published work only offered a qualitative “before and after” type of comparison, and many of them focus primarily on confirming successful surface modification rather than systematically correlating silane concentration with detailed structural and surface-property evolution. In particular, the relationship between concentration-dependent FTIR changes, surface elemental composition, and wettability behavior is still not always discussed in an integrated manner. There was also a notable absence of detailed studies that could offer an exact correlation between TMSPM concentration gradients, grafting density, and particle size of silanized SiO2 under ultrasonic conditions. The application of ultrasonics to improve the homogeneity of morphology, hydrophobicity, and structural characteristics of the silanized SiO2 is systematically evaluated using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), water contact angle (WCA) analysis, and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Methods

Materials

3-(Trimethoxysilyl)­propyl methacrylate as a silane coupling agent for surface treatment (5% w/w silane relative to SiO2 in 85% cyclohexane) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich with CAS Number: 2530-85-0; SiO2 nanopowder (10–20 nm particle size) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, and cyclohexane anhydrous, 99.5% was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Procedure

Surface Modification of SiO2 Nanoparticles

25 g each of SiO2 NPs was dispersed in 50 mL of cyclohexane and probe sonicated using a Qsonica sonicator (Model Q55, 50 W power, 20 kHz frequency, ϕ = 6 mm) for 10 min to achieve uniform dispersion and minimize particle agglomeration. The probe sonicator was set at 50% power. After dispersion, TMSPM was added dropwise into the suspension at specific concentrations (2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, 10%, and 12.5% w/w relative to SiO2 weight). The silanization reaction was conducted using GT SONIC-P3 Ultrasonic (power 100 W and 40 kHz frequency) for 30 min under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere. The reaction was carried out at 30 °C and allowed to proceed for 30 min under continuous stirring at 300 rpm to ensure homogeneous mixing and consistent interfacial contact between TMSPM and the SiO2 surface. Afterward, the silanized SiO2 were collected and rinsed using cyclohexane, followed by a quick rinse with methanol/water (50:50v/v) with 3 cycles of washes and recovery via centrifugation per solvent. Cyclohexane was used to remove unreacted TMSPM, and the mixture of methanol/water was used to remove unreacted polar reagent. Then, the solvent was removed using a rotary vacuum evaporator at 60 °C for 60 min. The silanized SiO2 was then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h, ground into fine powder, and stored in a desiccator before further characterization.

Characterization

FTIR Spectroscopy

The silanized SiO2 nanoparticles were subjected to FTIR spectroscopy measurements acquired at a temperature of 30 °C using an FTIR spectrometer (Agilent Cary 630 FTIR). The scanning ranges of the FTIR were configured from 400 to 4000 cm–1. The structure composition was determined by processing the spectra using Microlab software.

Scanning Electron Microscopy and X-ray Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

The impact of the TMSPM layer on the micromorphology and elemental composition of the tuff aggregates was investigated using scanning electron microscopy and X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS). The scanning electron microscope employed was a FlexSEM 1000, with specifications including an accelerating voltage of 0.3–30 kV, a magnification range of 8–10 k, and a working distance of 10 mm. The FlexSEM 1000 is a compact Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) that offers high-resolution imaging up to 4.0 nm at 20 kV. It features a tungsten electron gun, supports 0.3–20 kV accelerating voltage, and is equipped with SE and BSE detectors. The system includes a motorized 5-axis stage and supports both high vacuum and variable pressure modes. Samples were further gold sputtered in a sputtering device, Coxem type SPT-20, and analyzed using FlexSEM 1000 to observe the topographic changes on TMSPM silane-treated surfaces.

Water Contact Angle

The hydrophobic performance of both unsilanized SiO2 and silanized SiO2 with TMSPM was evaluated based on changes in water contact angle. Measurements were carried out using the KRÜSS DSA100 contact angle analyzer (Germany), which offers a measurement range of 0–180°, a precision of ±0.1°, and an image magnification capability of 0.7×. The measurement is performed by dispensing three water droplets onto the sample surface. The droplet is photographed after a contact period of 0.2 s, and the water contact angle is subsequently analyzed and measured from this image. For each sample, at least three independent droplets were deposited at different surface locations to minimize local heterogeneity effects.

X-ray Diffraction

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was utilized as the principal analytical technique to characterize the crystalline phases and determine the crystal structure of the investigated materials, using Rigaku MiniFlex 600. The apparatus was operated with X-rays (fluorescence X-rays, etc.), a tube voltage of 40 kV, a current of 50 mA, and scanning from 3° to 80° at a speed of 10°/min. The calculation formula for the crystallinity index and the results obtained from XRD analysis data are as follows: 1 I=Icr−IamIcrx100% In the above equation, I is the crystallinity index and expressed as a percentage, I cr is the maximum intensity (height) of the main crystalline peak, and I am is the minimum intensity (height) of the amorphous background.

Results and Discussion

FTIR Analysis of Unsilanized and Silanized SiO2

Silanization is highly effective in enhancing the adhesion properties of silica-based materials. Its mechanism involves the reaction between two reactive functional groups and the coupling with various inorganic and organic materials. As a result, silane acts as an adhesion promoter, improving the bonding between different materials. , The adhesive strength of silane depends on the material. The strongest adhesion is achieved with silica, glass, and quartz, which form strong siloxane (Si–O–Si) bonds through condensation with surface hydroxyl groups on the substrate. There are two steps involved in the bonding of resin substrates using a silane coupling agent. The schematics of TMSPM hydrolysis and dehydration condensation and the possible reactions between SiO2 and hydrolyzed TMSPM are indicated in Figure .

Schematic diagram of ultrasonic cavitation and silanization mechanism of the formation of silanized SiO2 using TMSPM.

As shown in Figure , the FTIR analysis confirmed that SiO2 was successfully silanized using TMSPM, as evidenced by the appearance of a carbonyl (CO) absorption band around 1699.7 cm–1 in the spectrum of the silanized SiO2. Furthermore, the intensity of Si–O–Si in the range of 1051.1 cm–1 increased, indicating the formation of bonds between SiO2 and TMSPM, commonly used silane coupling agent, is typically employed as a surface pretreatment for inorganic fillers to improve adhesion with polymer matrices. At pure SiO2, the absorption band at 805 cm–1 is attributed to the bending vibration of Si–O bonds, which is characteristic of the SiO2 bonding. These silanol groups are unstable and undergo dehydration condensation, forming oligomeric structures with Si–O–Si bonds. The band at 1051.1 cm–1 corresponds to the transverse optical (TO) and longitudinal optical (LO) modes of the Si–O–Si structure. The FTIR spectra of silanized SiO2 show that TMSPM forms covalent bonds with the SiO2 nanoparticles through hydrolysis and condensation reactions, leading to the formation of Si–O–Si bonds as indicated by peaks in the 1051.1 cm–1 region. The position of the band corresponding to the asymmetric stretch of the Si–O–Si group remained at a similar spectral region for both unsilanized and silanized silica samples with a slight shift, which could not be distinctly observed in Figure due to the broad nature of the absorption band of the silica material. At TMSPM concentrations ranging from 2.5% to 12.5%, the CC groups of TMSPM interact with hydroxyl (−OH) groups on the SiO2 surface. This interaction leads to a downward shift in the CC vibrational frequency from its typical free-state value (ca. 1632 cm–1) to approximately 1543 cm–1, suggesting the band of CC. These peaks all confirm the chemical surface modification of SiO2 nanoparticles with TMSPM. The broad absorption band in 3600–3900 cm–1 observed in the silanized SiO2 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of isolated OH groups, either from nonassociated hydroxyl groups or adsorbed water molecules, indicating the presence of surface silanol (Si–OH) groups associated with hydrogen-bonding interactions on the SiO2 surface. The absorption band found around 1408 cm–1 in the silanized sample is related to symmetric stretching vibration peak −CH2 or −CH3 groups, , derived from the organic part of the TMSPM molecule. The band confirms the presence of the silane species on the silica surface after the silanization reaction. The small inflection in the spectrum at around 900 cm–1 is probably associated with vibrations of silane or Si–OH interfacial bonds, possibly derived from residual silanol species or partially hydrolyzed silane molecules. ,

FTIR spectra of (A) TMSPM, SiO2 unsilanized, and SiO2 silanized using TMSPM and (B) SiO2 silanized using 10% TMSPM.

At higher TMSPM concentrations (>10%), TMSPM molecules that do not react with SiO2 may physisorb onto the first silane layer through weak interactions, namely, by forming hydrogen bonds between the free carbonyl groups (CO) of TMSPM and the hydroxyl groups (−OH) on the first TMSPM layer. This results in a double layer, where the first layer is strongly bonded due to covalent bonding, while the second layer is weakly attached.

From Table , it can be concluded that there is a significant trend of concentration-dependent grafting of TMSPM molecules onto the surface of silica, as confirmed by a corresponding increase in the normalized intensity ratio of A (CO)/A ref (Si–O–Si). The band located at ∼1699 cm–1 is attributed to the carbonyl stretching vibration of methacrylate groups in TMSPM, confirming the presence of organic groups, whereas the band located at ∼1058 cm–1 is associated with stretching vibrations of Si–O–Si groups, which represent silica. This band was chosen as a reference peak to avoid structural variations. When ultrasonic treatment was applied to a solution of 2.5% TMSPM, it was found that the normalized intensity ratio increased from 0.01032 (nonultrasonic solution) to 0.01389, confirming that ultrasonic treatment is beneficial for improving the efficiency of hydrolysis and condensation reactions. This is because ultrasonic waves can cause microturbulence, which helps to homogenize solutions, accelerate hydrolysis of methoxy groups (−Si–OCH3) to form silanol groups (−Si–OH) as shown in Figure , and enhance the rate of reaction between hydroxyl groups and hydrolyzed silane groups, resulting in a greater number of covalent bonds between hydroxyl groups and hydrolyzed silane groups.

When the concentration of TMSPM was increased from 2.5% to 10%, it was found that there was a significant increase in the normalized intensity ratio, reaching a maximum value of 0.04766 at a concentration of 10%. This confirms that increasing the concentration of TMSPM can lead to a greater coverage of silica surfaces, as a greater number of hydrolyzed silanol groups are present, which can participate in interfacial condensation reactions. However, it was found that at a concentration of 12.5%, the normalized intensity ratio decreased from 0.04766 to 0.04037, which suggests that at a certain critical concentration, a significant amount of self-reaction between silane groups can occur, resulting in a polymeric network. This can lead to a multilayer film, where a certain amount of silane groups is bonded to a previously grafted silane group instead of directly reacting with a hydroxyl group on the silica surface. The increase of the O–H band refers to the presence of the remaining −OH groups and the possibility of the formation of the new silanol group during the hydrolysis of TMSPM. The constant levels represent the fact that the hydroxyl group still remains on the surface even after the silanization process. − The impact of ultrasonic treatment could also be seen in the improved grafting efficiency of the silane molecules, leading to a more complete consumption of surface hydroxyl groups, which would be reflected in a further reduction of the O–H band intensity. , The decrease in the –OH stretching peak in the FTIR spectrum after silanization confirms the successful reaction of hydroxyl groups with silicon atoms from the siloxane, thereby reducing SiO2–SiO2 interactions that lead to particle agglomeration.

Acoustic cavitation results in the collapse of bubbles present in the liquid, thereby increasing the temperature and pressure. During the process of silanization, the presence of localized hot spots, an increase in the rate of nucleation, and the distribution of fine particles help in favoring the silanization process, as illustrated in Figure . − The increased intensity of the absorption peak at wavenumber 1720 cm–1 (CO, carbonyl group) in the FTIR spectrum, which correlates with a 31% increase in carbon content in the EDS data (for the 10% TMSPM sample), proves the high efficiency of this method. Ultrasonic energy facilitates the formation of denser covalent bonds between the methacrylate groups and the silica surface, surpassing the efficiency typically achieved through conventional magnetic stirring. , Ultrasonic energy optimizes the interfacial microstructure by reducing the number and size of bubbles, increasing the actual bonding area, and facilitating the formation of more chemical bonds at the interface. Ultrasonic energy induces cavitation, which generates localized high temperatures and pressures. During the high-pressure phase, microscopic bubbles undergo implosion or collapse, generating intense localized shear forces. This phenomenon not only forces entrapped bubbles to coalesce and rise to the surface (degassing process) but also provides sufficient mechanical energy to break the siloxane bonds on the silica or microcellulose surface. The cleavage of these bonds significantly increases the density of reactive silanol groups on the material’s surface. These active sites subsequently interact extensively with the methacrylate groups of TMSPM, triggering the formation of a dense covalent bond network, such as Si–O–Si.

For the nonsonicated sample, the proportionally higher value of intensity for the Si–O–Si indicates less absorption, and this suggests that the condensation of new siloxane bonds is less compared to the ultrasonic-assisted samples. This can be related to the less condensed level between the hydrolyzed TMSPM and the silanol groups, possibly due to the restriction of mass transfer and the unavailability of cavitation mechanisms. Without the aid of ultrasonic energy, the silanol group-bearing particles do not get properly dispersed, and the diffusion of the silane to all the available −OH sites is limited, resulting in an uneven distribution of the silane with more unreacted silanol groups available on the surfaces. Thus, while the CO band suggests the successful binding of the TMSPM to the substrate, the efficiency of the silanization process and the modification of the substrate from hydrophilic to organophilic is less compared to the ultrasonic-assisted sample.

SEM-EDS Analysis

The morphology and microstructure of silanized SiO2 were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), demonstrating a highly ordered and uniform surface architecture. The evolution of the microstructure in the thin film, as evidenced from the SEM images, indicates that there is a clear trend in the microstructure evolution that depends on the dopant concentration. In the absence of doping, the surface morphology is found to be globular in shape and uniformly distributed over the surface. The surface microstructure evolution is also accompanied by an increase in the size of the particles, as evidenced by the SEM results. From Figure , increasing the concentration of silane generally leads to an increase in particle size. This is due to the enhanced nucleation and growth processes facilitated by higher silane availability. , At 12.5% TMSPM, a pronounced increase in particle size (∼0.10 μm) is observed, accompanied by larger variability. This behavior likely results from excessive silane self-condensation and multilayer formation, promoting interparticle siloxane networking and aggregation. The marked particle growth at this concentration is consistent with the reduced hydrophobic performance observed in WCA measurements, suggesting increased surface heterogeneity.

The EDS spectra in Figure reveal significant changes in the surface elemental composition. SiO2 silanized and unsilanized tuff aggregates revealed significant differences in surface morphology, as shown in Figure . SEM image analysis shows that the self-assembled film layer of the silane coupling agent is only partially formed. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of SiO2 and silanized SiO2 using TMSPM at varying concentrations (2.5–12.5%) which is presented in Table revealed significant changes in surface composition, particularly in the carbon (C) from the TMSPM. EDS provides semiquantitative information and mainly reflects near-surface elemental signals; therefore, the carbon values reported in this study are interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute grafting density. , As shown in Table , pristine SiO2 exhibited 0% carbon, whereas all silanized samples showed a clear increase in carbon content, confirming the introduction of organic groups from TMSPM. The carbon signal increased progressively with TMSPM concentration and reached a maximum of 31% for the 10% TMSPM sample, indicating the most effective surface modification under the investigated conditions. However, silanization using 12.5% TMSPM shows signs of surface saturation or a self-condensation phenomenon between TMSPM molecules, which leads to the formation of a silane layer that is ineffective in uniformly covering the substrate surface. At higher TMSPM concentrations, excess silane molecules may undergo self-condensation reactions after hydrolysis, forming siloxane (Si–O–Si) networks between silane molecules rather than exclusively bonding with surface silanol groups. Once the available Si–OH sites on the SiO2 surface become saturated, additional silane molecules preferentially react with each other, leading to multilayer formation. This self-condensation process can produce a less uniform silane layer and increase surface heterogeneity, which may explain the reduced hydrophobic performance observed at concentrations above the most effective level. This reduces the number of silanol groups available to bond with the substrate, leading to a smoother surface morphology and a decrease in water contact angle. −

The 2.5% TMSPM sample prepared without ultrasonication showed only 4% carbon, while the corresponding ultrasonically treated sample exhibited 7% carbon, supporting the beneficial role of ultrasonication in improving interfacial contact and surface coverage. The silanol groups of TMSPM interact with the hydroxylated surface of SiO2 through a condensation reaction, forming covalent Si–O–Si bonds, while the methacrylate groups (which contain carbon) orient outward from the surface and become spectrally dominant in the EDS analysis. ,,, The success of surface silanization is influenced by the balance between concentration, reaction time, and the number of active hydroxyl groups on the substrate. The propagation of ultrasound in a medium produces acoustic cavitation effects, as well as chemical and physical effects associated with bubble oscillation and collapse, such as high-velocity liquid jets, turbulence, shock waves, high pressure, and extreme physical shear. , Ultrasound changed the structure of the material to a certain extent, exposing the internal interaction bonds of the molecules; thereby, changing the intermolecular or intramolecular forces, which in turn helped to form a new macromolecular structure. ,

The EDS data trend is correlated with both FTIR methacrylate-related functional bands results and the WCA trend with increased surface hydrophobicity, as mentioned before. Therefore, SEM-EDS supports successful surface functionalization, while the overall grafting conclusion is strengthened through multitechnique validation.

3D surface visualization was generated from the SEM images to provide a qualitative illustration of relative surface roughness trends among samples. It should be noted that SEM inherently provides two-dimensional morphological information; therefore, the 3D reconstruction does not represent a true quantitative topography measurement and should be interpreted cautiously. The 3D visualization is presented only to support qualitative comparison of surface uniformity, while the main conclusions regarding morphology are based on the original SEM images and are further supported by SEM-EDS and WCA results. SEM shows significant changes in the surface roughness of SiO2 after the silanization process with TMSPM. The topography of pure SiO2 was analyzed in a three-dimensional field with X, Y, and Z axes representing spatial parameters and surface elevation, as shown in Figure . The X and Y axes illustrate the length and width of the scanning area, indicating the observed surface area used to evaluate the overall morphological characteristics. Meanwhile, the Z axis represents the elevation or surface height (roughness amplitude). − In the pure SiO2 sample, the surface exhibited a rough and uneven topographic profile, characterized by sharp peaks and valleys, indicating that the SiO2 surface had not yet undergone any modification. The surface topography distribution does not show a regular pattern along the X–Y axis, indicating that the particles have a granular surface with many active areas or exposed silanol groups (−Si–OH). After silanization, the surface roughness decreases up to a TMSPM concentration of 10%, indicating the formation of a more homogeneous silane layer that evenly covers the particle surface. The reduction in the roughness level suggests a successful reaction between the silanol groups on the SiO2 surface and the methoxysilyl groups of TMSPM, forming covalent bonds and smoothing the SiO2 surface. The formed organosilane layer is capable of covering pores and microprotrusions on the surface. In silanization with 12.5% TMSPM concentration, the SiO2 surface shows an increase in roughness. , This is caused by TMSPM overloading, which leads to excessive silane aggregation and the formation of an uneven cross-linked siloxane network, resulting in a rougher surface morphology. This phenomenon is known as self-condensation or excessive silane oligomerization, which produces a nonhomogeneous silane layer and affects the final surface roughness. − Considering the impact of ultrasonic treatment, sonication during the modification process could enhance the dispersion of TMSPM and facilitate a more uniform reaction with the silica surface, potentially leading to a denser and more extensively cross-linked siloxane network. Sonication has been reported to produce more homogeneous and compact films, which can affect the surface roughness. This implies that sonication could lead to a smoother or more uniformly roughened surface of TMSPM-silanized SiO2. The integration of ultrasonic energy during silanization triggers the phenomenon of acoustic cavitation. The collapse of cavitation bubbles generates high-pressure microjets that effectively break down SiO2 particle agglomerates capable of breaking van der Waals forces between particles, resulting in a more stable and uniform dispersion and preventing the formation of uneven silane layers. This process increases the active surface area available for interaction with TMSPM molecules, resulting in a more homogeneous and chemically stable silane layer. In addition, the energy from cavitation is capable of removing physical contaminant layers such as water or organic residues present on the surface of SiO2, thereby increasing the density of hydroxyl groups that can directly interact with the methoxysilyl groups (−Si­(OCH3)3) of TMSPM through hydrolysis and condensation reactions, as shown in the following reaction in Figure .

Water Contact Angle Analysis

Silanization can increase the hydrophobicity of SiO2 because the modification reduces the number of hydroxyl groups (−OH) on the SiO2 surface and replaces them with hydrophobic organic groups. Silanization with silane coupling agents leads to similar increases in carbon content and changes in surface properties, such as hydrophobicity and surface energy. ,, The condensation results of silanol from TMSPM react with the –OH groups on the surface of SiO2, forming a stable Si–O–Si bond. After silanization, the reduced –OH groups are replaced by carbon chains from TMSPM, which are nonpolar and can reduce the affinity for water, thereby increasing the contact angle. From Figure , the contact angle tends to increase with increasing concentration of TMSPM. In the SiO2-10% TMSPM sample, the largest angle is 128°. In the SiO2-12.5% TMSPM sample, the angle is 63.75°, while in the pure SiO2 sample, the smallest angle is 8.25°. The results of this study indicate that the silanization process of SiO2 has succeeded in changing its surface properties, as well as increasing its hydrophobic properties. This is characterized by a water contact angle greater than 90°, which indicates that the silanized SiO2 surface is difficult for water. The increase in water contact angle at 10% TMSPM concentration was also statistically significant compared to all other concentrations at p < 0.05 by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s posthoc analysis. This further confirms the uniqueness of the 10% concentration with respect to surface coverage.

XRD Analysis

As illustrated in Figure , all samples exhibited diffraction patterns characteristic of predominantly amorphous silica, as indicated by the broad halo centered at 2θ ≈22°. This confirms that the fundamental bulk structure of SiO2 remained amorphous after the silanization process. The observed changes are more reasonably attributed to relative scattering intensity variations and short-range structural ordering effects associated with surface modification, improved particle dispersion, and the formation of a more homogeneous silane layer. SiO2 with 10% TMSPM exhibited the most pronounced intensity change, which is consistent with the most effective surface coverage suggested by SEM-EDS and WCA results. At higher concentrations, the intensity change tended to plateau, which may be related to silane self-condensation, multilayer formation, or increased heterogeneity. , Overall, the XRD results indicate that silanization primarily modifies the surface characteristics while preserving the amorphous bulk structure of SiO2, supporting its potential use as a functional filler for polymer-based composite systems.

X-ray diffraction analysis on SiO2.

In addition, FTIR gives direct chemical evidence on the presence of methacrylate-related functional bands (CO carbonyl) and enhanced siloxane-related features. SEM demonstrates structural variations and better surface evenness after silanization. The introduction of organic groups is also corroborated by SEM-EDS with concentration-dependent enhancement in surface carbon signals. WCA characterizes the change in surface wettability and reveals enhancement of hydrophobicity after TMSPM grafting and XRD results show that the bulk SiO2 structure is still largely amorphous suggesting that silanization happens primarily on the surface rather than triggering a change in its bulk phase. The highly comparable results obtained by these varied methods that have shown a strong consistency prove the successful surface silanization. At low TMSPM concentrations, surface coverage is incomplete, resulting in limited grafting efficiency and lower hydrophobicity. Increasing the concentration of TMSPM improves the availability of silane molecules and enhances surface coverage, leading to improved grafting and wettability. Among the investigated concentrations, 10% TMSPM exhibited the most effective silanization performance, with FTIR, SEM-EDS, XRD, and WCA results consistently indicating successful surface modification and improved morphological uniformity. At higher concentration (12.5%), excessive silane can decrease the performance of silanized SiO2.

Conclusions

(1)FTIR results confirmed the successful attachment of TMSPM through the enhanced Si–O–Si absorption bands and the appearance of methacrylate-related carbonyl (CO) bands, successfully grafting TMSPM onto SiO2. Quantitative analysis using normalized absorbance ratios (A CO/A ref (Si–O–Si)) identified a peak grafting density at 10% TMSPM, indicating the maximum concentration of functional groups attached to the silica surface.(2)The sample silanized with 10% TMSPM exhibited the most effective silanization performance. SEM observations showed a clear morphological transition from a rough and granular surface to a smoother and homogeneous surface at this concentration. This concentration successfully achieved the minimum mean particle size and the most uniform distribution, demonstrating that ultrasonic-assisted silanization effectively prevents reagglomeration at this specific threshold.(3)SEM-EDS analysis revealed a significant increase in surface carbon content, reaching 31% at 10% TMSPM, confirming the presence of organic functional groups introduced by the silane coupling agent.(4)Wettability measurements demonstrated enhanced surface hydrophobicity after silanization, with the maximum water contact angle of 128° obtained at 10% TMSPM, indicating more effective silane coverage on the SiO2 surface and improved morphological uniformity.
