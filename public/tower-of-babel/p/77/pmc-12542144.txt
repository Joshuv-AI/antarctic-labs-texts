A cross-sectional evaluation of rational antibiotic prescribing in Kabul based on WHO indicators
Authors: Hedayatullah Ehsan, Munir Ahmad Ibrahimkhil, Marhabah Ahmadi, Fazel Rahim Wardak, Hasibullah Aminpoor, Abdul Salam, Hira Tariq, Mohammad Yousuf Mubarak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInappropriate antibiotic use is a major driver of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly in low- and middle-income countries. To improve rational prescribing, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed core prescribing indicators and the AWaRe antibiotic classification. Evidence from Afghanistan remains scarce, especially in outpatient settings.MethodsThis cross-sectional prescription audit was conducted from June to December 2024 in six public and private hospitals in Kabul. Prescriptions were sampled from outpatient departments of internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, and ENT. Of 623 prescriptions screened, 306 (49.1%) contained at least one antibiotic and were analyzed using WHO prescribing indicators and the AWaRe classification. Data were processed with SPSS v25.ResultsA total of 374 antibiotics were prescribed, with an average of 1.5 per prescription. The most common agents were ceftriaxone (19.9%), metronidazole (16.0%), ciprofloxacin (12.4%), and amoxicillin (11.4%). Generic prescribing accounted for 63.3%, while 42.7% of prescriptions included parenteral antibiotics. All antibiotics were from the National Essential Medicines List. AWaRe analysis showed Watch antibiotics (58.6%) predominated, while Access antibiotics (41.4%) were underused; no Reserve antibiotics were prescribed. No culture and sensitivity testing supported prescribing.ConclusionAntibiotic prescribing in Kabul outpatient settings substantially exceeded WHO standards, with high reliance on broad-spectrum Watch-group agents, injectables, and non-generic formulations. These findings underscore the urgent need for Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs, prescriber training, and improved diagnostic capacity to ensure rational antibiotic use and contain AMR in Afghanistan.

Introduction

Antibiotics remain essential medicines for treating bacterial infections and have dramatically reduced morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. However, their widespread availability and frequent misuse have contributed to the alarming rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a major public health threat projected to cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050 if left unchecked [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that more than half of all medicines are prescribed or used inappropriately, with antibiotics among the most affected drug classes [3].

Irrational antibiotic use—including polypharmacy, over-prescription of broad-spectrum agents, inappropriate dosing, and unnecessary use of injectables—results in treatment failure, toxicity, prolonged illness, increased healthcare costs, and the rapid spread of resistant pathogens [4–6]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where weak regulation, limited laboratory diagnostics, and lack of treatment guidelines exacerbate inappropriate prescribing [7, 8].

To monitor and improve rational medicine use, the WHO developed five core prescribing indicators: (1) average number of drugs per encounter, (2) percentage of drugs prescribed by generic name, (3) percentage of encounters with an antibiotic prescribed, (4) percentage of encounters with an injection prescribed, and (5) percentage of drugs prescribed from the Essential Medicines List [9]. In addition, the WHO introduced the AWaRe classification system—categorizing antibiotics into Access, Watch, and Reserve groups—to guide appropriate use and promote stewardship [10]. Access-group antibiotics should be widely available and used as first-line therapy, whereas Watch- and Reserve-group antibiotics must be preserved to prevent resistance development.

Globally, studies using these tools have reported high levels of antibiotic misuse. For example, over-prescription of ceftriaxone and fluoroquinolones has been reported in India, Pakistan, and Ethiopia [11–13], while generic prescribing rates remain below optimal in many LMICs. In Afghanistan, where infectious diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity, systematic evidence on outpatient antibiotic prescribing is scarce. A few reports have suggested overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics and limited adherence to clinical guidelines [14, 15], but comprehensive evaluations using WHO indicators and the AWaRe framework are lacking.

This study aimed to evaluate antibiotic prescribing patterns in outpatient departments of major hospitals in Kabul, Afghanistan, using WHO prescribing indicators and AWaRe classification. By identifying current trends and deviations from global standards, the findings are expected to provide evidence for policymakers, prescribers, and healthcare educators to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship and curb AMR in the country.

Methodology

Study setting and design

This was a hospital-based, cross-sectional descriptive study conducted in Kabul, Afghanistan, from June 23 to December 22, 2024. Data were collected from selected public (n = 4) and private (n = 2) hospitals. These facilities represent major healthcare institutions in the capital, together accounting for approximately 35–40% of outpatient services in Kabul. Public hospitals were government-funded, providing low-cost services, while private hospitals served primarily urban patients and referral cases.

Data were extracted from outpatient departments of internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, and otorhinolaryngology (ENT), which are among the most common prescribers of antibiotics in Afghanistan. The study was designed as a retrospective prescription audit using WHO core prescribing indicators.

Study population

The source population consisted of all outpatients attending the participating hospitals during the study period.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A total of 623 prescriptions were screened across the study sites. Of these, 306 prescriptions (49.1%) contained at least one antibiotic and were included for final analysis.

While the WHO recommends reviewing at least 600 prescriptions for drug utilization studies, our analysis focused specifically on those containing antibiotics. Although this reduced the final sample size, it still provides a meaningful baseline for rational antibiotic use in Kabul.

Prescriptions were selected using a systematic random sampling method. Each day, every kth prescription from outpatient registers was chosen, ensuring representation across different facilities and departments.

Results

Prescription screening and inclusion

A total of 623 outpatient prescriptions were reviewed across six hospitals. Of these, 306 prescriptions (49.1%) contained at least one antibiotic and were included in the analysis. Prescriptions without antibiotics (n = 317, 50.9%) and those with incomplete documentation (n = 14, 2.2%) were excluded.

Patient characteristics

Among the 306 patients receiving antibiotics, nearly half were aged 21–40 years (49.0%), followed by those older than 40 years (30.0%), and under 20 years (21.0%). Females constituted 53.0%, and most patients (64.4%) had only primary education. Two-thirds (66.3%) resided outside Kabul city (Table 1).

Discussion

This study assessed antibiotic prescribing practices in outpatient departments of major public and private hospitals in Kabul, Afghanistan, using WHO core prescribing indicators and the WHO AWaRe framework. The findings reveal substantial deviations from recommended standards, reflecting both global and local concerns about irrational antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

Comparison with WHO prescribing indicators

The average of 1.5 antibiotics per prescription in this study was markedly higher than the WHO optimal range of 0.2–0.26. Similar trends have been observed in Ethiopia (0.93) [16], Pakistan (1.3–1.6) [17, 18], and India (1.2–1.4) [11, 13, 15, 19], suggesting that polypharmacy involving antibiotics is a persistent challenge across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The proportion of prescriptions with at least one antibiotic (49.1%) also far exceeded the WHO standard of ≤ 30%, consistent with studies from Afghanistan [20], Nepal [10, 21, 22], and sub-Saharan Africa [16].

Generic prescribing was 63.3%, well below the ideal 100%, similar to findings from Iran (65%) [25] and Pakistan (61%) [17], indicating over-reliance on branded formulations in the region. Prescriptions with injectable antibiotics (42.7%) were four times higher than the WHO threshold (≤ 10%), echoing trends in other LMICs where injectable overuse is often driven by prescriber preference and patient expectations [23, 24]. Encouragingly, 100% compliance with the National Essential Medicines List (NEML) was achieved, suggesting prescribers adhered to available essential drug policies, even if irrational prescribing patterns persisted.

AWaRe classification and implications

Analysis by AWaRe categories revealed a concerning reliance on “Watch” antibiotics, particularly ceftriaxone (19.9%) and ciprofloxacin (12.4%), which are associated with high resistance potential. In contrast, use of “Access” antibiotics, such as amoxicillin (11.4%), was comparatively lower. This imbalance mirrors findings from India and Pakistan, where Watch-group antibiotics dominate outpatient prescribing [9, 11, 17]. The overuse of Watch antibiotics without diagnostic justification raises the risk of accelerating AMR in Afghanistan.

Prescribing context in Afghanistan

Several contextual factors likely contribute to these patterns. First, the absence of microbiological testing—no prescriptions were supported by culture and sensitivity—limits evidence-based prescribing. Second, clinical documentation was often incomplete, complicating the assessment of appropriateness relative to patient conditions. Third, resource constraints in public hospitals and weak regulation in private settings create an environment where empirical broad-spectrum prescribing is routine.

Practical applications of findings

The practical value of this study lies in providing baseline evidence for policymakers, prescribers, and healthcare educators in Afghanistan. Specifically:

For policymakers: results highlight the need for a national antimicrobial stewardship policy, aligned with WHO’s Global Action Plan on AMR.For healthcare providers: findings can inform training on rational prescribing and encourage adherence to standard treatment guidelines.For public health programs: results underscore the urgency of strengthening diagnostic capacity and enforcing prescription audits in both public and private facilities.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. It was retrospective and did not link prescriptions to patients’ diagnoses or outcomes, which limits clinical appropriateness assessment. Data were drawn from six hospitals (~ 35–40% of Kabul’s outpatient services), so findings may not represent rural or smaller facilities. Only summer and fall months were covered, which may not capture seasonal peaks of infectious diseases such as influenza in winter. Finally, incomplete prescriptions (2.2%) were excluded, which could introduce selection bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into prescribing patterns in Kabul and establishes a foundation for future research.

Conclusion

Antibiotic prescribing in outpatient settings in Kabul substantially exceeds WHO-recommended standards, with nearly half of prescriptions containing antibiotics, high use of broad-spectrum “Watch” agents, excessive reliance on injectables, and suboptimal generic prescribing. These practices, coupled with the absence of diagnostic support, reflect irrational use and heighten the risk of antimicrobial resistance.

Strengthening Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs, promoting evidence-based prescribing, and improving diagnostic capacity are urgent priorities for Afghanistan. These findings should guide national policies and prescriber training initiatives to ensure safer and more rational antibiotic use.
