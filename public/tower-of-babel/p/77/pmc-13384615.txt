Effect of goal-directed fluid resuscitation therapy based on peripheral perfusion index on acute skin failure in elderly critically ill patients: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Bing Xu, Jiuying Ji, Yin Yan, Ling Lu, Zunguo Pu, Peirong Wang, Ying Wang, Yaqing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to evaluate the effect of the peripheral perfusion index (PPI)-based goal-directed fluid resuscitation strategy for reducing the incidence of acute skin failure (ASF) in elderly critically ill patients.Methods:We conducted a prospective, randomized, parallel-controlled study by enrolling 216 elderly critically ill patients who required early active fluid resuscitation from January 2025 to December 2025. They were randomly assigned to 2 categories: PPI-guided and conventional resuscitation groups. The intervention group considered PPI ≥1.4 as an additional resuscitation endpoint with conventional macrohemodynamic goals. Based on routine indicators, the control group received fluid management only. Our primary outcome comprised the incidence of ASF within 7 days of intensive care unit admission. Secondary outcomes included time to ASF onset, resuscitation quality indicators, organ function, and long-term prognosis. Data were analyzed according to the intention-to-treat principle.Results:Our study involved 204 patients (103 intervention and 101 control). The intervention group (14.6%, 15/103) experienced a significantly reduced 7-day incidence of ASF compared with the control group (29.7%, 30/101, P = .009). However, the median time to ASF onset was prolonged in the intervention group (5.2 days vs 3.1 days, log-rank P = .012). Regarding the resuscitation quality, the intervention group demonstrated a higher 6-hour lactate clearance rate (28.5% ± 12.3% vs 21.8% ± 14.1%, P = .001) and reduced cumulative positive fluid balance at 72 hours (+1250 ± 980 mL vs +1850 ± 1100 mL, P < .001). The 28-day all-cause mortality between the groups remained nonsignificant.Conclusion:A PPI-based goal-directed fluid resuscitation strategy significantly reduces the risk of ASF in elderly critically ill patients, improves early perfusion marker assessments, and optimizes fluid management. This noninvasive, bedside monitoring approach provides a new clinical rationale for protecting skin health in critically ill patients.

1. Introduction

Acute skin failure (ASF) is a fatal complication in elderly critically ill patients. It results from acute dysfunction and necrosis of the skin caused by severe systemic diseases and hypoperfusion.[1] In elderly critically ill patients, ASF significantly increases the risk of secondary infections and protein loss; moreover, it is independently associated with a higher mortality.[2,3] Its current clinical management entails a passive response, and there is a lack of early warning and systematic intervention strategies based on pathophysiological mechanisms.[4] Therefore, objective indicators should be explored that can accurately identify the risk of skin perfusion and guide early treatment. Thus, this parameter can become an important clinical research direction to improve the prognosis of such patients.

Fluid resuscitation is a key circulatory support measure for critically ill patients. However, traditional goal-directed fluid resuscitation primarily relies on macroscopic hemodynamic indicators, such as mean arterial pressure and central venous pressure. Even if these macroscopic indicators are adequate, such patients might exhibit persistent tissue-level hypoperfusion, that is, “microcirculation disturbance.”[5,6] This disconnection between macro- and microcirculation may cause insufficient resuscitation or fluid overload and damage end organs, including the skin.[7] Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore noninvasive tools that can assess tissue perfusion status in real time to achieve accurate personalized resuscitation.

As a derived parameter of pulse oximetry, peripheral perfusion index (PPI) provides a quantitative method for evaluating the peripheral microcirculation status by calculating the ratio of pulsatile to nonpulsatile blood flow in the peripheral vascular bed.[8] It has the advantages of simple measurement, continuous noninvasive, and intuitive interpretation. Studies have confirmed that lower PPI values are associated with poor prognosis and organ dysfunction in septic shock patients.[9,10] Notably, the contemporary consensus suggests that incorporating microcirculation parameters, including the peripheral perfusion treatment evaluation system, can become a crucial factor in individualized recovery.[11] These findings indicate that PPI is a key link between systemic hemodynamics and end-organ perfusion.

Nevertheless, most of the existing studies have focused on the predictive value of PPIs or used them as assessment tools. Currently, there is a lack of prospective intervention studies to systematically evaluate the direct effect of using PPI as an active and dynamic fluid resuscitation target for preventing specific organ dysfunction, especially of the skin. As skin is an end organ that is highly sensitive to perfusion changes, and its health status is associated with patients’ quality of life and long-term prognosis, this research direction is a crucial need.

Therefore, our hypothesis suggests that a PPI-based individualized fluid resuscitation strategy can effectively improve the skin tissue’s microcirculation perfusion, thereby significantly reducing the incidence and severity of ASF in elderly critically ill patients, compared with conventional fluid resuscitation techniques.

Hence, we designed a prospective, randomized, controlled clinical trial to provide high-level medical evidence for the early prevention and treatment of ASF, as well as to explore the clinical application of a microcirculation-oriented precision treatment strategy for multiorgan protection in critically ill patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Experimental design

We conducted a single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind (blinded outcome assessors and data analysts), parallel-controlled, superiority clinical trial. We also underwent the Hai’an People’s Hospital ethical committee review (approval number: HAYY-2025-LW-045), and this study was registered on Clinical Trials, registration number: NCT07367113. Informed consent was obtained from the participants involved. Our study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the implementation process was reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement.

2.2. Study subjects

From January 1, 2025, to December 28, 2025, we consecutively screened relevant elderly critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) of Hai’an People’s Hospital.

Our inclusion criteria were the following: age ≥65 years, those who met the diagnostic criteria of sepsis-3[8] or other types of shock that required active fluid resuscitation, participants with an Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation score ≥15 at ICU admission, expected ICU stay of ≥72 hours, and acquiring informed consent, either from patients or their legal representatives.

Our exclusion criteria were patients with stage ≥III pressure injury or skin necrosis at the time of enrollment, those undergoing evaluation of the impact of severe skin diseases or peripheral vascular diseases, inability to perform continuous finger pulse oximetry, those with a contraindication for restrictive liquid recovery (such as acute cardiac pulmonary edema), participants with expected survival time <24 hours, and those who had participated in other intervention studies.

2.3. Randomization and blinding

A computer-generated block randomization sequence was used in an independent web-based central randomization system to conduct the study. In a 1:1 ratio, participants were randomly assigned to either index-directed resuscitation (PPI group) or conventional resuscitation (control group). Owing to the nature of the intervention, resuscitation clinicians’ blinding was not possible. Thus, an independent, blinded review team, which was unaware of the trial-group assignments and treatment interventions, performed all skin health assessments, data collection, and imaging analyses to ensure the objectivity of outcome assessments. In addition, statistical analysts were also blinded to group assignments.

2.4. Interventions

2.4.1. Routine resuscitation group

We performed standard fluid resuscitation according to the International Guidelines of the Campaign to Save Sepsis.[9] Our goals were mean arterial pressure ≥65 mm Hg, central venous pressure 8 to 12 mm Hg, urine output ≥0.5 mL·kg−1·h−1, and decreased blood lactic acid level. The attending physician determined the type, rate, and volume of fluid as per the abovementioned indicators.

2.4.2. PPI-directed resuscitation group

Based on conventional resuscitation goals, maintaining PPI ≥1.4 was added as the core resuscitation endpoint.[12] Subsequently, the index finger PPI was continuously recorded every 15 minutes using a Mindray BeneVision N15 monitor. When PPI persisted <1.4 for >30 minutes, the assessment process was commenced. Initially, a passive leg lift experiment was conducted; if the PPI rose 10% or higher, 250 mL of fluid balance salt crystal rapid infusion was administered. In case of no response, additional volume loading was avoided, vasoactive agents were optimized, or cardiac function was evaluated. The patients entered the conservative infusion or de-resuscitation phase once the PPI had been maintained at the target level for an hour.

2.4.3. PPI measurement standardization

All PPI data were continuously monitored via pulse oximetry probes attached to the right index finger of each patient throughout the entire resuscitation period, and the same finger was fixed for repeated measurement without random replacement. Before recording valid PPI values at each 15-minute interval, the monitor waveform was stabilized for at least 60 seconds to eliminate transient signal fluctuations caused by limb movement, vasoactive agent fluctuation, or poor probe fitting. The fingertip skin was kept warm and free of compression to avoid interference with peripheral microcirculation signals. Persistent PPI values <1.4 maintained for >30 consecutive minutes triggered fluid responsiveness assessment.

2.5. Outcome measures

Our primary outcome comprised the incidence of ASF within 7 days after ICU admission. The diagnosis and staging of ASF were based on the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel/European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel 2014[2] criteria and were evaluated daily by a blinded assessor.

Our secondary outcomes were the onset time and most severe stage of ASF, lactate clearance at 6 and 24 hours after resuscitation, cumulative fluid balance at 72 hours, incidence of acute kidney injury as per the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria, duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay, all-cause mortality at 28 and 90 days, and Barthel index at discharge.

Safety indicators: resuscitation-related adverse events, such as pulmonary edema and new-onset arrhythmia, were recorded.

2.6. Sample size estimation

Literature data and preliminary trials suggested that the incidence of ASF within 7 days was 30% and 12% in the control and PPI recovery groups, respectively.[13,14] Keeping α = 0.05 (two-sided) and the test power at 80%, 92 cases were needed for each group. Hence, 216 patients were projected to be enrolled, considering a dropout rate of 15%.

2.7. Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk) and R 4.2.1 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://www.r-project.org) were used for data analysis. The measurement data were described as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) or median. The t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison between the groups. The count data were described as the number of cases (percentage; n [%]), followed by a chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Primary outcomes were compared by using the chi-square test. The Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test were used for evaluating time-to-event data. All analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle, and a P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Patient enrollment and baseline characteristics

After screening 268 elderly patients who met the diagnosis of critical illness, 216 were randomly assigned to either the PPI-directed resuscitation group (n = 108) or the conventional resuscitation group (n = 108). During the study period, 5 and 7 patients in the intervention and control groups dropped out, respectively. Finally, 204 patients were included in the intention-to-treat analysis (103 in the intervention group and 101 in the control group). Figure 1 exhibits the patients’ screening procedure. 3.3. Fluid resuscitation quality and organ function outcomes

The intervention group exhibited enhanced perfusion and more conservative fluid management early in resuscitation (Table 3). The lactate clearance rate at 6 hours post-resuscitation was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group; the cumulative fluid balance during the first 72 hours was significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group (all P < .01). 3.4. Long-term survival, functional status, and health economic indicators

Although the 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality rates were reduced in the intervention group than in the control group, they were not significant. Among the patients who were discharged, the intervention group displayed a significantly enhanced ability in activities of daily living (Barthel index) at discharge compared with the control group (P = .032). However, no significant differences were observed between the 2 groups with respect to the total hospitalization expenditure or the readmission rate within 30 days (Table 4).

3.5. Safety assessment

During the treatment period, there was no significant difference in the incidence of resuscitation-related serious adverse events between the 2 groups (8.7% in both groups, χ2 = 0.60, P = .440).

4. Discussion

This is the first randomized controlled trial to demonstrate that a PPI-based, goal-directed fluid resuscitation strategy significantly reduces the incidence of ASF (14.6% vs 29.7%) and delays its onset in elderly critically ill patients. This finding provides a crucial evidence-backed basis for using noninvasive, continuous peripheral perfusion monitoring with traditional macrohemodynamic indicators to guide precise fluid management in critically ill patients.

Our results support the concept of “microcirculation-oriented” resuscitation. A frequent uncoupling is observed between the traditional resuscitation endpoints (such as central venous pressure and mean arterial pressure) and the actual tissue perfusion status.[15] We found that the PPI-guided group achieved a lower cumulative positive fluid balance, an enhanced lactate clearance rate, and an improved prognosis. This suggests that strategies for restoring peripheral perfusion may accurately correct hidden tissue hypoperfusion and avoid the harmful effects caused by excessive resuscitation, such as aggravated tissue edema and impaired cardiac function.[16] The underlying mechanism may be that PPI can precisely reflect peripheral vasoconstriction triggered by sympathetic nerve excitation.[17,18] By targeting PPI normalization,[12] the treatment can directly antagonize the strong vasoconstrictive signal in the shock state, thereby improving microcirculatory blood flow in end organs such as the skin quickly and efficiently. Elderly patients are prone to skin atrophy, reduction in microvessel density, weakened self-regulation ability, and enhanced sensitivity to hypoperfusion.[7] Such age-related microcirculation dysfunction makes them more predisposed to perfusion-dependent organ damage.[19] Therefore, this strategy for improving peripheral perfusion might generate more significant organ protection effects.

Our study’s positive results on the specific organ injury endpoint (ASF) provide a new perspective for interpreting the clinical value of microcirculation-directed resuscitation. Although there were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups regarding the secondary endpoints of duration of mechanical ventilation, length of ICU stay, or long-term mortality, the PPI-guided group showed improved trends in these measures. Combined with the PPI-guided group’s enhanced lactate clearance rate, more conservative fluid balance, and improved functional status at discharge (Barthel index), these results suggest that the benefits of resuscitation strategies targeting a PPI may be concentrated primarily on preventing reversible, perfusion-sensitive early organ dysfunction such as ASF and creating a favorable physiological basis for subsequent recovery. This finding aligns with the results of the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK study by Hernandez et al,[14] which suggested that a perfusion-based resuscitation strategy might help reduce organ support requirements. A recent meta-analysis suggested that resuscitation strategies targeting peripheral perfusion can significantly improve organ-related outcomes.[20] Our results are also consistent with a secondary analysis by Kattan et al,[21] which reported that focusing on peripheral perfusion can help in precise fluid management. In addition, the potential long-term benefits of this strategy are supported by the improved functional status of the intervention group’s patients at discharge, as this is an important prognostic indicator for critically ill elderly patients.[22]

Our study’s strengths include its rigorous randomized, controlled design, blinded assessment of the primary outcome, and comprehensive evaluation of secondary outcomes. However, its limitations should also be considered. First, this was a single-center study, and the sample size was calculated primarily based on the incidence of ASF, so data extrapolation should be done with caution. Second, despite the implementation of outcome assessor blinding, the nature of the intervention made treatment blinding impossible. Third, although PPI monitoring is simple, its value might have been affected by factors such as the monitoring site’s local temperature, oxygen-probe fit, and vasoactive drugs. Finally, sepsis was prevalent in our study population. Hence, the efficacy of this strategy in patients with other types of shock should be verified. Apart from increasing the sample size and multicenter validation, future research can use critical care precision medicine to explore the integration of PPI with other dynamic parameters, such as stroke volume variation, to build an intelligent decision support system[23] and achieve the individualized as well as precise fluid management of such critically ill patients.

In conclusion, for critically ill elderly patients, especially those with sepsis, using a PPI-based fluid management strategy in the early resuscitation stage can be a feasible method to significantly reduce the risk of ASF. This strategy reflects the evolution of the resuscitation concept from “macro target” to “microcirculation optimization.” Future studies can explore the effects of this strategy on comprehensive clinical outcomes and its therapeutic application in a wider patient population.
