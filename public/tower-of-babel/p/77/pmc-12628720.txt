Retrospective analysis of impact of automated insulin delivery technology on acute care utilisation from 2019 to 2021
Authors: Janna Manjelievskaia, Viral N Shah, Anders L Carlson, Diana Isaacs, Sharon M Wang, Jordan E Pinsker, Laurel H Messer, Kimberly W McDermott, Kevin Lavelle, Stephanie Wall, Diana Brixner, Daniel C Malone, Charles A Stemple, Neel Vaidya, Bimal V Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim was to evaluate the impact of using an automated insulin delivery (AID) system versus multiple daily insulin injections (MDI) on the odds and costs of acute care utilisation among people with type 1 diabetes (PwT1D).MethodsThis was retrospective, observational cohort study that leveraged electronic health record (EHR) data and administrative claims data from 15 January 2019, through 31 December 2021. PwT1D treated with MDI or newly starting an AID system were identified between 15 January 2020, and 31 December 2020 (identification period), with index date defined as the earliest evidence of an AID device or MDI therapy during the identification period. Participants were required to be continuously enrolled in their health plan for ≥12 months pre‐ and post‐index date. Utilisation and costs of inpatient (IP) and emergency department (ED) services data from medical and pharmacy claims data were analysed. Multivariable modelling was used to assess odds and costs of IP and ED service utilisation among AID system users compared to MDI users while adjusting for pre‐index characteristics.ResultsThis study included 7264 AID system users and 27,973 MDI users. AID system users were younger and had a higher proportion of females than MDI users. After adjusting for pre‐index characteristics, AID users had lower odds of IP admission (point estimate [95% confidence interval]: 0.70 (0.63–0.77) or ED visit 0.76 (0.70–0.82)) at 12 months and incurred lower average 12‐month costs for all‐cause IP admission ($3054 vs. $4701; p‐value < 0.001) and ED visit ($1230 vs. $1793; p‐value <0.001) compared to MDI users.ConclusionsUse of AID systems among PwT1D was associated with lower odds of IP and ED utilisation and lower costs in the follow‐up period compared to MDI.Keywords: devices, economics, insulin therapy, type 1 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Achieving and maintaining optimal glucose control is critical to preventing the acute and chronic complications of type 1 diabetes (T1D). 1 , 2 A recent report by Hankowsky et al. 3 estimated that only ~25% of the 2 million people living with T1D in the US 3 are achieving the American Diabetes Association (ADA) target of glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of < 53 mmol/mol (<7.0%). 4

In addition to its adverse clinical outcomes, poor glucose control is associated with increased healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) and costs. 4 In 2022, the direct and indirect cost of diabetes in the US totalled $412 billion, accounting for 1 in 4 healthcare dollars spent in the US. 5 Although the proportion attributable to T1D is not well defined, one study estimated that people with T1D account for 5.7% of all people with diabetes (PwD) but 8.6% of the economic burden due to diabetes. 6 Cost estimates for people with T1D (PwT1D) range from $1482 to $1568 monthly, with 49%–58% attributable to managing T1D and 16%–18% attributable to hospitalisations and emergency department (ED) visits. 7 , 8 In a study by Simeone et al., investigators reported that the median costs per diabetes‐related hospitalisation ranged from $6548 to $8439; the median costs of ED visits were $972–$1499. 7

Ongoing innovations in insulin delivery and glucose monitoring technology have led to the development of automated insulin delivery (AID) systems that enable individuals with diabetes who are treated with basal‐bolus therapy to safely achieve their glycaemic targets. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Some of the more advanced AID systems utilise an algorithm to automatically increase or decrease basal insulin delivery from an insulin pump in response to glucose values obtained through an integrated continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) sensor. Some of these advanced AID systems also feature the added capability to deliver automated correction boluses. 14 In randomised controlled trials, these new technologies have been shown to improve glycaemic control, reduce the risk of hypoglycaemia, and improve quality of life in adults, adolescents, and paediatric patients with T1D. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 16 However, the impact of AID use on IP hospitalisations and ED visits has not been studied.

We investigated the impact of AID systems use on healthcare utilisation and associated costs compared with multiple daily insulin injection (MDI) therapy in a large cohort of individuals with T1D.

METHODS

Study data source

This retrospective, observational cohort study utilised data from electronic health record (EHR) sourced from the Veradigm Network EHR linked to healthcare administrative claims data from IP, outpatient, and pharmacy encounters in the United States between15 January 2019 and 31 December 2021. The EHR data documented the care received at contributing outpatient facilities, such as data recorded during a visit with a participating physician. This may have included current diagnoses, medical history, vital signs, lab results, procedures, and vaccination records, but not all data elements were available from each visit. Administrative claims data were obtained from insurance records and provided insights into the healthcare services provided, the diagnoses supporting the use of those services, and the cost of these services. These data were further linked with data from Tandem's Customer Relationship Management database that included information to identify the use of Tandem's t:slim X2 with Control‐IQ technology AID system. The data included US customers who initiated or were active users between 1 January 2019 and 30 November 2022.

Ethical compliance

The final linked data set only contained de‐identified data as per the de‐identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. The process by which the data was de‐identified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. Because this study used only de‐identified records, it is no longer subject to the HIPAA Privacy Rule and is exempt from institutional review board approval and from obtaining informed consent according to US law. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and used only de‐identified data.

Cohort identification summary

We identified individuals with a diagnosis of T1D who had evidence of insulin therapy with MDI or with an AID system manufactured by either Tandem (t:slim X2 with Control‐IQ technology) or Medtronic (MiniMed 670G or 770G), which were the only commercially available AID systems in the United States during the identification period of 15 January 2020, and 31 December 2020. The start date for the identification period was chosen to align with the availability of the Control‐IQ technology, which became commercially available on 15 January 2020.

All individuals included in the study were required to have at least 12 months of continuous enrolment with medical and pharmacy benefits pre‐ and post‐index date, with index date defined as the earliest evidence of an AID device or MDI therapy during the identification period.

We excluded individuals with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes, evidence of pregnancy, labour, or delivery, or bariatric surgery during the study period. Individuals were also excluded if they had evidence of dialysis, end‐stage renal disease, or a kidney transplant during the 12‐month pre‐index period. Cohort identification details

We first identified all individuals with ≥1 IP claim or ≥2 outpatient claims, at least 1 day apart, with a diagnosis code for T1D or T2D during the pre‐ and post‐index periods. Individuals with claims for both T1D and T2D were categorised using an approach similar to Simeone et al. 7 Specifically, individuals age ≤15 years were categorised as having T1D. Individuals age 16–45 years were categorised as having T1D if at least 50% of claims for diabetes indicated T1D diagnosis. Individuals over age 45 years were categorised as having T1D if at least 70% of claims indicated T1D diagnosis. Individuals identified as having T2D were excluded.

MDI therapy was defined consistent with the ADA guidelines. 17 Pharmacy or medical claims identified insulin consumption. To be eligible for the study, individuals had to have at least one claim for basal insulin during cohort identification and at least one claim for bolus insulin with overlapping days' supply. Acceptable combinations included: (1) a long‐acting insulin analogue with an ultra‐rapid‐acting analogue, (2) a long‐acting insulin analogue with a rapid‐acting analogue, (3) neutral protamine Hagedorn insulin with a rapid‐acting analogue, and (4) neutral protamine Hagedorn insulin with a short‐acting (regular) insulin.

The AID system cohort included individuals with evidence of Tandem or Medtronic insulin pump usage during the identification period, who were identified using both claim's data and the data provided by Tandem. For Tandem users, the index date was either their first claim indicating Tandem pump use or the first record in the Tandem data set confirming Tandem pump use for Control‐IQ during the cohort identification period. For Medtronic users, the index date was the date of the first claim for an AID system not identified as Tandem during the identification period. Individuals who had evidence of prior pump therapy in the pre‐index period, those whose insulin pump brand could not be identified, and those with evidence of any pump usage prior to their index date were excluded from the analysis; however, MDI users were required to be treatment‐experienced to avoid comparing individuals new to diabetes therapy versus pumps.

The MDI cohort included individuals with evidence of MDI therapy during the identification period if they met the following three criteria. First, they had evidence of MDI therapy in the pre‐index period. Second, they had no evidence of insulin pump usage during cohort selection, and third, they had no evidence of pre‐mixed insulin at any time during the pre‐ or post‐index periods.

Participant characteristics

We captured demographic characteristics, including age at index, sex, race, ethnicity, geographic region, and payer type. Using data from the 12‐month pre‐index period, estimates of body mass index (BMI), mean diabetes complications severity index (DCSI) score, 18 and individual comorbidities (e.g., hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, and/or hypoglycaemia), use of non‐insulin diabetes medications, and use of continuous glucose monitors were determined.

Acute care events

We captured all‐cause and diabetes‐related IP admissions and ED visits in the 12‐month pre‐index and follow‐up periods. Diabetes‐related events were defined as IP medical claims with a primary diagnosis of T1D or T2D (or other specified diabetes), outpatient claims with a diagnosis code for T1D or T2D (or other specified diabetes) in any position, and pharmacy claims for diabetes‐related medications. ED visits were considered diabetes‐related if they had a diabetes diagnosis in any position on the medical claim. We report the number of individuals with a visit, the number of visits per individual, and costs per individual obtained from the administrative claims data. Average 12‐month IP and ER costs were calculated among each cohort; these costs include participants with no acute care utilisation (e.g., zero costs for IP and/or ED visits). Additionally, average cost per IP stay and per ED visit were calculated for individuals with at least one IP admission or ED visit, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Study measures are reported descriptively, with mean and standard deviation (SD) reported for continuous variables and number and percent reported for categorical variables. Differences between cohorts are assessed using two‐tailed Student's t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared goodness‐of‐fit tests for categorical variables. An alpha level of 0.05 was set a priori.

Multivariable modelling was used to assess differences in utilisation and costs of acute care services between MDI users and AID system users. Odds of an IP admission and odds of an ED visit in the follow‐up period were derived using logistic regression. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Inpatient costs and ED costs were modelled using a two‐part modelling approach. The first part used logistic regression to estimate the probability of non‐zero costs in the follow‐up period. The second part used generalised linear regression with a log link and gamma error distribution to model increases in costs per unit change in the explanatory variables. Results are reported as cost ratios (CR) with 95% CIs.

The primary predictor of interest for all models was the index cohort (MDI or AID system). All models adjusted for age (continuous), sex, region, pre‐index DCSI, 18 payer type, index month, pre‐index hyperglycaemia, pre‐index hypoglycaemia, and pre‐index non‐insulin diabetes medication use. Models of IP hospitalisations also adjusted for pre‐index IP admissions (yes/no), whereas models of ED visits adjusted for pre‐index ED visits (yes/no). All data analysis was conducted using SAS V9.4 (Cary, NC). Forest plots were generated with R version 4.2.0.

RESULTS

Pre‐index participant characteristics

The final study cohorts of people with T1D included 7264 AID system users and 27,973 MDI users (Figure 2). AID system users were younger and had a higher proportion of females (p < 0.001), White and non‐Hispanic, and were more likely to be commercially insured participants than MDI users (Table 1). While individuals in the AID cohort had a higher proportion of metabolic complications, those in the MDI cohort had higher rates of other complications including neuropathy, retinopathy/ophthalmic, and nephropathy complications (all differences p < 0.01). Both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia were more common in the pre‐index period among individuals in the AID cohort than among those in the MDI cohort. Pre‐index usage of non‐insulin diabetes medications was less common in the AID cohort compared to the MDI cohort.

Inpatient and emergency department healthcare utilisation and costs

Between pre‐index and follow‐up, the percentage of individuals in the AID cohort with an all‐cause IP admission had a greater relative decrease in the admissions compared with the percentage of individuals in the MDI cohort (Table 2). The average IP cost per stay for participants with ≥1 IP hospitalisation was significantly lower among AID users ($11,710 [$9734]) compared with MDI users ($12,722 [$22,211]). Similarly, individuals with an all‐cause ED visit decreased by 14.5% in the AID cohort compared to a decrease of 8.4% in the MDI cohort. The average cost per ED visit for participants with ≥1 ED visit was significantly lower among AID users ($1513 [$1834]) compared with MDI users ($1547 [$1930]). Over 97% of individuals with an IP admission had a diabetes‐related admission, and over 85% of individuals with an ED visit had a diabetes‐related ED visit, regardless of cohort or timeframe.

DISCUSSION

In this analysis of 35,237 people with T1D, individuals who recently started an AID system tended to be younger, White, non‐Hispanic, and have commercial insurance compared to MDI users. This is consistent with other studies that showed notable racial/ethnic disparities in the use of diabetes technologies. 19 , 20 , 21 The reason(s) for these disparities require additional investigation. AID system users also had a lower burden of diabetes‐related complications, as measured by the DCSI, and higher rates of pre‐index hyperglycaemia. After adjusting for pre‐index demographic and clinical characteristics, individuals who recently started an AID system had significantly lower odds of IP admission or ED visit and had significantly lower costs for these services in the follow‐up period than individuals who managed their diabetes with MDI therapy.

These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown that better glycaemic control is associated with lower utilisation of acute care in PwT1D. 22 , 23 For example, a large retrospective EHR study found that suboptimal glycaemic control in PwT1D was associated with a higher likelihood of severe hypoglycaemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and utilisation of acute care services (IP admission and ED visits). 22 Similarly, an analysis of PwD in a Massachusetts health plan found that lower HbA1c was associated with fewer diabetes‐related hospital admissions and lower hospitalisation costs. 23 While we did not directly measure glycaemic control in this study, there is abundant evidence that switching to an AID system results in improvements in HbA1c and time in range. 10 , 24 , 25 , 26

Hospitalisations and ED visits are a significant burden to PwT1D and are associated with physical, mental, and financial tolls on PwD and their caregivers. 27 , 28 , 29 Prior hospitalisations in T1D are often predictive of future hospitalisations, indicating a cycle of emergency care use perpetuated by poor glycaemic control. 27 , 28 , 29 Severe hypoglycaemia events and associated hospitalisations are associated with reduced quality of life in both PwD and their caregivers. 27 , 30 Furthermore, hypoglycaemia hospitalisations have been associated not only with high initial costs but also with persistently increased costs in the months following the initial hospitalisation. 31 This cycle of emergency care could potentially be broken by initiating AID after they are discharged from their first IP hospitalisation or emergency room visit.

Value‐based care initiatives, such as Medicare's Hospital Readmission Reduction Program (HRRP), often include financial penalties for high rates of 30‐day rehospitalisation. 32 During the first 10 years of its implementation, 93% of general acute hospitals subject to the HRRP were penalised at least once. 33 While diabetes is not specifically included among the conditions tracked under the HRRP, it is a risk factor for several tracked conditions, including acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, and pneumonia. 34 , 35 , 36 Due to the high degree of comorbidity, reductions in re‐hospitalisations among PwD would likely reduce overall re‐hospitalisations and any associated penalties.

Strengths of this study include the use of claims data from a large national database that includes claims for >180 million individuals covered by diverse payer types (File S1). Furthermore, the 3‐year longitudinal analysis captured differences from pre‐index to therapy change, making clear the impact of therapy change in the advantage of AID.

There are limitations to the analysis as well. As this is retrospective claims data collected for other purposes, there is a risk of data entry errors, missing data, and coding specificity limitations. For example, diabetes duration and its associated burden may influence an individual's decision to initiate AID; however, the duration of diabetes is unavailable in administrative claims data and is often incomplete in EHR data. The EHR and claims data were supplemented with proprietary data supplied by Tandem to help identify pump users and assign their start date; however, similar data were not available on Medtronic users, so we relied more heavily on non‐specific pump codes in identifying index dates for these individuals. Because this study focused on individuals who newly started using a commercially available AID system to manage their T1D, the results may not apply to experienced AID system users or those who use an open‐source or do‐it‐yourself AID system. In addition, the data set used in this analysis only included insured individuals, and costs may not be representative of uninsured individuals. While we used multivariable modelling to adjust for confounding variables, there may be residual confounding due to variables not available in the contributing data sources, such as education level or other social determinants of health. Finally, we chose to model all‐cause events and costs rather than restricting to diabetes‐related events and costs. We took a broad, all‐cause approach because it would be hard to draw the line between diabetes‐related and non‐diabetes‐related events due to the systemic effects of uncontrolled diabetes. For example, the top causes of hospitalisation, outside of pregnancy or osteoarthritis, tend to be complications of diabetes such as cardiovascular events and infections. 37 Additionally, capturing severe hypoglycaemia would be challenging as previous analysis has shown that hypoglycaemia is significantly under‐documented in EHR and claims databases. 38 As with other large database analyses, the data set did not provide potentially relevant demographic characteristics such as education level, self‐management training, socioeconomic status, and other social determinants of health. Another limitation may be not knowing what percentage of the time the pump was in automation. Additionally, CGM data were not available and while HbA1c data were available in only 5%–6% of individuals, insufficient samples limited our ability to investigate correlations between improvements in glycaemic control and adverse events. Furthermore, the absence of newer AID systems that became available in the years following our analysis reduces the generalisability of our findings to PwD using current AID technologies. This is an important distinction given the ongoing advancements in AID. Specifically, seven new systems have been introduced since 2022, all of which utilise different algorithms which can impact performance. However, to date, there have been no head‐to‐head studies comparing the features and performance of current AID devices. The recent investigation by Di Molfetta et al. used a systematic review and network meta‐analysis design to elucidate this information; however, because the authors used different brands of CGM sensors, the authors listed this as a limitation of their study and state that their outcomes should be interpreted with caution. 39 While we acknowledge the usefulness of comparative AID analysis, the greater issue is that a large proportion of individuals are not at goal and can benefit from AID.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings in this study demonstrated that participants with T1D who used AID for glycaemic control had lower odds of IP admissions and ED visits in the follow‐up year than those who used MDI. Corresponding healthcare costs were also lower among AID users than MDI users. These findings suggest that AID system use positively impacts glycaemic control with reduced HCRU and associated costs. However, as AID technology continues to evolve, a series of head‐to‐head comparisons of the effectiveness of the newer devices will be required to provide meaningful guidance for patients, clinicians, and payers.
