Associations of Nephron Number, Birth Weight, and Causes of Death in a Community at High Risk of Hypertension and Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Michael D. Hughson, Rebecca N. Douglas-Denton, Wendy E. Hoy, Cheryl A. Winkler, Jeffrey B. Kopp, John F. Bertram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveLow birth weight (LBW) and low nephron number may increase the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD).Study DesignTotal glomerular number was determined by stereological analysis of autopsy kidneys from 336 participants. Health Department-recorded birth weights were obtained for 204 participants who were ≤55 years old.OutcomesBirth weight, nephron number.PredictorsHypertension, CVD, age, biological sex, body mass index, poverty index, and APOL1 genotype.Analytical ApproachRelationships were investigated using group comparisons and regression models.Settings & ParticipantsPerformance sites: Autopsies, University Medical Center, Jackson, MS; Stereology, Department of Anatomy and Developmental Biology, Monash University, Melbourne; APOL1 genotyping, Basic Research Laboratory, Center for Cancer Research, National Institutes of Health, Frederick, Maryland.ResultsIn total, 170 participants having birth weights were adults, 29% of adults were hypertensive, and 40% died of CVD. Genotyping identified 22 Black participants with 2 APOL1 risk alleles. A median of 718,409 glomeruli per kidney was found for participants with LBW (< 2.5 kg), which was significantly less than the median of 936,937 glomeruli found with birth weights from 3.0 to 4.0 kg (P < 0.001). Among adults ≤ 55 years old, hypertension correlated with age (P < 0.001) and the severity of glomerulosclerosis (P < 0.001), and CVD death correlated with hypertension (P = 0.01). However, neither LBW nor nephron number was associated with hypertension or CVD death or with an APLO1 high-risk genotype. Among participants aged 65-89 years, increased glomerulosclerosis was associated with a lower median glomerular number (759,623; P = 0.02) and with hypertension (P < 0.01).LimitationsRelationships of predictors to outcomes are abridged by death at recruitment.ConclusionsLBW and low nephron number were not related to hypertension or CVD in adults ≤ 55 years of age, but a glomerulosclerosis-related reduction in glomerular number may contribute to hypertension and CVD in later life.

The risk of deaths due to hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD) may be related to low birth weight (LBW).1,2 The hypothesis of “the fetal origins of adult disease” was derived from observations that residents of United Kingdom communities for which birth weights were lower than the national average had higher rates of hypertension and earlier CVD deaths.1 In a 1994 commentary, Brenner and Chertow2 suggested that hypertension might be attributed to a nephron deficit resulting from LBW.

Very low birth weight (VLBW), defined as a weight at birth of less than 1.5 kg, involves injury to the central nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, lung, and kidney.3, 4, 5 Approximately 20% of adolescents and young adults born at VLBW have radiologically small kidneys and later develop chronic kidney dysfunction.4 It is suggested that the small kidneys are nephron deficient. The kidney abnormalities found with VLBW are not observed in LBW infants within the range of 1.5 to 2.5 kg.4

The kidney deficits associated with VLBW can be lifelong. However, the population frequency of LBW rarely exceeds 10%, and that of VLBW rarely exceeds 2%.6 In contrast, hypertension affects 40% of US adults between 40 and 60 years old, and cardiovascular disease is the cause of 29% of US deaths.7,8

Stereological analyses of autopsy kidneys demonstrate a direct relationship between glomerular number and birth weight, but have generally not shown a relationship between low nephron number and hypertension.9, 10, 11, 12 There are no data addressing the relationship of nephron number to CVD. The nephron numbers of persons born at LBW or VLBW who have lived long enough for kidney development to be complete are not known.

Glomerulosclerosis further complicates the relationships between nephron number, aging, and hypertension. Glomerulosclerosis is often observed after age 40 years in the absence of hypertension and is variably termed focal global glomerulosclerosis (FGGS), glomerular hyalinization, or glomerular obsolescence. The effect on the kidney is diagnosed as arteriolar nephrosclerosis.13,14 FGGS causes glomerular loss, and some authorities suggest that essential hypertension may develop from primary arteriolar nephrosclerosis, in which blood pressure is not yet elevated.15 Black Americans with arteriolar nephrosclerosis and 2 high-risk APOL1 alleles have twice the risk of progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD) compared with Whites or Blacks without a high-risk genotype. Notably, the risk appears to be independent of blood pressure or blood pressure control.16

In the current study, we examine the relationships between nephron number, birth weight, hypertension, glomerulosclerosis, and cause of death in an attempt to reconcile differences in population frequencies of LBW, hypertension, and CVD. Because the Mississippi population is admixed racially, a relationship to a high-risk apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) genotype in Black individuals was also examined.

Materials and Methods

The research used human autopsy tissue and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Mississippi Medical Center, Jackson, MS. Written permission for research was obtained from the next of kin.

Kidney collection, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

From 1998 through 2005, 336 right kidneys were collected at autopsy for stereological analysis at the University of Mississippi Medical Center from persons without known kidney disease. The analysis of birth weight and glomerular number was conducted on 204 participants (170 adults aged ≥ 18 years and 34 children) for whom birth weight was recorded by the Mississippi Department of Health (Fig 1).Figure 1Diagram of 336 autopsy participants having a stereological analysis for right kidney glomerular number and the selection of 204 study participants with Department of Health-provided birth weights that allow a correlation of birth weight and glomerular number.

Diagram of 336 autopsy participants having a stereological analysis for right kidney glomerular number and the selection of 204 study participants with Department of Health-provided birth weights that allow a correlation of birth weight and glomerular number.

Kidneys showing arteriolar nephrosclerosis were included in the study if the subcapsular cortex was slightly granular. Kidneys were not collected if the cortex was coarsely pitted, if there were angular or depressed cortical scars, or if diabetic nephrosclerosis was histologically present. Also excluded were kidneys with ongoing nephrogenesis, as the final number of glomeruli would be undeterminable.

Blood Pressure Assessment and Assignment of Hypertension

Blood pressures were collected from the University of Mississippi Medical Center hospital and clinical records for 122 of the 204 participants, of whom 115 were adults. Participants were categorized as hypertensive, nonhypertensive, and hypertension status unknown. Hypertension was defined as a blood pressure > 140/90 mm Hg or a history of and treatment of hypertension.

Birth Weight Collection And Determinations of Gestational Age

The Public Health Statistics Division of the Mississippi Department of Health began recording birth weights in 1951. Birth weights were therefore unavailable for persons older than 48 in 1998 and 55 in 2005, the inclusive years of the collection. Estimates of gestational age were obtained from birth records or by birth weight charts.17

Social Stratification

School districts of residence were identified by ZIP codes on autopsy requests. The average annual family income and the % of families below the US poverty level were obtained from the US Census Bureau's Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates website (https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/saipe).

Stereological and Histological Analyses

Kidneys perfused with 10% formalin were cut into 4-mm thick slices. The sampled slices were sent to Monash University, where they were processed for embedding into glycol methacrylate for stereological estimation of total glomerular number and average glomerular volume using the physical disector/fractionator combination. Kidney tissue not sampled for stereology was embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and examined for the percentage of glomerulosclerosis on periodic acid-Schiff stains. The stereological and histological methods have been described in detail previously.9,11,18

All glomerulosclerosis consisted of FGGS. No focal segmental glomerulosclerosis or solidified glomeruli were seen.

APOL1 Risk Alleles

DNA was isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue on which stereology had been performed. APOL1 risk alleles were genotyped using TaqMan assays19. The APOL1 G1 allele was defined as the presence of the S342G variant with or without the I384M variant, and the G2 allele was defined as the deletion of N388Y389. High-risk APOL1 genotypes were defined as being homozygous for the G1 or G2 alleles or compound heterozygous for both G1 and G2 alleles. An APOL1 genotype was successfully determined on 202 participants, of whom 55 had 1 risk allele, 22 had 2 risk alleles, and 125 had no risk alleles. All participants with APOL1 risk alleles were identified as Black.

Statistical Methods

A supplementary file (Item S1) containing all data used in the study is provided. Analyses were performed with Stata/IC 10.0 (StataCorp Statistical Software, College Station, Texas). Two-way comparisons used t test if variables passed normality tests and rank-sum tests if they did not. Normality was evaluated by histograms and Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) tests. Categorical variables were evaluated using χ2 - χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests. Hypertension, CVD, age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and poverty index as predictors were tested using Huber’s regression for their effect on glomerular number and birth weight as outcomes, with a high-risk APOL1 genotype added as an independent variable for Blacks.

Results

Characteristics of the Autopsy Population

The study population consisted of 127 Black Americans and 77 White Americans, all having birth weights provided by the Mississippi Department of Health. The characteristics of participants and causes of death are summarized in Table 1. The average age was 31 ± 17 years, with a range of 3 days to 55 years. In total. 170 participants were ≥ 18 years old. A significantly greater proportion of Black Americans were hypertensive compared with Whites (P = 0.01), but a similar proportion of Black and White hypertensives received blood pressure-lowering treatment. A significantly greater proportion of Black participants lived in areas where the average family income was below the national poverty index (P < 0.001). Only 3 participants were identified as having diabetes, all of whom were Black and had FGGS without diabetic changes. The mean glomerular number (n = 3, 1,187,984 ± 241,694) and birth weight (n = 3, 3.43 ± 0.37) in diabetic participants did not differ significantly from those of Black participants without diabetes (glomerular number, P = 0.25; birth weight, P = 0.53). The proportion of obesity by race was similar, and there were no significant racial differences in average birth weight (P = 0.65), glomerular number (P = 0.34), or body height (P = 0.48). The frequency of LBW (defined as 1.50-2.50 kg) was 7.8 %. For VLBW (under 1.5 kg), the frequency was 1.6%, with VLBW involving only 2 Black participants. The proportion of participants born at a normal gestational age was 90%. Four Blacks and no Whites had very premature births. There were no racial differences in glomerular number among all participants (P = 0.34) or among participants with LBW (P = 0.89).

The most common cause of death was CVD (33.3%), which included coronary artery disease (21.6%), stroke, and dissecting or atherosclerotic aneurysms (3.9%), and community-occurring pulmonary emboli without a known predisposing cause other than hypertension or CVD (7.8%). External (nonmedical) deaths from accidents, homicides, suicides, and sudden infant death syndrome accounted for 32% of deaths. One of the 11 SIDS babies was born with LBW at 2.45 kg.

The distribution of causes of deaths is similar to that for Mississippi generally. In 2022, Mississippi attributed 23% of deaths to heart disease and 18% to cancer.21 Among children, 46% of deaths were classified as external. Sudden infant death syndrome was the most common external cause, followed by accidents and homicide.22

Detailed obstetrical information for children recorded surprisingly little maternal pathology. There were 11 elective C-sections. Premature rupture of membranes occurred before 3 spontaneous deliveries. One mother was identified as a cocaine abuser, and 2 mothers smoked more than 1 pack of cigarettes a day. Three mothers were treated for hypertension, including 2 with a single-agent methyldopa.

Stereological Results: Glomerular Number and Birth Weight

The histogram of glomerular number shows a right skew and a tall peak in the data distribution (Fig 2A). Birth weight exhibited a slight right skew and a low peak (Fig 2B). Glomerular number and birth weight failed K-S tests (both P < 0.001). Nevertheless, the data have enough symmetry around the median to be analyzed without transformation. Linear regression analysis shows a strong direct correlation between birth weight and glomerular number (r = 0.339, P < 0.001, constant variance 0.07) with a large range in glomerular number at all levels of birth weight (Fig 3). Race, hypertension, CVD, age, sex, BMI, poverty index, and high-risk APOL1 genotype (for Black individuals) were examined in multivariable analysis, with glomerular number and birth weight as outcomes for adults (Table 5). Glomerulosclerosis and Age

Based on Spearman rank correlations, hypertension was significantly associated with age (r = 0.384, P < 0.001) and the percentage of glomerulosclerosis (square root transformed) (r = 0.365, P < 0.001), and CVD death was significantly related to hypertension (r = 0.256, P < 0.01). Figure 4 shows the age-related development of glomerulosclerosis. The kidneys of children up to 4 years of age exhibited congenital glomerulosclerosis that averaged 1.9% but ranged up to 7.7% of glomeruli (Fig 5). The degree of congenital glomerulosclerosis showed no relationship with gestational age (r = –0.076, P = 0.28) or a high-risk APOL1 genotype (r = 0.072, P = 0.69). However, increased congenital glomerulosclerosis did correlate with increased glomerular numbers (P = 0.01). Discussion

The findings showed that below 55 years of age, hypertension was significantly correlated with increased age and increased glomerulosclerosis, and CVD death was associated with hypertension. Nevertheless, no correlation was found between hypertension or CVD death and either glomerular number or birth weight.

There was a strong direct association between glomerular number and birth weight, with a wide range of glomerular number at any given level of birth weight. The large range indicates that most Mississippi residents are adequately endowed with nephrons that serve well until middle age, when increasing glomerulosclerosis and hypertension supervene. When the age range of the study was extended to persons older than 55 years, increased glomerulosclerosis was associated with a significant reduction in glomerular number, and above 65 years old, the median and range of glomerular number were similar to those of persons born with a birth weight <3 kg.

We show that individuals with birth weight >3.0 kg have approximately 950,000 glomeruli per kidney, approximately 200,000 more than those born at LBW. We suggest that, in the United States, a single kidney glomerular number of approximately 720,000 might be anticipated for an individual born at LBW and that fewer than 720,000 glomeruli in a single kidney indicates low nephron endowment.

Although 720,000 glomeruli might indicate nephropenia in persons of European or African descent, there are ethnic variations, and a single kidney glomerular number of 720,000 in Japanese or Australian Aboriginals is above their normal range.23,24 This makes defining nephropenia both ethnicity and age-dependent. Only 3 groups of investigators, ourselves (Monash University), Mayo Clinic, and Jikei University, have analyzed a substantial number of kidneys for nephron number, and only the Monash group has correlated nephron number with birth weight.10,23,24 The Monash studies were limited by the number of participants that could be evaluated because of the technical aspects of counting glomeruli. Nevertheless, the mean and range of glomerular numbers were comparable to those observed in the Mayo Clinic studies, and birth weights showed a distribution similar to that observed in the US in general.10,11,23,25

Studies from the Mayo Clinic have shown that the kidneys of aging individuals without hypertension, who donate kidneys for transplantation, have a nearly 50% reduction in the number of functional nephrons by the age of 70.13 In individuals with 950,000 to 1,000,000 glomeruli per kidney, the glomerular filtration rate associated with a 50% reduction in functional nephrons may remain within the normal range.13 In an individual born with low birth weight (1.5-2.5 kg) and 700,000 glomeruli per kidney, a 50% nephron loss may lower the glomerular filtration rate to a level associated with CKD.

Epidemiological estimates of the risk of fetal death and disability generally depend on rates of VLBW. The Netherlands Pregnancy Outcomes Prediction Study and a similar Helsinki program followed children born with VLBW into early adulthood. Both studies found that VLBW was associated with higher blood pressure in young adults compared with normal-birth-weight controls.26,27 In the Helsinki program, the rate of hypertension was 9.3% with VLBW and 2.5% among controls.27

The Helsinki program’s 9.3% rate of hypertension among adults born at VLBW seems to be a generally accepted estimate.27 The Mississippi rate of VLBW is the highest in the United States at 2.9%.6 This would translate into a frequency of VLBW-associated adult hypertension of 2.7%, which is much lower than rates of hypertension in high or low-risk US communities. To account for the contribution of birth weight to the high rates of CVD and hypertension in US communities, the effects of birth weight and nephron number must remain latent through midlife and begin to be expressed with aging.

Our findings show a significantly lower number of glomeruli in individuals with birth weights in the range of 2.5-3.0 kg compared with individuals with higher birth weights and that the number of glomeruli in the 2.5-3.0 kg range of birth weight is not significantly different than persons born with LBW (1.5-2.5 kg). This similarity suggests that the risk of low nephron endowment is not limited to LBW and may affect 25% of the US population having birth weights <3.0 kg.25

At approximately age 30 years, FGGS began to increase in the Mississippi population. Within the 1- to 55-year-old age range, glomerulosclerosis was not associated with a reduction in glomerular number. However, in a larger sample of the Mississippi autopsy collection that includes participants over 65 years old, increasing glomerulosclerosis was significantly associated with reduced nephron numbers.

We found no association between an APLO1 high-risk genotype and glomerular number, birth weight, or hypertension. This is not unexpected because APOL1 variants are not generally considered a cause of hypertension, but rather are associated with the risk of developing CKD after hypertension begins.28 We have shown that the structural kidney changes associated with APOL1 high-risk variants parallel the severity of hypertensive vascular disease and glomerulosclerosis.29,30 Although there were too few LBW participants to determine whether APOL1 genotypes influenced preterm births, the high rates of LBW among Black Americans make this a priority area of investigation.31

We found no significant relationships among poverty, BMI, LBW, or glomerular number. Poverty affects the health of Black and White American communities and is shown to have strong associations with hypertension and CVD.32,33 The absence of positive findings may be related to the fact that the majority of participants, Blacks and Whites, lived in communities with poverty rates that were much higher than the average of 11.6% for US residents nationwide, and obesity affected nearly 40% of both races.34

Rates of hypertension and CVD in the Southeastern United States are high, and while greater in Black Americans, they are also high in Southern Whites compared with other regions of the US.7,8,32,33,35 We suggest that the factors contributing to hypertension and CVD in high-risk Southeastern communities might be associated with birth weight that does not meet the standard definition of LBW, yet still reduces nephron numbers to a point that can potentially compromise kidney function. Birth weight does not seem to be a factor in the hypertension or CVD deaths seen at or before 55 years of age but might influence CVD and hypertension in late middle age, after increasing FGGS secondarily lowers nephron numbers.

A notable finding in children was congenital glomerulosclerosis, a form of hyalinizing glomerulosclerosis that resembles adult FGGS. Infant glomerulosclerosis was described in the early 1900s.36,37 The term “congenital glomerulosclerosis” was introduced by Friedman et al,38 who regarded the lesions as developmental. Kaplan et al39 referred to childhood glomerulosclerosis as “abiotropic involutional sclerosis.” We cannot associate congenital glomerulosclerosis with a nephron deficit or LBW.
