Derivation and external validation of a portable method to identify patients with pulmonary embolism from radiology reports: The READ-PE algorithm
Authors: Matthew A. Christensen, William B. Stubblefield, Grace Wang, Alyssa Altheimer, Sarah J. Ouadah, Drew A. Birrenkott, Gregory A. Peters, Christiana Prucnal, Savanah Harshbarger, Kyle Chang, Alan B. Storrow, Michael J. Ward, Sean P. Collins, Christopher Kabrhel, Jesse O. Wrenn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Identification of pulmonary embolism (PE) across a cohort currently requires burdensome manual review. Previous approaches to automate capture of PE diagnosis have either been too complex for widespread use or have lacked external validation. We sought to develop and validate the Regular Expression Aided Determination of PE (READ-PE) algorithm, which uses a portable text-matching approach to identify PE in reports from computed tomography with angiography (CTA).Methods:We identified derivation and validation cohorts of final radiology reports for CTAs obtained on adults (≥ 18 years) at two independent, quaternary academic emergency departments (EDs) in the United States. All reports were in the English language. We manually reviewed CTA reports for PE as a reference standard. In the derivation cohort, we developed the READ-PE algorithm by iteratively combining regular expressions to identify PE. We validated the READ-PE algorithm in an independent cohort, and compared performance against three prior algorithms with sensitivity, specificity, positive-predictive-value (PPV), negative-predictive-value (NPV), and the F1 score.Results:Among 2948 CTAs in the derivation cohort 10.8 % had PE and the READ-PE algorithm reached 93 % sensitivity, 99 % specificity, 94 % PPV, 99 % NPV, and 0.93 F1 score, compared to F1 scores ranging from 0.50 to 0.85 for three prior algorithms. Among 1206 CTAs in the validation cohort 9.2 % had PE and the algorithm had 98 % sensitivity, 98 % specificity, 85 % PPV, 100 % NPV, and 0.91 F1 score.Conclusions:The externally validated READ-PE algorithm identifies PE in English-language reports from CTAs obtained in the ED with high accuracy. This algorithm may be used in the electronic health record to accurately identify PE for research or surveillance. If implemented at other EDs, it should first undergo local validation and may require maintenance over time.

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) affects approximately one million persons each year in the United States (US) and Europe [1–5]. Research and quality improvement efforts to improve care depend upon timely and accurate identification of patients with PE across a cohort. Computed tomography with angiography (CTA) of the chest is the gold standard for diagnosis of acute PE and results are stored as unstructured text within the electronic health record (EHR) [6,7]. Current methods to identify PE from these unstructured reports, or from other data in the EHR, are inadequate. Traditional manual review of CTA reports is slow, costly, and prone to human error [8]. Alternative approaches to identify PE using structured EHR data such as diagnostic codes [9,10], anti-coagulation prescriptions [11], and claims data [12,13] are inaccurate in >25 % of cases.

Validated tools are needed to identify PE in the EHR [14]. Recently, enthusiasm has grown for large-language-models like Chat-GPT to extract findings from unstructured radiology reports [15–17]. While technically exciting, various concerns over privacy, accuracy, model hallucination, and equity block immediate application [18,19]. More established natural language processing (NLP) methods have been used successfully to extract PE from CTA reports but require extensive technical expertise which limits transportability [20–24]. A simple and portable alternative approach is to use regular expressions, which underly basic text-search algorithms found in many programs, including Microsoft Word [25]. Whereas modern NLP pipelines consist of 8–12 steps that are configured and carried out in programming languages, regular expressions operate as a single command that defines a pattern of characters to match in a body of text. For example, the regular expression “embo*” will search a segment of text for all words that begin with “embo” and end with any combination of characters. The “embo” regular expression will return matching words such as “emboli”, “embolus”, “embolic”, and misspellings like “embolis”. Regular expressions can be combined with logic statements (and, or, not) to form complex and specific matches. Several regular expression-based PE identification algorithms exist but either lack external validation [26–28] or are not described in sufficient detail to replicate [29,30]. These existing algorithms also require additional software packages which add an extra layer of complexity to the data query from the EHR. Regular expressions, however, are supported by query software and can readily be integrated into the data extraction workflow without additional software.

Our objective was to develop and externally validate a simple and portable tool using regular expressions to identify cases of PE in CTA reports and compare the performance of this new tool to three previously described regular expression-based PE identification algorithms.

Materials and methods

We developed READ-PE (Regular Expression Aided Determination of Pulmonary Embolism), an algorithm in structured query language (SQL) that combines a set of regular expressions with simple logic to identify the presence of PE in the impression section of CTA reports. We externally validated the algorithm by measuring performance on an independent cohort of CTA reports. In this independent cohort we also compared the performance of READ-PE to three previously developed regular expression-based approaches: one a downloaded executable program, and two others that we implemented in SQL.

Settings & subjects

We identified derivation and validation cohorts of final radiology reports from CTAs obtained on adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) at two independent, quaternary academic emergency departments (EDs) in the United States (Fig. 1). All reports were written in English.

The derivation cohort consisted of all CTA reports completed between October 1, 2021 and September 30, 2022 for patients at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) EDs. Eligible CTA exams included CTA PE-protocol, CTA chest, CTA chest-abdomen-pelvis, and combined CT angiogram-venogram chest. The validation cohort consisted of a random sample of CTA reports completed between October 1, 2021 and August 25, 2022 for patients at Massachusetts General Brigham (MGB) EDs. A random selection of reports was used in the MGB cohort to select a cohort size feasible for manual review. In the MGB cohort, eligible CTAs included only CTA PE-protocol exams. This difference in exam type occurred since each cohort study was designed independently with the primary purpose of supporting epidemiological studies of PE. CTA PE-protocol exams are often obtained when the primary concern is for possible PE whereas other CTA exams are obtained for multiple other additional reasons. For the purpose of this study, since the reporting of PE on a CTA is independent of the underlying type of exam, we will refer to the exams collectively as CTAs and treat them as equivalent. The study periods at both centers were chosen to acquire a sample over approximately 1-year to reduce any potential impact of seasonality on how CTA reports are formulated.

The unit of analysis was the radiology report, and we included repeat CTA exams on individual patients during the study periods. If there was a duplicate report from a single CTA only one report was kept. Reports with missing impression sections were excluded. We used only the impression sections of CTA reports since the language there is typically clearer and less ambiguous compared to the body of the report, and thus more amenable to regular expression-based identification algorithms. To estimate the frequency with which the body of the report and impression sections disagreed, we independently reviewed the body of the report for presence of PE in a random selection of 50 reports in each cohort and found no disagreements.

The study protocol was approved by institutional review boards at both institutions which issued a waiver of informed consent because the study was retrospective, observational, and presented results in aggregate. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [31]. This work was supported by training grants from the National Institutes of Health, which played no role in the study.

Data source

Data for both cohorts were obtained by query of the EHR clinical databases at each institution using SQL. Both institutions followed similar data collection procedures and use EHRs from Epic Systems Corporation (Verona, Wisconsin). All CTAs were read by staff radiologists, including trainees with attending oversight. The use of direct text entry, dictation software, and interactive templates was at the discretion of each radiologist. Radiology reports were stored in each institution’s EHR with the narrative and impressions sections saved in separate fields. We extracted the impression section for each qualifying report into a secure REDCap™ database for manual review [32,33]. We extracted patient-specific variables at the time of CTA exam from the EHR including age in years, sex, race, and ethnicity. The VUMC cohort has a significant proportion of patients with missing race and ethnicity status because of a change made in 2022 to how race and ethnicity were ascertained, and data ascertained prior to this change were deleted before our study began.

Reference standard by manual review

For the derivation cohort at VUMC, two trained medical students (AA and SJO) independently reviewed all CTA report impressions for the presence or absence of PE. In cases with disagreement, a third reviewer (WBS), a practicing emergency medicine physician, reviewed the report and acted as a tie-breaker. We defined a report as positive for PE if there was any assertion of the presence of PE anywhere in the impression section of the radiology report, regardless of chronicity, timing, or location. For the validation cohort at MGB, a trained research team including four resident physicians (DAB, GAP, CP, SH), and two medical students (GW, KC) each reviewed an independent subset of reports. Each reviewer then over-read a 10 % random sample of another reviewers’ cases to obtain a measure of agreement, but the original reviewers’ classification of absence or presence of PE was maintained.

Algorithm development and validation

An independent researcher (JOW) not involved in manual report review developed READ-PE on CTA report impressions using regular expressions in SQL. The derivation cohort was randomly split into a two-thirds training set and one-third testing set. Each CTA report impression section was pre-processed by dividing the text into individual sentences using the period (‘.’) character. Using an iterative process in the training set, JOW developed a set of regular expressions to identify phrases indicating positivity for PE and phrases indicating negation or an alternative to PE. These rules were combined and applied to each sentence independently. If any sentence in the report had a match for a positive phrase without any negative phrases, then the CTA report was considered positive for PE. The READ-PE algorithm was adjusted until sensitivity and specificity could no longer be jointly improved without accruing losses in the other. We applied the final algorithm to the VUMC testing set for internal validation and to the MGB cohort for external validation. The final algorithm is described as pseudocode (i.e., a code recipe) in Appendix A and a copy of the SQL code is available upon request.

Implementation of existing algorithms

We implemented three existing regular expression-based algorithms and measured their performance in the VUMC testing set [26–28]. These algorithms were selected based upon the availability of the code itself or sufficient instructions to implement the algorithm. We downloaded and ran a standalone executable file developed by Swartz et al. in 2017 [26]. Using pseudocode in the published manuscripts, we wrote SQL code to implement algorithms developed by Tian et al. [27] in 2017 and by Verma et al. in 2022 [28].

Statistical analysis

We used standard descriptive statistics to summarize patient characteristics at the time of the first CTA imaging study. We measured interrater reliability of manual chart reviews with Cohen’s Kappa in the VUMC derivation cohort [34] and with raw percent agreement among the 10 % cross-checked cases in the MGB validation cohort. Algorithmic performance of READ-PE was evaluated with sensitivity, specificity, positive-predictive value, negative-predictive value, and F1 score [35,36]. Algorithm development, performance measures, and cohort descriptive statistics were computed using SQL Server Management Studio version 19 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). Measures of interrater reliability were calculated using R version 4.3.1 [37].

Results

Derivation and validation cohorts

There were 2977 CTA exams performed in VUMCs EDs during the study period yielding 2948 CTA reports included in the derivation cohort after exclusions for duplicate and incomplete reports (Fig. 1). Among 2754 patients in the VUMC derivation cohort the median age was 54 (IQR 39–66) years, 56.7 % were female, 21.6 % were Black or African American, 70.9 % were White, 5.6 % were Hispanic, and 5.8 % received two or more CTA exams during the study period (Table 1). There were 3981 CTA PE-protocol exams completed in MGB EDs during the study period of which 1208 were randomly selected and 1206 reports remained in the validation cohort after excluding two duplicates. Among 1164 patients in the MGB validation cohort the median age was 64 (IQR 54–74) years, 51.9 % were female, 9.6 % were Black or African American, 71.0 % were White, 11.7 % were Hispanic, and 3.5 % received two or more CTA exams during the study period. Following manual review of CTA reports, the incidence of PE in CTA was 11 % in the VUMC derivation cohort and 9 % in the MGB validation cohort. Inter-rater agreement among manual reviewers was high, with Cohen’s kappa 0.94 in the VUMC derivation cohort and overall agreement 88 % in the cross-checked cases in the MGB validation cohort.

Algorithm performance

After iterative development in the two-thirds randomly selected training set from the VUMC derivation cohort, the READ-PE algorithm reached high performance (sensitivity 94 %, specificity 100 %, F1 score 0.96). This high performance was maintained in the one-third testing set (sensitivity 93 %, specificity 99 %, F1 score 0.93). In applying the final algorithm to the external validation cohort from MGB, specificity decreased slightly from 99 % to 98 %, as expected, but sensitivity increased from 93 % to 98 %, and overall performance remained high with F1 score 0.91 (Table 2).

Contingency tables comparing results of our algorithm against manual review are shown in the Appendix A. The algorithm by Swartz et al. implemented using their downloadable program performed well but with lower PPV (79 % vs. 85 %) and F1-score (0.85 vs 0.91) compared to our algorithm [26]. The algorithms described by Tian et al. and Verma et al., both implemented in SQL, performed worse with lower sensitivity, specificity, and F1 scores (Table 3) [27,28].

Discussion

We developed READ-PE, a regular expression algorithm to identify PE in CTA reports among adult ED patients at VUMC and externally validated the algorithm in an independent cohort of CTAs obtained at MGB EDs. The algorithm demonstrated excellent test-characteristics on external validation. The success of READ-PE has implications for the general process of extracting information from unstructured text and offers two notable advantages over previously published NLP approaches.

This study has implications for the general process involved in turning unstructured EHR data into structured outcomes. We are likely to see various applications of large-language-models and other artificial intelligence (AI) technologies which can abstract or perhaps interpret medical records [15]. The performance of our tool over more complex NLP approaches highlights the importance of choosing the right tool for the job. AI may be the ideal tool for identifying complex concepts such as social determinants of health [38], but for more well-defined concepts, established tools such as regular expressions combine sufficient performance with greater transportability and explainability. The ability to classify unstructured text to a label (positive/negative for PE) also contributes to the discussion of whether radiologists should provide the label at the time of report creation [7]. Enforcing too much structure upon radiology reports risks information loss, miscommunication between clinicians, and misdiagnosis [39]. Furthermore, prompting radiologists to enter a coded label, termed a “synoptic report”, for PE during report generation has high accuracy, but may disrupt workflow [40]. Future research may consider the feasibility of applying an algorithm such as READ-PE during the final stages of report drafting to automate report labeling in real time [41].

Compared to prior attempts to identify PE in radiology reports, our approach has two major advantages. First, our approach offers substantially higher transportability across healthcare systems and research teams. Modern NLP pipelines involve between eight and 12 steps which map text to concepts then classify the group of concepts using logic, traditional statistical models, or machine-learning algorithms [21]. NLP has been used successfully to extract PE among other radiology results [20,22–24,42,43]. However, these require extensive expertise and resources to implement. Our approach relies upon regular expressions and simple logic and can be implemented using nearly any data analysis software (e.g. SQL, R, Python, JAVA). A research team seeking to use this algorithm need only include the algorithm in the SQL script that is already used to extract CTA reports from the EHR.

Second, although a handful of other regular-expression and logic-based approaches to extract PE have been published, we report the first externally validated tool. In 2011, Chapman, et al. released the peFinder algorithm for the Python programming language that used regular expressions and a custom negation algorithm and showed moderate performance on external validation [30]. We hoped to replicate their work but found the code library was retired [44], and were unable to implement the code ourselves. We successfully replicated three prior regular expression-based algorithms, but all were inferior to our approach. The standalone program by Swartz, et al. performed comparably to READ-PE, but the use of additional software may be disadvantageous in some settings. For instance, our algorithm in SQL could be used directly in the EHR real-time for clinical decision support whereas using Swartz’s program would require IT configuration, which can add substantial cost and delay to any such program. We replicated algorithms by Tian, et al. and Verma, et al. in SQL, which would have similar portability to ours, but their accuracy was worse with substantially lower F1 scores. This difference in performance was not likely due to overfitting of our algorithm to the derivation data because we demonstrated similar high performance on external validation. The algorithms by Tian, et al. and Verma, et al. were developed on radiology reports in the province of Quebec, Canada and hence may be tailored to language of radiologists in the area which could differ from that in the US-based VUMC and MGB cohorts. Given our cohorts were sourced from different geographically distinct academic medical centers in the US, generalizability to other US academic medical centers is plausible.

Limitations

Our study must be considered in the context of several limitations. First, the finding of PE was based upon manual review of CTA reports and dichotomized as either present or absent. Details surrounding thrombus location and right heart strain were not incorporated into READ-PE, and although reviewers underwent informal training and inter-rater agreement was high, manual review is imperfect as a reference. Second, both our development and validation cohorts were sourced from academic medical centers which use the same EHR vendor (Epic) with similar, though not identical, underlying clinical data infrastructure. Using the EHR vendor with the largest US market share promotes scalability and opportunity for additional validation [45]. However, we did not directly test READ-PE on other SQL software platforms or EHR databases. The algorithm was applied to both cohorts using Microsoft SQL Server Management Studio version 19 to query radiology reports stored in Epic Systems EHR relational databases. We do not expect, given the maturity of regular expression implementations [25], that READ-PE would perform differently in other software. Third, READ-PE is not perfect, and with PPV of 85 % in the validation cohort this means that 15 % of reports labeled positive will not have PE by manual review. However, manual review is resource intensive and human performance decays with fatigue while the performance of a machine is stable over many repetitions. Fourth, although we externally validated READ-PE, our algorithm may not perform as well in in other settings if drastically different wording may be used to formulate the report. Fifth, all types of CTA of the chest were included in the derivation cohort whereas the validation cohort included only CTA PE-protocols. In theory this difference could artificially inflate or deflate the test-characteristics in the derivation cohort, but since 82 % of exams in the VUMC derivation cohort were CTA PE-protocol this is unlikely to change the overall conclusion. Sixth, READ-PE was developed in the English language for CTA reports. READ-PE should not be directly translated into another language nor directly applied to reports for other radiology modalities (e.g. ventilation-perfusion scans), although a similar development strategy could be used. We also did not include CT exams in which PE is occasionally found incidentally (e.g. CTA neck, CT Chest without contrast). The READ-PE algorithm may perform well in other CTs, since the language in the reports is likely similar, but we did not test this assertion. Finally, language and practices around generating CTA reports may shift over time leading to drift in performance [46]. We anticipate the READ-PE algorithm will require surveillance and maintenance over time.

Conclusions

READ-PE is an externally validated, portable text-matching regular expression-based algorithm in SQL that identifies PE in English-language radiology reports from CTA chest imaging obtained in the ED. It demonstrates excellent test characteristics and may be used to accurately identify PE in radiology reports for retrospective research or prospective surveillance with minimal investment of time and technical expertise. The READ-PE algorithm may require maintenance over time. If implemented in other settings, it should first undergo local validation according to local policies.
