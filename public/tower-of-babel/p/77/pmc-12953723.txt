Comparative Efficacy of SGLT2 Inhibitors in MASLD: Bayesian Network Meta‐Analysis of CAP–LSM Outcomes and Time Effects
Authors: Demver P Gomez, Wilmyr F Hababag, Celeste C Ong‐Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disorder worldwide. Sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucose‐lowering agents with cardiovascular benefits, have shown hepatoprotective potential. Their comparative effects on steatosis and fibrosis, their interrelationship, and the role of treatment duration remain uncertain.ObjectivesTo compare the effects of individual SGLT2 inhibitors on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, and to assess correlations between controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM), as well as the impact of treatment duration.MethodsRandomized controlled trials of SGLT2 inhibitors in MASLD were synthesized using Bayesian network meta‐analysis. Hepatic steatosis (CAP) and fibrosis (LSM) were analyzed separately as mean differences (MDs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Prespecified study‐level effect modifiers were evaluated using Bayesian network meta‐regression. Comparative rankings were derived using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). Trial‐level concordance between CAP and LSM was assessed using bivariate Bayesian meta‐analysis.ResultsFourteen RCTs (n = 855) were included. Compared with standard care, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced CAP (MD 10.48 dB/m; 95% CrI 0.53 to 18.72) and LSM (MD 0.57 kPa; 95% CrI 0.02 to 1.12). Dapagliflozin showed the most consistently favorable estimates across both outcomes. Trial‐level treatment effects on CAP and LSM were highly concordant. Treatment duration was not significantly associated with treatment effects; most trials lasted ≤ 24 weeks.ConclusionsSGLT2 inhibitors, particularly dapagliflozin, were associated with improvements in non‐invasive markers of steatosis and fibrosis in MASLD. CAP and LSM show aligned trends but remain biologically distinct, underscoring the need for longer, histology‐driven, multiethnic trials to confirm true antifibrotic efficacy.RegistrationPROSPERO CRD420251146413.Keywords: controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), liver stiffness measurement (LSM), MASLD, NAFLD, SGLT2 inhibitors

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide, affecting an estimated 10%–30% of the US population [1]. In the Philippines, the disease impacts approximately 12.2% of individuals, with rising incidence paralleling global trends in obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [2]. As the disease progresses, it can lead to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, underscoring the need for effective therapeutic strategies and precise disease monitoring.

Noninvasive imaging modalities have become central to both clinical practice and research [3]. The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) quantifies hepatic steatosis, while liver stiffness measurement (LSM) estimates hepatic fibrosis. Both indices, derived from elastography‐based techniques, are now established endpoints in clinical trials and real‐world disease monitoring for staging MASLD and evaluating treatment response. Importantly, while steatosis and fibrosis are pathophysiologically linked, they do not always progress in parallel [4]. Whether fibrosis regression simply reflects steatosis reduction or occurs independently remains an open question, and clarifying this relationship is critical for understanding drug mechanisms and selecting reliable surrogate endpoints.

Sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, initially developed as glucose‐lowering agents, have demonstrated broad cardiometabolic benefits that extend beyond glycemic control. These include potential hepatoprotective effects through weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity, and attenuation of lipotoxicity and inflammation. Emerging evidence from randomized controlled trials suggests benefits on both steatosis and fibrosis; however, the extent, timing, and comparative effectiveness across individual agents remain uncertain. Prior systematic reviews [5, 6] have largely relied on pairwise comparisons versus control, analyzed CAP and LSM as independent outcomes, and seldom accounted for treatment duration or the temporal interplay between fat and fibrosis. These methodological limitations hinder precise treatment selection and obscure mechanistic insights.

To address these gaps, we conducted a Bayesian network meta‐analysis to compare the efficacy of individual SGLT2 inhibitors on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in adults with MASLD. Our study applied joint modeling of CAP and LSM to explore their interrelationship, evaluate whether fibrosis improvements were directionally concordant with reductions in steatosis or suggestive of effects beyond steatosis reduction, and to assess the influence of treatment duration on these outcomes.

Methodology

Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review and network meta‐analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251146413) and conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines [7]. We restricted inclusion to RCTs, irrespective of blinding, that reported transient elastography–based CAP and/or LSM outcomes. Trials that assessed other imaging or biochemical endpoints without CAP/LSM data were excluded.

Inclusion Criteria

(1) participants aged ≥ 18 years with NAFLD/MASLD or T2DM, regardless of comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, obesity); (2) intervention with any SGLT2 inhibitor, irrespective of treatment duration, frequency, or dosage; (3) comparator groups receiving placebo or standard care; and (4) availability of full‐text articles.

Exclusion Criteria

(a) RCTs that did not report elastography‐based CAP or LSM outcomes; (b) animal studies; (c) studies involving pregnant or breastfeeding women; (d) non‐RCT designs; (e) other publication types such as reviews, conference abstracts, meta‐analyses, incomplete reports, or case studies.

Primary Outcomes

Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP), an elastography‐based measure of hepatic steatosis reported in dB/m.

Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP), an elastography‐based measure of hepatic steatosis reported in dB/m.

Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM), a transient elastography‐derived estimate of fibrosis reported in kilopascals (kPa).

Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM), a transient elastography‐derived estimate of fibrosis reported in kilopascals (kPa).

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic literature search using PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Library for studies published from January 2000 to December 2025, without language restrictions. MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms were utilized and entered into the PubMed database using the following subject terms: (Sodium‐Glucose Transporter 2 Inhibitors) OR (SGLT2 Inhibitors) OR (Dapagliflozin) OR (Ipragliflozin) OR (Empagliflozin) OR (Canagliflozin) OR (Licogliflozin) OR (Ertugliflozin) OR (Luseogliflozin) AND (Non‐alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease) OR (NAFLD) OR (Fatty Liver) OR (Liver Disease). Study screening and data extraction were conducted independently by two reviewers (D.P.G. and W.F.H.), with disagreements resolved by consensus. A medical librarian was not involved in the design or execution of the search strategy.

Data Extraction and Assessment for Study Quality

The collected data included the following information: title and primary author with citation details, study design, location of the study, total duration, and type and number of participants. Additionally, patient baseline information, type of drug used, treatment duration, dosages, and administration frequencies were included. Cochrane Bias tool 2.0 was used to evaluate the quality of RCTs, focusing on the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and reporting bias (selective reporting). Studies were then classified as having “low risk,” “high risk,” or “some concerns” of bias.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted a Bayesian network meta‐analysis (NMA) to compare the effects of individual SGLT2 inhibitors on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, measured using the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM). NMA allows the integration of direct and indirect evidence to estimate comparative treatment effects even in the absence of head‐to‐head trials [8]. Analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1) [9] using the bnma package with JAGS (v4.3.1). Four chains of 20,000 iterations were run, discarding the first 10,000 as burn‐in and applying a thinning interval of 10, yielding 8000 posterior samples. Convergence was verified via trace plots and the Gelman–Rubin statistic (R̂ < 1.01).

We selected a Bayesian framework as the primary analytic approach because of its advantages in sparse treatment networks and dual‐outcome modeling. With only 14 randomized controlled trials and limited head‐to‐head evidence, frequentist network meta‐analysis models can be unstable, whereas Bayesian methods allow full propagation of posterior uncertainty, probabilistic treatment ranking, and straightforward incorporation of meta‐regression.

Choice of Fixed‐ vs. Random‐Effects Models

The choice between fixed‐ and random‐effects models was guided primarily by a priori clinical and methodological considerations rather than by post hoc statistical testing. Given the clear diversity across included trials, such as differences in patient populations (MASLD with and without type 2 diabetes), baseline disease severity, specific SGLT2 inhibitors evaluated, comparator regimens, treatment duration, and imaging methodologies for CAP and LSM, a single common underlying treatment effect could not be reasonably assumed. Accordingly, random‐effects models were prespecified and adopted as the primary analytic framework, allowing true treatment effects to vary across studies and providing inference that is more generalizable beyond the included trials. Fixed‐effect models were additionally estimated for comparison and sensitivity purposes, reflecting the hypothetical scenario of homogeneity across studies, but were not used for primary inference. The appropriateness of the random‐effects approach was further supported empirically by non‐zero between‐study variance and I 2 values exceeding conventional thresholds in several pooled analyses. All Bayesian network meta‐analyses and meta‐regression models were therefore implemented within hierarchical random‐effects frameworks, consistent with established methodological guidance for evidence synthesis in heterogeneous and sparsely connected networks.

Effect Size Computation and Model Evaluation

Continuous outcomes were summarized as mean differences (MDs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Change‐from‐baseline values were used preferentially; endpoint values were included only when baseline measures were balanced. Missing SDs were derived from SEs, CIs, or p‐values or imputed using accepted methods. Model adequacy was assessed using residual deviance and the deviance information criterion (DIC). Treatment hierarchies were summarized using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used exclusively within the bivariate model, whereas all primary univariate analyses and treatment rankings retained MDs to ensure clinical interpretability of effect sizes.

All treatment effects were parameterized relative to standard care as the reference comparator. Accordingly, regression coefficients for SGLT2 inhibitors represent the contrast (SGLT2i—Standard Care), while coefficients for placebo represent (Placebo—Standard Care). Indirect comparisons between SGLT2 inhibitors and placebo were derived by subtraction of these coefficients, consistent with the transitivity principle in network meta‐analysis.

Assessment of Transitivity and Clinical Comparability

The feasibility of network meta‐analysis depends on the validity of the transitivity assumption, which requires that key effect modifiers be similarly distributed across treatment comparisons within the connected network. Transitivity was evaluated by constructing a comprehensive effect‐modifier table (Table S1) summarizing age, sex, BMI, glycemic status, baseline CAP/LSM, and background metabolic therapies. Baseline demographics and disease severity showed substantial overlap across studies, supporting biological comparability. CAP and LSM values clustered within similar ranges, indicating broadly comparable degrees of steatosis and mild–moderate fibrosis.

However, background therapy revealed notable methodological heterogeneity. Standard‐care regimens ranged from lifestyle‐only interventions to metformin‐, pioglitazone‐, sulfonylurea‐, GLP‐1 receptor agonist–, or insulin‐based combinations, all of which may independently influence hepatic fat content and fibrosis progression. Accordingly, background metabolic therapy was prespecified as a key effect modifier with particular relevance to transitivity. Several trials permitting GLP‐1 receptor agonists or insulin did not share a common comparator, potentially limiting coherence across portions of the network.

To address this, exploratory meta‐regression analyses were used to contextualize the impact of variation in standard of care on treatment effects. While these analyses supported the robustness of class‐level estimates, indirect comparisons anchored on intensified standard‐care regimens (e.g., GLP‐1 receptor agonists or insulin) should be interpreted cautiously due to potential intransitivity. Consequently, SUCRA‐based treatment rankings are presented as probabilistic summaries rather than definitive measures of comparative efficacy.

Stratified Analyses by Metabolic Phenotype

To contextualize heterogeneity and support assessment of transitivity, exploratory analyses were stratified by metabolic phenotype. Studies were categorized according to enrollment of patients with MASLD with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) versus MASLD without diabetes. Within each stratum, treatment effects on controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) were summarized descriptively using Bayesian network meta‐analysis when network connectivity was preserved and pairwise random‐effects meta‐analysis otherwise. These analyses were not intended to formally test effect modification. Given the limited number of studies within strata and resulting loss of network connectivity (MASLD‐only: 3 studies; T2DM‐only: 1 study), stratified analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating.

Bivariate Bayesian Network Meta‐Analysis

To jointly evaluate the effects of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, a bivariate Bayesian network meta‐analysis was conducted with CAP and LSM modeled simultaneously. This framework allows the correlation between CAP and LSM to be explicitly accounted for, thereby improving statistical efficiency and coherence compared with separate univariate analyses. Because CAP and LSM are measured on different physical scales and units, treatment effects within the bivariate model were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs), placing both outcomes on a common scale while preserving the direction and uncertainty of treatment effects.

Meta‐Regression and Effect Modification

Because network meta‐regression was limited by sparse data for some covariates, we additionally conducted complementary frequentist study‐level meta‐regression analyses to further explore potential effect modification by metabolic factors. Pairwise mean differences and standard errors were calculated from arm‐level means and standard deviations, and random‐effects models using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) were fitted. Each moderator was entered individually as a study‐level covariate, including baseline HbA1c (glycemic severity), baseline BMI (adiposity), and diabetes status (T2DM vs. MASLD‐only).

These analyses were considered exploratory because study‐level meta‐regression is subject to ecological bias and because the small number of available studies (k = 14) limited statistical power. For the same reasons, subgroup network meta‐analysis was not attempted, as metabolic subgroups lacked sufficient studies to preserve network connectivity.

Assessment of Inconsistency, Heterogeneity, and Publication Bias

Inconsistency was assessed through comparisons of consistency and inconsistency models. Heterogeneity was quantified using τ 2 (very low < 0.04; low–moderate 0.04–0.15; high > 0.15). Publication bias was examined using funnel plots, Egger's regression, and Begg–Mazumdar tests, interpreted cautiously given the small number of available trials.

Study Selection and Study Characteristics

The detailed study selection process and search results are presented in Figure 1. A total of 974 studies were identified through the literature search: 726 from PubMed, 135 from CENTRAL, 56 from Embase, and 57 from trial registries. After removing duplicates, 646 records were screened. Of these, 430 were excluded based on title and abstract, leaving 216 studies for further evaluation. Failure to report elastography‐based CAP or LSM outcomes (n = 18), non‐randomized controlled trials (N = 142), conference abstracts (N = 30), and incomplete trial results (N = 10) were excluded. Fourteen studies were deemed eligible for inclusion.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection process for randomized controlled trials assessing SGLT2 inhibitors in MASLD.

In the included trials, placebo groups consistently received structured lifestyle counseling (dietary advice and exercise), while standard care groups typically continued background antidiabetic therapy (e.g., metformin, pioglitazone, insulin). Thus, both placebo and standard care arms functioned as active comparators rather than inert controls. To account for this, placebo and standard care were modeled as separate nodes in the primary network, with sensitivity analyses combining them into a unified non‐SGLT2i comparator.

The included studies, published between 2019 and 2025, were predominantly conducted in Asia and primarily involved patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Exceptions were four studies: Tontivuthikul Lee et al., Taheri et al., and Taha et al. which focused on either T2DM alone or NAFLD alone. The meta‐analysis comprises 855 randomized patients, with sample sizes ranging from 23 to 131. The primary interventions were dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, with treatment durations varying between 12 and 48 weeks. Risk of Bias in Studies

The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool. Two reviewers independently evaluated all domains, achieving substantial agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.84). Overall, six trials were judged at low risk of bias. Seven trials: Weng et al., Fukada et al., Taha et al., Gharabagh et al., Shimizu et al., Hu et al., and Takeshita et al. were rated as having some concerns, mainly related to unclear allocation concealment and open‐label designs. One trial (Yoneda et al.) was rated high risk for its open‐label design and limited reporting. Although one open‐label trial assessing both CAP and LSM posed potential performance bias, the objective nature of FibroScan minimizes this risk. Thus, while the evidence base is generally robust, approximately 57% of studies had at least one domain rated as “some concerns” or “high risk,” introducing potential uncertainty into the pooled estimates (Figure 2).

Node size reflects the total number of randomized participants per intervention, while connecting lines indicate direct treatment comparisons, with thickness proportional to the number of contributing studies.

Network geometry for CAP (A) and LSM (B).

For CAP (A), the network is predominantly anchored on placebo and standard care, with empagliflozin representing the most connected active comparator. Evidence for canagliflozin and ipragliflozin is limited and contributes mainly through indirect comparisons, while dapagliflozin is primarily linked via standard care, resulting in a sparsely connected network with few closed loops.

For LSM (B), the network is centered on standard care, connecting dapagliflozin, tofogliflozin, empagliflozin, and placebo. Direct placebo‐controlled evidence is largely restricted to empagliflozin, with other agents informed mainly through indirect comparisons via standard care.

Overall, the network geometry reflects the predominance of placebo‐ and standard care–controlled trials and the limited availability of direct head‐to‐head comparisons between individual SGLT2 inhibitors for both steatosis and fibrosis outcomes.

Effect on Hepatic Steatosis (CAP)

Effect on Hepatic Steatosis (CAP)

As shown in Figure 4, compared with standard care, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with a favorable reduction in hepatic steatosis (posterior median MD 10.48 dB; 95% CrI 0.53–18.72). The placebo versus standard care comparison was imprecise (median 5.49 dB; 95% CrI −11.51 to 21.31). Indirect comparisons favored SGLT2 inhibitors over placebo (median 4.90 dB; 95% CrI −9.99 to 19.57). Overall, SGLT2 inhibitors showed the most consistent signal toward CAP improvement.

Forest Plot of Bayesian NMA Comparing SGLT2 Inhibitors, Placebo, and Standard Care on Hepatic Steatosis.

Pairwise comparisons among individual agents are shown in Figure S1. Relative to standard care, dapagliflozin (median 13.05 dB; 95% CrI −0.23 to 23.81), ipragliflozin (18.83 dB; 95% CrI −12.54 to 49.65), and empagliflozin (4.39 dB; 95% CrI −14.64 to 20.81) showed numerically favorable estimates, whereas canagliflozin demonstrated more variable and generally unfavorable effects (median −12.44 dB; 95% CrI −48.65 to 22.28). Wide credible intervals across all comparisons and head‐to‐head contrasts precluded reliable differentiation among individual agents, supporting a class‐level rather than agent‐specific effect.

Stratified analyses by metabolic phenotype (Figure S2) further reduced precision. Among patients with T2DM and MASLD, SGLT2 inhibitors versus standard care yielded a posterior median difference of 11.08 dB/m (95% CrI 24.20 to −3.89). In NAFLD without diabetes, treatment effects were highly uncertain (13.39 dB/m; 95% CrI 73.40 to −55.68).

IIEffect on Hepatic Fibrosis (LSM)

Effect on Hepatic Fibrosis (LSM)

As shown in Figure 5, compared with standard care, SGLT2 inhibitor therapy was associated with reduced liver stiffness (median 0.57 kPa; 95% CrI 0.02–1.12). In contrast, placebo showed no clear effect versus standard care (median 0.01 kPa; 95% CrI −1.26 to 1.17). Indirect comparisons favored SGLT2 inhibitors over placebo (median −0.55 kPa), although the credible interval crossed the null (95% CrI −1.76 to 0.53).

Forest Plot of Bayesian NMA for the Effect of SGLT2 Inhibitors, Placebo, and Standard Care on LSM.

Detailed pairwise comparisons among individual agents are shown in Figure S3. Dapagliflozin showed statistically probable reductions in liver stiffness versus standard care (median 0.94 kPa; 95% CrI 0.20–1.71) and placebo (median 1.44 kPa; 95% CrI 0.05–2.99). All other comparisons, including those involving empagliflozin, tofogliflozin, and head‐to‐head contrasts among SGLT2 inhibitors, had credible intervals crossing the null.

Stratified analyses by metabolic phenotype (Figure S4) further reduced precision. Among patients with T2DM and MASLD, SGLT2 inhibitors versus standard care yielded a posterior median difference of 0.53 kPa (95% CrI 1.09 to −0.02). Among patients with MASLD without diabetes, estimates were highly uncertain (0.07 kPa; 95% CrI 82.69 to −82.27).

As shown in Figure 6, SUCRA rankings indicated variability in the relative performance of individual SGLT2 inhibitors across outcomes. For CAP, ipragliflozin ranked highest (SUCRA 0.84), followed by dapagliflozin (0.80) and empagliflozin (0.56), whereas canagliflozin (0.16) and placebo (0.26) ranked lowest. For LSM, dapagliflozin ranked highest (SUCRA 0.89), followed by tofogliflozin (0.79) and empagliflozin (0.33), with standard care (0.38) and placebo (0.12) ranking lowest.

SUCRA rankings of SGLT2 inhibitors versus placebo and standard care for (A) CAP reduction and (B) LSM reduction.

Overall, dapagliflozin demonstrated consistently favorable rankings across both steatosis and fibrosis outcomes. However, as SUCRA reflects relative ranking rather than effect magnitude or certainty, these findings should be interpreted in conjunction with the corresponding effect estimates, credible intervals, and the limited connectivity of the evidence network.

Concordance of Treatment Effects Between CAP and LSM

In the joint bivariate Bayesian model, SGLT2 inhibitor therapy was associated with concordant, moderate improvements in hepatic steatosis and liver stiffness. Compared with standard care, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced CAP (median SMD 0.36; 95% credible interval [CrI] 0.20–0.53) and LSM (median SMD 0.31; 95% CrI 0.15–0.47), with credible intervals excluding the null for both outcomes. Similar benefits were observed versus placebo for CAP (median 0.39; 95% CrI 0.13–0.65) and LSM (median 0.38; 95% CrI 0.12–0.64). In contrast, placebo versus standard care comparisons for both outcomes were centered near zero, with credible intervals spanning the null. Taken together, these findings indicate strongly concordant treatment effects across steatosis‐ and fibrosis‐related measures, supporting a coherent beneficial profile of SGLT2 inhibitors on both CAP and LSM at the study level.

IIIMeta‐Regression for Effect Modification (Patient Characteristics)

Meta‐Regression for Effect Modification (Patient Characteristics)

Prespecified Bayesian network meta‐regression analyses were performed to examine potential effect modification and sources of between‐study heterogeneity for hepatic steatosis (CAP) and liver fibrosis (LSM). Across all models, none of the evaluated study‐level patient characteristics demonstrated a statistically probable association with treatment effects, with all posterior estimates characterized by wide 95% credible intervals spanning the null.

For CAP (Table S2), none of the evaluated study‐level covariates demonstrated a statistically credible effect‐modifying association. Mean age showed a directionally positive but imprecise association (posterior median 13.29; 95% CrI −17.83 to 35.22), while baseline HbA1c (posterior median −21.32; 95% CrI −143.08 to 146.78), baseline BMI (posterior median 2.38; 95% CrI −229.99 to 295.22), and baseline CAP severity (posterior median 1.58; 95% CrI −28.18 to 18.73) likewise showed no statistically credible modification, with wide credible intervals reflecting limited precision. Adjustment for these covariates did not materially alter treatment estimates: SGLT2 inhibitors continued to demonstrate reductions in CAP versus standard care, with 95% credible intervals excluding zero (posterior medians −10.37 to −13.88), whereas placebo showed no probabilistic benefit and direct SGLT2 inhibitor–placebo comparisons remained uncertain.

Similarly, for LSM (Table S3), no study‐level covariate including age, baseline HbA1c, BMI, or baseline LSM demonstrated a statistically credible effect‐modifying interaction. Across covariate‐adjusted models, SGLT2 inhibitors retained a consistent reduction in liver stiffness compared with standard care (posterior medians −0.56 to −0.60; 95% CrIs excluding zero), while placebo showed no clear benefit. Although baseline HbA1c did not function as an effect modifier, adjustment for HbA1c shifted the conditional treatment contrast, yielding a statistically probable advantage for SGLT2 inhibitors versus placebo (posterior median −1.23; 95% CrI −2.19 to −0.26).

Overall, these findings indicate that no statistically detectable effect modification was observed at the study level across evaluated covariates. However, these analyses were underpowered, subject to residual heterogeneity, and ecological in nature, precluding inference regarding patient‐level biological uniformity of treatment effects.

IVEffect of Treatment Duration on CAP and LSM

Effect of Treatment Duration on CAP and LSM

In prespecified Bayesian network meta‐regression analyses, treatment duration (weeks) was modeled as a continuous covariate to assess potential time‐dependent effects. Duration was not credibly associated with changes in either CAP (β = +0.21 dB/m per week; 95% credible interval [−1.28, 1.75]) or LSM (β = −0.012 kPa per week; 95% credible interval [−0.0338, 0.0103]), with credible intervals spanning the null for both outcomes. Inclusion of treatment duration did not meaningfully improve model fit, as reflected by minimal changes in the deviance information criterion. Overall, within the observed follow‐up range (12–48 weeks), treatment duration did not appear to materially modify the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on hepatic steatosis or liver stiffness.

The network meta‐regression plots (Figure S5) display the predicted treatment effects on CAP and LSM across increasing treatment duration, with standard care as the reference. For CAP, SGLT2 inhibitors demonstrated a consistently favorable effect relative to standard care throughout the observed duration range, while placebo effects remained centered near the null. For LSM, SGLT2 inhibitors showed directionally greater reductions than placebo at comparable follow‐up times. However, for both outcomes, credible intervals widened at longer durations and overlapped substantially between treatment groups, reflecting increasing uncertainty driven by sparse long‐term data.

VConsistency Assessment

Consistency Assessment

In the SGLT2 inhibitor network for MASLD, study‐specific deviance contributions clustered closely around the line of identity in the inconsistency plots (Figure S6), indicating that allowing for inconsistency did not meaningfully improve model fit. This finding supports the assumption of network consistency, with good agreement between direct and indirect evidence for both CAP and LSM outcomes. No contrasts demonstrated substantially better fit under the inconsistency model, suggesting an absence of important local incoherence. However, the network geometry was relatively sparse, with few closed loops, which limits the power to formally detect inconsistency. Between‐study heterogeneity was low (τ 2 = 0.032 for CAP and τ 2 = 0.011 for LSM), further supporting overall network coherence.

VIReporting Biases in Synthesis

Reporting Biases in Synthesis

Visual inspection of the comparison‐adjusted funnel plot (Figure S7) did not reveal clear evidence of small‐study effects or publication bias. Study estimates were distributed symmetrically around the network‐adjusted pooled effect, with greater dispersion among smaller studies as expected from sampling variability, and no preferential absence of unfavorable or null results for SGLT2 inhibitors. Egger's regression test yielded a p‐value of 0.257, and the Begg–Mazumdar rank correlation test yielded a p‐value of 0.333. Neither test provided statistically significant evidence of small‐study effects; however, the limited number of included trials substantially reduces the power and reliability of these assessments.

Discussion

In this Bayesian network meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with a moderate reduction in hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter, CAP) and a smaller but statistically credible reduction in liver stiffness (LSM), a non‐invasive surrogate of fibrosis. Dapagliflozin showed the most consistently favorable estimates across both outcomes versus standard care. It emerged as the most consistent agent, but this may partly reflect trial design advantages. It was evaluated in multiple RCTs with larger sample sizes and varied populations. Thus, its robust evidence base should not be interpreted solely as pharmacologic superiority, but also as a function of stronger supporting data. Tofogliflozin also reduced LSM, although with smaller magnitude. Other agents showed numerically favorable but statistically inconclusive effects with wide credible intervals crossing the null. Given sparse head‐to‐head data and imprecision in several comparisons, agent‐level differences should be interpreted cautiously.

Several placebo arms demonstrated apparent improvements in CAP or LSM. These trials predominantly enrolled patients with MASLD without T2DM, a population that generally responds more favorably to lifestyle modification. In addition, lifestyle counseling (dietary advice and exercise) was consistently provided as part of placebo treatment, which is expected to contribute to reductions in hepatic steatosis and stiffness in at least a subset of participants. These observations indicate that the apparent placebo response is best interpreted as the combined effect of background lifestyle interventions and inherent disease variability, rather than evidence of a true pharmacologic effect of placebo itself. Notably, our exploratory meta‐regression did not detect baseline HbA1c, BMI, or diabetes status as statistically significant study‐level effect modifiers of changes in CAP or LSM, suggesting that although clinical differences between MASLD‐only and MASLD+T2DM populations may influence responsiveness to lifestyle counseling, any such effects were either modest in magnitude or inadequately captured by the available study‐level aggregate covariates.

These findings align with prior meta‐analyses (e.g., Zhou; Ong Lopez) showing favorable effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on hepatic fat and stiffness. Unlike prior pairwise analyses that modeled CAP and LSM independently, we jointly evaluated these outcomes within a Bayesian network meta‐analytic framework. This joint modeling approach allows coherent estimation of treatment effects across hepatic steatosis (CAP) and liver stiffness (LSM) while accounting for shared trial‐level information and time‐dependent effects. To our knowledge, this is the first network meta‐analysis in MASLD to jointly evaluate these two non‐invasive endpoints within a unified Bayesian framework, demonstrating concordant treatment effects across outcomes rather than independent univariate estimates.

We observed a directionally aligned trial‐level association between treatment effects on controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM), indicating concordant changes across outcomes at the study level. This association reflects between‐trial patterns rather than patient‐level coupling and does not imply a direct mechanistic relationship, particularly given that steatosis and fibrosis may evolve along distinct biological trajectories. Consistent with this interpretation, meta‐regression analyses did not identify a statistically credible association between treatment duration (12–48 weeks) and changes in CAP or LSM. This likely reflects early saturation of steatosis response and the limited duration of available randomized trials, rather than absence of therapeutic effect. Reductions in hepatic steatosis have been shown to occur rapidly, with measurable improvements as early as 8–12 weeks [24, 25], which may explain the apparent plateau in CAP by 24 weeks. In contrast, fibrosis remodeling is biologically slower and may require longer treatment exposure to demonstrate consistent benefit; however, most included RCTs were limited to ≤ 48 weeks of follow‐up [26]. Accordingly, the null duration slope should be interpreted as reflecting both early stabilization of steatosis and insufficient trial duration to reliably capture slower antifibrotic changes.

Dapagliflozin ranked highest for both outcomes by SUCRA; however, rankings derived from a sparse and heterogeneous network should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Notably, although dapagliflozin demonstrated the most consistent effect estimates for controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) across trials, ipragliflozin ranked highest for CAP by SUCRA, a discrepancy that reflects the instability of ranking metrics in sparse networks rather than a contradiction in effect estimates or evidence of superior efficacy. This apparent advantage is biologically plausible but not established. Dapagliflozin is relatively lipophilic and undergoes substantial hepatic metabolism via CYP3A4, which may increase intrahepatic exposure compared with other SGLT2 inhibitors. In contrast, empagliflozin is predominantly renally eliminated; canagliflozin undergoes hepatic glucuronidation with partial SGLT1 inhibition at higher doses, and ipragliflozin and tofogliflozin are hepatically metabolized but have been evaluated in fewer and smaller trials. These pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences, together with variability in trial design and limited comparative evidence, may contribute to heterogeneity in observed liver outcomes across the class rather than reflecting true differences in intrinsic efficacy. Supporting a class effect, preclinical studies of SGLT2 inhibition demonstrate activation of AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), suppression of NF‐κB signaling, and downregulation of NLRP3 inflammasome and TGF‐β1 pathways [27, 28, 29], mechanisms consistent with improvements in hepatic steatosis and fibrosis.

Only two RCTs to date (Takeshita et al. and Lin et al.) have reported histology‐confirmed outcomes, demonstrating improvements in steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. These biopsy‐based findings provide biological plausibility and external support for our results, which showed favorable effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on non‐invasive surrogates (CAP and LSM). While CAP and LSM are valuable for detecting steatosis and fibrosis, they cannot replace biopsy‐proven resolution of steatohepatitis or fibrosis, particularly given their asynchronous trajectories. Notably, both RCTs were conducted in Asian populations, which may constrain generalizability given known ethnic differences in adiposity, insulin resistance, and disease progression among different races and regions.

Limitations

The evidence base was limited, with sparse network geometry, restricting the precision of drug‐specific estimates. Consequently, SUCRA‐based treatment rankings should be interpreted cautiously. Because SUCRA assumes a broadly comparable standard of care across trials, heterogeneity in background metabolic regimens challenges the transitivity assumption and may destabilize ranking metrics; accordingly, SUCRA results should be considered exploratory rather than definitive.

Most trials were short to moderate in duration (12–48 weeks) and relied primarily on non‐invasive imaging rather than histology, with only two studies reporting biopsy‐confirmed outcomes. Differences in imaging protocols, probe selection, and operator technique likely contributed to additional measurement heterogeneity, while variability in baseline disease severity and concomitant therapies further limits causal interpretation.

All analyses were conducted at the study level; therefore, observed associations including concordant treatment effects across CAP and LSM reflect ecological patterns rather than patient‐level relationships and cannot establish mediation or temporal sequencing. The sparse network and small number of studies also limited power to detect inconsistency or effect modification, and null findings from meta‐regression should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, all included RCTs enrolled Asian populations, which may limit generalizability to broader MASLD populations.

Conclusion

SGLT2 inhibitors appear to be associated with modest improvements in non‐invasive markers of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in MASLD, although the certainty of comparative effectiveness between individual agents remains limited. While dapagliflozin ranked highest by SUCRA, treatment rankings should be interpreted cautiously given the small and heterogeneous evidence base, variability in standard‐of‐care comparators, and the instability of ranking metrics in sparse networks. Trial‐level treatment effects on CAP and LSM were directionally concordant, but this observation is derived from few studies with relatively short follow‐up and does not establish causal or temporal relationships between steatosis and fibrosis improvement. The lack of a clear duration–response signal may reflect limited follow‐up rather than absence of longer‐term effects. Overall, larger, longer‐duration randomized trials with standardized background therapy, histologic endpoints, and more diverse populations are needed to better define class effects and determine whether clinically meaningful differences exist among individual SGLT2 inhibitors, and the present findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating.
