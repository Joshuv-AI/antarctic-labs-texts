Enhanced proteolytic stability and distinct mechanisms of a D-amino acid-modified antimicrobial peptide against Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Sirinthip Khlaychinda, Utid Suriya, Sittiruk Roytrakul, Ratchaneewan Aunpad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising alternatives to conventional antibiotics for the treatment of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens; however, their clinical translation is limited by proteolytic degradation. We developed dPA-13, an all-D-enantiomer of the 13-mer AMP PA-13, to improve resistance. dPA-13 exhibited potent bactericidal activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, superior protease stability, and a favorable safety profile. Confocal imaging and comparative proteomics revealed distinct mechanistic differences: while PA-13 localized to the membrane, dPA-13 exhibited enhanced cytoplasmic translocation. Proteomic analysis showed dPA-13 triggers a comprehensive cellular response, upregulating oxidoreductases (e.g., catalase), DNA repair machinery, and metal-ion binding proteins, while downregulating metabolic enzymes, transport permeases, and virulence factors (PilT and flagellar systems). Molecular docking corroborated these shifts, identifying high binding affinities toward critical targets, notably PilT, stabilized by specific electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding motifs at Glu394 and His397. Furthermore, P. aeruginosa developed resistance to dPA-13 significantly slower than to ciprofloxacin. Collectively, dPA-13 is a proteolytically robust, dual-action antimicrobial that disrupts both membrane integrity and intracellular homeostasis, positioning it as a promising candidate for recalcitrant MDR infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49275-8.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health concern that causes at least a million deaths worldwide each year, and an estimated 5 million deaths in 20191. One of the most common antimicrobial-resistant pathogens is Pseudomonas aeruginosa2. It typically affects hospitalized patients or those with compromised immune systems. Some strains exhibit multidrug resistance (MDR), making them resistant to nearly all antibiotics, including carbapenems, which are often used as the last-line drug for treatment of these infections3. Thus, there is a critical need to find an effective alternative therapy to fight against antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) provide a promising alternative therapeutic strategy due to their broad-spectrum activity, including those drug-resistant. As key components of the innate immune system4, AMPs exert antimicrobial effects through both membrane-disruptive and non-membrane mechanisms, reducing the likelihood of resistance development5. However, their clinical use as therapeutic agents is limited by poor stability in proteolytic environments6. To address proteolytic instability, D-amino acid substitution is commonly employed to enhance resistance to enzymatic degradation. This modification alters peptide stereochemistry, improving stability while generally preserving physicochemical properties and antimicrobial efficacy7. For example, the D-enantiomer of AP19 has been shown to retain potent antimicrobial activity even under proteolytic conditions8. Similarly, D1018, a D-amino acid-modified analog of IDR-1018, exhibits significant resistance to enzymatic degradation while maintaining strong antibacterial activity9. These examples support the use of D-amino acid substitution as a rational strategy to improve peptide stability without compromising bioactivity.

Previously, we demonstrated that the 13-mer peptide PA-13 has potent activity against MDR P. aeruginosa and low toxicity toward human cells. However, its therapeutic potential is limited by poor stability in proteolytic environments10. To address this, all L-amino acids in PA-13 were substituted with their D-enantiomers to generate a proteolytically stable analog, dPA-13. In this study, we evaluated the antimicrobial efficacy of dPA-13 under proteolytic stress, focusing on cytotoxicity, hemolytic activity, structural characteristics, and resistance induction following prolonged bacterial exposure.

AMPs are known to exert diverse effects on bacterial physiology, not only through membrane disruption but also by modulating intracellular processes such as protein synthesis, oxidative stress response, and metabolic pathways11. While these mechanisms have been extensively studied using natural L-amino acid AMPs, the cellular impacts of D-amino acid-containing AMPs remain poorly characterized. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the proteomic response of P. aeruginosa to a D-amino acid-substituted AMP, providing novel insights into its mechanisms of action. Given the stereochemical differences and enhanced stability conferred by D-substitution, it is essential to elucidate how such modifications influence bacterial responses at the proteome level. Therefore, to gain additional insight into the mechanisms of action of dPA-13, we included a mass spectrometry-based proteomic approach to analyze global changes in protein expression in P. aeruginosa following peptide treatment11,12. This comprehensive analysis aimed to bridge the current knowledge gap between the known molecular effects of D-amino acid AMPs and their use in the rational development of stable and effective antimicrobial agents.

Methods

Peptide synthesis and preparation

PA-13, dPA-13, TAMRA-labeled PA-13, and Cy5-labeled dPA-13 peptides were synthesized by a solid-phase method using 9-fluorenyl methoxycarbonyl chemistry (Fmoc) from China Peptides (China) and purified by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) on trifluoroacetate salts (TFA) with a purity of more than 95%. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) was used to analyze the mass of the peptides. The peptide was dissolved in deionized water (10 mg/mL) and stored at -20 °C until use. The D-form peptide was obtained by substituting all L-amino acids with D-amino acids.

Determination of in vitro antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of the peptide against P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was investigated. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using a modification of the National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards (NCCLS) broth microdilution method as previously described13. Peptides were serially diluted to final concentrations of 0.98–250 µg/mL in 0.01% (v/v) acetic acid and 0.2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA). Fifty µL of the bacterial suspensions in Mueller-Hinton Broth (MHB) with an approximate concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL (OD620 = 0.05) were mixed with 50 µL of peptide solution in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with continuous shaking. The lowest peptide concentration that inhibited discernible growth was defined as the MIC. Negative and positive controls were a bacterial suspension in 0.01% (v/v) acetic acid with 0.2% (w/v) BSA, and a bacterial suspension with melittin, respectively. To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), 50 µL from each well that showed no visible bacterial growth was spread on a tryptic soy agar (TSA) plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration with no colony growth observed after 24 h. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

Determination of peptide stability in protease enzymes

To investigate the tolerance of peptides in a proteolytic environment, peptide solutions were prepared by serial dilution to final concentrations of 0.98–250 µg/mL, and pre-incubated with 1 mg/mL of protease enzymes, including trypsin (AppliChem, Germany), proteinase K (Sigma, Germany), and pepsin (Sigma, Germany) at 37 °C for 1 h 14. Subsequently, as previously described, the MIC and MBC of the pre-incubated peptides against P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were determined. All the assays were run in triplicate.

Determination of hemolytic activity

The capability of peptides to cause hemolysis of human red blood cells (hRBCs) was examined using a hemolysis assay10. hRBCs were collected in a heparinized blood collection tube from a healthy volunteer and centrifuged at 1,000 x g for 10 min to remove serum. The hRBC pellet was washed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 min or until the supernatant appeared clear. The hRBCs were resuspended in PBS to 2% (v/v) and incubated with serially diluted peptides (0.98–250 µg/mL), also in PBS. hRBCs suspended in PBS served as the negative control (0% hemolysis), while those treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 were used as the positive control (100% hemolysis). After incubating at 37 °C for 1 h, the supernatant was assayed for released hemoglobin at 405 nm using a Multiskan Sky high microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, UK). All assays were run in triplicate. The hemolysis percentage was calculated using the following equation:

Determination of cytotoxicity

L929 mouse fibroblast cells (NCTC clone 929) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.2% (w/v) sodium bicarbonate, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 units/mL penicillin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C, under 5% CO2 and high humidity. Cell viability was assessed using a modified 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay10. Approximately 3 × 104 cells/well of L929 cells were seeded onto 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The serially diluted peptides (0.98–250 µg/mL) were added and incubated for 24 h. Cells treated with melittin served as the positive control, while untreated cells served as the negative control. After 24 h of incubation, the media was discarded from the culture before washing with PBS. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL; Sigma, Germany) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 3 h at 37 °C. After removing the supernatants, 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well to solubilize formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader. Determination of time-kill kinetics

The time-kill kinetics of the dPA-13 peptide were evaluated against P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 10. The bacterial suspensions in MHB with an approximate concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL were incubated with peptides at MIC and MBC concentrations at 37 °C with constant shaking at 200 rpm. Samples were taken out at different times (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 24 h) and serially diluted tenfold using 0.85% NaCl. Each dilution was plated on TSA plates. After overnight incubation, bacterial colonies were counted and standardized as CFU/mL. Each run included a bacterial suspension that was treated with 0.01% (v/v) acetic acid and 0.2% (w/v) BSA as a negative control for bacterial growth. All the assays were run in triplicate.

Characterization of secondary structure

The circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopic analysis was performed to determine the secondary structure of PA-13, dPA-13, TAMRA-labeled PA-13, and Cy5-labeled dPA-13 peptides in four different environments on the Jasco-815 Spectropolarimeter (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan) as described in a previous study15. Peptides were adjusted to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL in each of four different environments: deionized water (DW) to mimic an aqueous environment; 30 mM sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; Sigma, Germany) micelles to simulate negatively charged bacterial membranes; 50% (v/v) trifluoroethanol (TFE; Sigma, Germany) to simulate a membrane-mimicking hydrophobic environment and 0.1% (w/v) lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from P. aeruginosa serotype 10 (Sigma, Germany). CD spectra were recorded within the 190–260 nm range at a scanning speed of 10 nm/min. Each condition was measured in triplicate. Flow cytometric determination

Flow cytometric analysis was performed to evaluate the membrane permeabilization and depolarization caused by the peptides to bacterial cell membranes16. The bacterial suspensions in MHB at approximate concentrations of 107 CFU/mL were treated with peptides (1× MIC) and incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking for 0.5, 1, and 2 h. The cells were incubated with propidium iodide (PI; Sigma, Germany) and bis-(1,3-dibutylbarbituric acid) trimethine oxonol (BOX; Sigma, Germany). Untreated cells (without peptide) served as a negative control, and colistin-treated cells served as a positive control. The fluorescence signals were recorded by counting 25,000 cells using flow cytometry (Cytoflex, Beckman Coulter). . Each experiment was run in triplicate.

Scanning electron microscopy determination (SEM)

Changes of bacterial cell surfaces were examined by SEM as previously described17 with modifications. The bacterial suspensions in MHB with approximate concentrations of 1 × 107 CFU/mL were incubated with the peptide at a concentration equivalent to 1× MIC at 37 °C for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min and then rinsed three times with PBS. Bacterial pellets were collected by filtration through 0.22 μm mixed cellulose ester membrane filters and fixed overnight at 4 °C with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in PBS. Post-fixation was done with 1% OsO4, followed by rinsing with PBS. Samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol (20%-100%) for 15 min in each grade, then in absolute ethanol twice for 20 min. After lyophilization, the samples were platinum-coated and observed under SEM (Hitachi SU8020; Hitachi, Japan). Untreated cells served as negative controls.

Transmission electron microscopy determination (TEM)

Changes in membrane integrity and intracellular content of bacteria were examined using TEM as previously described18 with modifications. Samples were prepared under the same conditions as described above for the SEM experiment. After being fixed overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, the bacterial pellets were rinsed twice with PBS and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The samples were washed twice with PBS and then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series of 50%, 70%, 90% and 100% for 15 min each. The samples were dehydrated in absolute acetone for 2 cycles of 10 min each, followed by infiltration with the mixtures of acetone and epoxy resin for 1 h at ratios of 1:1 and 1:3, respectively. Samples were immersed in pure epoxy resin three times, each for 3 h, before embedding in a flat mold. Polymerization was carried out at 70 °C for 8 h. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica EMUC7; Leica), and samples were post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and analyzed using TEM (Hitachi HT7700; Hitachi, Japan).

Confocal laser-scanning microscopy determination (CLSM)

CLSM was performed to visualize peptide localization within bacterial cells10. PA-13 was conjugated to tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) (λex/em: 550/575 nm), and dPA-13 was conjugated to Cyanine-5 (Cy5) (λex/em: 651/670 nm). These fluorophores were selected for their distinct spectral profiles to facilitate simultaneous multi-channel imaging without spectral bleed-through (crossover). In brief, bacterial suspensions in MHB (adjusted to an OD620 = 0.05) were co-treated with TAMRA-labeled PA-13 and Cy5-labeled dPA-13 at final concentrations of 1× MIC for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, the cells were washed with PBS to remove unbound peptides and collected by centrifugation (10,000 × g for 10 min). Cell membranes were counterstained with CellBrite Fix 488, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and rinsed twice with PBS. The samples were immobilized in 2% low-melting agarose (Sigma, Germany) on glass slides, sealed with nail polish, and observed using CLSM in Airyscan super-resolution (SR) mode and further processed using Airyscan Joint Deconvolution (LSM 900 with Airyscan 2; ZEISS, Germany). The Cy5 channel was pseudo-colored violet in the final figures to provide optimal visual contrast against the red TAMRA signal.

Determination of peptide effects on the global proteome of P. aeruginosa

The protein profiles (or proteomes) of bacteria following peptide exposures were determined using shotgun quantitative proteomics. Bacterial suspensions in MHB with approximate concentrations of 1 × 107 CFU/mL (OD620 = 0.05) were treated with dPA-13 or PA-13 peptide at a concentration of 1× MIC for 30 min. Untreated cells were included as a negative control. The treated cells were pelleted via centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min and rinsed twice with distilled water.

Bacterial cells were lysed with lysis buffer (0.5% SDS, 50 mM TEAB, pH 8.5), and the lysate was obtained by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min. Proteins were precipitated with cold acetone overnight at -20 °C. The pellet was air-dried and solubilized in 50 mM TEAB containing 0.15% sodium deoxycholate (DOC). Protein concentrations were calculated using the Lowry assay19. Protein samples were resuspended in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (AMBIC), and protein disulfide bonds were reduced by 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 1 h at room temperature. Sulfhydryl groups were alkylated using 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min in the dark, followed by trypsin digestion (1:20 ratio) for 16 h at 37 °C. Tryptic peptides were dried in a speed vacuum concentrator and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Peptides from each sample were separated using an Ultimate3000 Nano/Capillary LC System (Thermo Scientific, UK) with a C18 column (75 μm x 15 cm, Acclaim PepMap RSLC, 2 μm) and then injected into a ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA). Three independent biological replicates were prepared for each group. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode; the top 50 most abundant precursor ions per MS1 survey scan (intensity threshold > 150 cps) were selected for fragmentation (MS/MS).

Raw tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data were processed and quantified with MaxQuant software20, and MS/MS spectra were then matched to a P. aeruginosa protein database from the UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb?query=P.+aeruginosa) using the Andromeda search engine (accessed on 24 February 2025). Label-free quantitation was performed using MaxQuant (version 2.2.0.0) following standard settings. Enzyme specificity was set to Trypsin/P with a maximum of two missed cleavages. Fixed modification included carbamidomethylation of cysteine, while variable modifications included methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation. The main search peptide tolerance was 0.6 Da for MS/MS fragments. Protein identification required a minimum of 7 amino acids and at least two peptides, including one unique peptide. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 1% for both proteins and peptides. For statistical analysis, mass intensities were log2-transformed. Principal component analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), and volcano plot analysis were generated using MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined using a calculated fold-change (FC) threshold of > 2 or < 0.5 and an adjusted p-value < 0.0521. Functional categorization of DEPs was conducted via GO enrichment analysis using ShinyGO, version 0.8222. Additionally, Venn diagrams were generated by SRplot to display the overlap of DEPs across different sample groups23.

Molecular modelling

The three-dimensional structures of selected proteins [twitching motility protein PilT (UniProt ID: A0A6H1QAN3), mercuric reductase (MerA; UniProt ID: A0A6H0JM56), AAA+ ATPase domain-containing protein (UniProt ID: Q9I5L2), probable acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (UniProt ID: Q9I4V4), succinate dehydrogenase subunit B (SdhB; UniProt ID: Q9I3D4), fatty acid desaturase (UniProt ID: A6UZR7), and methionine import ATP-binding protein MetN (UniProt ID: A0A5E5QU67)] were constructed from their amino acid sequences using AlphaFold324. The generated structures were subsequently refined through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In brief, the protonation states of ionizable residues were assigned at pH 7.4 using the PDB2PQR web server (available at https://server.poissonboltzmann.org/)25. The protein was treated with the AMBER ff19SB force field26, and the system was solvated with the TIP3P water model. MD simulations were conducted under periodic boundary conditions in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble. The production phase of each simulation was run for 100 ns using the AMBER 24 software package.

The PA-13 peptide (NH₂–KIAKRIWKIRRR–NH₂) was initially built with AlphaFold 3 and subsequently converted to its D-amino acid form using Discovery Studio Visualizer 2021. All protein and peptide structures were converted into docking-ready formats, and protein-peptide docking was performed using HPEPDOCK 2.027 on a Linux operating system. The docking pose with the lowest binding energy score was selected for further structural and energetic analyses.

Resistance development assay

To assess the tendency of bacterial resistance after long periods of exposure to peptides and antibiotics, MICs following serial passages were determined, as previously described28. Briefly, 50 µL of two-fold serially diluted dPA-13, colistin, or ciprofloxacin was mixed with 50 µL of bacterial suspension in MHB, adjusted to approximately 1 × 107 CFU/mL (OD₆₂₀ = 0.05), in sterile 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, 50 µL from sub-MIC wells (showing visible growth) were transferred into fresh TSB and incubated again at 37 °C. After 16–18 h, the MICs were re-evaluated using the broth microdilution method, with cultures adjusted to approximately 1 × 107 CFU/mL (OD₆₂₀ = 0.05). An increase in MIC over seven serial passages was considered a measure of resistance development. Each assay included a negative control consisting of the bacterial suspension in MHB without peptide. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was defined using a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). GraphPad Prism software (version 7.0, GraphPad Software, USA) was used to perform one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to evaluate differences among groups, including comparisons with the controls.

Results

dPA-13 maintains potent antibacterial efficacy under proteolytic stress.

A major limitation of AMPs is their susceptibility to degradation by protease enzymes, significantly hindering their clinical application. To assess resistance to proteolytic degradation, we evaluated the antibacterial activity of PA-13 and its D-enantiomer (dPA-13) following exposure to representative digestive and broad-spectrum proteases, including trypsin, proteinase K, and pepsin. These enzymes were selected to simulate proteolytic conditions encountered in host tissues and mucosal environments. MIC and MBC assays were performed against P. aeruginosa after pre-treatment with each enzyme (Table 1). As expected for a peptide composed of L-amino acids, PA-13 completely lost antibacterial activity following enzymatic digestion, demonstrating its susceptibility to proteolytic cleavage. In contrast, dPA-13 retained substantial antibacterial efficacy under all tested conditions. The MIC values of dPA-13 ranged from 3.91 to 31.25 µg/mL across the three enzymatic treatments, indicating preserved inhibitory activity despite protease exposure.

dPA-13 demonstrates low host toxicity and a favorable safety profile.

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the peptides, the hemolytic activity of dPA-13 and its L-amino acid counterpart (PA-13) was assessed using human red blood cells over a concentration range of 0.98 to 250 µg/mL. Melittin, a well-characterized hemolytic peptide, was included as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 1A, dPA-13 demonstrated negligible hemolytic activity across all tested concentrations. Notably, dPA-13 exhibited significantly lower hemolysis compared to PA-13 at high concentrations (125 and 250 µg/mL), suggesting that D-amino acid substitution effectively reduced membrane-disruptive toxicity.

The cytotoxic effects of dPA-13 and PA-13 on mammalian cells were assessed using L929 mouse fibroblast cells in a cell viability assay. Melittin, a known cytotoxic AMP, was included as a positive control and exhibited a pronounced dose-dependent reduction in cell viability, confirming the sensitivity of the assay. As shown in Fig. 1B, dPA-13 demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity across all tested concentrations (0.98 to 250 µg/mL), with cell viabilities consistently exceeding 80%. The lower cytotoxic profile of dPA-13 suggests that the substitution of L-amino acids with D-amino acids enhanced its biocompatibility.

dPA-13 adopted a mirror-image α-helical structure in membrane-mimicking environments.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was employed to analyze the secondary structure of the dPA-13 under both aqueous and membrane-mimetic environments. Spectra were recorded in deionized water (DW) and in the presence of a membrane-mimetic environment, including 50% TFE, 30 mM SDS, and 0.1% LPS (Fig. 2). In aqueous solution, dPA-13 displayed a predominantly disordered structure, characterized by a single positive peak near 200 nm, indicative of random coil conformation. However, upon exposure to membrane-mimetic conditions, the peptide adopted an α-helical structure, evidenced by two positive peaks near 208 and 222 nm with one positive peak around 190 nm, which is a mirror image of the L-form peptide. This result was consistent with its composition of D-amino acids, which inverts the peptide’s chiral optical activity. These findings suggest that dPA-13, like its L-amino acid counterpart, undergoes a structural transition to an α-helical conformation in membrane-like environments.

dPA-13 exhibits rapid bactericidal activity and physical membrane disruption via a mechanism distinct from PA-13.

The bactericidal activity of dPA-13 over time was evaluated against P. aeruginosa using time-kill kinetics, as shown in Fig. 3A. The negative control group exhibited continuous bacterial growth throughout the 24 h, indicating an absence of inhibitory effects. In contrast, treatment with dPA-13 at both the MIC and MBC resulted in a rapid and substantial reduction in bacterial viability. Within the first 30 min of exposure, viable bacterial counts dropped sharply to approximately 104 CFU/mL at both the MIC and MBC. Complete eradication of P. aeruginosa was achieved by 3 h at the MBC, while the MIC achieved full bacterial clearance after 24 h of incubation. These results confirmed that the bactericidal effect of dPA-13 was potent and rapid.

To investigate whether dPA-13 exerted its antimicrobial effect through a membrane-disruptive mechanism, we assessed its ability to induce membrane permeabilization and depolarization in P. aeruginosa using PI and BOX fluorescence assays. PI is a membrane-impermeable dye that intercalates with nucleic acids only when membrane integrity is compromised, while BOX detects membrane potential collapse by accumulating in depolarized cells16. As shown in Fig. 3B, treatment with dPA-13 resulted in moderate membrane permeabilization, with PI uptake levels of 35 ± 3%, 29 ± 3%, and 29 ± 2% at 0.5, 1, and 2 h, respectively. In contrast, PA-13 caused extensive permeabilization, with values reaching ~ 95% at all time points. Likewise, BOX fluorescence measurements (Fig. 3C) revealed that dPA-13 induced partial membrane depolarization, with fluorescence intensities of 38 ± 1%, 34 ± 5%, and 26 ± 1% at the corresponding time intervals. PA-13, however, led to near-complete depolarization (> 90%) over the same duration. Thus, dPA-13 had significantly lower membrane-disruptive capacity than PA-13. While both peptides were effective against P. aeruginosa, the reduced membrane permeabilization and depolarization associated with dPA-13 suggest it may act through alternative or supplementary intracellular targets, rather than relying solely on membrane lysis.

SEM and TEM were performed to further elucidate the impact of dPA-13 on the structural integrity of P. aeruginosa. Bacterial cells were treated with dPA-13 at the MIC for 0.5 h, and the changes were compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3). Compared to untreated bacteria, which exhibited smooth surfaces and typical rod-shaped morphology (Fig. 3D-E), treated cells had irregular morphologies characterized by surface roughness, shrinkage, and membrane deformation (Fig. 3F-G). Untreated cells maintained intact continuous membranes with well-defined cell envelopes and homogenous cytoplasmic contents, indicative of healthy physiological conditions (Fig. 3H-I). In contrast, dPA-13-treated cells exhibited substantial ultrastructural damage. Notable features were observed, including severe membrane disintegration, detachment of inner membranes, and the formation of electron-dense intracellular aggregates (Fig. 3J-K). These changes reflect profound disruption of membrane integrity and intracellular organization, likely contributing to bacterial cell death. Collectively, the SEM and TEM observations support that dPA-13 exerts its antibacterial activity through physical disruption of bacterial membranes and intracellular structures.

Subcellular localization of dPA-13 and PA-13 in P. aeruginosa

To investigate intracellular localization, P. aeruginosa cells were co-treated with TAMRA-labeled PA-13 and Cy5-labeled dPA-13. Utilizing distinct fluorophores allowed for the simultaneous visualization of both peptides within the same population (Fig. 4). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used to visualize peptide distribution to the bacterial membrane, stained with Cellbrite Fix 488 (Fig. 4C). Both peptides exhibited strong colocalization with the membrane stain, confirming their affinity for the bacterial surface. However, their internal distribution patterns were distinct: PA-13 (red) was predominantly sequestered at the bacterial membrane with minimal cytoplasmic penetration (Fig. 4A). In contrast, dPA-13 (violet) exhibited a broader distribution, with fluorescence observed throughout the intracellular space (Fig. 4B). The merged image (Fig. 4D) clearly illustrates that while PA-13 remains surface-associated, dPA-13 successfully translocates across the membrane to interact with intracellular targets.

Proteomic profiling of P. aeruginosa treated with PA-13 or dPA-13

To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms responsible for the antibacterial properties of PA-13 and dPA-13, the label-free quantitative proteomics of P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were determined following treatment with each peptide using MaxQuant. The UniProt database for P. aeruginosa contains 175,227 protein entries, including 3,492 reviewed (Swiss-Prot) and 171,735 unreviewed (TrEMBL) sequences. Proteomic analysis of P. aeruginosa samples treated with PA-13, dPA-13, or untreated (control) resulted in the identification of 117,998 independent mass spectral counts. These included 40,390 spectral counts from control samples, 39,226 from PA-13-treated samples, and 38,382 from dPA-13-treated samples. Based on spectral assignment to protein sequences using the spectral library and established proteomic resources29, a higher number of annotated protein sequences was identified in the control samples (31,405 sequences) compared to PA-13-treated (30,498 sequences) and dPA-13-treated samples (29,892 sequences). After filtering for unique protein sequences, 10,070 annotated proteins were selected from a total of 66,978 identified proteins for subsequent analysis (Table S1).

Differential protein expression analysis was performed by comparing PA-13- and dPA-13-treated samples with the untreated control using a fold-change threshold of > 2 or < 0.5 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Based on these criteria, 97 proteins were significantly regulated in the PA-13 treatment (58 upregulated and 39 downregulated), whereas 93 proteins were differentially expressed in the dPA-13 treatment (51 upregulated and 42 downregulated) compared with the control.

To further evaluate the global variance structure of the dataset, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed (Fig. 5A). The PCA score plot showed clustering trends among the control, PA-13, and dPA-13 groups, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 15.2% and 14.1% of the total variance, respectively. Because PCA reflects overall variance in the dataset rather than treatment-specific variation, partial overlap among the groups was observed. Therefore, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was subsequently applied to enhance discrimination among the experimental conditions. The first two components explained 13.7% (Component 1) and 12.5% (Component 2) of the variance, respectively, indicating treatment-dependent differences in the proteomic profiles (Fig. 5B). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by a volcano plot using a fold change ≥ 2 and a p-value < 0.05 (peptide-treated vs. control) as shown in Fig. 5E and F. PA-13 treatment induced the modulation of 97 proteins (58 upregulated, 39 downregulated), whereas dPA-13 treatment impacted 93 proteins (51 upregulated, 42 downregulated) (Fig. 5C; detailed in Tables S2 and S3). A comparative Venn analysis identified a core group of 23 shared DEPs (Fig. 5D), suggesting that a subset of the antimicrobial response is conserved across both enantiomers, likely representing a common baseline of membrane-induced stress.

Functional enrichment analysis showed distinct and overlapping mechanisms.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that PA-13 treatment forces P. aeruginosa into a defensive physiological state characterized by metabolic adaptation and structural repair. Upregulated proteins were significantly enriched in pathways essential for maintaining cellular integrity under threat, including GDP-mannose metabolism, nucleotide-sugar biosynthesis, and methylglyoxal detoxification (Fig. 6A). The induction of protein-DNA cross-link repair mechanisms and RNA helicases suggests that PA-13 treatment triggers a secondary stress response affecting transcriptional and translational machinery. This indirect effect likely stems from the perturbation of the proton motive force or the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) following membrane binding, which necessitates the activation of genomic maintenance pathways to preserve cellular viability despite the peptide remaining primarily localized at the cell envelope.

In contrast, the proteins downregulated by PA-13 highlight a systematic collapse of the bacteria’s bioenergetic capacity. Specifically, we observed a significant suppression of core metabolic enzymes, including glucokinase, nitronate monooxygenase, enoyl-CoA hydratase, and succinate dehydrogenase (Fig. 6B). The collective downregulation of these oxidoreductase-related functions signifies a profound impairment of cellular respiration and carbon flux that likely drives the bactericidal activity of PA-13.

The proteomic signature of dPA-13-treated cells exhibited hallmarks of more intensive intracellular engagement. Upregulated DEPs in this group were predominantly enriched in aldo-keto reductase activity, NADP+-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase activity, and metal ion binding (specifically cobalt and molybdenum) (Fig. 6C). The enrichment of 5′-3′ exonuclease activity further suggests that dPA-13 induces substantial DNA damage, potentially through the accumulation of intracellular ROS) or direct interaction with the genome. Furthermore, the modulation of molybdopterin-binding oxidoreductases and other cofactor-dependent enzymes points to a widespread disruption of the bacterial redox homeostasis that exceeds the response seen with the L-enantiomer.

Functional enrichment analysis of downregulated proteins after dPA-13 treatment (Fig. 6D) revealed a pronounced suppression of pathways related to core metabolism and redox processes. Notably enriched terms included oxidoreductase activity, particularly those acting on CH-CH groups, succinate dehydrogenase activity, and components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, indicating impaired cellular respiration. Proteins containing 4Fe-4 S clusters, alpha-helical ferredoxins, and iron-sulfur binding domains were also significantly downregulated, pointing to disruptions in electron transport and redox homeostasis. Reduced expression of ATP-dependent proteins, including AAA-domains containing protein and the type IV pilus retraction ATPase PilT, suggesting impairment of ATP-driven cellular processes. These findings collectively indicate that dPA-13 exerts its antimicrobial action by targeting essential metabolic and redox-related pathways in P. aeruginosa, further supporting the conclusion that it disrupts bacterial metabolism by impairing respiratory and redox enzymes.

Importantly, the 23 DEPs were found to be commonly affected by both AMPs, which were enriching oxidoreductase-related functions, particularly those acting on CH-CH groups with quinone acceptors (Fig. S1). These shared DEPs highlight a core mechanism likely centered on interference with the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation. However, the divergence between the two peptides is stark: dPA-13 uniquely targets pathways associated with DNA repair and cytoplasmic enzyme modulation, reflecting a high degree of intracellular activity. When correlated with CLSM results, which demonstrated superior cytoplasmic accumulation of dPA-13, these proteomic data confirm a dual-action model. While PA-13 primarily compromises the membrane and slows metabolism, dPA-13 bypasses initial defenses to execute a comprehensive multi-target attack on the bacterial respiratory chain, genomic integrity, and redox machinery.

As summarized in Table 2, docking energy scores exhibited a pattern consistent with the proteomic data, where proteins showing altered expression levels also displayed stronger predicted binding affinities (lower docking energy score) to the AMP. This indicated that the designed peptide may interact with these proteins and potentially modulate their expression. Among the identified targets, the twitching motility protein PilT stands out due to its well-established role in bacterial motility and virulence and its pronounced downregulation.

The dPA-13 peptide exhibited less propensity for inducing resistance in P. aeruginosa.

P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was cultured for seven days in the presence of dPA-13, ciprofloxacin, or colistin. Bacterial growth was monitored daily to determine the fold-change in MIC over time. As shown in Fig. 8, ciprofloxacin exhibited a rapid and pronounced increase in MICs, rising from 0.25 to 62.5 µg/mL over seven serial passages, representing a 250-fold increase. This sharp elevation indicated the rapid emergence of resistance under continuous exposure to ciprofloxacin. In contrast, dPA-13 demonstrated a slower and less dramatic increase in MIC, rising from 3.91 to 62.5 µg/mL over the same number of passages, a 16-fold rise. Furthermore, colistin showed no increase in MIC throughout the seven passages, maintaining its initial potency. Despite dPA-13 eventually reaching the same final MIC value as ciprofloxacin, the trajectory of resistance development was markedly different between the two compounds. Ciprofloxacin induced high-level resistance quickly and at a much lower initial concentration than dPA-13. This suggested that dPA-13 may offer a therapeutic advantage over ciprofloxacin in reducing the risk of resistance during prolonged treatment.

Discussion

The increasing resistance of P. aeruginosa to conventional antibiotics has intensified the need for effective alternative therapies2. AMPs have gained attention as promising candidates due to their broad-spectrum activity and multifaceted mechanisms of action, which reduce the likelihood of resistance development30. However, a significant limitation hindering the clinical application of AMPs is their poor stability. These peptides are highly vulnerable to proteolytic degradation by both host and bacterial enzymes, leading to diminished bioavailability and therapeutic effectiveness31. To address this issue, structural modifications have been explored to enhance AMP stability32. One particularly effective approach involves the substitution of L-amino acids with D-amino acids. This modification confers protease resistance by altering the peptide’s stereochemistry, making it less recognizable to proteolytic enzymes. Consequently, D-amino acid incorporation enhances the peptide’s stability in physiological environments, thereby improving their potential for clinical application8.

In this study, dPA-13 was designed by substituting all L-amino acids in PA-13 with their D-enantiomeric counterparts. In the absence of proteolytic enzymes, dPA-13 exhibited MIC and MBC values comparable to those of the parent peptide PA-13 against P. aeruginosa. Proteolytic stability assays revealed that PA-13 was highly susceptible to degradation by trypsin, proteinase K, and pepsin, resulting in complete loss of activity across all tested concentrations. In contrast, dPA-13 retained its antimicrobial function, exhibiting resistance to all enzymes tested. The full D-amino acid substitution can preserve antimicrobial activity, as observed with AMPs such as D-AP19, D1018, and D-anoplin8,9,33. This enhanced stability is attributed to the distinct stereochemistry of D-amino acids, which are poorly recognized by most proteases that are evolutionarily adapted to cleave peptides composed of L-amino acids34,35. This underscores the effectiveness of D-amino acid substitution in enhancing proteolytic resistance without compromising bioactivity8,9,33. To further assess the safety profile of the dPA-13, hemolytic activity and cytotoxicity were evaluated against hRBCs and L929 mouse fibroblast cells. dPA-13 exhibited significantly reduced hemolytic activity compared to PA-13, and both peptides maintained low cytotoxicity, preserving over 80% cell viability in L929 cells. These findings align with previous findings of D-form peptides, such as DMPC-10B and D-MPI, which have reduced cytotoxic and hemolytic effects relative to their L-form counterparts36,37.

To elucidate the membrane-related effects of dPA-13 on P. aeruginosa, a combination of circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, flow cytometry, and electron microscopy (SEM and TEM) was employed. CD spectroscopy analysis revealed that dPA-13 adopted a random coil conformation in aqueous solution (DW), but transitions to an α-helical structure in membrane-mimicking environments, including 30 mM SDS, 50% TFE, and 0.1% LPS environments. This conformational plasticity suggests that dPA-13 underwent structural reorganization upon interaction with lipid membranes, which may enhance its membrane-disruptive potential38. Importantly, the peptide adopted a left-handed α-helical conformation, representing the enantiomer mirror image of the right-handed α-helix adopted by PA-1339.

By flow cytometric analysis, dPA-13 induced moderate, time-dependent membrane permeabilization and depolarization. These signals, at approximately 30% for PI uptake, were significantly lower than those observed with PA-13, suggesting that dPA-13 does not rely solely on massive, immediate membrane lysis. Instead, it likely induces transient membrane perturbations sufficient for cytoplasmic translocation but below the threshold of total lytic collapse11,40,41. This profile is consistent with dual-action peptides such as indolicidin, which transiently permeabilize bacterial membranes to gain intracellular access, where they inhibit DNA synthesis, rather than causing extensive membrane disruption or lysis42. Furthermore, our findings are consistent with intracellular-targeting AMPs such as buforin II and translation-inhibiting peptides, which demonstrate that efficient bacterial killing can occur despite minimal membrane permeabilization, reflecting a mechanism that relies on intracellular interference rather than membrane lysis43,44.

The CLSM findings supported this distinction: PA-13 was primarily localized to the membrane, whereas dPA-13 exhibited both membrane-bound and intracellular fluorescence. To confirm that this translocation leads to direct interaction with intracellular components, we performed a DNA retardation assay. The results (Fig. S2) demonstrated that dPA-13 binds directly to P. aeruginosa genomic DNA in a dose-dependent manner, effectively inhibiting its migration. This direct interaction with nucleic acids provides a mechanistic basis for the intracellular activity of the peptide. This translocation and subsequent DNA binding are further corroborated by TEM ultrastructural insights, which revealed electron-dense intracellular aggregates. Unlike purely lytic peptides that typically result in ghost cells with emptied cytoplasmic contents, these dense granules represent the precipitation of internal machinery, signaling a dual mechanism of action45,46. While SEM revealed surface shrinkage and irregularities without extensive lysis, the combination of moderate membrane perturbation sufficient for entry but insufficient for total lysis supports a model in which dPA-13 translocates to target intracellular pathways while maintaining the structural integrity of the cell envelope during the initial stages of killing.

To elucidate the molecular bases underlying the antibacterial activity of PA-13 and its D-enantiomer dPA-13, label-free quantitative proteomic profiling was performed on treated P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853. While initial exploratory PCA showed overlapping trends, the subsequent application of PLS-DA successfully resolved the treatment groups into distinct clusters. This revealed distinct clustering for each treatment group, signaling that despite their identical primary sequences, the L- and D-enantiomers elicit unique biological fingerprints. These findings reflect peptide-specific perturbations of bacterial protein expression profiles, consistent with a prior study that utilized proteomics to explore AMP action mechanisms47,48. Differential expression analysis revealed that PA-13 modulated 97 proteins (58 upregulated, 39 downregulated), while dPA-13 modulated 93 proteins (51 upregulated, 42 downregulated). The 23 shared differentially expressed proteins suggest a degree of mechanistic overlap, a phenomenon commonly observed among peptides with similar membrane affinities but differing structural properties47,48.

The proteomic response to PA-13 is indicative of a cellular response aimed at maintaining homeostasis under membrane-localized stress. The upregulation of pathways related to GDP-mannose metabolism and nucleotide-sugar biosynthesis suggests a compensatory attempt to stabilize the cell envelope. The significant induction of key enzymes, including a DUF86 domain-containing protein and glutathione S-transferase, suggests the activation of an antioxidant defense system, likely in response to peptide-induced oxidative stress49,50. Furthermore, the observed shift in nucleic acid metabolism, evidenced by the upregulation of a transcriptional regulator, Ren protein, and various Nucleotidyltransferases, is interpreted as a systemic effort to maintain genomic stability and stress signaling under environmental challenge51. This structural stress is further underscored by the induction of alkaline phosphatase for potential phosphate scavenging and membrane turnover46, alongside peptidase S8/S53, which may function to manage the degradation of misfolded or damaged proteins52. The emergence of Ig-like domain-containing proteins and uncharacterized DUF-domain proteins suggests a potential remodeling of the cell surface architecture to mitigate peptide penetration53,54.

Conversely, the downregulation of respiratory enzymes, such as succinate dehydrogenase, glucokinase, and enoyl-CoA hydratase, correlates with a decline in metabolic activity, which is a calculated proteomic signature typical of bactericidal agents that compromise energy homeostasis40,55. The observed suppression of general permeases and ABC transporters suggests a potential impairment of nutrient acquisition and efflux-mediated resistance pathways56,57. Notably, the reduced abundance of motility factors (PilT, flagellin N-methylase) and genetic mobility elements (DNA primase/TraC, IS21/IS66 transposases) indicates a broad downregulation of proteins associated with bacterial virulence and horizontal gene transfer capacity58,59. These findings suggest that the presence of PA-13 impacts the expression of motility systems and genetic adaptability, further contributing to the compromised physiological state of the pathogen.

While dPA-13 shares certain metabolic inhibitory traits with PA-13, its proteomic profile indicates a more profound intracellular engagement. The enrichment of aldo-keto reductase and alcohol dehydrogenase (NADP⁺) activities suggests a significant recalibration of catabolic pathways, likely to meet the energy demands of high-intensity stress adaptation60,61. The upregulation of catalase, pyochelin biosynthesis proteins, 5′-3′ exonuclease activity, cobalt ion binding, and the oxidoreductase molybdopterin-binding subunit (IorB-related) is indicative of an acute response to reactive oxygen species (ROS) and metal ion dyshomeostasis. These proteomic signatures are often characteristic of antimicrobial peptides that penetrate the cytoplasm to target internal machinery62–64.

The induction of the methionine ATP-binding protein and formate/nitrite transporters is consistent with a shift toward nutrient scavenging, a known survival mechanism in conditions of respiratory failure65,66 The activation of 5′-3′ exonuclease activity and DNA repair superfamilies suggests that PA-13 treatment triggers significant genotoxic stress; while this may result from direct interaction with DNA, it is likely a secondary response to oxidative damage or the loss of cytoplasmic homeostasis47,57,67,68. Structural remodeling was also evident through the upregulation of the Zorya protein ZorC and BNR repeat-containing proteins, reflecting a broad transcriptional reprogramming in response to the peptide69. The impact of dPA-13 treatment is most evident in the downregulation of the electron transport chain (ETC) components. The suppression of 4Fe-4 S cluster assembly factors, succinate dehydrogenase, and nitrate monooxygenase correlates with a terminal collapse of respiratory metabolism61,70. This bioenergetic failure, coupled with the reduced abundance of detoxification proteins like the rhodanese domain protein and mercuric reductase, is likely to increase cellular vulnerability to oxidative damage59,71–73. Finally, the observed repression of virulence-associated proteins (PilT and flagellin N-methylase) suggests a potential dual-action effect involving both bactericidal activity and the attenuation of virulence factors58,59.

The shared proteomic core centered on oxidoreductase activity and quinone acceptors suggests that both peptides impact with the bacterial ETC as part of their bactericidal effect71,72. However, dPA-13’s unique ability to trigger DNA repair mechanisms and metal-scavenging pathways identifies a proteomic signature consistent with a multi-target antimicrobial. While PA-13 is largely restricted to membrane-localized perturbations, dPA-13 exhibits a more robust “dual mechanism.” This interpretation is supported by our CLSM findings, which show efficient cytoplasmic translocation of dPA-13, and is further substantiated by its capacity for direct DNA binding. This physical interaction with the genome is highly concordant with the observed upregulation of DNA repair machinery, suggesting that dPA-13 targets nucleic acid stability upon translocation. Importantly, the enantiomeric stability of dPA-13 likely facilitates resistance to periplasmic proteases degradation, thereby promoting deeper intracellular penetration and a more comprehensive metabolic and structural collapse.

Docking-based screening is widely used for preliminary assessment of molecular interactions and binding affinity in various biomedical fields, including cancer, diabetes, inflammation, and viral research74–80. Here, we utilized this approach to predict the binding affinity between the dPA-13 peptide and the seven most affected bacterial targets derived from the observed proteomic shifts. Based on molecular docking and proteomic analysis, the results suggest that dPA-13 peptide exerts an antibacterial effect, potentially by targeting multiple proteins involved in energy metabolism as well as membrane, motility, cellular defense/transport, and virulence regulation. The highly favorable binding affinities observed for the twitching motility protein PilT suggest it as a primary and high-impact intracellular target. PilT is a cytoplasmic AAA+ ATPase that powers type IV pilus retraction and is essential for twitching motility81. Previous studies have shown that the mucosal glycoprotein DMBT1 suppresses twitching motility and virulence in P. aeruginosa by binding directly to extracellular pili82. The predicted strong interaction between dPA-13 and PilT suggests interference at the level of the intracellular retraction motor. Such targeting may disrupt ATP-dependent pilus dynamics, thereby attenuating bacterial motility and pathogenicity. Similarly, the targeting of acyl-CoA dehydrogenase was consistent with proteomic analyses of BCp12, a casein-derived antibacterial peptide, which is involved in fatty acid degradation83. The suppression of this pathway likely disrupts fatty acid metabolism and consequently impairs bacterial cell membrane synthesis and integrity. The observed downregulation of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) aligns with earlier transcriptomic reports on the peptide MOp2 from Moringa oleifera, which significantly reduced SDH activity in S. aureus, thereby disrupting the TCA cycle and energy metabolism84. The convergence between proteomic alterations and computational binding predictions supports the hypothesis that these proteins may be potential biomarkers affected by the peptide’s activity. However, docking simulations have inherent limitations due to the use of a rigid receptor model and the absence of explicit solvent. Therefore, further validation using approaches such as site-directed mutagenesis, protein-ligand binding assays, enzyme inhibition studies, binding kinetics measurements, and western blot analysis will be required to confirm the precise molecular targets and biological mechanisms.

Compared to PA-13, which primarily exerted membrane-associated effects, dPA-13 engaged a broad range of cellular stress pathways, reflecting its deeper intracellular penetration and proteolytic stability. This expanded scope of molecular disruption likely contributed to its potent antibacterial activity and reduced the likelihood of resistance development by overwhelming multiple bacterial defense systems simultaneously. This model is further supported by the resistance induction analysis. While ciprofloxacin induced rapid, high-level resistance (250-fold increase), dPA-13 demonstrated a markedly slower trajectory with a significantly lower 16-fold rise in MIC. In contrast, colistin showed no detectable change in MIC throughout the study, consistent with the complex and multifactorial nature of colistin resistance85,86. The comparatively stable resistance profile of dPA-13 indicates a greater resilience to resistance development than conventional antibiotics such as fluoroquinolones, and more closely resembles that of last-resort agents like colistin. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of dPA-13 as a robust antimicrobial candidate and support a mechanism of action involving multiple cellular targets.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that total D-amino acid substitution of PA-13 yields a proteolytically robust enantiomer, dPA-13, which maintains potent antibacterial efficacy against P. aeruginosa with a superior safety profile. Unlike its parent peptide, dPA-13 leverages cytoplasmic translocation to exert a dual-action mechanism. Integrated proteomic and molecular docking analyses provide evidence that dPA-13 interferes with intracellular homeostasis, potentially by inducing redox imbalance and DNA repair pathways while downregulating core metabolic and virulence proteins, notably PilT. The high binding affinity toward these targets, supported by specific electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding motifs, is in consistent with a multi-target strategy. This mechanism likely contributes to the observed delay in resistance development relative to ciprofloxacin, positioning dPA-13 as a candidate for further investigation in the treatment of persistent infections.
