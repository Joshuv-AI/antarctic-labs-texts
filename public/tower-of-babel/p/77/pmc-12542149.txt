Factors affecting patient outcomes in pulmonary artery thromboendarterectomy under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest and cardiopulmonary bypass support----a single center’s experience
Authors: Ming Guan, Xiaofang Yang, Lin Fu, Xueting Zhao, Feilong Hei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the factors affecting patient outcomes in pulmonary artery thromboendarterectomy (PTE) under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) support and to provide a reference for further improving the effect of PTE.MethodsEighty-five patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) who underwent PTE under DHCA and CPB support at Beijing Anzhen Hospital from January 2015 to October 2023 were enrolled, including 56 males (65.88%) and 29 females (31.42%), aged 23–75 years (mean 57.05 ± 15.03 years). The diagnostic criteria for the patients with CTEPH met with the diagnostic criteria described in the 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension. Based on whether major adverse events (MAE) occurred during the hospitalization after PTE (MAE was defined as all-cause death and major complications related to surgery), patients were divided into the MAE group (n = 36) and the non-MAE group (Control group, n = 49). The differences in the preoperative and intraoperative indicators were compared between the two groups. A logistic regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) analysis were performed to identify risk factors affecting the outcomes of PTE.ResultsPTE under DHCA and CPB support was performed in 85 cases. Duration of CPB was 214.84 ± 49.08 min. The duration of aortic cross-clamping (ACC) was 125.88 ± 24.22 min. The duration of DHCA was 24.36 ± 7.25 min, and the number of DHCA episodes was 3.14 ± 1.52. After the PTE 36 patients (42.35%) experienced one or more MAEs, including 7 all-cause deaths (8.24%), 14 cases (16.47%) with reperfusion pulmonary edema, 20 cases (23.53%) with residual pulmonary hypertension (defined as mPAP > 25 mmHg), 6 cases (7.06%) with pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome, 12 cases (14.12%) with pneumonia, 12 cases (14.12%) with delirium, 6 cases (7.06%) with pericardial tamponade, 12 cases (14.12%) with pleural effusion, and 6 cases (7.06%) with acute kidney injury. When compared with the factors affecting the patient outcomes between two groups, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in the preoperative factors including gender (female), body mass index, heart failure with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class Ⅲ-Ⅳ, tricuspid valve pressure gradient value, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) value, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), 6-minute walking distance, mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP), systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac index. Significant intraoperative differences (P < 0.05) were also found in the parameters including CPB time, ACC time, DHCA time, the longest single DHCA time, maximum temperature difference between nasopharynx and bladder, and peak value of lactic acid. However, logistic regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and area under the curve (AUC) analysis revealed that among these factors, only sPAP, PVR, PAWP, CPB time, DHCA time, and the longest single DHCA time were independent risk factors for MAE. ConclusionThe results of this study indicate that preoperative right heart catheterization parameters—including mPAP, PAWP, and PVR—and intraoperative parameters—such as prolonged CPB time, DHCA time, and the longest single DHCA time—are independent predictors of MAE after PTE. Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare disease with an incidence of 8.9 cases per million inhabitants [1]. It is characterized by dyspnea, fatigue, and decreased exercise tolerance. The pathogenesis of CTEPH primarily involves recurrent pulmonary thromboembolism and thrombosis, leading to endothelial proliferation and a progressive increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), ultimately resulting in pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure [1–3]. CTEPH is defined as precapillary pulmonary hypertension, characterized by a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg at rest, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and PVR >2 Wood units (WU) on right heart catheterization [4, 5]. Pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) is a potentially curative therapy and the preferred treatment for this disease [1–4]. However, like other cardiac surgeries, PTE carries a risk of death or severe complications in some patients [2–4, 6, 7]. Studies on factors affecting patient outcomes after PTE are limited. This study retrospectively analyzed 85 patients who underwent PTE with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) support, identified risk factors influencing postoperative outcomes, and provided insights for further improving PTE treatment efficacy.

Data and Methods

Patient population

From January 2015 to October 2023, 85 consecutive patients undergoing PTE with DHCA and CPB support at Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, were enrolled. The diagnostic criteria for patients with CTEPH met with the diagnostic criteria described in the 2022 ESC/ERS Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension [4]. The diagnosis of CTEPH met the following three criteria: (1)The effective anticoagulant therapy has been performed for at least 3 months to exclude subacute pulmonary embolism; (2) mPAP ≥ 20mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), PAWP ≤ 15 mmHg and PVR >2 WU on right heart catheterization examination; (3) Pulmonary ventilation-perfusion scan shows at least one pulmonary segment perfusion defect, or specific signs of CTEPH (annular stenosis, grid sign/gap sign, and arterial occlusion) are detected by multi helix computed tomographic pulmonary angiography, magnetic resonance imaging, or direct pulmonary angiography. In the enrolled patients, those aged >50 years old routinely underwent coronary angiography or coronary computed tomographic angiography, before PTE to rule out coronary heart disease with a diameter stenosis of ≥ 70% in major vessels (right coronary artery, left main trunk, left anterior descending branch, and left circumflex artery branch) or those with typical exertional angina symptoms. Exclusion criteria included the patient with PAWP >15 mmHg (isolated post-capillary pulmonary hypertension and combined post- and pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension), coronary heart disease with diameter stenosis of ≥ 70% in major vessels, cardiomyopathy with heart failure which was defined as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class Ⅲ-Ⅳ, malignant tumor, anemia (hemoglobin < 110 g/L), and liver and kidney dysfunction. Based on the occurrence of major adverse events (MAE) during hospitalization post-PTE, patients were divided into MAE group (n = 36) and control group (n = 49). The MAE was defined as all-cause mortality or surgery-related major complications. This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital (Approval No.: Anzhen 2024006x). Written informed consent for CPB was obtained from all patients preoperatively. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Anesthesia and surgical methods

Anesthesia induction was performed with intravenous injection of etomidate 0.2—0.3 mg/kg, fentanyl 10–20 µg/kg, and vecuronium bromide 0.2 mg/kg. After endotracheal intubation, a central venous catheter and pulmonary artery catheter(Swan-Ganz catheter)were placed. When central venous catheterization was established fentanyl 1–2 µg/kg/h, vecuronium bromide 0.08 mg/kg/h, and propofol 1–2 mg/kg/h were infused to maintain anesthesia. The surgery was performed with a median sternotomy, followed by the CPB established. The main pulmonary artery was longitudinally incised for thrombus removal. After the thrombus was completely removed, rewarming, weaning off cardiopulmonary bypass, hemostasis, and chest closure were performed routinely.

Deep hypothermia method and cardiopulmonary bypass management

All 85 PTE procedures were performed under DHCA with CPB support. The CPB priming solution (compound electrolyte 1000 ml and human serum albumin 40 g and heparin 50 U/Kg) was used. The operation was performed with a median sternotomy, following the establishment of CPB by inserting catheters into the superior and inferior vena cavae. CPB was started when the value of activated clotting time of whole blood was greater than 480 s. During the operation, myocardial protection was achieved with perfusion of histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate solution of a single dose. Rapid cooling was achieved by a membrane oxygenator and an ice cap on the head. The temperature difference between the water bath and the bladder was less than 10℃. Circulation was arrested when the nasopharyngeal temperature dropped to 20℃, and the bladder temperature dropped to 22℃. The duration of each circulation arrest was limited to 20 min. Regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) was monitored in real-time during CPB. In principle, if rSO2 decreased by more than 50% of the baseline level or rSO2 < 40% lasted for 5 min, circulation was restored for 10 min to allow the mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) to recover to the level of the pre-arrest of circulation. The number of times of circulation arrest and reperfusion depended on the progress of the operation and the value of rSO2. After the PTE was completed, circulation was restored. After the oxygen debt was paid off, rewarming was carried out until the nasopharyngeal temperature was 35.0℃, following to removal of the ice cap. During CPB, ultrafiltration and blood transfusion were carried out based on the patient’s blood volume status, to maintain hematocrit between 0.21 and 0.24. The CPB was stopped when the nasopharyngeal temperature rose to 36.5℃, the bladder temperature rose to 36.0℃, and hemodynamic parameters were stable.

After the end of CPB (weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass), protamine sulfate was used to neutralize heparin, and the remaining blood in the cell saver was reinfused back to the patient. During the PTE procedure, 15–30 mg/kg methylprednisolone was given two times during anesthesia induction and rewarming, respectively. A 20% mannitol injection was added 2.5 ml/kg to the reinfusion chamber when the bladder temperature rewarmed to 28℃.

Data collection and the primary outcome measure and its definitions

History of pulmonary thromboembolism, laboratory test results, the parameters of right heart catheterization, indicators of CPB and DHCA during PTE surgery, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores on the second day after surgery were collected and compared between the two groups. The primary outcome measure was MAE during the postoperative hospital stay. MAE was defined as all-cause mortality, major complications related to PTE (including reperfusion pulmonary edema, pulmonary hemorrhagic syndrome, residual pulmonary arterial hypertension, pneumonia, pneumothorax, stroke, coma, pericardial effusion, and acute kidney injury, either individually or in combination). Measured indicators and their definitions are as follows: (1) Heart Failure: refers to patients with NYHA class Ⅲ-Ⅳ on admission and echocardiography showing right ventricle (RV) diameter > 20 mm, or left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 0.50 and LV diameter > 52 mm; (2) RV enlargement refers to RV diameter > 20 mm; (3) Hyperuricemia was defined as the uric acid level > 375 umol/L on admission; (4) Abnormal liver function was defined as any of the following exceeding the upper limit of normal reference values: aspartate transferase (AST) > 40u, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 40u, total bilirubin (TBIL) > 20 µmol/L, and direct bilirubin (DBIL) > 6.0 µmol/L; (5) Patient with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 80 ml/min/1.73m2 and blood creatinine > 133 µmol/L was defined as kidney dysfunction (eGFR was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula); (6) Elevated D-Dimer was defined as D-Dimer value > 250 ng/ml on admission; (7) Elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) refers to the value of BNP > 200 pg/ml on admission; (8) Anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 110 g/L. Patients were followed up for 12 months by phone or outpatient. The follow-up indicators were symptoms during general physical activities and survival status. The follow-up ended on October 31, 2024.

Statistical methods

All data were entered into the Solutions Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) database and analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM SPSS statistics 25.0 USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the differences between the two groups were assessed using independent sample t-tests. Categorical data were expressed as percentages (%), and the differences were assessed with a chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Two-tailed tests were used, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Risk factor assessment was performed using a binary logistic regression equation and analysis of the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve in variables. Variables entering the equation with a significance level of P < 0.05 were considered as a significant difference and a risk factor associated with MAE. The performance of the regression model is evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), the Youden index, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A VIF value less than 5.00, a Youden index greater than 0.50, and a p-value for the Hosmer-Lemeshow test greater than 0.05 indicate that the model’s performance is excellent, and the regression results are reliable.

Incidence of MAEs and changes in pulmonary artery pressure after PTE

The postoperative hospital stay was 5–29 days with a mean of (11.33 ± 6.17) days in all patients. It was 5–16 days in 73 cases (85.88%), 19 days in 6 cases (7.06%), and 29 days in 6 cases (7.06%) with multiple complications. Of the 85 patients, 36 (42.35%) experienced one or more MAE after PTE during hospital stay, including 7 (8.24%) all-cause deaths, 14 (16.47%) with reperfusion pulmonary edema, 20 (23.53%) with residual pulmonary hypertension (defined as mPAP > 25 mmHg at rest), 6 (7.06%) with pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome, 12 (14.12%) with pneumonia, 12 (14.12%) with delirium, 6 (7.06%) with pericardial tamponade, 12 (14.12%) with pleural effusion, and 6 (7.06%) with acute kidney injury. Seven cases died of a combination of complications in 5–29 days post-operation. Among the 20 patients with residual pulmonary hypertension, 5 died in the hospital, and 15 underwent BPA 6–12 months after surgery, which relieved their symptoms. During follow-up, the patients had no significant symptoms of palpitations or dyspnea during general physical activity, and the survival rate at 12 months was 91.76% (78/85) after the PTE. After PTE, sPAP decreased from 80.00 ± 15.82 to 38.25 ± 9.47 mmHg in the MAE group and decreased from 68.18 ± 28.41 to 31.68 ± 5.18 mmHg in the control group. The mPAP decreased from 53.85 ± 6.45 to 20.00 ± 6.86 mmHg in the MAE group and decreased from 44.65 ± 10.48 to 17.05 ± 3.05 mmHg in the control group. After the PTE, sPAP and mPAP in the MAE group were still higher than that in the control group (P < 0.05). MOCA score and MMSE score in the MAE group were significantly lower than that in the control group (P < 0.05). The duration of ventilator use, ICU stay, and hospital stay in the MAE group were significantly longer than that in the control group (P < 0.05). Analysis of area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve in variables and model performance evaluation

When the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was performed, we found that the AUC of mPAP, PAWP, and PVR was 0.783, 0.900, and 0.872 respectively, with P values < 0.001. The cut-off value is 10.90 mmHg with a sensitivity of 0.833 and specificity of 0.837 in the PAWP, 52.84 mmHg with a sensitivity of 0.667 and specificity of 0.837 in the mPAP, and 12.18 WU with a sensitivity of 0.833 and specificity of 0.837 in the PVR (See Fig. 1, Table S2, and Table S6 in the Supplementary Information).

ROC Curve on PAWP, mPAP and PVR

The AUC values for CPB time, DHCA time, and the longest DHCA time were 0.795, 0.844, and 0.813, respectively (p < 0.05). The cutoff values were 249.00 min (sensitivity: 0.583, specificity: 0.950) for CPB time, 25.50 min (sensitivity: 0.833, specificity: 0.766) for DHCA time, and 14.50 min (sensitivity: 0.750, specificity: 0.862) for the longest DHCA time (see Fig. 2, Table S3, and Table S6 in the Supplementary Information). The regression model performance analysis showed that the VIF values for the six variables (mPAP, PAWP, PVR, DHCA time, CPB time, and the longest DHCA time) ranged from 1.195 to 1.782 (all p < 0.01 except for CPB time, p = 0.053), and the Youden indices ranged from 0.503 to 0.670 (see Tables S4, S5, and S6 in the Supplementary Information). The result of Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated that the regression model had a good fit (χ² = 15.022, p = 0.059) with a prediction accuracy of 97.2% in MAE (see Table S7 and Table S8 in the Supplementary Information). These results demonstrate that the regression model performs excellently, has no multicollinearity, and is reliable.

Discussion

Pathophysiological mechanisms of CTEPH

CTEPH is a rare condition that arises from the incomplete resolution of thrombi following acute pulmonary embolism. Residual thrombi undergo fibrosis, triggering chronic inflammation, intimal thickening, and vascular remodeling, ultimately leading to arterial stiffening, increased PVR, and sustained pulmonary hypertension [7–9]. Recurrent deep vein thrombosis and repeated pulmonary artery embolization further contribute to disease progression. Over time, unresolved thrombi organize, promoting vascular remodeling and in situ thrombosis, which narrow or occlude the pulmonary vasculature. This obstruction elevates PVR and pulmonary arterial pressure, exacerbating pulmonary hypertension [10]. The resulting increase in RV afterload may progress to right heart failure [1, 3, 6, 11]. A hallmark of CTEPH is the persistent obstruction of pulmonary arteries due to unresolved thrombi and their fibrotic transformation within the vascular lumen [12–15].

The treatments of CTEPH and the complication related-PTE

During the acute phase of pulmonary artery thrombosis, anticoagulation and intravenous thrombolysis are the mainstays of therapy. For more severe cases, thrombus aspiration with adjunctive thrombolytics may be considered in addition to anticoagulation. Patients with CTEPH require lifelong anticoagulation therapy. In the chronic stage of CTEPH, the two primary treatment options are PTE with CPB and balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) [9, 16, 17]. BPA is mainly indicated for patients who are not candidates for PTE or those with residual pulmonary artery stenosis after PTE, demonstrating favorable outcomes [18]. According to the latest European Society of Cardiology guidelines, BPA now carries a Class Ⅰ recommendation for inoperable CTEPH and residual/recurrent CTEPH [19]. In this study, 15 patients with residual pulmonary hypertension underwent BPA, showing both efficacy and safety, further supporting the role of BPA in inoperable or recurrent/post-PTE CTEPH [20].

PTE is an open surgical procedure that is performed under DHCA and CPB support, during which a surgeon manually removes these chronic thrombi. Consequently, PTE is regarded as a potentially curative therapy and the cornerstone of treatment for operable CTEPH. It is also considered the gold standard of care for patients with operable CTEPH due to its excellent long-term outcomes and low mortality rates. Studies have shown that patients undergoing PTE have higher survival rates than those managed medically [21]. However, PTE is associated with potential surgical complications, including perioperative mortality, reperfusion pulmonary edema, residual pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary hemorrhage, acute kidney injury, and others [4, 22, 23]. In the study, among the 85 patients who underwent PTE, 42.35% (36/85) experienced one or more MAEs, including 8.24% (7 cases) all-cause deaths, 16.47% (14 cases) with reperfusion pulmonary edema, 23.53% (20 cases) with residual pulmonary hypertension, 7.06% (6 cases) with pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome, 14.2% (12 cases) with pneumonia, 14.12% (12 cases) with delirium, 7.06% (6 cases) with pericardial tamponad, 14.12% (12 cases) with pleural effusion, and 7.06% (6 cases) with acute kidney injury. The incidence rates of these complications were consistent with those reported in previous studies [21, 24].

The mortality related to PTE and its affecting factors

The mortality related to PTE in the different studies was extremely heterogeneous, ranging from 0.8% to 24.8% [23] in view of several large sample studies. In this study, 8.24% (8/85) patients died of post-operative multiple complications during the in-hospital stays (The in-hospital stay of the postoperative patients was 5–29 days with a mean of 11.33 ± 6.17 days in all patients), which was speculated to be related to the large number of critically ill patients in this study. The mortality of 8.24% is similar to that (11.8% [6], 24.4% [23], 8.42% [25], and 8.9% [26]) reported by several large sample study groups. The mortality related- residual pulmonary hypertension was 1.8%−4.3% [22, 27, 28]. The differences in mortality reported by study groups are related to the complexity of the pulmonary lesions and the worse pre-operative condition of the patients. The pre-operative sPAP, mPAP, PVR, NYHA heart functional class, comorbidities and, in particular, the CTEPH team’s experience should be evaluated to improve the patient outcomes after PTE [29, 30]. Notably, preoperative PVR >15 WU is associated with a threefold increase in in-hospital mortality. In our study, the MAE group had significantly higher PVR (12.92 ± 1.00 WU) than the control group (8.27 ± 3.88 WU, P < 0.001). A PVR cutoff of 12.18 WU predicted MAE with 83.3% sensitivity and 83.7% specificity, confirming PVR as a strong prognostic marker for post-PTE complications. Most MAE-group patients exceeded this threshold, likely contributing to their higher mortality risk.

Factors affecting MAE after PTE surgery

There are few reports on the risk factors affecting MAE after PTE surgery. In this study, it is firstly reported that a total of 35 potential predictor variables (those variables are listed in Tables 1, 2 and 3 and Table S1 in Supplement Information) may be affecting patient outcomes after PTE. However, Logistic regression analysis showed among 35 variables 6 parameters (that were included in Tables 3 and 4, and Tables S2 and S3 in Supplement Information) are associated with the incidence of MAE. It is found by Logistic regression analysis and AUC of ROC analysis that the preoperative parameters including mPAP, PAWP, and PVR, and the intraoperative parameters including CPB time, DHCA time, and the longest single DHCA time are risk factors associated with the occurrence of MAE. The aforementioned 6 parameters are statistically significant predictors of major complications confirmed by logistics regression analysis. The results of AUC of ROC analysis also showed that the AUC value of mPAP, PAWP, PVR, CPB time, DHCA time, and the longest single DHCA time are 0.783, 0.900, 0.872, 0.795, 0.844 and 0.813, respectively (all p < 0.05), those demonstrate the accuracy rates for predicting MAE in post-operation are 78.3%, 90.0%, 87.2%, 79.5%, 84.4% and 81.3%, respectively. The cut-off values of mPAP, PAWP, PVR, CPB time, DHCA time and the longest single DHCA time were 52.84 mmHg, 10.90 mmHg, 12.18 WU, 249.00 min, 25.50 min and 14.50 min respectively, with the high sensitivity of 0.583 to o.833 and specificity of 0.766 to 0.950 (See Table S6 in the Supplementary Information). In clinical practice, it may reduce MAE and mortality rates that these indicators are controlled within their cut-off values by preoperative medication. It is recommended to administer anticoagulant therapy and improve cardiac function therapy before PTE, which can help to reduce the new thrombosis in the pulmonary artery, to improve hemodynamic indicators, and to lower the incidence of MAE. Anticoagulant therapy is recommended to use vitamin K antagonists (warfarin), FXa inhibitors (tirofiban), or sodium heparin. The new drugs riociguat, treprostinil, and macitentan can improve the patient’s six-minute walking distance and exercise endurance [30]. The combination of phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (sildenafil) and endothelin receptor antagonist (bosentan) can improve hemodynamic parameters in CTEPH patients with deteriorating hemodynamics.

Heart failure (NYHA class Ⅲ-Ⅳ), low 6-minute walk distance, increased lactate level and elevated BNP were also recognized as risk factors associated with MAE [31, 32]. In our study, while these parameters (Female, low body mass index, heart failure decreased LVEF, elevated BNP, low 6 min walk test value, and elevated TVPG, and sPAP and lactate peak value) showed significant differences (P < 0.05) when compared with two groups, these would be considered as risk factors for MAE. However, most of these indicators did not show statistically significant differences in terms of impact on MAE when incorporated into the Logistic regression equation analysis. In the Logistic regression equation, the p-value of the cardiac index was 0.351, but its RR value was 7.915, so the significance of the cardiac index value affecting MAE would not be denied in this study. Therefore, it may reduce the incidence of MAE if the preoperative risk factors are adjusted with medical therapy and the intraoperative parameters of CPB and DHCA including CPB time, ACC time, DHCA time, and the longest single DHCA time are controlled.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations.

Conclusion

The results of the study show that preoperative hemodynamic parameters (including mPAP, PAWP, PVR, and cardiac index) assessed by right heart catheterization, along with intraoperative variables (such as prolonged CPB duration, ACC time, total DHCA time, and maximum single DHCA duration), serve as significant predictors of MAE following PTE. Optimizing these modifiable factors may lead to improved postoperative outcomes.
