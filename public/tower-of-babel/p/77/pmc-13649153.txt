Avatrombopag Versus Placebo for Persistent Chemotherapy-Induced Thrombocytopenia in GI Cancers: The Phase II ACT-GI Trial
Authors: Hanny Al-Samkari, Joseph J. Shatzel, Sandhya R. Panch, Julia H. Keating, Elizabeth P. Walsh, Colin D. Weekes, Adel Kardosh, Stacey A. Cohen, Jonathan J. Cohen, Pamela G. Hodges, Shilton E. Dhaver, Nina Strojny, Carolyn Donovan, Christos Belibasakis, Gerald A. Soff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEChemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia (CIT) is a challenging and common complication of cytotoxic chemotherapy in patients with GI cancers. There is no widely available approved treatment. Avatrombopag is a potent, second-generation oral thrombopoietin receptor agonist promising for CIT management.METHODSWe conducted a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, investigator-initiated US trial of avatrombopag or placebo (1:1) in GI cancer patients with persistent CIT (platelets ≤85 × 109/L on day 1 of a chemotherapy cycle despite adequate time to recover from the prior cycle). The primary end point was successful correction of CIT and prevention of recurrence (defined as recovery of platelets to ≥100 × 109/L, no chemotherapy dose modification/delay, and prevention of CIT recurrence [platelets ≥100 × 109/L] at end of the on-study cycle).RESULTSThe trial was closed to enrollment by the data and safety monitoring board at the planned interim analysis after meeting prespecified stopping criteria for efficacy. Sixteen of 23 patients (70% [95% CI, 47 to 87]) receiving avatrombopag achieved the primary end point versus 4/24 patients (17% [95% CI, 5 to 37]) receiving placebo (Z = 3.67, P < .001). The median (IQR) platelet count at the end of the on-cycle treatment period was 157 (136-202) × 109/L with avatrombopag versus 72 (68-134) × 109/L with placebo. Adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs occurred in 78% and 17% of patients receiving avatrombopag and 46% and 0% of patients receiving placebo, respectively. No serious AEs were study drug-related. There were no treatment-related AEs leading to death or discontinuation of study drug.CONCLUSIONIn this randomized, placebo-controlled trial, avatrombopag was efficacious in the management of persistent CIT in patients with GI cancers. These findings are promising for a common, challenging oncologic complication that prevents delivery of full-dose, on-time, cancer-directed therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia (CIT) is a common, challenging complication of cytotoxic chemotherapy.1-4 In addition to increasing the risk of major bleeding, a highly morbid complication in patients with cancer,5 CIT often results in compromised cancer treatment as there are no widely available, licensed agents for management. Platelet transfusion in patients without active bleeding or potentially life-threatening thrombocytopenia is impractical for the management of CIT, given the relative scarcity, transient benefit, and infection risks of transfused platelets.6 Therefore, reduction of chemotherapy relative dose intensity (RDI) through chemotherapy treatment delays, dose reductions, and agent discontinuation remains the standard of care in the management of CIT. Even modest reductions in chemotherapy RDI have been shown to negatively affect principal oncologic outcomes such as progression-free survival and overall survival over multiple tumor types and treatment regimens.7-13 Persistent CIT, a particularly pernicious entity typically defined as a platelet count <100 × 109/L on day 1 of a chemotherapy cycle despite adequate time to recover from the prior cycle,4,14-16 usually emerges after prior receipt of many chemotherapy cycles. It results from substantial depletion of hematopoietic progenitors and disruption of the bone marrow microenvironment from repeated cytotoxic insult.17 Persistent CIT is particularly challenging to manage as it is often chronic and recurrent, prompting repeated treatment delays and compounding chemotherapy dose reductions as additional cycles are given and the underlying hematopoietic compromise worsens. Modern CIT investigation has recognized persistent CIT as the major relevant CIT entity in clinical practice, where it remains a substantial unmet need in the treatment of patients with solid tumors.4,15,16

Given the routine use of platinum, taxane, gemcitabine, and fluoropyrimidine-containing multiagent cytotoxic regimens over many planned treatment cycles, CIT is a particularly common occurrence in patients with GI cancers.17 CIT complicates the course of nearly two thirds of patients with colorectal cancer.3 For the subset of these patients who ultimately develop persistent CIT, management is particularly challenging.

Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs), which stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of megakaryocytes, megakaryocyte progenitors, and hematopoietic stem cells, represent a promising treatment modality for persistent CIT.14,18-21 TPO-RAs such as romiplostim, eltrombopag, and avatrombopag are licensed worldwide to safely treat other etiologies of thrombocytopenia and hematopoietic progenitor compromise, including immune thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopenia of chronic liver disease, severe aplastic anemia, and acute radiation syndrome.22 However, data for the use of TPO-RAs to treat persistent CIT are more limited. The TPO-RA with the most data by far in CIT is the weekly subcutaneous injectable peptibody agent romiplostim, which has demonstrated safety and efficacy in the management of persistent CIT, including a single-center phase II randomized trial in patients with solid tumors, a phase III randomized, placebo-controlled trial in patients with GI cancers, and in real-world data.14,18–20,23,34 Given these data, clinical practice guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis recommend consideration of romiplostim use off-label for persistent CIT (particularly when a clinical trial of a TPO-RA for CIT is not available).2,24

However, romiplostim use in CIT requires weekly hours-long clinic visits for platelet count measurement, dose preparation, and drug administration, further medicalizing the lives of patients whose days are already governed by their cancer and its treatment. An oral TPO-RA may provide for a more patient-centric option to manage CIT. Avatrombopag, a second-generation once-daily oral TPO-RA, is taken once daily with no dietary restrictions or evidence of hepatotoxicity (in contrast to eltrombopag, its first-generation counterpart). These traits make avatrombopag appealing as a potential treatment for persistent CIT.21,25 Therefore, we conducted an investigator-initiated trial, Avatrombopag for Chemotherapy-induced Thrombocytopenia in GI Cancers (ACT-GI, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05772546), to assess the safety and efficacy of avatrombopag for the management of persistent CIT. Herein, we report the primary results of ACT-GI.

METHODS

Trial Overview and Patients

This multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, investigator-initiated, phase II US trial enrolled adults receiving cytotoxic chemotherapy for GI cancers (gastroesophageal, small bowel, colorectal, pancreatic, or hepatobiliary cancer, excluding GI lymphomas) of any stage with persistent CIT. Persistent CIT was defined as a platelet count ≤85 × 109/L on day 1 of a scheduled chemotherapy cycle (despite adequate time to recover from the prior cycle, so as to exclude spontaneously recovering nadir thrombocytopenia). Patients were required to receive 14-day, 21-day, or 28-day chemotherapy cycles including at least one cytotoxic agent (fluoropyrimidine, platinum, gemcitabine, irinotecan, taxane, and/or anthracycline), with plan to continue the current CIT-causing regimen at the same dose and schedule for at least one more cycle if the platelet count was adequate (≥100 × 109/L). Targeted and immunotherapeutic agents given as part of the patient's antineoplastic regimen were permitted. Key exclusion criteria included history of hematologic malignancy, known bone marrow invasion by tumor or multiple metastatic bone lesions, prior irradiation to the pelvic bones of a dose of >20 Gy, other known etiologies of thrombocytopenia, recent clinically significant bleeding, and spontaneous recovery of the platelet count to >100 × 109/L before random assignment; full eligibility criteria are listed in the Appendix (online only). Patients with a history of arterial or venous thromboembolism were eligible if they were tolerant of ongoing antithrombotic therapy or had completed the prescribed course of antithrombotic therapy.

After random assignment, patients received oral avatrombopag or matched placebo daily from study day 1 through reinitiation of chemotherapy and, if their platelet count recovered to ≥100 × 109/L within 14 days, for one additional chemotherapy cycle. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the protocol/amendments were approved by local institutional review boards at the four trial sites in the United States (Appendix Table A1). Informed consent was obtained from each participant (or guardian) before trial participation.

Trial Procedures

Eligible patients were randomly assigned 1:1 to avatrombopag or placebo, stratified by number of cytotoxic agents (1 v ≥2). Randomization was blinded for patients and site study staff, and unblinding occurred only if necessary for emergent clinical management.

The study design is illustrated in the Appendix Figure A1. The double-blind treatment period consisted of two sequential phases: a lead-in period and an on-cycle period. After random assignment, patients entered a lead-in period lasting up to 14 days, receiving study drug at a starting dose of 40 mg once daily. Upon platelet count recovery to ≥100 × 109/L, patients resumed chemotherapy at the same dosing as the cycle administered before study enrollment for one on-study cycle (on-cycle period). Patients failing to achieve a platelet count of ≥100 × 109/L by study day 15 during the lead-in period were considered a treatment failure with respect to the primary end point and proceeded to the end of the double-blind treatment period. All patients who completed double-blind treatment per protocol were eligible to receive avatrombopag for up to 180 days in an optional ongoing open-label extension. All patients had a safety follow-up visit 30-42 days after the final dose of study drug.

During the double-blind period, the dose of study drug was titrated at once-per-week intervals (to a minimum of 20 mg three times per week and a maximum of 60 mg once daily) according to protocol-defined thresholds (Appendix Table A2) to maintain a goal platelet count between 100 and 250 × 109/L.

Enrollment took place from October 4, 2023, to October 10, 2025, the data cutoff date was January 2, 2026, and the last double-blind period visit occurred on October 30, 2025.

Study End Points

The primary end point was successful CIT treatment and prevention of recurrence, defined as achieving a platelet count ≥100 × 109/L within the 2 week lead-in period and finishing the on-study cycle of chemotherapy without CIT recurrence (no on-cycle dose reduction or treatment delay due to thrombocytopenia plus ability to receive a subsequent cycle of chemotherapy without dose reduction or treatment delay due to CIT after the on-study cycle, defined as achieving a platelet count of ≥100 × 109/L at the start of the following cycle whether or not a subsequent post-study cycle was planned). Platelet transfusions administered at any time during the double-blind treatment period rendered the participant a treatment failure with respect to the primary end point.

Secondary end points included the rate of platelet count recovery during the lead-in period, the requirement for platelet transfusion, the rate of clinically relevant bleeding events, and safety (including the rate of thromboembolic events and serious treatment-emergent adverse events [AEs]).

Statistical Analysis

The study was designed to compare the rate of successful CIT treatment and prevention of recurrence between the avatrombopag and placebo groups with a target enrollment of 60 participants (30 per group). Assuming a 65% success rate in the avatrombopag group and a 25% success rate in the placebo group, with 60 patients randomly assigned 1:1 to avatrombopag or placebo, the study had 96% power for the primary end point. For the primary end point, the rate of successful CIT treatment and prevention of recurrence in each treatment group was estimated using proportions with corresponding 95% exact binomial CIs and compared between groups using a one-sided Z test for proportions with significance level 0.10. Participants who did not receive protocol therapy after enrollment were replaced. An interim analysis for futility and efficacy was planned when 67% of information was obtained (20 patients per group had completed the double-blind treatment period). Futility and efficacy boundaries for the interim analysis were calculated in EAST version 6.5 using the Lan-DeMets spending function with an O'Brien-Fleming stopping boundary. The primary analysis was performed on all randomly assigned participants, excluding participants who were replaced. Any patients prematurely discontinuing the study, developing progression of disease during the double-blind period such that their chemotherapy regimen had to be changed, and those with missing data such that the primary end point could not be adjudicated were all considered treatment failures with respect to assessment of the primary end point.

Secondary end points were evaluated descriptively without formal hypothesis testing. Efficacy analyses were performed on all randomly assigned participants, excluding participants who were replaced, and safety analyses were performed on all participants who received at least one dose of study drug. All analyses were performed with R version 4.3.2.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics and Disposition

The trial was closed to enrollment by the data and safety monitoring board at the planned interim analysis after meeting the prespecified stopping criterion for efficacy (Z = 3.87, P < .001) after 47 patients had been enrolled and at least 20 patients had completed the double-blind treatment period in each group. Patient disposition is illustrated in Figure 1. 42 of 47 enrolled patients completed study drug; four discontinuations were due to physician decision and one was due to an AE (see Exposure and Safety below).

Demographic, oncologic, and clinical characteristics of patients were similar in the two groups (Table 1, Appendix Table A3). The median (IQR) patient age was 59 (47-68) years and 30% were female. Successful CIT Treatment and Prevention of Recurrence (primary end point)

The primary end point of successful CIT treatment and prevention of recurrence was achieved in 16 of 23 patients (70% [95% CI, 47 to 87]) in the avatrombopag group versus four of 24 patients (17% [95% CI, 5 to 37]) in the placebo group (Z = 3.67, P < .001; Figure 2A). Sensitivity analyses by chemotherapy backbone (oxaliplatin-based v not oxaliplatin-based) and chemotherapy cycle length (14-day v 21- or 28-day) had similar findings (Appendix Table A4). After initiation of the study drug, higher platelet counts throughout the course of the study were seen with avatrombopag compared with placebo (Fig 2B).

Secondary Efficacy End Points

Platelet improvement to ≥100 × 109/L during the lead-in period was achieved by 19 of 23 patients (83%) in the avatrombopag group versus 11 of 24 patients (46%) in the placebo group. For patients eligible to proceed from the lead-in period to the on-cycle period, the median (IQR) platelet count at the end of the on-cycle treatment period was 157 (136-202) ×109/L in the avatrombopag group and 72 (68-134) ×109/L in the placebo group. One patient in the avatrombopag group had a clinically relevant bleeding event, unrelated to thrombocytopenia: an intestinal stoma site bleed (grade 2) with a platelet count of 105 × 109/L; this patient completed protocol treatment. No patient received platelet transfusion.

Exposure and Safety

Patients in the avatrombopag group received a median (range) average daily dose of 40 mg (20-55 mg). The final avatrombopag dose level was 20 mg once daily in three (13%) patients, 40 mg once daily in 14 (61%) patients, and 60 mg once daily in six (26%) patients. Median (range) duration of study drug exposure in the double-blind period was 23 (1-58) days in the avatrombopag group and 15 (8-29) days in the placebo group.

Any AE and serious AEs occurred in 78% and 17% of patients in the avatrombopag group and 46% and 0% of patients in the placebo group, respectively (Table 2). Grade 3 or higher AEs occurred in 22% and 8% of patients in avatrombopag and placebo groups, respectively. No grade 3 or higher AEs or serious AEs were study drug-related. The most frequent AEs were headache (avatrombopag 17%, placebo 8%), as well as diarrhea and nausea (both avatrombopag 13% and placebo 0%), and neutrophil count decreased (0% avatrombopag, 13% placebo) occurring in 0% and 13% in the avatrombopag and placebo groups, respectively. No AEs of thrombocytosis were reported. There were no treatment-related AEs leading to death or discontinuation of study drug or chemotherapy in either group.

DISCUSSION

CIT remains a significant unmet need, posing challenges for oncologists and patients with cancer worldwide.4,16 A prior major observational study of 609 patients with CIT reported major bleeding (WHO grade 3-4) in 3.4% of chemotherapy cycles, with a median overall survival of 5.9 months in those experiencing major bleeding during a cycle complicated by CIT compared with over 15 months in those who did not.5 The notable absence of widely available licensed CIT treatment, impracticality of long-term platelet transfusion support, and widespread recognition of the serious morbidity of major bleeding in patients with cancer have shaped the current clinical management of persistent CIT around chemotherapy RDI reduction. Our study, which demonstrated clear efficacy of avatrombopag in successfully treating persistent CIT and prevention of its recurrence, represents a significant step forward in the goal of improving care of these patients.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate efficacy of avatrombopag in the treatment of CIT, providing patients with significantly greater opportunity to receive chemotherapy on time and at full dose. By contrast, AVA-CIT-330, a prior randomized, placebo-controlled trial of avatrombopag in 122 patients with lung, ovarian, or bladder cancer and primarily mid-cycle nadir CIT failed to achieve its primary end point due to high rates of spontaneous platelet count recovery in the placebo group.26 Critically, persistence of significant thrombocytopenia into the following chemotherapy cycle was not required. Appropriately, this study served as the proverbial nail in the coffin for investigations into the treatment of nonpersistent CIT, because it provided good evidence that most of these patients likely do not need thrombopoietic support.27,28 Despite its failure, AVA-CIT-330 demonstrated higher platelet counts in patients receiving avatrombopag compared with placebo and suggested that avatrombopag was safe in patients with cancer. This study laid the foundation for ACT-GI, which was designed to evaluate avatrombopag in the appropriate patient population: those with persistent CIT.

ACT-GI focused on patients with GI cancers. This was done both to reduce patient and chemotherapy regimen heterogeneity and because intensive multiagent cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens remain the foundation of both curative and palliative treatment for most GI cancers. Even relatively modest cytotoxic chemotherapy RDI reduction has been observed to be deleterious.7-13 For example, in a multivariable analysis of prospective clinical trial data, a ≥20% reduction of irinotecan RDI in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer treated with FOLFIRI was associated with inferior progression-free survival (hazard ratio of 3.18, P < .01) and overall survival (hazard ratio of 2.72, P < .01).13 Although it is important to note that, to our knowledge, no study has yet been reported demonstrating specifically that correction of CIT-induced chemotherapy RDI reduction improves survival, we also have no reason to expect that RDI reduction due to CIT is any less deleterious than RDI reduction from other causes. Also, there is foundational evidence to support the safety of thrombopoietin agonism in patients with solid tumors: in contrast to erythropoietin, nonmyeloid tumor cells do not exhibit meaningful expression of the thrombopoietin receptor and thrombopoietin agonism does not stimulate nonmyeloid tumor cell growth.29

As with AVA-CIT-330, ACT-GI supports the safety of avatrombopag in patients with cancer. The higher incidence of non–study drug–related mild-to-moderate AEs in the avatrombopag group is consistent with the greater exposure duration to chemotherapy in patients on this treatment group. The primary concern with the use of TPO-RAs is a theoretical increase in the risk of thromboembolism from manipulation of the platelet count, and ACT-GI provides additional reassuring data in this regard. To better reflect the real-world GI cancer patient population and in contrast to AVA-CIT-330, ACT-GI rather broadly allowed patients with prior venous and arterial thromboembolism to enroll. Although one patient randomly assigned to avatrombopag who had a history of prior cerebrovascular disease and strokes did experience an ischemic stroke, this event occurred less than an hour after the first dose of the patient's study drug—far too soon to have any possible relationship with avatrombopag effect.30 The pharmacodynamics of thrombopoiesis require at least 3-5 days before a TPO-RA can begin to have an impact on the platelet count,30 and none of these agents, including avatrombopag, have any impact on platelet function—they do not induce platelet hyperreactivity, increased platelet activation, or spontaneous platelet aggregation.31-33

Our study has several limitations. Although it was adequately powered for the primary end point, appropriate clinical care of these patients (chemotherapy RDI reduction when indicated) means that the rates of bleeding events are typically too low for meaningful statistical analysis in ethically designed CIT trials. Similarly, it is not feasible to perform a prospective CIT trial adequately powered to assess a difference in thromboembolic event rates. The study did not evaluate survival outcomes, as it enrolled patients across the spectrum of tumor type, stage, chemotherapy regimen, and line of therapy. It remains unproven whether RDI improvements specifically achieved with successful CIT management improve survival outcomes, and this requires further investigation. Due to the infeasibility of recruiting enough patients with persistent CIT with the same tumor type, stage, chemotherapy regimen, and line of therapy into a randomized trial, we expect that real-world evidence studies of much longer duration will be needed to address this question. Finally, the ACT-GI open-label period, which will provide data on longer-term durability of avatrombopag in management of persistent CIT, is still ongoing.

In conclusion, avatrombopag was safe and efficacious in the management of persistent CIT in patients with GI cancers. Given the significant unmet need in the management of persistent CIT, our findings provide evidence that avatrombopag may be a suitable therapy for managing persistent CIT in these patients.
