Clinical Outcome of Rifabutin-based Treatment for Pulmonary Tuberculosis in Solid Organ Transplant Recipients
Authors: Jinyoung Yang, Jae-Hoon Ko, Sun Young Cho, Cheol-In Kang, Doo Ryeon Chung, Kyong Ran Peck, Jinsoo Rhu, Jong Man Kim, Gyu-Seong Choi, Kyo Won Lee, Jae Berm Park, Wooseong Huh, Darae Kim, Jin-Oh Choi, Kyungmin Huh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsRifabutin is often used instead of rifampin to treat tuberculosis (TB) in solid organ transplant recipients (SOTRs) due to fewer drug interactions with immunosuppressants. However, data on its efficacy are limited.MethodsA retrospective, case-control study was conducted at a tertiary care center in Korea. SOTRs aged ≥18 years with culture-positive pulmonary TB treated with isoniazid and rifabutin for >80% of the treatment duration were included. Those with rifampin-resistant TB or who discontinued immunosuppressants prior to TB diagnosis were excluded. Each SOTR was matched to three non-SOTR controls treated with rifampin-based regimens. The primary outcome was treatment completion without early relapse. Logistic regression with and without overlap weighting was used for analysis.ResultsForty SOTRs and 120 non-SOTRs were analyzed. Baseline TB severity markers (cavitary lesions, smear/culture-positivity) were comparable, but extrapulmonary TB and isoniazid resistance were more common in SOTRs. Treatment duration was longer in SOTRs (median 272 vs 187 days, P < .001). The primary outcome occurred in 90% of SOTRs and 96.7% of controls (P = .108). Treatment completion was lower in SOTRs (92.5% vs 100%, P = .015). No significant differences were observed in TB recurrence or 1-year mortality. TB-attributable deaths were absent in both groups. After overlap weighting, no significant difference was found in the primary outcome (aOR 0.36; 95% confidence interval, 0.01–10.41). Allograft rejection and failure occurred in 10% and 12.5% of SOTRs, respectively.ConclusionsRifabutin-based therapy in SOTRs achieved treatment outcomes comparable to rifampin-based regimens in non-SOTRs, supporting its use in this population.

Tuberculosis (TB) is an important opportunistic infection in solid organ transplant recipients (SOTRs). It is associated with significant risks for mortality and allograft loss. The incidence of TB in SOTRs ranges from 1.2% to 6.4%, with rates as high as 15% in endemic areas [1, 2]. The risk of TB is 20–74 times higher in SOTR than in the general population and TB infection in SOTR is associated with a high fatality rate [2, 3]. TB is associated with a 2.8-fold increase in mortality risk and a 3.4-fold increase in the risk of allograft failure in kidney transplantation recipients [4].

Rifamycins are bactericidal DNA-dependent RNA polymerase inhibitors that can inhibit the RNA synthesis of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, acting as key drugs for TB treatment that can shorten the standard treatment period from 18 to 6 months [5]. Rifampin is the most commonly used rifamycin for treating of TB. It is recommended as a part of the standard first-line treatment regimen [6]. However, rifampin is a potent inducer of both the cytochrome P-450 (CYP) enzyme system and the P-glycoprotein transport system, leading to significant drug-drug interactions [7]. Drug interaction of rifampin is a critical hurdle for a rifampin-based regimen in SOTRs as rifampin can lead to significant reductions in serum concentrations of cyclosporine, tacrolimus, sirolimus, and steroid [8]. Maintaining adequate levels of immunosuppressive drugs is often very difficult with concomitant use of rifampin. Increased incidence of allograft rejection during TB treatment with rifampin has been reported [1]. Rifabutin is a less potent inducer of CYP450-3A4. It is known to have less drug-drug interactions than rifampin. Thus, it has been suggested as an alternative option to rifampin for SOTRs [7, 9]. Rifabutin and rifampin have similar activities against M tuberculosis. Successful treatment of TB in individuals with HIV using rifabutin has been reported [10]. However, there are only a few small-scale retrospective studies on treatment outcomes of substituting rifampin with rifabutin in SOTRs [11–13].

Thus, this study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes and safety of rifabutin-based treatment for pulmonary TB in SOTRs compared to the standard rifampin-based treatment in non-SOTR patients.

METHODS

Study Population and Patient Selection

This retrospective case-control study was conducted at a tertiary care referral hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea. This hospital operates a large-volume SOT program. We included adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with pulmonary TB with who received SOT from January 2004 to December 2023. The diagnosis of pulmonary TB was defined as a positive M tuberculosis culture in respiratory specimens including sputum, induced sputum, transtracheal aspirate, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Each SOTR was matched to three control non-SOTR patients who were diagnosed with pulmonary TB in the same year and treated with a rifampin-based regimen.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) SOTR who had discontinued immunosuppressants before the diagnosis of TB (eg, allograft failure), (2) those with Mycobacterium isolated that was resistant to rifampicin, (3) SOTR who stayed alive but received anti-TB drugs for less than 6 months, (4) those with isoniazid or rifampin/rifabutin administered for ≤20% of the treatment period due to side effects or drug-drug interactions. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (IRB file number: 2004-02-009). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was completion of TB treatment without recurrence within 1 year after treatment completion. Treatment completion was defined based on the WHO criteria as follows: a patient who completed treatment as recommended by the national policy whose outcome did not meet the definition for treatment failure [14]. Treatment failure was defined as termination or permanent change of treatment regimen due to the lack of clinical or microbiological response.

Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, TB-attributable mortality, acute kidney injury, hepatotoxicity, graft survival, graft rejection, cytomegalovirus (CMV) disease that occurred after TB diagnosis, and whether immunosuppressants were changed after starting TB treatment. Acute kidney injury was evaluated according to the Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss of kidney function, and End-stage kidney disease (RIFLE) criteria [15]. Hepatotoxicity was evaluated according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [16].

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate differences between the two groups, Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test was used to compare continuous variables while χ2 test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. The probability and the risk difference of primary outcome was compared between the two groups using a multivariable logistic regression with overlap weighting to adjust for baseline imbalance by transplant status [17]. Propensity score for overlap weighting was calculated from a multiple regression model with SOT as a dependent variable and age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), previous history of TB, presence of cavitary lesions, smear positivity, extrapulmonary involvement, use of ≥ 3 active anti-TB agents, and treatment duration as covariates. Absolute standardized mean difference <0.1 was set as a threshold to evaluate adequate balancing.

All tests were two-tailed with P < .05 as a threshold for statistical significance. R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) were used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

From January 2004 to December 2023, a total of 52 SOTRs were diagnosed with culture-positive pulmonary TB and treated with rifabutin. Among them, one patient with isolated rifampin-resistant M tuberculosis, one patient who was treated for less than 6 months while alive, and 10 patients who failed to receive isoniazid or rifampin for >20% of the total treatment duration were excluded. Finally, 40 patients were included for the SOTR group. They were matched at a 1:3 ratio with non-SOTR people with TB (n = 120) who were treated with rifampin-based regimens.

Table 1 shows baseline characteristics of the SOTR group treated with rifabutin and the non-SOTR group treated with rifampin. There was no significant difference in age or sex between the two groups, although the median CCI of the SOTR group was significantly higher than that of the non-SOTR group (median CCI: 5 with interquartile range (IQR) of 4–6 vs 3 with IQR of 1–4, P < .001). In terms of TB-related characteristics, there was no significant difference in the presence of cavitary lesions, initial smear-positivity, culture-positivity after 2 months of treatment, or previous history of TB between the two groups. However, because treatment for latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) was indicated for SOTRs, patients treated previously for LTBI were only found in the SOTR group (10% vs 0%, P = .003). There were significantly more patients with extrapulmonary involvement in the SOTR group than in the non-SOTR group (22.5% vs 5.8%, P = .005). Isoniazid-resistant M tuberculosis was found only in the SOTR group (10% vs 0%, P = .003). The most common initial treatment regimen in both groups consisted of rifamycin with isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide. It was used in 82.5% of patients in the SOTR group and 95.0% of patients in the non-SOTR group. Regimens containing three or more active agents were significantly more common in the non-SOTR group than in the SOTR group (95.8% vs 90.0%, P = .003). The median interval between transplantation and TB diagnosis was 391 days (IQR 160–1375 days). Median treatment duration was longer in the SOTR group than in the non-SOTR group: 272 (IQR, 186–298) days versus 187 (IQR, 181–220) days (P < .001).

Treatment outcomes and adverse events of each group are shown in Table 3. The primary outcome (survival treatment completion without early relapse) occurred in 90.0% of patients in the SOTR group and 96.7% of patients in the non-SOTR group, showing no significant difference between the two groups (P = .108). The adjusted model also demonstrated consistent results (adjusted odds ratio: 0.36; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.01–10.41; adjusted risk difference: −5.0%; 95% CI: −17.2% to 5.6%, Table 4). The weighted population was balanced adequately according to the preset threshold (Supplementary Figure 1). When analyzing composite outcome components separately, the treatment completion rate was higher in the non-SOTR group than in the SOTR group (100% vs 92.5%, P = .015). However, there was no significant difference in recurrence rate within 1 year (2.5% vs 3.3%, P = .633). Among secondary outcomes, all-cause mortality was higher in the SOTR group (10.0% vs 2.5%, P = .066), although the difference did not reach statistical significance. There was no TB-attributable mortality in either group.

Grade 3 or higher acute kidney injury and hepatotoxicity were significantly more common in the SOTR group than in the non-SOTR group (12.5% vs 0% and 12.5% vs 1.7% for each). Patients with grade 3 or higher hepatotoxicity in the SOTR group were mostly liver transplant recipients, and all patients with hyperbilirubinemia were liver transplant recipients. The average rifabutin treatment duration in the SOTR group was 8 months. A total of seven patients needed to discontinue rifabutin prematurely (four liver transplant recipients and three kidney transplant recipients). The most common reason for discontinuation was thrombocytopenia, which occurred in 4 patients. One patient discontinued rifabutin due to a decrease in voriconazole levels, another due to cytopenia, and one due to edema. None of the patients treated with rifabutin developed severe leukopenia or neutropenia that required drug cessation or G-CSF treatment. Among our initial screening cohort of 52 patients, 11.5% discontinued rifabutin early (before reaching 20% of the total treatment period) and were excluded from the study (Supplementary Table 1). When patients were compared based on the primary outcome, no significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the two groups (Supplementary Table 2).

In terms of outcomes related to transplanted organs, 10% of patients encountered allograft rejections requiring anti-rejection therapy and 12.5% of patients experienced allograft failure. Immunosuppressants were changed in 42.5% of patients during TB treatment, with antimetabolite being discontinued in 42.5% of cases. A total of 24 (60%) patients had significant changes in serum concentrations of immunosuppressants during TB treatment. Of these, 19 patients experienced decreased drug concentrations, most commonly with tacrolimus. Among the four patients who experienced acute rejection, three experienced changes in immunosuppressant concentrations. The incidence of CMV syndrome/disease after TB diagnosis was 7.5%.

DISCUSSION

This study found that SOTRs treated with rifabutin-based regimens for pulmonary TB had similar treatment outcomes to non-SOTRs treated with standard rifampin-based regimen. Both groups showed high cure rates of over 90%. Hepatotoxicity and renal toxicity were more frequent in SOTRs than in non-SOTRs. However, there was no significant difference in TB-attributable mortality, suggesting that treatment with rifabutin-based regimens was relatively safe.

Few studies have focused on rifabutin for TB treatment in SOTR. Most studies reported previously included a small number of patients. A 2021 retrospective observational study has compared rifabutin (n = 12), rifampin (n = 14), and nonrifamycin (n = 5) regimens and found no significant differences in clinical cure rates, side effects, or mortality [10]. A literature review has identified 59 SOTRs treated for TB with rifabutin-containing regimens and found a clinical cure rate of 92.3%, a graft rejection rate of 5.1%, and a mortality of 6.8% [11]. In another study performed in 2021, rifabutin (n = 12) and rifampin (n = 10) were compared among living donor liver transplantation recipients [13]. While recurrence of TB and hematologic abnormalities did not occur in either group, the rifabutin-based regimen showed a significantly smaller percentage of those with reductions in serum sirolimus and tacrolimus concentrations compared to the rifampin-based regimen (25% vs 62% from baseline, P = .009). The American Society of Transplantation Guidelines strongly recommend the use of rifamycin but stops at stating that “rifabutin is typically substituted for rifampin” on the recommendation of a specific agent probably due to the lack of clinical evidence to support the use of rifabutin [9]. On the contrary, the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases Study Group suggested avoiding the use of rifamycin for localized, nonsevere forms of TB [18]. However, “non-severe” TB was not defined in this consensus manuscript, and the authors acknowledged that rifamycin-sparing regimens may be associated with a higher risk of recurrence and resistance. Furthermore, rifamycin-sparing regimens need a significantly longer treatment of 12–18 months. In this context, our study provides important evidence for the use of rifabutin in place of rifampin in SOTRs. In addition, studies in immunocompromised patients treated with rifampin-sparing regimens reported favorable responses only in approximately 80% of cases, mostly in younger patients with fewer comorbidities, although the median treatment duration was longer than standard therapy (10.2 vs 6 months), highlighting the potential tradeoff between drug sparing and extended treatment [19].

Mismatches of disease burden and resources by country are the most critical hurdle to a clinical study on rifabutin for SOTR. The price of rifabutin is substantially higher than that of rifampin, which might hinder its use in low-to-middle income countries where the burden of TB is high enough to conduct studies focusing on SOTRs [20]. South Korea has a higher prevalence of TB than other countries with similar socioeconomic status. In 2022, the TB incidence rate in South Korea was 39 per 100 000 population, ranking 107th among 219 countries but second among Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development member countries [21, 22]. At the same time, the volume of SOT is very high with 405 deceased donor organ transplants and 2468 living organ transplants in 2022 [23]. The unique situation of South Korea, a high-income country with an intermediate burden of TB, enabled us to conduct this study in a single-center setting.

In this study, the incidence of acute rejection during TB treatment or within 6 months of diagnosis was approximately 10%, which was comparable to rates reported in previous studies (6%–25%) [24–26], with a similar range observed even in studies exclusively involving rifampin. Rifamycin can induce activities of CYP enzymes, which can increase clearance of prednisolone and reduce levels of calcineurin inhibitors, resulting in an increased risk of acute allograft rejection [27]. TB-associated immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome can also increase the risk of rejection [28]. All four patients who experienced acute rejection during TB treatment in this study were on tacrolimus and steroids. This was a relatively small proportion compared to the 24 out of 40 patients who experienced decrease in serum immunosuppressant concentration during treatment, suggesting that appropriate control could have been achieved through therapeutic drug monitoring during the use of rifabutin. One kidney transplant recipient developed allograft failure at 3 months after finishing TB treatment, while one liver transplant recipient developed acute rejection at 3 months after TB diagnosis and died. Despite the small number of patients, our study reaffirmed the significant indirect consequence of TB in SOTRs despite a high cure rate of TB itself.

The total treatment duration of the SOTR group (median 272 days) was longer than the non-SOTR group (median 187 days). This result is consistent with findings from other studies: the mean treatment duration of the 52 SOTR cohorts combined was 10.54 months, which was longer than the typical 6 months [1, 2], suggesting that the observed effects are attributable to the characteristics of SOTR rather than the specific type of rifamycin used. Extended treatment durations are commonly adopted by physicians treating immunocompromised patients due to concerns about treatment failure and relapse. In our study, only four SOTR participants completed treatment within 6 months, rendering subgroup analysis impossible. Although our patient population did not permit a direct comparison between rifabutin- and rifampin-containing regimens for 6 months, this scenario accurately reflects clinical practice, where extended regimens are typical for transplant recipients irrespective of the specific drug combinations used. Consequently, the differences in treatment duration between SOTR and non-SOTR groups observed in our study are unlikely to diminish the relevance of the comparative outcomes. Future research is necessary to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of shortening the treatment duration to 6 months in SOTR populations. In addition, in our initial screening cohort, 11.5% of patients discontinued rifabutin prematurely. For comparison, a study in Korea evaluating non-MDR pulmonary TB patients reported that only 2.3% discontinued rifampin due to adverse effects, with the main reasons being hepatotoxicity (59.6%), skin rash and/or pruritis (54.4%), and gastrointestinal discomfort (50.9%) [19]. Previous Korean studies in nontransplant patients with rifabutin-sensitive MDR-TB reported discontinuation rates ranging from 0% to 19% [29, 30]. Although direct comparison is limited by differences in study populations, rifabutin discontinuation in SOTRs appears higher than that of rifampin but is generally comparable to the rates reported in other nontransplant populations treated with rifabutin.

Our study is the first to compare a rifabutin-containing regimen for treating TB in SOTR with a rifampin-containing regimen. Unlike previous studies, we compared treatment outcomes to non-SOTRs with normal immunity (which could be considered the best scenario) and still demonstrated comparable outcomes. To adjust for baseline differences by transplant status, we applied a robust statistical analysis using propensity score-based weighting. Another strength of this study was that we included a variety of SOTR-specific outcomes, such as outcome of allograft, changes in immunosuppressants, and occurrence of CMV diseases.

This study also has several limitations. First, comparing SOTRs treated with rifabutin-containing regimens to non-SOTRs introduced significant differences between the two groups. Our institutional practice rarely uses rifampin for SOTRs, preventing a direct comparison of both rifamycins within this population. Comparison with transplant recipients who did not receive any rifamycin was also not possible due to this practice pattern and the retrospective nature of the study. Although these differences in patient populations do not diminish the clinical implications that rifabutin can substitute for rifampin in SOTRs, the fundamental difference in baseline characteristics must be acknowledged. The similar outcomes in SOTRs treated with rifabutin, despite these differences, suggest its potential as a substitute. However, the lack of studies using both rifamycins in SOTRs underscores the need for more robust evidence. Second, it had a retrospective study design with an inherent risk of potential bias. While we used a regression model to adjust for baseline imbalance, the possibility of residual bias by unobserved variables could not be excluded. Third, the number of patients included in this study was relatively small. Nevertheless, we sought to address this limitation by implementing a 1:3 patient matching strategy. A larger, multicenter study that includes a significantly larger number of patients is needed in the future. Last, we did not examine the effects of rifabutin on serum concentrations of immunosuppressants. Thus, caution should be exercised when using rifabutin and drug concentrations should be closely monitored in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

The use of rifabutin in place of rifampin for treating pulmonary TB in SOTR patients has comparable treatment outcomes to rifampin-based therapy in a non-SOTR group. Our results provide evidence for the use of rifabutin in this setting, which has been a common practice despite its insufficient clinical evidence.
