Incidence and related risk factors of post-stroke fatigue in patients with hemorrhagic stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhiyu He, Li Zhong, Yingxian Chen, Yanhong Peng, Zhimin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke fatigue (PSF) is a long-lasting sequela in patients with hemorrhagic stroke, substantially hindering neurological recovery and social integration. The complex and multifactorial nature of PSF complicates its clinical management, highlighting the need for systematic studies to elucidate its risk factors and facilitate the development of targeted interventions.AimTo evaluate the incidence and developmental trends of PSF and analyze the related risk factors, we conducted a meta-analysis.MethodsThe databases Web of Science, MEDLINE, PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, and SinoMed were systematically searched for publications from inception to February 2025. Two reviewers independently screened the literature, assessed quality, and extracted data. The PSF incidence was performed using Stata 18.0, risk factor analysis was determined using RevMan 5.4.ResultsSixteen studies involving 1,997 patients with hemorrhagic stroke were included ultimately. The incidence of PSF was 51%. Subgroup analyses revealed regional and clinical differences between groups. Marital status(r = 0.24, p < 0.001), sleep disturbance(r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and postoperative complications(r = 0.26, p < 0.001) were weakly correlated with PSF. Moderate correlations were identified for the modified Rankin Scale score(r = 0.37, p < 0.001), Hunt–Hess grade(r = 0.36, p < 0.001), anxiety(r = 0.35, p = 0.001), and social support(r = 0.31, p < 0.001), whereas depression(r = 0.63, p = 0.008) emerged with a strong correlation.ConclusionNearly half of patients with hemorrhagic stroke were susceptible to PSF. Variations in the reported PSF incidence across studies may be attributed to the area of onset, study type, disease type, and time of assessment. The early identification of PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke is essential for implementing timely and effective interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04724-9.Keywords: Systematic review, Hemorrhagic stroke, Post-stroke fatigue, Risk factors, Meta-analysis

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease data, stroke, comprising two principal pathological types (hemorrhagic and ischemic), is the third leading cause of mortality globally [1], and the disability-adjusted life years, number of deaths, and associated costs attributable to stroke will nearly double from 2020 to 2050 [2]. Hemorrhagic stroke is a pathological condition characterized by cerebral parenchymal hemorrhage and non-traumatic vascular rupture, encompassing intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) [3], and accounts for over 30% of all stroke cases and contributes to over 40% of the global mortality associated with stroke [4]. Approximately 80% of survivors experience severe neurological deficits [5]. The clinical presentation and prognosis of ICH are not only determined by the initial site of bleeding, with a poorer early prognosis in lobar ICH, but are also significantly worsened by the extension of the hemorrhage into the ventricular system, which markedly increases in-hospital mortality [6, 7]. Moreover, hemorrhagic stroke may lead to sequelae, including sleep disorders, limb movement dysfunction, dysphagia, dysregulation of blood lipids, hypertension, and glucose level [8], which substantially impair patient health and impose a socioeconomic burden.

Post-stroke fatigue (PSF) is a multidimensional experience encompassing motor-sensory, emotional, and cognitive domains, characterized by the onset of early fatigue during physical or mental activities, accompanied by symptoms such as weariness, lack of energy, and aversion to exertion, with the condition typically remaining unrelieved by rest [9]. It is a common and long-lasting sequela, reported to be present in 66% of patients with hemorrhagic stroke, nearly double that of ischemic stroke [10]. Considering that a generally objective measurement of fatigue is lacking, structured questionnaires such as the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) [11] are commonly used to measure PSF in patients with stroke [12]. Accumulating evidence indicates that PSF is associated with a decreased probability of returning to work [13]. Several patients still experience problems in resuming previous activities in the chronic phase of hemorrhagic stroke, influenced by current work status and fatigue [14], and PSF could independently predicts reduced short-term survival and poorer functional outcomes after stroke [15]. Medical personnel need to identify the risk factors of PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. However, PSF has a complex multifactorial etiology, and its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. Survivors of hemorrhagic stroke may experience a higher incidence of PSF than those with other stroke subtypes. Furthermore, predictors of PSF appeared to differ from those associated with ischemic stroke [16]. Numerous factors have been verified in the development of PSF, including demographic characteristics (e.g., sex [17] and educational level [18]), clinical factors (e.g., smoking status [19] and stroke severity as evaluated by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [20]), cognitive function [21], and psychosocial elements, such as coping strategies [22]. Survivors of hemorrhagic stroke experiencing PSF demonstrate heightened susceptibility to comorbid neuropsychological symptoms, particularly anxiety and depression [23].

The consistency of the identified risk factors across diverse study methodologies remains variable, systematic reviews specifically focusing on patients who have experienced hemorrhagic stroke are lacking. Hence, we conducted a systematic study with the objective of investigating the incidence of PSF among patients with hemorrhagic stroke and evaluating the associations between risk factors and PSF, which provides critical evidence-based guidance for standardizing the clinical management of hemorrhagic stroke survivors and enhancing their quality of life.

Methods

This review was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24], and it was registered on the international registration platform PROSPERO (CRD420251026797).

Data sources and search strategy

Two reviewers systematically conducted a computerized search of 12 databases from their inception to February 28, 2025. The English databases included the Web of Science, MEDLINE, PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and PsycINFO. The Chinese databases included CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, and SinoMed. A comprehensive and systematic complementary search was performed for references cited in the literature that had been included in this study. The full search strategy can be found in Online Supplementary Materials 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for our study were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with ICH or SAH confirmed by head computed tomography; (2)PSF measured by using a structured questionnaire at any time post stroke; (3) the research content included the incidence of PSF and the risk factors, influencing factors, or predictive factors of PSF; (4) the language was English or Chinese, and the study design was a cross-sectional, case-control or cohort study; and (5) the study explicitly reported correlation coefficients or transformable raw data. Our exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the full text was not attainable; (2) duplicate publications; (3) literature of low quality; and (4) conference abstracts, case reports, reviews, and other irrelevant categories of scholarly publications.

Study screening and data extraction

Following the removal of duplicates using the EndNote X9 software, two reviewers independently conducted a systematic screening of the literature by reading each article’s title, abstract, and full text content, strictly adhering to the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All exclusions that occurred during this stage were meticulously documented and subsequently illustrated in the PRISMA flow chart. When the two reviewers were unable to reach a consensus, a third reviewer was promptly invited to join the discussion and decide whether the article should be incorporated into the study. The following data were extracted: authors, publication year, study type, country, measurement, enrollment year, sample size, incidence, and risk factors of PSF.

Quality assessment

Two trained reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias of the included studies. Should any disagreements arise, a third reviewer would be consulted to reconcile the differences. The case–control and cohort study was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [25], which measures three main domains: selection of participants (0–4 points), comparability between groups (0–2 points), and outcome assessment (0–3 points). In terms of quality classification, a score of 5 or below is regarded as low quality, a score between 6 and 7 is considered moderate quality, and a score of 8 to 9 is classified as high quality [26]. For the cross-sectional study, quality was appraised based on the criteria recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, comprising 11 items rated as “yes” (1 point), “no”, or “unclear” (0 point) [27]. Literature scoring 3 or below was classified as low quality, those scoring from 4 to 7 as moderate quality, and those scoring 8 or above as high quality [26].

Statistical analysis

A single-group rate meta-analysis was systematically conducted using Stata 18.0, to explore the PSF incidence in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. The pooled estimate of the PSF incidence rate was derived using a log-transformation approach. In the study on the association between related risk factors and PSF, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used as the index of effect size, and this meta-analysis only included the factors mentioned in at least two studies. Correlation coefficients or data that could be approximately converted into correlation coefficients were recorded [28]. Fisher’s z and standard error were input into the Review Manager 5.4 software to perform a comprehensive meta-analysis after Fisher’s r-to-z transformation using this formula. The summary effect and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were converted back to summary r for interpretation [29]. Cohen proposed the following criteria for judging the effect size of the correlation coefficient: <0.3, 0.3 ~ 0.5, and > 0.5 were identified as weak, moderate, and strong correlations [30].

The I2 statistic was used to analyze the heterogeneity among the included studies. If I2 < 50% and p ≥ 0.10, the heterogeneity was considered low, and the fixed-effect model was used for analysis. Conversely, the random-effect model was used. When heterogeneity was high, subgroup analysis was performed to explore the source of heterogeneity according to the literature and patient characteristics. Egger’s test was performed using Stata to determine potential publication bias, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sensitivity was conducted to ascertain the robustness and stability of the pooled results using the leave-one-out analysis.

Results

Search results and selection

Overall, we retrieved 1405 studies from 12 databases, of which 607 were excluded because of duplication. After preliminary screening of title and abstract, 262 studies were selected. After further reading the full text, 13 studies were included, and 3 studies were identified by reviewing the references of the included studies (depicted in Fig. 1). Consequently, 10 studies that reported both the incidence and risk factors of PSF, four studies that reported incidence, and two studies that reported risk factors were ultimately encompassed.

Meta-analysis

PSF incidence in patients with hemorrhagic stroke

When analyzing the incidence of PSF, only baseline incidence data were used for cohort studies that reported changes in incidence across multiple time periods. The random-effects model was used for meta-analysis (Fig. 2) because the outcome of the heterogeneity test indicated notable heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 90.5%, p < 0.001). The incidence of PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke ranged from 33% to 71%, and the overall pooled incidence of PSF was 51% (95% CI: 44%–59%).

Forest map of PSF incidence in patients with hemorrhagic stroke

Subgroup analysis of PSF incidence

We conducted subgroup analyses based on the area of onset, study type, disease type, and time of assessment to account for heterogeneity among the studies in Table 2. Considering the area of onset, the incidence of PSF in Europe was 54% (95% CI: 45%–62%), which was higher than that in Asia (42%; 95% CI: 31%–54%) (p < 0.001). Moreover, patients with ICH had an incidence of 42% (95% CI: 25%–60%), whereas those with SAH had an incidence of 52% (95% CI: 44%–61%). Furthermore, regarding the time of assessment, patients evaluated at 6 months and 3 years after onset had the lowest incidence of 42% and 47%, respectively, whereas those assessed at 1 year after onset were 58%. Additionally, no significant differences between cross-sectional and cohort studies were observed (50% vs. 52%). Forest maps of the subgroup analysis are shown in Online Supplementary Material 2.

The related risk factors of PSF

As shown in Table 3, the meta-analysis indicated that eight risk factors were considered, and these factors were included in two or more studies that provided relevant data. Single/divorced status (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), sleep disturbance (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and postoperative complications (r = 0.26, p < 0.001) were identified as risk factors for PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke with weak correlations. The mRS score (r = 0.37, p < 0.001), Hunt–Hess grade (r = 0.36, p < 0.001), anxiety (r = 0.35, p = 0.001), and low/general social support (r = 0.31, p < 0.001) were moderately correlated with PSF. Particularly, depression (r = 0.63, p = 0.008) was strongly correlated with PSF.

Descriptive analysis of the related risk factors of PSF

We performed a narrative review and analysis of 12 studies that focused on PSF and identified 12 risk factors. Educational level, weight, HRQoL [17], smoking, GCS at admission [19], cortical atrophy [39], cognitive function [20], serum cystatin C [35], coping strategy [21], functional outcome [22], knee extension and flexion abilities [34], and the Rs110402 genotype [19] were associated with PSF in patients diagnosed with hemorrhagic stroke. All the aforementioned factors were reported in a single study, and data to conduct an integrated analysis of the effect size were lacking. However, these findings require further validation through additional research.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis of PSF incidence demonstrated consistent and robust findings, as evidenced by the fact that the aggregated effect size exhibited stability when each study was excluded by iteratively removing one study at a time (Fig. 3). The funnel plot revealed an asymmetrical distribution of scatter points on both sides, suggesting the possibility of publication bias (Fig. 4). Egger’s test (t = − 1.34, p = 0.205) demonstrated that the likelihood of potential publication bias was small. Moreover, publication bias was not conducted because the number of included studies for all risk factors was less than 10.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the PSF incidence and its associated risk factors specifically in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. The pooled incidence of PSF was 51%. Nearly half of all patients with hemorrhagic stroke are at risk of developing PSF, rendering this population particularly vulnerable and necessitating targeted clinical attention. It is recommended that fatigue screening be integrated into routine follow-up protocols, and that personalized, multidimensional management strategies—such as energy conservation, rehabilitation training, and psychological support—be implemented to effectively alleviate fatigue symptoms, ultimately improving long-term rehabilitation outcomes.

The significant heterogeneity in PSF incidence underscores the need for further investigation

Subgroup analyses revealed variations in the incidence of PSF across regions and disease types. A higher incidence has been observed in European populations than in Asian populations, a disparity potentially attributable to geographical, environmental, and cultural factors. A study has suggested that the lower incidence in Asian populations may stem from distinct psychosocial influences shaped by prevalent cultural differences [43]. Moreover, patients with SAH exhibit a higher susceptibility to PSF than those with ICH, attributable to a higher prevalence of associated neuropsychological disorders (e.g., depression and anxiety), and prolonged physical deconditioning due to extended bed rest. However, this finding may be influenced by a potential selection bias owing to the limited number of included studies focusing specifically on ICH (n = 2) compared with SAH (n = 12). Additional high-quality studies focusing on the PSF in patients with ICH are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these observed differences. Furthermore, the incidence of PSF initially increasing before subsequently declining over time, in contrast to findings reported in prior studies [10, 14]. Analysis of individual longitudinal studies revealed conflicting results; two studies have investigated a progressive increase in PSF incidence over time [36, 38], whereas one study observed a declining trend [17]. All three studies were limited by small sample sizes, potentially increasing the susceptibility to the influence of high inter-individual variability and random chance. In addition, the PSF incidence did not significantly differ between the cross-sectional and cohort studies, which may be related to the prolonged assessment period inherent to cross-sectional designs. Although cross-sectional studies are designed for single-time-point data collection, their practical implementation often involves procedures, such as participant recruitment and data collection spanning months or longer [19, 21, 31]. This extended timeframe potentially allowed some participants to transition naturally from the acute phase to the recovery phase, resulting in dynamic changes in fatigue status during the assessment window.

Multidimensional determinants indicates an integrated clinical management strategy for PSF

This study demonstrates that PSF is significantly associated with a range of sociodemographic, clinical, and psychological variables. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of PSF and support the necessity for an integrated, multidimensional management approach that simultaneously addresses biological, functional, and psychosocial domains to effectively mitigate fatigue and improve long-term patient outcomes. Analysis of factors influencing PSF revealed statistically significant associations with the following variables.

Sociodemographic factors

A meta-analysis revealed a weak association between marital status (unmarried/divorced) and the development of PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. Spouses can provide adequate care and companionship with the positive psychological effects of a supportive marital relationship, potentially contributing to reduced fatigue [44]. Clinical practice should incorporate the assessment of marital status. For divorced or unmarried individuals, healthcare providers should encourage family members to offer enhanced understanding and support to mitigate negative psychological symptoms. Additionally, implementing structured post-discharge community support and nursing care is essential to minimize fatigue severity, thereby enhancing patients’ quality of life outcomes.

Disease-related factors

Our analysis indicated a moderate association between the mRS scores and PSF, potentially mediated by neurological deficits [37]. The mRS serves as a core indicator of functional independence and neurological recovery post-stroke, and elevated scores reflect increasing impairment across the motor, cognitive, and linguistic domains. Persistent neurological deficits may indirectly exacerbate fatigue by adversely affecting physiological function, psychological state, and social participation. A moderate association was also observed between Hunt–Hess grade and PSF. The Hunt–Hess scale assesses the initial severity in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage; higher grades correlate with poorer post-operative physical recovery and increased susceptibility to physical fatigue [39]. Furthermore, the patients who experienced postoperative complications were more likely to develop PSF. Complications impede rehabilitation progress, leading to a diminished quality of life [40] and impose a significant psychological burden, fostering negative emotional states that contribute to both mental and physical fatigue. Finally, poor sleep quality was weakly associated with PSF. Patients with hemorrhagic stroke often experience elevated inflammatory markers and dysregulated neurotransmitter levels, predisposing them to insomnia exacerbated by disrupted sleep-wake cycles [45]. Moreover, severe obstructive sleep apnea in stroke patients may influence inflammatory pathways. Sleep-related breathing disorders (SRBDs) also exert multifaceted impacts on the cerebrovascular system, including potentially altering cerebral reactivity and vascular regulation during postural changes, which can ultimately disrupt normal cerebral blood flow control [46]. Normal sleep architecture is crucial for maintaining physiological homeostasis, and sleep disturbances can consequently induce endocrine dysregulation, impaired immune function, and compromised mental well-being, collectively manifesting as fatigue. The precise nature of the relationship between SRBDs and PSF, along with its multifactorial pathways, remains to be fully elucidated, warranting further investigation.

Psychosocial factors

Patients with anxiety or depression are more susceptible to PSF. Although fatigue and depression share overlapping symptoms such as lethargy and impaired concentration, they represent distinct clinical entities [47]. PSF can persist even after accounting for depressive symptoms, suggesting that these sequelae should be considered independent targets during stroke rehabilitation [48]. A bidirectional relationship exists between depression and fatigue; depression increases susceptibility to fatigue, whereas fatigue exacerbates depressive symptoms, creating a detrimental cycle. One study identified a significant association between anxiety and PSF; however, this association markedly diminished after controlling for depressive symptoms, implying that anxiety may indirectly influence PSF development, primarily through the induction or exacerbation of depression [49]. Furthermore, low or moderate social support was moderately associated with the PSF scores. Inadequate social support diminishes psychological resilience and increases vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and internal psychological conflict, ultimately contributing to fatigue. Healthcare providers should implement regular psychological screening for patients with stroke, provide timely psychological support to mitigate negative emotional states, and prevent the exacerbation of fatigue, which could impede functional recovery.

Limitations

We systematically searched and screened multiple databases to ensure the comprehensiveness and diversity of data. Literature screening and quality assessment were rigorously conducted according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. We synthesized the extracted data using multiple analytical methods to enhance the validity and reliability of the findings. However, this study has some limitations. First, despite performing subgroup analyses for PSF incidence, significant unexplained heterogeneity persisted, potentially attributable to variations in sample size, ethnicity, and assessment timing across studies. Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting the meta-analysis results. And the available data from Asian regions predominantly originate from China, with a notable scarcity of relevant studies from other countries, which may compromise the representativeness of the sample. Second, the odds ratios for risk factors could not be effectively combined and analyzed because of differences in the statistical methodologies used in the included studies. To maintain the scientificity and accuracy of the study, Fisher’s z-transformation was conducted. Although our study have identified certain factors associated with PSF, we have not yet been able to establish definitive causal relationships, which presents certain challenges for gaining a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of PSF. Third, the numerous factors can influence PSF in patients with hemorrhagic stroke, the common factors involved in more than two studies are relatively few. This may introduce bias into the meta-analysis results. Further investigation is therefore needed to verify the role of demographic factors. Fourth, most of the included studies predominantly involved patients with SAH. Given that different types of stroke may influence both the mechanisms and manifestations of PSF, future research should further differentiate and examine the associations between various subtypes of hemorrhagic stroke to enhance the clinical applicability and accuracy of the findings. Finally, although the literature was comprehensively searched and references were scanned to minimize potential bias, as in other review studies, the complete exclusion of residual bias remains challenging.

Conclusion

Post-stroke fatigue is a prevalent and persistent sequela in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. Our findings indicate that early risk assessment and the establishment of an interdisciplinary collaborative framework—involving psychiatry, rehabilitation, and related clinical specialties—can help reduce the incidence and severity of PSF, thereby accelerating functional recovery, enhancing the quality of life, and improving overall rehabilitation outcomes. Future research should focus on investigating the multidimensional influencing factors of PSF, establishing objective and accurate assessment systems, and developing systematic rehabilitation management pathways along with risk prediction models. These efforts will contribute to the optimization of clinical practice and the advancement of personalized interventions.
