Effect of educational intervention on physicians and researchers’ knowledge, practices and perceptions towards predatory journals: an interventional study
Authors: Sumayyia D Marar, Amani Abu-Shaheen, Mohsen Ayyash, Alaa Ashraf AlQurashi, Ahmed Hamza, Muaawia A Hamza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the current scholarly landscape, predatory journals have increasingly emerged along with open-access journals. These journals aim for profit rather than trusted science. Therefore, the main purpose of the current study is to assess the impact of an educational intervention on physicians' and researchers' knowledge, practices, and perceptions regarding predatory journals.MethodsThis is an interventional, randomized, pretest–posttest, control group study. Physicians and researchers from King Fahad Medical City were randomly assigned either to an intervention group (received educational training) or a control group without training. A structured questionnaire was developed and validated to assess knowledge, practices, and perceptions toward predatory journals was used.ResultsA total of 304 participants enrolled in the study at baseline were allocated equally to intervention and control groups (n = 152, each). The distributions of baseline characteristics were similar in both groups. Post-intervention, participants in the intervention group showed significantly higher adjusted mean knowledge scores compared to the control group (10.26 ± 2.9 vs. 7.12 ± 4.1; p < 0.001) with moderate effect size; = 0.095. Post-intervention practice scores were also significantly higher in the intervention group (45.81 ± 10.3) than in the control group (40.61 ± 12.1; p = 0.001), although the effect size was small (ƞₚ2 = 0.0048). Perception scores showed no significant difference between groups post-intervention (32.83 ± 3.9 vs. 33.55 ± 3.8; p = 0.316; ƞₚ2 = 0.005).ConclusionsThe educational intervention significantly improved participants’ knowledge and practices but not their perceptions. Moreover, the post-intervention knowledge and practice scores reflected a moderate to small effect size, suggesting a room for further enhancement through sustained educational efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08944-y.Keywords: Predatory journals, Knowledge, Practices, Perceptions, Educational intervention, Pretest–posttest, King Fahad Medical City

Introduction

Medical researchers publish their study findings for various reasons such as sharing knowledge, advancing career opportunities, achieving academic qualifications, influencing healthcare policies, promoting evidence-based medicine, and promoting further research. These publications are made in peer-reviewed journals where the validity, methodology, and results of the studies are critically appraised by experts. This process helps prevent the publication of falsified information [15]. Therefore, publication in these journals is considered the “gold standard” for communicating research findings. Consequently, researchers should select an appropriate peer-reviewed journal to publish their research findings. Currently, the publication of peer-reviewed journal articles has undergone a significant revolution by supporting open-access publications, and the possibility of low-cost electronic publications. However, the concept of open-access publication has been exploited by predatory journals, which lack legitimate foundations, basic industry standards, and sound peer-review processes, but present a seemingly legitimate face [6–8]. Predatory journals exploit authors’ pressure to publish and readers’ desire for access, luring them for financial rather than scientific gain while compromising the quality of scientific information which is very harmful to the whole community as well. These predatory journals have rapidly increased the volume of their publications over time, from approximately 53,000 articles in 2010 to 420,000 articles in 2014 [25]. In Middle Eastern countries, there is a large body of literature that shows that prospective authors are commonly invited to publish in predatory journals [5, 16, 19, 21, 29].

Efforts to expose predatory journals have led to the development of whitelists (trustworthy journals) and blacklists (predatory journals) such as Beall’s list, which is a blacklist that contains publishers and journals that meet the criteria of a predatory journal and highlights how to identify such journals; the list was forcibly removed from the internet [29]. Other efforts include publications in Science [5] exposing compromised peer-review processes in these journals and expository articles in the New York Times [16], Nature [6, 7], and on publisher websites and various blogs [8]. However, despite these efforts, many prospective authors (including experienced editors and researchers) remain unaware of these predatory journals.

The definition of predatory journals continues to evolve. A consensus definition was proposed for predatory journals in 2019 as “entities that prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship and are characterized by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and/or the use of aggressive and indiscriminate solicitation practices” [14]. Moreover, new detection strategies have emerged to help researchers identify and avoid predatory journals such as Think.Check.Submit, and Cabells’ whitelists and blacklists [27].

Physicians and researchers are some of the potential authors targeted by predatory journals. Therefore, assessing the physician's and researchers' knowledge, practices, and perceptions toward predatory journals in medical institutions is the first step in upholding the validity, quality, and applicability of medical information reported by studies.

Despite the growing number of predatory publications nowadays, there is a dearth of research on this subject. Previous studies have examined authors' awareness of predatory journals; however, these studies included small sample sizes and therefore did not accurately demonstrate the awareness of predatory journals among the study populations [8, 21]. As a result, the present study aims to assess the impact of an educational intervention on physicians' and researchers' knowledge, practices, and perceptions regarding predatory journals.

Materials and methods

This is an interventional, randomized, pretest–posttest control group study. The study population consisted of physicians and researchers from King Fahad Medical City (KFMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All physicians and researchers working at KFMC were eligible to participate in this study. The active researchers were identified from the database of the KFMC Institutional Review Board, having published atleast one article in the past three years. Physicians were included as well because they read research papers and change their practices related to patient care based on these articles. Moreover, they regularly participate in journal clubs and need to ensure they choose the right journals and avoid predatory journals.

The study participants were randomly invited and assigned either to an intervention group that received an educational training program or a control group without training. Randomization was performed using computer-generated random numbers.

In the beginning, a pretest was performed for both groups using a structured questionnaire to assess their knowledge, practices, and perceptions related to predatory journals. Then, the participants in the intervention group received educational training regarding predatory journals. As this study was conducted during the COVID-19 restrictions, the training was provided through ZOOM. Once we collected 20–25 responses from the interventional group, we organized and sent invitations to the participants to attend the training session. We proposed different times to allow all participants to join based on their convenient time. The educational training included a recorded video lecture (23 min in length), followed by a panel discussion with the research team to answer the participants’ questions. Written materials (slides and reading resources) were also provided.

The video lecture and written materials covered topics and themes related to predatory journals such as definition, historical background and evolution, potential impact of publishing in predatory journals on the quality of scientific research and the reputation of both researchers and institutions, key characteristics and warning signs that distinguish predatory journals from reputable academic ones, practical tools and strategies to help participants verify the credibility of journals before submission, along with a discussion of the latest global and institutional efforts to combat predatory publishing such as characteristics of whitelists and blacklists. After the training session, a posttest was performed using the same questionnaire.

The questionnaire was developed in the English language based on previously published studies [4, 8, 25]. A group of ten academic and clinical researchers from different countries with expertise in research and publication reviewed and provided their comments and feedback on the face and content validity of the questionnaire. The content validity index was calculated for all items. The items with a content validity index < 0.80 were deleted. Of the original 59 questions and items, 3 were deleted and 10 were revised. Thus, the pre-final questionnaire consisted of 56 questions and items. Then, a pilot study was carried out among 40 participants to ensure the clarity and readability of the questionnaire and it was modified according to the participants' comments.

The final questionnaire consisted of five parts (see supplementary file). The first part included sociodemographic characteristics, number of publications, and training related to predatory journals. The remaining four parts assessed knowledge (16 items), practices (19 items which included 17 items using a four-point Likert scale and 2 items of multiple responses), and perceptions (10 items) regarding predatory journals.

The knowledge section included the following questions: what are predatory journals; have you ever heard of the blacklist that tackles predatory journals; have you ever heard of a whitelist that provides a list of the legitimate journals; and what suggested features would you look for to identify predatory journals. Responses to the knowledge questions were assessed using the options "yes", "no" and "not sure". For calculating participants’ knowledge scores, correct answers ("yes") were assigned a score of one, while incorrect answers ("no" or "not sure") or unanswered questions were given a score of zero. Four items were reverse-scored on the scale. Higher scores indicated a higher level of knowledge, with a maximum score of 16.

The practices component of the questionnaire consisted of four questions rated on a four-point Likert scale ("always", "often", "rarely", and "never"). The questions were: (1) have you ever submitted articles to suspected predatory journals; (2) do you have publications in suspected predatory journals; (3) have you ever accepted the invitation as a reviewer or editorial board member for what sounds to you like a predatory journal; and (4) have you ever checked the details of a target journal before submitting your manuscript (which consists of 12 items). The total four-point Likert scale contains 17 practice items (see Table 6 in the Appendix). The total practice score was calculated by summing all item scores, with a possible range from 17 to 68 points. Higher scores exhibit better practices while lower values show poorer practices. Moreover, the practice section consisted of 2 multiple-choice questions: why do you publish in predatory/fake journals? and what do you personally do when you get invited to publish, review or serve on the editorial board for what sounds to you like a predatory/fake journal?

The perceptions section consisted of 10 items measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree". Perception scores were summed to give a total score ranging from 10 to 50 points. Higher scores suggested high perceptions while lower values exhibit low perceptions.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at KFMC. The participants were recruited into the study voluntarily, and after they had read, understood, and signed written informed consent.

Sample size

Assuming an equal number of participants in the intervention and control groups, a sample size calculation was conducted to achieve 80% power at a 5% level of significance. The required sample size to detect a 60% increase in knowledge between the intervention and control groups was 152 subjects in each group, resulting in a total of 304 participants in this study. The prevalence of good knowledge was estimated from a pilot study where it was found to be 30%.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean and standard deviations for continuous variables and in terms of counts and percentages for categorical variables. The Chi-Square test was used to test the association between participants’ characteristics and groups. Wilcoxon Singed Ranked test was used to test pre-test and post-test differences in primary outcomes. Mann–Whitney test was used to test for differences in primary outcomes between the intervention and control groups. Unadjusted and adjusted Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was used to assess how the intervention group explained the variance in primary outcomes. Statistical significance was sought at values lower than 0.05.

Results

A total of 304 physicians and researchers from KFMC were enrolled in this study. Next, they were randomized with a 1:1 allocation ratio to an intervention group and control group with group size of 152 participants. The intervention group lost 13 (8.6%) participants and did not complete the questionnaire, leaving 139 participants for the post-intervention final analysis. The control group lost 9 (5.9%) participants after the intervention, leaving 143 participants for the final analysis of post-intervention responses. Knowledge of predatory journals

Regarding knowledge, after the intervention, participants in the intervention group showed significantly higher adjusted mean knowledge scores of 10.26 ± 2.9 compared to the control group (7.12 ± 4.1; p < 0.001). That is, after controlling for pretest differences, the intervention accounted for 9.5% of the residual variance (Effect size; = 0.095). We also compared the pre-test and post-test in each group using the Wilcoxon signed ranked test between pre- and post-intervention. The results of the Wilcoxon signed ranked test showed the difference in average total knowledge scores before and after the intervention was statistically significant in the intervention (5.32 3.8 versus 9.92 3.1, p < 0.001) and control group (5.42 3.9 versus 7.06 4.3, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Practices toward predatory journals

Regarding practices toward predatory journals, as shown in Table 2, post-intervention mean total practice scores were significantly improved whereby participants in the intervention group scored higher than those in the control group (45.50 10.4 versus 40.40 12.2, p = 0.004) as well as after adjusting for pre-intervention total practices score (45.81 10.3 versus 40.61 12.1, p = 0.001). Although the effect size was considerably small ( = 0.0048), indicating a modest magnitude of difference between the groups, the results suggest that the intervention program was found to be effective in practices towards predatory journals.

When comparing the mean total score of practices before and after intervention for each group separately using the Wilcoxon signed ranked test, the differences between pre-test and post-test mean total practices were statistically significant (intervention group: 39.5 11.0 versus 45.50 10.4; control group: 38.73 12.4 versus 40.40 12.2; p < 0.001). This indicates that the average total practice scores had significantly improved in both intervention and control groups.

As shown in Table 6 Appendix, at the disaggregated level, differences were statistically significant between both groups after the intervention for the following items: (1) check the "blacklist" of predatory Publisher and journals, (2) check if the publisher and journals are in the "whitelist”, (3) check if the journal clearly describes its copyright and licensing information on its website, (4) check if the publisher affiliation with a professional publishing organization, and (5) did you use any tool to identify the predatory journals? (p-values < 0.05).

Perceptions toward predatory journals

Regarding perceptions toward predatory journals, as shown in Table 2, the differences in total mean perception scores were not significant post-intervention (32.82 3.8 versus 33.53 3.7, p = 0.119) between both groups. The adjustment of pre-intervention perception scores did not seem to be significant and the differences in total perception scores remained insignificant between both groups (32.83 3.9 versus 33.55 3.8, p = 0.316). Therefore, the intervention program did not impact the perceptions of participants toward predatory journals.

Furthermore, when comparing mean total perception scores before and after intervention for each group individually using the Wilcoxon signed ranked test between pre-and post-intervention, the results suggested that there were no statistically significant differences between before and after intervention in either intervention (32.36 5.1 versus 32.82 3.8, p = 0.342) or control group (32.74 4.8 versus 33.5 3.7, p = 0.184). Discussion

Knowledge of predatory journals

At baseline, the findings of this study suggested low levels of knowledge among all participants regardless of which group they belonged to. Post-intervention knowledge levels had significantly improved in intervention and control groups but this increase was more pronounced in the intervention group. This indicates that the intervention contributed significantly to raising knowledge levels.

However, despite the intervention program having significantly improved participants’ knowledge of predatory journals, the level of knowledge is still less than expected. This is consistent with previous international studies which reported that authors are unaware of predatory journals [1, 8, 10, 11, 18, 21, 23, 24, 28]. For example, Kinde [1] indicated that a one-day training program on predatory journals had improved researchers’ knowledge, awareness, and ability to differentiate between predatory and legitimate journals [1]. Furthermore, Cohen et al. interviewed authors and editors of predatory journals and showed that knowledge of predatory journals and Beall’s list was higher among editors as compared to authors [11].). A potential explanations for low level of knowledge may be attributed to the fact that most respondents are engaged in clinical practice rather than academic institutions. Another study showed that university professors' levels of awareness and confidence in identifying predatory journals were higher than medical faculty [28].

Factors influencing knowledge

The ANCOVA results showed that the mean total knowledge score was significantly explained by group differences, and the effect size was increased to 9.5% after adjusting for pretest knowledge scores. However, total knowledge scores were not significantly related to participants’ demographics including age, gender, years of experience, professional occupation, workplace, and the number of publications either as co-authors or corresponding authors. This result is not congruent with the findings of Maurer et al. who reported higher awareness among corresponding authors, clinical directors and consultants in comparison to students, retired, residents, and private practices researchers [18]. However, our findings are similar to their results that age, gender, and professional activity were not significantly associated with knowledge [18]. On the other hand, Richtig et al. indicated that researchers in the field of dermatology with higher levels of education, work experience, and familiarity with predatory journals tend to have higher knowledge about predatory journals, which differs from our findings [23, 24].

Practices toward predatory journals

The practice scores after the intervention were found to be significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (p = 0.001), although the effect size was small (ƞₚ2 = 0.0048). Similarly, practice scores related to predatory journals were found to improve significantly (p < 0.001) in a previous educational interventional study conducted at King Hussein Cancer Center, Amman, Jordan [2].

When asked about the reasons to publish in predatory journals, the most frequent responses included were aspiration towards promotion, inadequate research skills, fear of job loss, and incentive allowance payment linked to the number of publications. Previous studies reported reasons such as lack of awareness that the journal was predatory, pressure to publish, lack of confidence in ability to publish in a high-quality journal, and lack of research experience [10, 17, 28, 30], which is similar to our findings. Furthermore, in Austria, Richtig et al. found that early-career dermatologists were at higher risk of being victims to predatory journals due to lack of knowledge and experience in peer-review publishing [23, 24]. Clark and Thompson also listed reasons why nursing authors publish their papers in predatory journals, including not caring about their external reputation, not believing in themselves or their work, publication numbers count most, cannot be bothered to read, and they have given up [9].

Response to predatory journal invitations

In the current study, participants reported deleting email invitations from predatory journals as the most frequent response to email invitations to review, publish, or serve on an editorial board. A few participants reported that they replied stating that they are a predatory journal and they do not want to receive any future invitations. Among other responses, they were never invited and ignored the email or moved it to spam, and very few participants stated that they checked the journal in the Web of Science database.

This study also showed that most post-intervention answers were not improved in both groups, indicating that the intervention program was not effective in changing the practices related to handling the invitation from predatory journals. Kinde showed that a one-day training program was effective in improving authors’ way of correctly differentiating between predatory and legitimate journals, [1]. Beall provided tips for avoiding predatory journals such as being cautious of unprofessional publisher’s websites or emails, and referring to Beall’s lists when in doubt. Since most invitation emails for paper submissions come from predatory journals, students and early career researchers should consult with mentors or senior colleagues before submitting their articles to an unknown publisher [3].

Perceptions toward predatory journals

The study showed that participants had somewhat moderate perceptions in both groups and the educational intervention program was not effective in improving post-intervention total perception scores. This can be due to a potential ceiling effect since baselines scores were already moderate. Moreover, previous studies suggest perceptions toward predatory journals can be shaped by institutional factors such as promotion requirements. For instance, a study in Turkey indicated that faculty members strongly felt the “publish or perish” pressure and had a tendency to publish in predatory journals to meet the criteria for academic promotions [20]. Swanberg et al. indicated that recognizing journals as predatory was as a major problem facing researchers [28]. Additionally, the removal of Beall’s List and potential inaccess to blacklisted journals may further lead to uncertainity in journal selection. In the context of Arab countries, where academic publishing pressures [13] and low awareness regarding predatory journals [2] persist, the risk of publishing in predatory journals may be higher. Our results showing improvement in knowledge and practices but unchanged perceptions further point towards this trend. Taken together, this indicates a need for long-term educational interventions as well as institutional support to change the perceptions towards predatory journals.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the current study among the first-ever research to examine the effect of an educational intervention on the researcher’s knowledge, practices, and perception of predatory journals in Saudi Arabia. Despite the important results revealed in the current study, it was subject to potential limitations. Firstly, the intervention was conducted during the COVID-19 lockdown, which restricted the face-to-face delivery of the educational intervention. Given the previous studies indicating the higher effectiveness of face-to-face teaching methods for skill development and social interaction [22, 26], this may have influenced the study findings especially the low impact of the intervention on perception. Moreover, the researchers were not able to follow up on the authors long-term because many of them were lost during the program period (we lost nearly more than one-third of the participants in both groups). Thus, for future studies, it would be recommended to combine in-person learning with online training sessions, and re-implementing this program and following up on participants over longer periods of six months, 12 months, or more, and if institutional policy permits, incentives can be considered to minimize drop-out rate. Secondly, this study included only researchers from KFMC who were included in the IRB. Thus, not all researchers in KFMC could be reached. Thirdly, this study had a follow-up period of one month after the intervention, which restricted long-term assessment. Therefore, future studies could adopt the current educational intervention with a longer follow-up duration. Finally, since all the study participants were employed by KFMC and were mostly engaged in clinical practice rather than academia, the generalizability of the results is limited and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could repeat the intervention among authors from different disciplines and institutions in Saudi Arabia.

Implications and recommendations

Publishing in a predatory journal could have serious implications on scientific quality and harm its evidence due to the poor peer-review process. Therefore, research institutions should formulate detailed guidelines on how to identify predatory journals and ways of selecting high-quality journals. This can be done by utilizing Beall’s list, assessing journal metrics, and indexing in Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed, especially for early career researchers [8, 12]. For researchers in medical fields, courses and training programs on scientific publishing can be included during medical education or residency program to guide and protect future researchers from predatory publishing practices.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that an educational interventional program significantly improved participants’ knowledge and practices but not their perceptions related to predatory journals. Moreover, the post-intervention knowledge and practice scores reflected a moderate to small effect size, suggesting a room for further enhancement through sustained educational efforts. Therefore, the threat of predatory journals needs further discussion. Developing new training strategies and conducting long-term follow-up studies can enable researchers to differentiate between scientifically accurate and predatory journals.
