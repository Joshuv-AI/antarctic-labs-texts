Kidney Transplantation in Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Autoantibody–Associated Vasculitis: Where do we Stand?
Authors: Pierre Pfirmann, Hannah Kaminski
Published: 2025

Kidney transplantation (KT) remains the preferred renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) caused by antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV). Approximately 20% to 30% of patients with AAV progress to ESKD within 10 years, despite remission-induction strategies. In this population, KT offers a clear survival advantage over maintenance dialysis, with a reduction in all-cause mortality > 50%, particularly because of decreased cardiovascular mortality, as demonstrated in several national registry studies.1,2 These survival benefits were recently confirmed in a nationwide French study that used more recent data to demonstrate improved survival among transplanted patients with AAV compared with those who remained on dialysis.3 Moreover, observational studies and registry data support a relatively favorable graft survival profile, with reported 10-year patient and graft survival rates exceeding 60% to 70%.2, 3, 4 Furthermore, relapse rates in transplant recipients are consistently low. In various series, the relapse rate has been estimated between 0.006 and 0.08 events/patient-yr, recurrence may occur at any time posttransplantation, even many years after the graft procedure,5 and was not influenced by remission duration. KT is therefore considered the treatment of choice for patients with AAV and ESKD.

The 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of AAV recommends deferring KT until patients have achieved at least 6 months of clinical remission, and emphasize that persistent ANCA positivity should not delay transplantation in patients otherwise deemed fit.6

In this issue of Kidney International Reports, Dekervel et al.7 present an analysis of KT outcomes in patients with AAV-associated glomerulonephritis (AAV-GN). This multicenter retrospective study included 206 AAV-GN patients transplanted between 2005 and 2023 across 12 French centers, compared with 412 matched controls with non-vasculitis ESKD. The findings provide valuable insights into both short- and long-term post-transplant outcomes in patients with AAV-GN.

Short-term outcomes, including delayed graft function, acute rejection, and early kidney function recovery were similar between AAV-GN and control patients. However, patients with AAV-GN exhibited higher mortality over time (hazard ratio: 1.48, P = 0.034) and a trend toward inferior graft survival (hazard ratio: 1.55, P = 0.077), especially in the earlier transplant era (2005–2014). Although infrequent (7.3%), relapses had a major clinical impact and were strongly associated with graft loss (hazard ratio: 3.49, P = 0.008). Most patients who experienced relapse were ANCA-positive at the time of transplantation. One persistent area of uncertainty has been the prognostic value of ANCA positivity at the time of transplantation. Some studies have suggested that persistent ANCA positivity (particularly with the proteinase 3 subtype) may be associated with a higher risk of posttransplant relapse.5,8 However, other larger cohorts have failed to confirm this association. In this paper, interpretation is constrained by the low number of relapse events and the heterogeneity of ANCA detection methods across centers. The debate remains unresolved.

Notably, ANCA positivity appeared protective against acute rejection (hazard ratio: 0.31, P = 0.016), a paradoxical but intriguing observation that may reflect clinician-driven decisions to intensify immunosuppression in perceived high-risk cases.

The study identified azathioprine use as an independent risk factor for both acute rejection and graft failure. This reinforces the importance of optimizing maintenance immunosuppression in patients with AAV-GN and limiting the use of azathioprine posttransplant to patients with contraindications to mycophenolate, as performed currently in all kidney transplants.

The optimal timing of KT in patients with AAVs is still debated. The authors report that the previously observed survival gap between patients with AAV-GN and controls has narrowed in recent years, likely because of advances in both vasculitis care and transplant management. Moreover, the lack of association between the interval from remission to transplantation and posttransplant outcomes suggests that fixed temporal thresholds may be less relevant than overall clinical stability, comorbidity profile, and immunological risk. However, the optimal timing of KT could not be assessed in this study because very few patients received a KT within 12 or even 24 months, in contrast to the findings of Little et al.,9 who reported higher mortality when KT occurred < 1 year after remission. Consequently, this question remains unresolved, although it is rarely encountered except in the context of living donation because of organ scarcity.

In summary, this study confirms in the new era that KT is a safe and effective treatment for patients with AAV-GN with ESKD. Although the relapse risk is low, its consequences are potentially severe, particularly because of the risk of graft loss. Acute rejection incidence is comparable to that of other patients with ESKD, and long-term outcomes continue to improve. Although ANCA positivity at the time of transplantation may inform posttransplant surveillance, it should not be used to delay transplantation, as emphasized by the last Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines. Avoiding azathioprine in favor of mycophenolate-based regimens is also recommended. Future prospective studies with harmonized definitions of remission and centralized ANCA monitoring will be essential to refine risk stratification and further improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
