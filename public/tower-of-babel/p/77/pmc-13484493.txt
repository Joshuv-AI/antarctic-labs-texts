Invas: an inversion-aware method for transcriptome assembly
Authors: Xuedong Wang, Feiyue Wang, Chenzhao Feng, Chaoyang Sun, Shuaicheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntragenic inversions reverse sequence orientation within genes and create non-collinear splice junctions that standard transcript assemblers miss, resulting in incomplete reconstruction and biased quantification. Here we show Invas, an inversion-aware framework operating with bulk short-read sequencing data. Invas integrates whole-genome sequencing breakpoints with transcriptomic sequencing evidence, rescues unmapped reads, and assembles isoforms using conjugate flow optimization. In silico across 42,000 events, Invas demonstrates high precision and recall and improves quantification of unaffected transcripts compared with conventional tools. We apply Invas to seven disease cohorts, identifying recurrent germline susceptibility variants and somatic drivers. Furthermore, Invas facilitates the discovery of somatic inversion-derived tumor-specific antigens with strong predicted immunogenicity. We release Invas and InvasDB as community resources to enable accurate characterization of inversion-affected genes, filling a critical gap in structural variant-aware transcriptomics.Subject terms: Software, Structural variation, Cancer genetics, Transcriptomics

Introduction

Intragenic inversions represent a specific class of structural variations (SVs) where a DNA segment within a single gene undergoes a localized reversal of orientation. These inversions fundamentally alter the linear organization of exonic and intronic sequences, frequently leading to exon rearrangement at the transcript level. Such rearrangements disrupt canonical splicing mechanisms and often give rise to noncanonical splicing patterns or aberrant isoforms, which can substantially impact gene function1–3. Mechanistically, intragenic inversions are driven by molecular processes such as non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR), microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ), or transposon-mediated insertions. These mechanisms introduce reverse complement sequences within genes, destabilizing genomic integrity1–3. Furthermore, the presence of inverted repeat elements, such as short or long interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs or LINEs), within introns increases susceptibility to inversions. These elements promote the formation of secondary structures like hairpin loops, which can stall replication forks and predispose loci to erroneous repair or rearrangement4–6. At the transcriptomic level, intragenic inversions disrupt the normal splicing landscape by activating cryptic splice sites, altering exon-exon junctions, or introducing reverse complement RNA sequences. These disruptions can compromise RNA stability, translation fidelity, and protein functionality7. Such structural perturbations are not merely stochastic events but have been increasingly implicated in diverse pathological contexts, underscoring their potential as key drivers of disease mechanisms.

Accumulating evidence underscores the clinical importance of intragenic inversions in human diseases. These genomic rearrangements have been linked to cancers, neurodevelopmental disorders, and inherited conditions by introducing noncanonical exon junctions and translation defects. Notable cases include inversions in ADNP, EGFR, AR, and SMN1, which contribute to Helsmoortel–Van der Aa syndrome, glioblastoma, castration-resistant prostate cancer, and spinal muscular atrophy, respectively8–11. In Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy, an intragenic inversion in the DMD gene, combined with an out-of-frame deletion, exemplifies how such events can reshape transcript structure and modulate disease severity12.

Intragenic inversions represent a source of tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) with potential relevance to cancer immunotherapy13,14. Unlike conventional TSAs derived from SNVs and small indels, intragenic inversions induce substantial genomic reorganization through reversal of nucleotide sequences, altering transcript architecture and potentially generating distinct immunogenic peptides. These structural perturbations can facilitate the exonization of typically non-coding intronic regions or create alternative open reading frames (ORF) at inversion breakpoints, enabling translation of altered sequences15,16. When presented by MHC molecules, these inversion-derived TSAs can bypass central immune tolerance and elicit robust T cell responses17,18. Despite current TSA discovery pipelines predominantly focusing on SNVs and indels19,20, intragenic inversions represent a source of TSAs that warrants further investigation, particularly in malignancies with low mutation burden21,22.

Despite their widespread occurrence, intragenic inverted transcripts remain challenging to detect and assemble with existing tools. Genomic SV callers, which rely on signals such as discordant read pairs, split reads, or depth-of-coverage variations, aim to reveal large-scale genome rearrangements but offer limited information on transcript-level changes. Thus, they fail to capture how these intragenic events alter RNA splicing or reshuffle exons23,24. Meanwhile, current reference-guided transcript assemblers (e.g., StringTie25, Cufflinks26, Scripture27) rely on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) that assume collinear alignment between RNA-seq reads and the reference genome, thereby misassembling and ignoring isoforms spanning inversion breakpoints. Similarly, de novo assemblers like Trinity28 construct de Bruijn graphs from k-mer overlaps, which become ambiguous at inversion junctions, leading to fragmented contigs. Most fundamentally, intragenic inversions can create cycles in splice graphs-a transcript may traverse through an inverted region and return to splice with an upstream exon, violating the acyclic assumption. To address these limitations, integrating whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing data offers a promising solution. This approach enables the identification of genomic breakpoints and their corresponding transcripts, providing a more comprehensive understanding of intragenic inversions and their impact on RNA structure.

In this study, we present Invas, an inversion-aware transcriptome assembler capable of reconstructing transcripts with cyclic structures caused by intragenic inversions. Invas initially applies a consensus-based strategy to extract high-confidence inversion breakpoints from state-of-the-art SV callers29–32. Then Invas integrates genome annotations to identify genes harboring intragenic inversions and constructs a conjugate graph (CG) based on clustered RNA-seq reads. Next, Invas iteratively derives the most compatible paths in the CG, enabling accurate reconstruction of inversion-associated transcripts and estimation of their expression levels. We benchmarked Invas using in silico datasets and demonstrated that Invas achieves high precision and F1-score in detecting and assembling intragenic transcripts, whereas the conventional RNA-seq assemblers, including StringTie25, Cufflinks26, and Trinity28 failed to reconstruct these transcripts. Application of Invas to RNA-seq data from seven disease types revealed prevalent intragenic inversions across both malignant and non-malignant conditions. In hepatocellular carcinoma and acute myeloid leukemia, these rearrangements introduce frameshift mutations that enhance oncogenic activity. In ovarian, head and neck, and soft tissue tumors, they generate premature stop codons, disrupting protein translation and promoting tumor progression. Notably, intragenic inversions were absent in lung cancer, suggesting possible tumor-type specificity. We also identified frameshift variants in adipose tissue of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a non-malignant disorder, highlighting the broader relevance of these rearrangements. In non-disease genomics, an inversion within MSC-AS1 was also validated in a non-disease Ashkenazi trio with Mendelian inheritance, indicating intragenic inversion events can arise in normal tissues. Furthermore, we discovered that many of these intragenic inversions generate distinct peptide sequences at their junctions, resulting in TSAs that could be recognized by the immune system. In silico prediction of MHC binding affinity revealed that several inversion-derived peptides exhibit strong potential for presentation on common human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles. These results demonstrate the utility of Invas in uncovering intragenic inversions with potential roles in disease and normal transcriptional regulation.

Results

Overview of Invas

Invas is a CG-based RNA assembler designed for the precise detection of intragenic inversions and transcriptome assembly (Fig. 1a). It leverages a conjugate flow network model and integrates RNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data. The workflow consists of four steps (Fig. 1b–g). First, Invas performs quality control and alignment of raw RNA-seq and WGS data, followed by SV breakpoint detection. It also conducts preliminary transcriptome assembly and realigns unmapped RNA-seq reads. Second, candidate inverted splicing events are identified based on SV breakpoints. Third, Invas refines the splicing positions of inverted segments. Fourth, a CG is constructed using pre-assembled RNA segments and re-aligned segments, incorporating their splicing connections. The customized flow algorithm is then applied for transcriptome assembly. Through a four-step process, Invas accurately detects expressed intragenic inversions and reconstructs both canonical and inversion-derived transcript isoforms.

We systematically compared Invas with conventional RNA-seq assemblers, including StringTie25, Cufflinks26, and Trinity28, to demonstrate its distinct advantages in reconstructing transcripts derived from intragenic inversions, which are typically not resolved by standard splicing models. Notably, Invas integrates whole-genome SV breakpoints from WGS data with RNA-seq evidence of inverted splicing to robustly detect intragenic inversions. By employing an abundance-aware CG, Invas constructs a graph representation that explicitly models inverted splicing events neglected by conventional RNA-seq assemblers. This framework achieves high-fidelity assembly of both canonical transcripts and those arising from intragenic inversions.

We applied Invas to real-world datasets and reported results with attention to germline, somatic, and cohort-specific constraints (Fig. 1h). A comprehensive summary of all disease cohorts analyzed in this study, including sample size, availability of matched normal controls, sequencing modalities, and the number of detected inversion events, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Where matched normal and population SV resources are available, we separate germline and somatic contexts: for germline, we confirm recurrent intragenic inversions (e.g., in TSPAN8 and PTPRF) and assemble their inversion-derived isoforms to enable association and regulatory analyses; for somatic, we require tumor-supported and normal-absent evidence to catalog tumor-restricted intragenic inversions (e.g., intronic inversions in PTEN, exonic frameshift inversions in RHOH, and recurrent events in NOL10), reconstruct the corresponding transcripts, and quantify splicing disruption while nominating junction-derived TSAs. In cohorts where strict classification is not feasible (e.g., certain AML datasets lacking matched normals or adequate population references), we provide an “uncertain” label with explicit evidence (tumor RNA support, available WGS/SV context, and blacklist filters) and report findings without attributing germline or somatic origin. All curated inversions are compiled into InvasDB, a searchable catalog that records source tumor, genomic location, structural category (exonic, intronic, or exon-intronic), functional impact (frameshift inversion, regulatory impact, or benign), affected protein domain, regulatory elements, strand and gemline/somatic types (Supplementary Data 1). These applications underscore the importance of accurately detecting intragenic inversions and generating high-quality assemblies across diverse study designs.

Invas accurately characterizes intragenic inversion transcripts with in silico data

Invas demonstrates high specificity and sensitivity for intragenic inversion detection, achieving an overall precision of 99.2%, a recall of 86.3%, and an F1-score of 92.3% across 42,000 inversion events at varying sequencing depths. We designed six scenarios to investigate intragenic inversions (“Methods”). These scenarios include: (i) partial inversion of exon fragments, (ii) complete inversion of a single exon, (iii) intron retention inversions, (iv) inversion of multiple exons, (v) alternative splicing events, such as exon skipping and mutually exclusive exons, and (vi) intragenic inversions that disturb a single intron (Fig. 2a). Performance improved with increasing sequencing depth (Supplementary Fig. 1). Partial exon-fragment inversions were detected with 99.18% precision, 83.66% recall and an F1-score of 90.76%. Complete single-exon inversions yielded 99.33% precision, 84.01% recall, and an F1-score of 91.03%. Inversions associated with intron retention achieved 98.79% precision, 84.59% recall, and an F1-score of 91.14%. Inversions spanning multiple contiguous exons reached 99.28% precision, 86.24% recall, and an F1-score of 92.30%. The strongest performance was observed for alternative-splicing-associated inversions, with 99.39% precision, 88.47% recall, and an F1-score of 93.61%. Inversions disturbing a single intron achieved 97.53% precision, 85.43% recall, and an F1-score of 91.05% (Supplementary Fig. 2). These in silico results underscore the robustness of Invas across diverse inversion architectures and sequencing depths.

In stark contrast, conventional assembly tools StringTie25, Cufflinks26, and Trinity28 were unable to reconstruct intragenic inversion-bearing transcripts, producing fragmented assemblies even at optimal sequencing depths (110 × WGS and 1,050 × RNA-seq). Representative examples include three genes associated with tumor prognosis and cancer progression, TNFSF9, HES3, and PRDX5, whose expression patterns across cancer types further support their biological relevance (Supplementary Figs. 3–5). The assembly comparison for TNFSF9 is shown in Fig. 2b. Invas successfully detected and assembled a heterozygous intragenic inversion at chr19:6532786–6532816, whereas StringTie25 and Cufflinks26 failed to assemble inversion-bearing transcripts, and Trinity28 produced highly fragmented outputs lacking both normal and inverted transcripts. Additional assembly examples for HES3 and PRDX5 are provided in Supplementary Fig. 6. For HES3, Invas identified a homozygous inversion at chr1:6244375–6244430 and successfully assembled two alternatively spliced transcripts in the first exon, whereas conventional tools generated truncated fragments terminating at inversion breakpoints. For PRDX5, Invas detected heterozygous inversion breakpoints flanking exons four and five (chr11:64320862–64321069) and assembled five distinct inversion-bearing transcript isoforms, while StringTie25, Cufflinks26, and Trinity28 failed to reconstruct the complete inversion-bearing transcripts, with Trinity producing transcripts uniformly truncated at breakpoints. The superior performance of Invas across diverse inversion architectures, together with the inability of conventional assembly tools to reconstruct inversion-bearing transcripts, establishes Invas as a robust solution for intragenic inversion detection and transcript reconstruction.

Intragenic inversion breakpoints complicate transcript quantification by causing mapping ambiguities, soft-clipping, and unmapped reads, which lead to biases in conventional tools33,34 (Supplementary Note 1). For transcripts that do not contain intragenic inversions, Invas reconstructed more accurate sequences than other tools by reconstructing accurate transcript assembly. Using clipped alignment ratio, clipped alignment reads count, and good mapping rate obtained from TransRate35 as evaluation metrics, Invas achieved the lowest median clipped alignment ratio (0.97%) compared to StringTie25 (2.86%), Cufflinks26 (2.83%), and Trinity28 (12.95%) (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, Invas maintained stable performance across sequencing depths (Fig. 2c, d) and achieved the highest good mapping rate (95%), outperforming StringTie25 (92%), Cufflinks26 (93%), and Trinity28 (79%) (Fig. 2h, i).

We also assessed quantification accuracy for transcripts without intragenic inversions using Pearson correlation and root-mean-square error (RMSE). Invas achieved the highest Pearson correlation (0.9722) and the lowest RMSE (0.0395), surpassing StringTie25 (0.9665, 0.0467), Cufflinks26 (0.9648, 0.0480), and Trinity28 (0.9539, 0.0692) (Fig. 2e–g). To statistically validate these improvements, we performed one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests comparing Invas against each competing method, applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Invas demonstrated significantly higher Pearson correlation coefficients compared to StringTie (adjusted P < 0.05), Cufflinks (adjusted P < 0.05), and Trinity (adjusted P < 0.05). Consistently, Invas exhibited significantly lower RMSE than StringTie (adjusted P < 10−4), Cufflinks (adjusted P < 10−4), and Trinity (adjusted P < 10−4). Detailed results across sequencing depths are provided in Supplementary Figs. 7–10. Invas demonstrates distinct advantages over conventional tools. For transcripts containing intragenic inversions, Invas effectively detects and reconstructs sequences that other tools fail to address. For transcripts without inversions, Invas consistently outperforms existing assemblers in both reconstruction accuracy and quantification, highlighting its robustness and versatility.

Application of Invas in HCC reveals TSPAN8 intragenic inversion as a candidate germline susceptibility variant

We identified intragenic inversion events in 12 of 34 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples obtained from TCGA-LIHC36 and Sung et al.37. Among these, the intragenic inversion within the pro-metastatic gene TSPAN8 emerged as the most prevalent structural variant in our HCC cohort, occurring at a frequency of 14.7% (5/34). The TSPAN8 inversion was enriched in HCC compared with other cancer types (two-sided Fisher’s exact test, FDR = 0.015; Fig. 3a, b; Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Table 5). This recurrent and HCC-enriched pattern suggests that the TSPAN8 intragenic inversion may represent a germline genetic polymorphism associated with hepatocellular carcinoma susceptibility.

The TSPAN8 intragenic inversion constitutes a substantial germline structural aberration that disrupts both regulatory elements and critical protein domains essential for normal cellular function. For instance, sample ICC070015 demonstrates that the TSPAN8 gene generates six normal transcripts, alongside one aberrant transcript that harbors an intragenic inversion. High-quality breakpoints localized this intragenic inversion to chromosomal coordinates chr12:71532793-71533753. This SV manifests as an exonic inversion where the breakpoints flank exon 4, resulting in an in-frame inversion of the complete fourth exon among the nine total exons comprising the TSPAN8 gene. The genomic positioning of this inversion breakpoint is notable, as it occurs 374 base pairs upstream of a documented STAT1-binding site (OREG1878105; Fig. 3d, e). Given that STAT1 functions as a pivotal transcription factor orchestrating immune responses and cellular growth regulation, the disruption of its binding capacity may fundamentally alter TSPAN8 downstream signaling cascades. Furthermore, the inverted genomic region encompasses sequences encoding both the transmembrane38 (P19075) and cytoplasmic39 (Q53GA9) domains of the TSPAN8 protein (Fig. 3e). The structural disruption of these functional domains is anticipated to substantially compromise membrane anchoring mechanisms and intracellular signaling pathways. Such perturbations likely result in diminished protein stability and functional capacity, potentially establishing a mechanistic link to hepatocellular carcinoma progression through aberrant cellular communication and growth control, particularly in individuals harboring this germline variant.

The TSPAN8 inversion destabilizes its corresponding mRNA structure. RNA secondary structure predictions by RNAfold40 revealed that the TSPAN8 inversion destabilized its RNA structure (Supplementary Fig. 11). The minimum free energy (MFE) increased from −184.6 kcal/mol (wild-type) to −164.3 kcal/mol (inverted), indicating reduced thermodynamic stability. Similarly, the ensemble free energy rose from −198.86 to −180.02 kcal/mol, and the centroid structure free energy increased from −134.08 to −74.81 kcal/mol. The ensemble diversity also rose from 178.16 to 251.39, reflecting a higher propensity for alternative structural conformations. These findings suggest that the intragenic inversion may make the TSPAN8 transcript more susceptible to degradation. RNA instability can lead to shortened mRNA half-life, potentially reducing TSPAN8 protein expression levels. This also explains why the inverted splice junctions were less abundant than normal linear junctions in sequencing data. In the context of a germline polymorphism, such RNA-level effects could contribute to lifelong alterations in TSPAN8 function, thereby enhancing susceptibility to HCC initiation and metastatic progression.

The TSPAN8 inversion alters protein interactions, shifting the mechanism from CD44-driven epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to integrin-mediated invasion. Previous studies have shown that TSPAN8 interacts with CD44, EpCAM, integrin α3, and STAT3, key regulators of tumor invasion, stemness, and oncogenic signaling38,41,42 (Fig. 3c). To rigorously assess the functional repercussions of the TSPAN8 inversion, we first confirmed that the coding sequence remains intact by preserving the ORF (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Note 2), thereby ensuring continuous protein synthesis. Subsequently, AlphaFold-based modeling revealed that inverted TSPAN8 exhibits reduced binding affinity to CD44 (ΔG = +2.6 kcal/mol), suggesting an attenuation of CD44-mediated EMT and the related metastatic processes (Fig. 3g). In contrast, the inversion markedly enhances affinities for EpCAM (ΔG = −2.9 kcal/mol) and integrin α3 (ΔG = −8.1 kcal/mol), which supports a compensatory shift toward integrin-mediated adhesion and invasion that may bypass classical EMT-dependent dissemination. Notably, the binding affinity with STAT3 remains largely unchanged, indicating that STAT3-mediated oncogenic signaling is sustained. Collectively, these findings support the notion that the TSPAN8 inversion, as a germline susceptibility variant, may favor integrin-dependent metastatic routes while preserving cancer stemness and STAT3-driven tumorigenesis. This is in line with emerging evidence that integrin-mediated metastasis, cancer stem cell maintenance, and persistent STAT3 activation are critical in advanced HCC and highlights TSPAN8 as a candidate therapeutic target in genetically predisposed individuals.

Application of Invas identifies a germline PTPRF intragenic inversion in HGSOC

We identified intragenic inversion events in 34 of 50 whole-genome and RNA sequencing samples from 15 patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), which were collected from Yang et al.43. The average number of inversion events per sample varied across different anatomical sites (Fig. 4a). Specifically, ovarian tissues exhibited an average of 3.95 events per sample (79 events across 20 samples), omental tissues showed 3.50 events per sample (42 events in 12 samples), and fallopian tube samples had a lower average of 1.00 event per sample (4 events in 4 samples). Other metastatic tissues presented an intermediate average of 2.11 events per sample (19 events across 9 samples) (Fig. 4b, c, e; Supplementary Data 3), indicating that the ovary and omentum are major loci of genomic instability in HGSOC. Importantly, HGSOC samples exhibited the highest proportion of potentially damaging inversion events (27/42) among all cancer types analyzed, suggesting a more active role for these structural variants in the tumorigenesis of HGSOC. Further analysis of ovarian tissues by laterality revealed notable asymmetry. Left ovarian samples averaged 3.38 inversion events per sample (27 events in 8 samples), whereas right ovarian samples exhibited a higher burden, averaging 4.33 events per sample (52 events in 12 samples). The observed variation in inversion burden between anatomical sites and between the left and right ovaries suggests that tissue-specific biological factors influence the accumulation of genomic alterations. Differences in vascular supply, hormonal exposure, or DNA repair capacity may contribute to the elevated inversion burden observed in the right ovary. Collectively, these findings highlight ovarian and omental tissues as primary sites of genomic instability in HGSOC and underscore the importance of further investigating the underlying mechanisms driving these site- and side-specific differences.

Among these, the intragenic inversion within the PTPRF gene emerged as a highly prevalent structural variant in our HGSOC cohort, exhibiting its highest detection rates in ovarian tissue, appearing in 5/8 of left-ovary and 6/12 of right-ovary HGSOC samples (Fig. 4d), surpassing its prevalence in other metastatic or extra-ovarian sites. The frequent occurrence and tissue distribution of this inversion in HGSOC strongly suggest that the PTPRF intragenic inversion represents a germline genetic polymorphism that confers increased susceptibility to HGSOC development and progression. The PTPRF intragenic inversion constitutes a substantial germline structural aberration that disrupts both regulatory elements and critical protein domains essential for normal cellular function. The intragenic inversion resides within the PTPRF locus on chromosome 1 (chr1:43593624-43594215) and specifically disrupts intron 11 of 33, representing an intronic inversion event. The PTPRF intragenic inversion in proximity to a cis-regulatory element and key transcription factor binding sites indicates a substantial impact on local transcriptional governance. Specifically, the intragenic inversion of the PTPRF lies approximately 710 bp downstream of the cis-regulatory element (cCRE EH38E1342636) and 1000 bp downstream of multiple transcription factor binding sites (e.g., ZNF263, MAX, EBF1) (Fig. 4f, g). The physical adjacency increases the likelihood of altered three-dimensional chromatin structures, thereby depriving PTPRF of requisite regulatory inputs or relocating enhancers away from their target promoter regions. Recognizing that structural rearrangements may also compromise post-transcriptional control, we employed RNAfold40 to assess whether the inversion could perturb RNA secondary structure. The secondary structure predictions revealed a slight decrease in MFE (from −323.80 to −328.00 kcal/mol) and an increase in ensemble diversity (from 166.80 to 180.26) (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating a more heterogeneous folding landscape. These changes in RNA conformation may compromise splicing fidelity, translation efficiency, and RNA-protein interactions, providing a plausible mechanistic basis for altered PTPRF function in HGSOC. Furthermore, the disruption of PTPRF at the protein level further implicates the intragenic inversion in malignant transformation. ORFfinder44 identified a premature termination codon in PTPRF (ORF12), creating a truncated isoform devoid of critical phosphatase domains (Fig. 4h). AlphaFold 345 modeling confirmed the loss of C-terminal motifs essential for dephosphorylating β-catenin and epidermal growth factor receptor pathway substrates (Fig. 4i). Inactivation of these regulatory domains correlates with enhanced oncogenic signaling, disrupted cell adhesion, and increased EMT, a triad often implicated in ovarian cancer metastasis46. The truncated protein may also exert dominant-negative effects on the wild-type phosphatase, further amplifying its oncogenic potential. In the context of a germline polymorphism, such multilevel disruptions could contribute to lifelong alterations in PTPRF function, thereby enhancing susceptibility to HGSOC initiation and metastatic progression, particularly in genetically predisposed individuals.

The intragenic inversion of PTPRF was also profiled across diverse malignancies by Invas, highlighting a broader oncogenic mechanism and potential therapeutic vulnerability. Parallel intragenic rearrangements were also identified in soft tissue tumor samples (95T1000, Macchia et al.47; Supplementary Data 4; Supplementary Fig. 13) and head and neck tumor samples (MSK-HN1, Yang et al.48; Supplementary Data 5; Supplementary Fig. 14), consistently correlating with unchecked β-catenin and EGFR signaling, disrupted cell adhesion, and promotion of EMT. The discovery of the PTPRF intragenic inversion across distinct tumor types implies that attenuation of PTPRF function is a convergent pathway in oncogenesis. Collectively, these findings underscore both the diagnostic value of PTPRF alterations and the clinical imperative of targeting aberrant phosphatase regulatory networks across multiple cancer contexts, with particular relevance to germline susceptibility in HGSOC.

Invas identifies the NOL10 inversion as a disruptor of RNA folding and AATF-NGDN-NOL10 interactions

The NOL10 represents the most frequent intragenic inversion locus in AML, indicating a substantial perturbation of transcriptional regulatory networks potentially contributing to leukemogenesis. We collected 21 paired whole-genome and RNA sequencing samples from L’Abbate et al.49. The cohort spans 8 distinct AML stages, covers an age range from 29 to 89 years, and consists of 13 female and 8 male patients (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 6). Among the genes analyzed, NOL10 displayed the highest inversion frequency, occurring in 43% of samples (Fig. 5a). The inversion spans chr2:10826021-10827089 (Fig. 5b) and disrupts intron 1 of 20, constituting an intronic inversion. This structural alteration is located approximately 1500 bp upstream of the regulatory element OREG1264634 in the ORegAnno database (Fig. 5c). The NOL10 intragenic inversion enhances NOL10-NGDN binding but diminishes NOL10-AATF interactions, reshaping proliferative and apoptotic pathways. At the protein level, we annotated transcripts with ORFfinder44 and employed AlphaFold 345 to predict 3D structures (Fig. 5d–f). NOL10 partners with Neuroguidin (NGDN) and AATF (Apoptosis-Antagonizing Transcription Factor) to form the AATF-NGDN-NOL10 (ANN) complex, which oversees ribosome biogenesis and nucleolar organization50. Molecular docking and affinity predictions using PRODIGY51 demonstrated that the normal NOL10 isoform interacts with AATF through 158 intermolecular contacts (including 12 charged-charged, 8 charged-polar, and 43 charged-apolar contacts), yielding a binding affinity of −13.6 kcal/mol and a dissociation constant (Kd) of 1.00 × 10−10 M at 25.0 ∘C. In contrast, its interaction with NGDN involves 144 contacts, leading to a more favorable binding affinity of −17.1 kcal/mol and a Kd of 2.80 × 10−13 M. For the inversion-bearing NOL10 isoform, the interaction profile shifts markedly: binding with AATF is reduced to 125 contacts (resulting in an affinity of −11.4 kcal/mol and a Kd of 4.30 × 10−9 M), whereas interaction with NGDN is enhanced to 193 contacts, yielding a binding affinity of −20.6 kcal/mol and a Kd of 7.40 × 10−16 M. These contrasting interaction profiles suggest that the inversion not only weakens AATF-mediated, p53-dependent apoptotic regulation but also strengthens NGDN-driven ribosomal subunit maturation, thereby shifting the balance between proliferative and apoptotic pathways and contributing to leukemogenesis. Additionally, the NOL10 intragenic inversions were also identified in adipose tissue sample PCOS-015 from PCOS patients52 (Supplementary Data 7 and Supplementary Fig. 16), suggesting a shared mechanism of transcriptional dysregulation across AML and PCOS. Both diseases are associated with metabolic and immune dysregulation53,54, and altered NOL10 function may link these conditions by impairing ribosome biogenesis, promoting inflammation, and disrupting cellular homeostasis, ultimately contributing to pathogenesis in distinct tissue contexts.

Population-level and functional annotation demonstrate that the MSC-AS1 intragenic inversion is prevalent and functionally benign. Population annotations reveal that the inversion is a common polymorphic structural variant, being observed in 185 individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project and exhibiting an approximate allele frequency (AF) of 50% in the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium panel (Fig. 6g). The oppositely oriented LINE1 elements (L1MEg2 and L1MA6) likely contributed to the MSC-AS1 intragenic inversion through NAHR, as misalignment of these non-allelic repeats frequently leads to common genomic inversions1,2,56. However, these LINE1 subfamilies may lack the requisite sequence identity for efficient recombination, suggesting that alternative processes, such as MMEJ57–59, fork stalling and template switching (FoSTeS)60, microhomology-mediated break-induced replication (MMBIR)61, or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)62—could also be involved. In the absence of breakpoint-level resolution, NAHR remains the most plausible mechanism given the presence of inverted repeats (Fig. 6h). Moreover, several factors likely contribute to the inversion’s high prevalence: its location within a non-coding intron minimizes functional constraint63; the local repeat-rich architecture may promote common inversion formation4,6; its relatively small size reduces selective pressure64; neutral drift may have facilitated its spread65; and any minor regulatory impacts appear to be tolerated or even slightly beneficial66. Somatic inversions disrupt critical cell signaling regulators in cancer progression

Somatic inversions represent critical variants that can impact tumor biology through diverse functional mechanisms. Our comprehensive analysis of somatic inversions across PCOS, HCC, and HGSOC samples revealed recurrent alterations in genes critical for tumor biology (Supplementary Fig. 18). Among the cancer types analyzed, HGSOC samples exhibited the highest proportion of potentially damaging events (27/42, 64.3%). NOL10 inversions were detected across all three cancer types, with the highest frequency in HGSOC (9.5%), suggesting its potential role in multiple cancer contexts. Functional characterization identified several clinically relevant somatic inversions with important implications for tumor biology. In HCC, we detected intronic inversions in the PTEN tumor suppressor gene (chr10:89661048–89661631) that disrupted critical branch point sequences and introduced cryptic splice sites. These alterations resulted in aberrant splicing patterns and reduced PTEN expression, contributing to constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway-a hallmark of hepatocellular carcinogenesis67. Importantly, HCC tumors with PTEN negativity exhibited aggressive clinicopathological features, including increased proliferation indices and poorer patient outcomes68. GPHN inversions were highly recurrent in HGSOC (9.5%). An intronic inversion in GPHN (chr14:66703608-66705281) disrupted a conserved intronic enhancer element, resulting in aberrant expression patterns and isoform switching. While GPHN has been primarily characterized for its role in inhibitory synaptic transmission69, another evidence suggests its involvement in cytoskeletal organization and cellular adhesion processes relevant to cancer progression70. Additionally, we characterized an exonic frameshift inversion in RHOH (chr4:40233405-40235439) that simultaneously affected coding sequences and regulatory elements. RHOH, an atypical member of the RAS superfamily predominantly expressed in hematopoietic lineages, plays critical roles in immune receptor signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics71. In HGSOC, variants-altered RHOH exhibited impaired interactions with canonical signaling partners while promoting enhanced activation of RAC1 and CDC42, a profile that is compatible with increased cellular invasiveness and therapeutic resistance72.

Disrupted genomic architecture impacts TSAs emergence via intragenic inversions

Intragenic inversions create ideal conditions for TSA formation through multiple distinct mechanisms. Starting from 673,942 inversion-derived candidate peptides generated by translation of 196 inversion-containing regions, 412 MAPs were retained after stringent MHC-I binding, processing, and presentation filtering. Following self-peptide removal against the human reference proteome, 381 non-self MAPs remained. Subsequent immune-tolerance filtering based on mTEC/DC and GTEx expression further reduced the candidates to a final set of 30 high-confidence TSAs. These peptides were then mapped back to the reference genome (GRCh38) using BLAT to confirm their genomic origins, and candidates located in hypervariable regions were excluded. As this step primarily served as a quality-control filter, no additional peptides were removed at this stage. Overall, these sequential filters reduced the initial candidate pool by more than four orders of magnitude (~0.004% of the original peptides), yielding a highly stringent set of 30 high-confidence TSAs (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Data 9), with 17 specifically derived from somatic inversions.

Discussion

Intragenic inversions represent a pervasive class of SVs whose systematic identification has the potential to broaden our understanding of transcriptomic complexity. By leveraging an integrative pipeline that couples short-read whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing data, we developed Invas to systematically detect, reconstruct, and quantify intragenic inversions at isoform resolution. Our in silico analyses demonstrated that conventional de novo and reference-based assemblers exhibit pronounced blind spots around cyclic structural breakpoints, thereby neglecting or misassembling transcripts subject to inverted splicing. In contrast, Invas achieved consistently high precision and recall in detecting these events, thereby recovering both the transcripts with cyclic structures caused by intragenic inversions and adjacent canonical isoforms with minimal quantification error. These results highlight how explicitly modeling reverse-complemented splice junctions, informed by short-read whole-genome structural variant calls, can resolve local complexity in transcriptomes that standard approaches overlook23,73.

Our benchmarking results reveal that Invas addresses a gap in transcriptome analysis by enabling the detection and assembly of transcripts affected by intragenic inversions. This analytic capability is fundamentally absent in existing RNA-seq assembly tools. Conventional assemblers such as StringTie25, Cufflinks26, and Trinity28 are inherently constrained by linear reference models and canonical splicing assumptions, rendering them incapable of recognizing or reconstructing transcripts that involve reverse-complemented splicing junctions introduced by genomic inversions. As a result, such tools fail to capture inversion-derived isoforms, leading to incomplete transcriptome reconstructions and a methodological limitation in SV-aware transcriptomics. In silico evaluations revealed that Invas achieves an average precision of 99.2% and an F1-score of 92.3% in detecting intragenic inversions. Furthermore, Invas also corrects quantification distortions in canonical transcripts located within intragenic inversion regions. These transcripts, although not directly altered in structure, often span or flank inversion breakpoints, which disrupt local read alignment due to mapping ambiguity, soft- or hard-clipping, and increased rates of unmapped reads. These artifacts introduce substantial bias in transcript abundance estimation34,74. For users who only have RNA-seq data available, we provide an RNA-seq-only mode in Invas. In this setting, Invas relies on (i) breakpoint coordinates recorded in InvasDB and (ii) the inverted splicing signals described in the Methods section. Based on whether a breakpoint is cataloged in InvasDB, we classify events into common intragenic inversions and novel intragenic inversions. Notably, the detection performance of this RNA-seq-only mode is comparable to the joint WGS+RNA-seq setting (Supplementary Table 6). By leveraging high-confidence breakpoint information from WGS data when available, or from InvasDB together with inversion-aware splicing signals when only RNA-seq is available, and by re-aligning previously unmapped RNA-seq reads in a strand- and structure-aware manner, Invas reconstructs both aberrant and canonical isoforms with improved fidelity. This integrated framework allows for the correction of expression-level biases introduced by SV, thereby enhancing the accuracy of downstream differential expression analysis and isoform-level quantification.

Real-world applications across multiple cancer types have revealed intragenic inversions as frequent modulators of lineage-specific tumor progression and functional disruption. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; 35.29% of 34 samples), frequent TSPAN8 inversions (14.7% frequency, HCC-enriched; FDR = 0.015) emerged as a germline susceptibility variant, potentially predisposing individuals to HCC development through constitutive genetic polymorphism75. These inversions destabilized mRNA secondary structure (MFE: −164.3 kcal/mol vs. −184.6 kcal/mol wild-type) and disrupted transmembrane/cytoplasmic domains, attenuating CD44 binding (ΔG = +2.6 kcal/mol) while enhancing integrin α3 affinity (ΔG = −8.1 kcal/mol)38,41,42. This reconfiguration shifts metastatic mechanisms from CD44-dependent EMT to integrin-mediated invasion, potentially conferring resistance to targeted therapies and underscoring TSPAN8’s role as a susceptibility locus in HCC etiology. Similarly, in (HGSOC; 34/50 samples), PTPRF inversions (up to 62.5% in ovarian tissues) introduced premature stop codons, truncating phosphatase domains critical for antagonizing EGFR and β-catenin signaling, thereby promoting constitutive oncogenic activation and metastatic spread in omental tissues46,76. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML; 43% NOL10 inversions), these rearrangements altered RNA folding (MFE: −549.60 kcal/mol inverted vs. −520.50 kcal/mol normal) and AATF-NGDN-NOL10 complex interactions, diminishing AATF-apoptotic affinity while bolstering NGDN-ribosome biogenesis, favoring proliferation over cell death49,50. Convergent PTPRF and NOL10 inversions in head and neck, soft tissue tumors, and PCOS adipose tissue further implicate these variants in transcriptional and metabolic dysregulation across malignant and non-malignant contexts47,48,52,53,77. Notably, Invas also identified a Mendelian-inherited 245-bp intragenic inversion in the non-disease Ashkenazi trio (heterozygous in child, homozygous in mother), validating its utility in normal genomics55. This inversion, located within the long non-coding RNA gene MSC-AS1 and supported by multi-platform sequencing (PacBio, Nanopore), exhibited no evident disruption of miRNA-lncRNA interactions and minimal RNA structural perturbation, suggesting a tolerable evolutionary impact likely mediated by NAHR between inverted LINE1 elements1,57. The ability to detect and characterize such polymorphic variants in non-disease contexts demonstrates the broader applicability of Invas in elucidating SV and its role in transcriptional regulation. In our analysis, intragenic inversions were notably absent in the lung cancer cohort, in contrast to their prevalence in HGSOC and HCC. This discrepancy likely reflects the distinct genomic instability profiles inherent to these tumor types. Lung cancer genomes, particularly those associated with tobacco exposure, are predominantly characterized by a high burden of somatic point mutations (SNVs) and specific mutational signatures (e.g., Signature 4), rather than the extensive structural rearrangement phenotypes observed in other carcinomas78,79. Conversely, HGSOC is defined by ubiquitous homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), a mechanism that actively promotes complex SVs, including inversions, as a primary driver of tumorigenesis80. While our data suggest a distinct lack of intragenic inversions in lung cancer consistent with its mutation-driven classification, further investigations utilizing larger, multi-centric cohorts are warranted. It remains possible that such structural events occur at low frequencies or are restricted to specific, rare histological subtypes not fully represented in the current dataset.

Extending the insights into immunological consequences, an unexpected yet intriguing observation pertains to the potential identification of TSAs derived from inverted transcripts. Analysis of ORFs near inversion breakpoints revealed candidate peptides generated through reverse-complement splicing and frameshift events. Several high-confidence epitopes exhibited strong predicted binding affinities to a diverse range of HLA I alleles and were notably absent in healthy mTEC or DC-derived expression profiles. The findings suggest that intragenic inversions could expand the immunopeptidome by introducing distinct peptide sequences, thereby providing a potential source of TSAs. However, the immunogenicity of the candidate TSAs and their relevance in the tumor microenvironment remain to be experimentally validated. Further studies are required to assess the capacity to induce T cell responses and determine the feasibility as targets in TSA-based immunotherapies, particularly for tumors with limited nonsynonymous mutations.

Despite the promising applications and insights, several limitations of Invas merit consideration. The reliance on high-quality RNA-seq and WGS data may constrain applicability in low-coverage or degraded samples, such as formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. Future refinements could optimize the tool for degraded or sparse input data. Although Invas effectively resolves intragenic inversions, challenges may arise in detecting larger or more complex SVs, such as translocations or tandem duplications, that span multiple genes. Integration of long-read sequencing technologies, such as Oxford Nanopore or PacBio, could enhance resolution and accuracy of inversion detection. Finally, the functional implications of intragenic inversions identified by Invas require further experimental validation. For instance, the observed disruption of RNA secondary structures and protein interactions in genes like TSPAN8 and NOL10 should be confirmed through in vitro and in vivo studies. Such validations could provide deeper insights into the contribution of intragenic inversions to disease mechanisms and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

To guide practitioners in applying this tool effectively, it is important to define the appropriate use cases and limitations of Invas. The method is recommended for any researcher performing transcriptome assembly, particularly when the goal is to reconstruct complex SVs that standard RNA-seq assemblers fail to capture. Conventional tools often miss intragenic inversions because they cannot resolve the non-collinear splice junctions created when sequence orientation is reversed within genes. Invas addresses this gap and is most robustly applied in studies where paired WGS and RNA-seq data are available, as WGS provides genomic evidence to validate the inverted loci. However, even in scenarios where only RNA-seq data is available, Invas serves as a valuable discovery tool, offering an alternative perspective for identifying variant transcripts that may impact protein structure or serve as candidate TSAs. Regarding limitations, we advise caution when applying Invas to datasets characterized by low sequencing depth or poor library quality. In such suboptimal conditions, the reduced coverage across breakpoints may compromise assembly accuracy; therefore, we recommend that results from low-quality data be treated as preliminary and subjected to manual visual inspection.

In summary, the findings of the present study highlight the pivotal role of intragenic inversions in shaping the transcriptomic landscape, particularly in cancer biology. By addressing persistent challenges in transcriptome assembly, Invas provides a robust framework for advancing precision medicine. The distinct capacity of the tool to detect and reconstruct inversion-associated isoforms may enable more accurate identification of disease biomarkers and patient stratification, while also facilitating the development of isoform-specific therapeutic strategies. Beyond applications in disease genomics, Invas contributes to the understanding of SV in healthy populations, yielding insights into the evolutionary and functional significance of such events. Collectively, Invas advances transcriptome analysis through integration of RNA-seq and WGS data with graph-theoretic algorithms, offering enhanced resolution in detecting inversion-affected transcripts. The approach opens avenues for basic research and clinical translation, establishing Invas as a valuable resource for the genomics community. Future efforts could extend applicability to a broader array of datasets and structural variant types, thereby amplifying impact on precision genomics and personalized medicine.

Definition of inversions, intragenic inversions

Chromosomal inversions are characterized by structural rearrangements where a segment within a chromosome undergoes a reversal of orientation. This process is delineated by two breakpoints,b1 and b2, on the same chromosome, leading to the excision of the segment S that spans these breakpoints. The segment S is then inverted (S′) and reinserted between b1 and b2, resulting in an altered sequence order within the chromosome. For a given chromosome with a linear sequence represented as C=g1g2…gb1−1gb1…gb2gb2+1…gm, where gi denotes a genetic sequence. The sequence of the chromosome after an inversion event can be represented as C′=g1g2…gb1−1invgb1…gb2gb2+1…gm, where invgb1…gb2 denotes the reverse complement sequence of the genetic sequence between breakpoints b1 and b2.

Intragenic inversions, a subtype of chromosomal inversions, occur specifically within the boundaries of a single transcript. Considering the pre-mRNA sequence T composed of an ordered array of exons and introns, the mature mRNA sequence after splicing is a concatenation of selected exons: T = E1E2…En. An intragenic inversion between breakpoints b1 and b2 in this mature transcript is represented as: T′=E1E2…Eb1−1invEb1…Eb2Eb2+1…En. Here invEb1…Eb2 signifies the reversed order of the exonic sequence between b1 and b2. Note that the sequences denoted by Eb1 and Eb2 could be partial exons if the breakpoints interrupt exonic regions, with the full exons situated within the breakpoints being unaffected. The implications of such intragenic inversions are context-dependent, altering splicing patterns, protein-coding frames, and possibly leading to the synthesis of a protein with an altered amino acid sequence or altered functional domains.

Algorithm of Invas

Quality control and preprocessing

Invas requires paired RNA-seq and WGS data as inputs. For RNA-seq, Invas applies adapter trimming and quality filtering with Trimmomatic81 to obtain high-quality reads, followed by alignment to the GRCh38 genome using Hisat2 (v2.2.1)82 by default. We collect unmapped reads that, due to genomic rearrangements, and subsequently realign them to the reference genome employing the BWA MEM algorithm83. For WGS data, quality control is conducted utilizing fastp84 to yield high-quality reads. The filtered reads are aligned using the BWA MEM algorithm83 to the same reference genome. To ensure robust intragenic inversion breakpoint detection, Invas proceeds to perform SV calling using four state-of-the-art tools: Manta (v1.1.0)30, DELLY (v0.7.7)29, LUMPY (v0.2.13)32, and SvABA (v1.2.0)31, chosen for their validated efficacy in the International Cancer Genome Consortium85 (ICGC), Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes BamQuery86 (PCAWG) project (Fig. 1b) and previous SV benchmarking study87. Subsequently, the individual SV callsets were merged and consolidated using SURVIVOR (v1.0.7)88 to eliminate redundant calls. To balance sensitivity and specificity, we applied a consensus strategy requiring an inversion event to be supported by at least two of the four callers. We further validated this integration strategy by benchmarking the performance of individual callers versus the consensus approach on intragenic inversion detection using two Genome in a Bottle (GIAB) samples: NA12878 and NA24385. Our evaluation demonstrated that the consensus approach achieved the most balanced performance on intragenic inversion, yielding the highest F1 scores (0.9032 for NA12878 and 0.8824 for NA24385) compared to any single tool (Supplementary Fig. 21). Consequently, Invas utilizes these consensus DNA breakpoints, which are further refined by requiring supporting splice-reversal evidence from RNA-seq reads.

Candidate inverted splicing event detection

Optimized SV VCF files are examined to identify inversion coordinates in the reference genome. These coordinates are subsequently mapped against an annotated reference database encompassing both protein-coding and non-coding genes from the NCBI RefSeq repository by default, corresponding to the genome build accession number NCBI: GCA_000001405.20. Any gene locus overlapping the identified inversion coordinates is initially designated as a preliminary candidate, after which coordinates falling within complex genomic regions are removed23,73.

Next, putative inverted splicing events are identified through a rigorous evaluation of the alignment of remapped RNA-seq reads to the candidate loci (Fig. 1c). First, each region must demonstrate continuous read coverage exceeding the minimum depth threshold (β = 5). Second, both flanking regions must contain soft-clipped reads with counts surpassing the defined threshold (θ = 3). Third, all reads meeting these coverage and clipping criteria must have a mapping quality score of at least 30 and be classified as primary alignments. Finally, a gene locus is excluded if more than 95% of its aligned reads are multi-mapped, ensuring the specificity and reliability of the detected events.

Spliced fragments identification

For normal linear spliced RNA reads, overlapping RNA-seq alignments are clustered according to their positions on the reference genome. From these reads, a comprehensive list of splice junctions is compiled to delineate the full boundaries of the corresponding transcripts, spanning the entire gene locus and the specific intronic sites where reads arise or cease (Fig. 1b). These splice junctions, in conjunction with transcript start and end sites, segment the gene locus into various exonic or partially exonic regions, with additional details provided in Supplementary Fig. 22.

For inverted spliced RNA reads, clipped sequences flanking the inverted regions are collected, and their clipped portions are reverse-complemented for subsequent alignment. Specifically, clipped sequences derived from right-clipped reads are realigned in a leftward direction on the complementary strand of the reference genome, and vice versa. Given the broad gaps between exons and the high prevalence of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small indels within the genome, accurately mapping these short clipped sequences and identifying splice sites presents significant challenges. To address this, we applied an SSW-based dual-sequence alignment algorithm89 that takes into account the lengths of both the query and reference sequences for identifying splice events within the clipped portions (Fig. 1c). Further details are provided in Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24. After the optimal splice sites in the clipped sequences are identified, these regions and the inverted segments coalesce into inverted spliced fragments, as illustrated in Fig. 1d.

Constructing conjugate alternative splice graph

Given the spliced fragments T=t1,t2,…,tm, where m represents the number of distinct types of spliced fragments, we compute the abundance A=a1,a2,…,am based on the count of each fragment type within the set. All the segments constituting splice fragments are collected, denoted as S=s1,s2,…,sn, where n is the number of segments collected. In order to fully capture both canonical and inverted splicing linkage, we introduce a conjugate alternative splice graph (CASG) G = (U, V, E) at each local genome region from the spliced fragment, which is akin to the alternative splice graph (ASG) utilized by prevailing reference-based transcriptome assemblers. Invas incorporates several innovations. In the ASGs, nodes represent segments within splice fragments oriented in the 5′-3′ direction, and the edges between nodes denote the sequential connectivity of these segments. For each segment si, 1 ≤ i ≤ n, we derive two complementary chains sidi and si−di, where di ∈ {+, −}. The segment si can be either the positive chain si+ or the negative chain si−. For sidi(si−di), we present the start point (5′ end) as a vertex uidi(ui−di) and the end point (3′ end) as a vertex vidi(vi−di), uidi,ui−di∈U and vidi,vi−di∈V. We refer to uidi and vi−di as a pair of conjugate vertices; similarly, ui−di and uidi are also a pair of conjugate vertices. Denote a pair of conjugate vertices as u and u~. Graph pruning and transcripts path identification

After constructing the CASG, we perform graph pruning by eliminating nodes and edges with weights below the threshold β = 2. We then identify the heaviest path inspired by StringTie25, characterized as the path exhibiting maximum path-compatible read coverage between source and sink vertices. Constructing conjugate graph from CASG

Invas employed the generalized concept of network flows to calculate the coverage associated with the most compatible transcript path previously derived from the CASG. Invas constructs a CG, the conjugate vertices within the network are directly derived from the corresponding conjugate vertices in the CASG. Edges between these conjugate nodes are established based on the alignment of reads that start at one conjugate node and end at another, regardless of whether the reads are spliced or not. We set the capacity of each edge equal to the count of spliced fragments aligning with the corresponding node in the CASG. If a read aligns with splicing at n distinct locations, then that particular fragment alignment will contribute a fractional capacity of 1/n to each respective edge capacity. Our goal is to stitch compatible fragments together that will explain a maximal number of reads for the underlying transcript path in the CFN. We provide an example illustrating the construction of CASG in Supplementary Note 1. The edge construction within the CFN exhibits subtle differences from that of the CASG. During the CASG construction, Invas aims to reconstruct potential transcript structures based on RNA-seq reads, necessitating precise identification of splicing junctions and the connection of exons according to spliced read information. Therefore, edges are formed between two nodes only if reads span splicing junctions between these nodes. In contrast, when constructing the flow network for quantifying expression levels, Invas accounts for both spliced and unspliced reads. Edges in the flow network are established based on any reads that map between two nodes, irrespective of their splicing status. This is because the objective of expression quantification is to determine the coverage of each potential transcript, requiring the consideration of all reads that may contribute to transcript representation.

Due to sequencing errors and alignment inaccuracies34,74, some edges in the network may be constructed incorrectly, resulting in “noisy edges”. However, given the typical rates of sequencing and alignment errors, these erroneous edges constitute a minor proportion of all edges in the graph. Therefore, we establish a lower bound ξe for each edge capacity. That is, only edges with a capacity greater than ξe are utilized in the path. Iteration and transcripts filtering

Following the computation of the transcript’s expression level, we subtract the abundance of splice fragments associated with it. Concurrently, we update the weights of the nodes and edges within the CASG. Subsequently, Invas updates the per-base read coverage in the CASG. Following this, Invas iteratively performs graph pruning, path identification, and expression calculation, and updates CASG until certain stopping criteria are satisfied: First, the average depth of the most compatible path drops below the threshold η, set by default at 1.5 reads per base pair. Second, the maximum flow value drops below a fixed value of 1 (Supplementary Note 6).

After collecting all expressed transcripts and their expression levels, we further eliminate transcripts that are either too short or have particularly low expression levels. Transcripts shorter than L0 + Ne ⋅ L1 are filtered out, where Ne is the number of exons in the transcript. We adopted the standard parameters used by established assemblers (e.g., StringTie25, Cufflinks26), setting L0 = 150 and L1 = 50. These values serve as conservative lower bounds to exclude assembly noise. Additionally, transcripts with expression levels below a threshold μ are also excluded; for multi-exon transcripts, μ is set to 20, while for single-exon transcripts, it is set to 1.

Identification of inversion-associated tumor-specific antigens

We systematically analyzed transcripts disrupted by intragenic inversions to assess their potential to generate TSAs. First, transcripts containing inversions identified by Invas were processed to generate 33-nucleotide fragments (k-mers) using a sliding window approach (step size = 1). These contigs were underwent six-frame translation by transeq(v6.6.0.0)92 to capture all potential ORFs, regardless of canonical start codon presence. Translated sequences were filtered to retain peptide fragments ≥8 amino acids in length without internal stop codons, generating a comprehensive set of candidate tumor-derived peptides for immunogenicity prediction.

Candidate peptides were paired with sample-specific HLA genotypes to generate peptide-HLA combinations, excluding peptides containing unknown residues (’X’). HLA genotypes were obtained using OptiType(v1.3.5–hdfd78af_2)93. We used MHCflurry (v2.1.5)94 and NetMHCstabpan (v1.0)95 to comprehensively evaluate MHC-I associated peptides (MAPs) potential, including binding, processing and presentation. The thresholds were based on commonly used criteria for neoantigen and TSA prediction but were further tightened to define a high-confidence subset96–100. Peptides were retained only if they satisfied all of the following criteria: binding affinity <100 nM, binding and presentation ranks <0.1%, and processing and presentation scores >0.9, ensuring high MHC binding, processing, and presentation potential. To eliminate false positives arising from self-peptides, MAPs were compared against the human reference proteome (GRCh38, Ensembl release 104)101. Any peptide sequence matching known protein-coding regions was removed, ensuring final TSAs were truly non-self.

Central tolerance filtering was performed using RNA-seq data from 8 medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs) and 19 dendritic cells (DCs). TSA candidates whose coding regions exhibited consistently low or absent expression in both mTECs and DCs were retained, as they are less likely to induce central tolerance and thus more likely to be immunogenic. We used BamQuery102 to identify genomic coordinates with consistently low (Reads Per Hundred Million mapped reads (RPHM) < 8.55) across these samples, termed minimum covered sequences (MCS). RPHM was defined as the number of TSA-supporting reads multiplied by 108 and divided by the total number of primary RNA-seq reads. These MCS regions represent loci potentially invisible to central immune tolerance. Then, the expression of TSA candidates in normal tissues was assessed using uniformly processed GTEx RNA-seq data from the recount3 resource (v1.14.0) via the snapcount package, which quantifies read coverage based on the genomic coordinates of TSA-coding regions. TSA candidates exhibiting high expression (RPHM > 10) in normal peripheral tissues were excluded, while expression in immune-privileged tissues (including brain, nerve, and testis) was not considered for filtering.

Genomic locations were assigned to all TSA candidates by mapping reads containing TSA-coding sequences to the reference genome (GRCh38) using BLAT103. TSA candidates whose reads matched hypervariable regions (HLA, immunoglobulin, or TCR genes) were excluded. After this filtering step, the remaining TSA candidates were defined as high-confidence TSAs. Among them, Non-mutated TSAs were classified as aberrantly expressed TSA (aeTSA) candidates.

Representative aeTSAs were further analyzed at both the structural and genomic levels. AlphaFold 345 was used to model the interactions between selected aeTSAS and their corresponding MHC-I molecules, and the predicted aeTSA-MHC-I complex structures were visualized using MolViewSpec104. In addition, IGV105 was used to visualize the genomic positions of aeTSAs and their associated inversion events within the corresponding genes.

Somatic versus germline classification

We classified inversion events as somatic, germline, or uncertain using an integrated framework that combined matched tumor–normal WGS evidence, when available, with population structural variant (SV) resources (Supplementary Fig. 27). For cohorts with case–control designs (e.g., HGSOC, HCC, HNC, LUNGC), classification relied on matched tumor–normal data; in cohorts lacking matched controls (e.g., AML, PCOS, SOFT), events were provisionally assigned as uncertain.

Input data comprised: (i) consensus-called SV breakpoints from tumor WGS and, when available, matched-normal WGS; (ii) population SV catalogs (gnomAD-SV, DGV/dbVar, 1000 Genomes SV, and Human Pangenome Reference Consortium assemblies); and (iii) breakpoint colocalization within ±50 bp with orientation concordance. Tumor support was quantified using split reads (SR), discordant pairs (DP), and local read-depth patterns consistent with inversion architecture. Normal support was assessed by SR/DP at each breakpoint and by coverage, with ≥20 × denoting sufficient power to assert absence. Population evidence was evaluated by presence/absence and AF across resources, incorporating ancestry-stratified AF where available.

Prespecified decision rules were applied. Somatic inversions required: tumor support of ≥2SR and/or ≥3DP at both breakpoints and tumor variant allele fraction (VAF) ≥0.05 (relaxed to ≥0.03 for purity ≥70%); absence in the matched normal, defined as 0SR and 0–1DP at both breakpoints with ≥20× coverage at each; and a 95% exact binomial upper bound for normal VAF ≤0.01. Germline inversions were assigned when present in both tumor and matched normal WGS (each with ≥2 SR and/or ≥3 DP) or detected in normal RNA-seq by ≥2 inversion-junction reads; alternatively, presence in population databases at AF ≥0.1% with coordinate and orientation concordance was sufficient, contingent on detectability in our normals given coverage. Variants were classified as uncertain in cohorts without matched controls and in cases with insufficient evidence owing to low or uneven coverage, localization to segmental duplications or blacklist regions, or discordant signals across data modalities.

Experiment settings

In silico processing

To simulate intragenic inversion events, we randomly sampled 1151 target genes from the 24 primary chromosomes of the GRCh38 reference genome. Gene selection was based on the GENCODE V47 annotation106, which represents a comprehensive merge of HAVANA manual annotations and Ensembl automated annotations. We excluded genes located in segmental duplication Regions and Problematic Regions23,73 of the Genome identified using the segmental duplication tracks from the UCSC Genome Browser, as well as genes overlapping centromeric regions107.

In total, we designed 42,000 intragenic inversion instances. These instances included cases of incomplete exon inversion, multiple exon inversions, single exon inversion, intron-retained inversion, alternative-splicing-associated inversion, and inversions disturbing a single intron. The scale of simulation is provided in Supplementary Tables 2–4. The distribution of inversion size is shown in Supplementary Fig. 28. For WGS data, we first generated the ground truth by integrating the reverse complement sequences of the inversion regions. Using wgsim108, we then produced FASTQ files containing the simulated sequencing reads and aligned these reads to the GRCh38 reference genome with bwa. Candidate SV breakpoints were subsequently identified using a multi-SV caller pipeline.

For RNA-seq simulations, we first extracted the genomic coordinates of all isoforms for the target genes. We then employed BioPython’s sequence module109 to generate both normal transcripts and transcripts with intragenic inversion events. These transcripts were randomly assigned relative proportions prior to simulation. Next, we used wgsim108 to simulate next-generation sequencing data and aligned the resulting RNA-seq reads to the GRCh38 reference using Hisat282.

To assess the impact of sequencing depth on software performance, we set the WGS coverage from 30× to 110× in increments of 20× and the RNA-seq depth from 150× to 1050× in increments of 150×.
