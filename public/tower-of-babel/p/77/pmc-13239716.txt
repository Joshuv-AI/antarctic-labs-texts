A fovea‐centered retinal signature linked to plasma biomarkers in prodromal Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Sara Teijeira‐Portas, Ane Murueta‐Goyena, Aitor Zamora‐Otaola, Raquel Perez, Manuel Fernandez, Javier Almeida, Elisa Blanco, Tirso Gonzalez, Maitane Barrenechea, Unai Ayala, Garazi Zuazo, Juan Carlos Gomez‐Esteban, Marian Acera, Rocío Del Pino, Iñigo Gabilondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood biomarkers detect Alzheimer's disease (AD), but tissue correlates of early tau pathology and glial activation remain unclear. We investigated whether multimodal retinal measures capture a focal prodromal AD (pAD) pattern linked to plasma biomarkers.METHODSThirty‐eight individuals with pAD and 40 controls underwent optical coherence tomography (OCT), OCT‐angiography (OCT‐A), and electroretinography (ERG). Plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau181, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) Aβ42 and Aβ40 were quantified, adjusting for age, sex, and vascular risk factors.RESULTSBetween‐group differences were restricted to parafoveal OCT‐A measures. Within the pAD group, higher p‐tau217/Aβ42 and GFAP showed consistent associations with foveal measures: increased ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness, capillary density, altered foveal avascular zone (FAZ), and reduced photopic negative‐response amplitude. These associations were absent in controls. Mediation analyses indicated that foveal GCIPL thickness largely accounted for blood biomarker–microvascular/ERG associations.DISCUSSIONpAD exhibits a spatially consistent, fovea‐centered neurovascular and functional signature‐tracking plasma tau phosphorylation and glial activation, supporting the fovea as a sensitive region in early disease‐related processes.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) has a prolonged prodromal phase during which pathological processes accumulate before dementia onset. Blood‐based measures, particularly plasma hyperphosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217) 1 and the p‐tau217/amyloid beta (Aβ)42 ratio, 2 , 3 enable the reliable detection of AD biology but do not capture organ‐level correlates of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, underscoring the need for complementary tissue‐based biomarkers.

The retina is an accessible in vivo surrogate for central CNS pathology because of its shared embryological origin and vulnerability to neurodegeneration. 4 Non‐invasive imaging techniques such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT‐angiography (OCT‐A) enable high‐resolution assessment of the retinal structure and microvasculature, positioning the retina as a practical and biologically meaningful “window” into the neurovascular unit. OCT has consistently demonstrated thinning of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) and the ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer complex (GCIPL) in AD, with associations with cognitive decline and visual dysfunction. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Longitudinal evidence further suggest that OCT parameters may predict progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to AD dementia. 7 , 11 The advent of OCT‐A has expanded retinal biomarker assessment by enabling dye‐free quantification of vascular alterations, including reduced perfusion density, foveal avascular zone (FAZ) abnormalities, and altered vascular complexity, consistent with early microangiopathy and neurovascular remodeling in AD. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, retinal structural and vascular measures alone may not capture early neuronal dysfunction. Electrophysiological alterations, particularly detectable by the photopic negative response (phNR), may precede overt structural loss and reflect early retinal ganglion cell dysfunction. 20 Despite these advances, prior studies have typically examined OCT, OCT‐A, or electroretinography (ERG) in isolation, and relied on global or parafoveal metrics that may obscure focal pathology. More importantly, no study has systematically integrated multimodal retinal imaging and electrophysiology with state‐of‐the‐art plasma AD biomarkers, including phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217) and tau/amyloid ratios in early‐stage AD.

We combined OCT, OCT‐A, and full‐field photopic ERG to investigate retinal structural, vascular, and functional correlates of plasma biomarkers in prodromal AD.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The literature supports retinal imaging and electrophysiology as promising biomarkers across the Alzheimer's disease continuum. However, prior studies have largely examined individual retinal modalities in isolation and have seldom integrated structural, vascular, and functional retinal measures with state‐of‐the‐art plasma Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers. In particular, evidence linking retinal readouts with plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217) and tau/amyloid ratios in prodromal AD remains limited. Interpretation: These findings support the retina—particularly the foveal region—as a sensitive site for detecting early Alzheimer's disease–related neurobiological changes. The coupling of plasma AD biomarkers with fovea‐centered structural, vascular, and functional retinal alterations suggests that early AD pathology may manifest through localized neuroinflammatory and neurovascular processes rather than diffuse neurodegeneration. Central macular thickening appears to play a pivotal intermediary role, potentially reflecting glial activation that links systemic AD pathology to retinal vascular dysregulation and dysfunction in the prodromal stage. Future directions: Longitudinal, multicenter studies are needed to determine whether this fovea‐centered retinal signature predicts disease progression and therapeutic response, and to harmonize optical coherence tomography (OCT), OCT‐angiography, and electroretinography (ERG) acquisition within biomarker‐defined frameworks of Alzheimer's disease.

METHODS

Study population

This cross‐sectional analysis included baseline data from 38 individuals with prodromal AD (pAD) and 40 cognitively unimpaired controls (Figure S1) enrolled between January 2024 and August 2025, in the BegiAlz cohort. 21 Inclusion criteria for pAD were: age between 50 and 80 years; abnormal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers consistent with AD neuropathologic change according to the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) research framework, 22 and a clinical diagnosis of MCI attributable to AD. CSF biomarker abnormality was defined using assay‐specific validated cutoffs for the fully automated LUMIPULSE G platform, as described previously in independent analytical and clinical validation studies. 23

Controls showed normal performance on standardized neuropsychological testing and no history of neurological or psychiatric disease. Controls were frequency‐matched to the pAD group by age and sex. Only participants with complete OCT assessments and available plasma biomarker data were included in the final analyses.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included neurological, psychiatric, systemic, or ophthalmological conditions (e.g., glaucoma, spherical equivalent refractive error >4D, astigmatism >3D, or any known retinal or optic nerve pathology) known to affect retinal structure, visual function, or cognition, in accordance with OSCAR‐IB criteria. 24 All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation to ensure eligibility, including refraction, best‐corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and retinal imaging with OCT.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Comité de Ética en Investigación con Medicamentos–Euskadi (CEIm‐E; approval code PI2023126). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical assessment

Baseline demographic and clinical data included age, sex, history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, current smoking status, and current medications. To summarize vascular risk burden, we calculated a study‐specific vascular risk factor score (VRS) as the unweighted sum of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and current smoking (1 point each; range, 0–4). Global cognitive status was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR).

Visual and cognitive evaluations

Visual function was evaluated using the Precision Vision Visual Acuity Test (PVVAT) digital software (Precision Vision, Woodstock, IL) at 4 m under controlled lighting. Binocular best‐corrected high‐ and low‐contrast visual acuity (VA) were assessed using standardized letter charts. Contrast sensitivity was measured using letters of decreasing contrast (60% to 1%), recording the lowest detectable contrast and total number of correct responses. Color vision was evaluated using Ishihara plates included in the PVVAT software and the Farnsworth D‐15 test, for which the Color Confusion Index (CCI) was calculated, with higher values indicating greater difficulty in distinguishing colors.

Cognitive performance was evaluated using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), alongside tests of executive function and processing speed (Stroop test and Trail Making Test [TMT] Parts A and B).

Retinal OCT and OCT angiography

Retinal imaging was performed using the Spectralis OCT system (HRA2 Acquisition Module version 6.16.6.0, Heidelberg Engineering, Germany). Macular volumetric scans (20° × 20°, 25 horizontal lines) were segmented with built‐in software. All OCT images met established OSCAR‐IB quality criteria. 24

Segmented macular scans were exported from the Spectralis software in “.vol” format and processed using the RETIMAT software developed by Mondragon University and IIS Biobizkaia. 25 The average thickness within the foveal zone (central 1‐mm diameter disc), parafoveal area (3‐mm diameter ring), and perifoveal area (3‐ to 6‐mm diameter ring) were computed for five layer complexes with a special focus on ganglion cell‐inner plexiform complex (or GCIPL)

Macular OCT‐A (10° × 10°) were exported as 768 × 768‐pixel JPEG files to obtain quantitative microvascular metrics (vessel density, fractal dimension, lacunarity, and FAZ area and circularity) for the superficial and deep vascular complexes using MATLAB‐based image processing pipelines. 26 Foveal (1‐mm diameter disc) and parafoveal (2.5‐mm diameter ring) regions were defined a priori to ensure consistent spatial analysis. All OCT‐A acquisitions had a quality score of 30 or higher.

Electroretinography

Full‐field photopic electroretinography (ERG) was performed according to International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) standards to assess retinal ganglion cell function via the phNR. 27 The phNR amplitude and implicit time were quantified from artifact‐free recordings. Only high‐quality, artifact‐free ERG waveforms were included in the analyses (n = 38 pAD; n = 35 controls).

Plasma biomarker assessment

Blood samples were collected and processed following standardized preanalytical procedures. 28 Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 10 min at room temperature, immediately aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until analysis. All samples were collected within 1 month of OCT imaging and ERG recordings. Plasma AD biomarkers, including phosphorylated tau (p‐tau217 and p‐tau181), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), amyloid beta (Aβ)1‐42, Aβ1‐40, and derived ratios, were quantified using the fully automated LUMIPULSE G immunoassay platform. 29

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in combination with visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Between‐group comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The OCT, OCT‐A, and ERG measurements of both eyes were averaged to account for inter‐eye within‐patient dependencies unless any pathological condition affected one of the eyes, in which case only the healthy eye was included in the analysis.

Pearson correlation analyses were treated as exploratory and used to identify spatial patterns between plasma AD biomarkers and retinal structural and microvascular measures derived from OCT and OCT‐A.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess (1) group differences between individuals with pAD and cognitively unimpaired controls, and (2) associations between plasma AD biomarkers and multimodal retinal measures derived from OCT, OCT‐A, and full‐field ERG within each group, focusing on a priori–defined retinal regions and selected plasma biomarkers identified in bivariate correlations. For group differences, diagnostic group was the primary independent variable, whereas within‐group analyses modeled plasma AD biomarkers as predictors of interest. All regression analyses were adjusted for age, sex, and VRS. When cognitive outcomes were analyzed, models were additionally adjusted for years of education. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparison was applied. To assess the robustness of the regression estimates, we performed bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) and derived percentile‐based confidence intervals (CIs) for the main associations. This approach evaluated effect size stability and reduced the influence of sampling variability.

Mediation analyses were conducted with foveal GCIPL thickness specified as the mediator, plasma AD biomarkers as predictors, and OCT‐A‐ or ERG‐derived measures as outcomes. The significance of indirect effects was estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping with 5000 resamples. All models were adjusted for age, sex, and the VRS. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), with a two‐tailed significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Demographics and clinical characteristics

The overall mean (SD) age was 67.9 (5.6) years and 44.9% of participants were female. Baseline demographic, clinical, and cognitive characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Controls and participants with pAD were comparable in age, sex distribution, depressive symptoms, and vascular risk factors. Participants with pAD had fewer years of formal education than controls. Standard measures of visual function were poorer in the pAD group. As expected, the pAD group showed significantly lower performance on global cognitive measures and on tests of processing speed and executive function (Table S1). Most participants with pAD were classified as CDR = 0.5 (94.7%), and estimated disease duration ranged from 0.5 to 1.2 years, consistent with an early symptomatic stage.

Plasma biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease

The interval between plasma sampling and OCT/ERG acquisition (days) was short and comparable between groups (median [interquartile range (IQR)], controls 10.5 [IQR 6–24.5], and pAD 10.5 [IQR 7–19.5]).

Plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and GFAP concentrations were significantly higher in pAD than in cognitively unimpaired controls, whereas the Aβ42/40 ratio was lower (all p’s < 0.001; Table 1). Among individual biomarkers, p‐tau217 showed the highest discriminatory performance between groups (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.974; DeLong 95% CI, 0.944–1.000). Plasma Aβ42/40, GFAP, and p‐tau181 were significantly associated with global cognitive measures. The p‐tau217/Aβ42 ratio showed the strongest and most extensive cognitive associations, relating to global cognition (MoCA and RBANS total score) as well as executive function and processing speed (TMT‐B, time(s)). No plasma biomarker, whether considered individually or as a ratio, was significantly associated with disease duration.

Group differences in retinal measures

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, and vascular risk score, individuals with pAD exhibited selective alterations in retinal microvascular parameters, predominantly localized to the fovea‐centered macular region. Specifically, within the superficial vascular complex (SVC), pAD participants showed reduced vessel density and decreased FAZ circularity compared with controls in the central 2.5‐mm region (Table 1). However, these between‐group differences did not survive correction for multiple comparisons.

Structural retinal thickness measures derived from OCT, including macular RNFL, GCIPL, and other inner and outer retinal layers, did not differ significantly between groups in either the foveal or parafoveal regions (Table S2). Similarly, full‐field ERG measures showed no significant between‐group differences in phNR amplitude or implicit time, indicating preserved global retinal ganglion cell electrophysiological function at this prodromal stage.

Associations between plasma biomarkers and retinal measures

Exploratory bivariate correlation analyses, performed to identify patterns of association between plasma biomarkers and retinal measures, are shown in Figure S2. In the pAD group, a consistent spatial pattern was observed, with associations localized predominantly to the foveal region and involving mainly the inner retinal layers, whereas no clear pattern was evident in cognitively unimpaired controls. In pAD, the plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 ratio correlated positively with foveal GCIPL thickness and fovea‐centered microvascular metrics, including vessel density in both SVC and deep vascular complex (DVC). Plasma GFAP showed a similar, slightly broader pattern of correlations with foveal structural and vascular parameters. The associations were weaker in parafoveal regions.

These findings were further evaluated using confounder‐adjusted multivariable linear regression models (Table 2). Standardized beta coefficients confirmed that the strongest associations were localized to the foveal region, particularly for GCIPL thickness and microvascular parameters in relation to plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 and GFAP (Figure 1). Both biomarkers were associated with increased foveal GCIPL thickness. Higher p‐tau217/Aβ42 was also associated with increased vessel density in the foveal SVC, whereas GFAP showed broader fovea‐centered microvascular associations, including increased vessel density, higher fractal dimension and lacunarity, and reduced FAZ area in both vascular complexes. These associations were spatially restricted to the foveal region, with no corresponding parafoveal effects. Regarding retinal function, higher p‐tau217/Aβ42 was associated with reduced phNR amplitude on full‐field ERG, with no effect on implicit time. Foveal—but not parafoveal—GCIPL thickness was strongly associated with phNR amplitude, supporting a localized structure–function relationship. None of these associations was observed in controls.

Significant associations were observed primarily in the pAD group and were localized to the foveal region, whereas no consistent relationships were detected in cognitively unimpaired controls. The estimated β coefficients and p‐values are reported in Table 2.

Bootstrap analyses confirmed the stability of these findings, with consistent effect size estimates and CIs not crossing zero for the main fovea‐centered associations, except for the p‐tau217/Aβ42–phNR relationship. Notably, some associations that were borderline in the original models (e.g., foveal fractal dimension and FAZ area) showed increased stability after resampling (Table S3).

Mediation of retinal microvascular and functional measures by foveal GCIPL thickness

To examine whether focal retinal neurostructural changes mediate the relationship between systemic AD pathology and retinal microvascular or electrophysiological alterations, mediation analyses were performed using plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 and GFAP as predictors, foveal retinal outcomes as dependent variables, and foveal GCIPL thickness as the mediator. Full mediation models are provided in Table S4 and Figure S3.

The association between plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 and foveal SVC vessel density was fully mediated by foveal GCIPL thickness, with a significant indirect effect and no remaining direct effect after inclusion of the mediator (Figure 2). A similar pattern was observed for retinal functional integrity: the association between p‐tau217/Aβ42 and reduced phNR amplitude was largely mediated by foveal GCIPL thickness, accounting for ≈70% of the total effect.

Mediation analyses involving plasma GFAP demonstrated a consistent, predominantly indirect pattern. Associations between GFAP and multiple foveal OCT‐A microvascular metrics—including vessel density, fractal dimension, lacunarity, and FAZ area—were largely or entirely mediated by foveal GCIPL thickness across both superficial and deep vascular complexes. Together, these findings indicate that fovea‐centered retinal neurostructural alterations constitute a key mechanistic pathway linking systemic tau‐related pathology and glial activation to downstream retinal microvascular and functional changes in pAD.

DISCUSSION

In this multimodal study integrating OCT, OCT‐A, ERG, and plasma biomarkers, we identified a spatially localized, fovea‐centered retinal pattern in pAD, consistently associated with abnormal tau phosphorylation and glial activation in the blood. This focal pattern spans structural, microvascular, and functional domains. Despite comparable demographics and vascular risk profiles, individuals with pAD showed markedly higher plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and GFAP levels, along with lower Aβ42/40 ratios. These systemic biomarker alterations were associated specifically with fovea‐centered retinal findings, including increased GCIPL thickness, altered capillary density and FAZ geometry, and reduced phNR amplitude. Of note, mediation analyses demonstrated that foveal GCIPL thickness acted as an intermediate mechanism linking plasma biomarkers to retinal microvascular and electrophysiological changes. These findings support a focal, fovea‐centered retinal phenotype in pAD that may reflect early AD‐related neurovascular and neuroinflammatory processes, positioning the fovea as a sensitive, accessible window into these changes. We therefore interpret this pattern as a biologically coherent and hypothesis‐generating signature, pending validation in larger and longitudinal cohorts.

Previous retinal studies in MCI and AD provide important context for these findings. Structural OCT investigations have consistently reported thinning of inner retinal layers—particularly the RNFL and GCIPL—in MCI and AD compared with cognitively healthy individuals. 13 , 14 , 15 , 19 , 30 Complementary OCT‐A studies have described reduced parafoveal capillary density, FAZ enlargement or distortion, and other microvascular abnormalities indicative of retinal small‐vessel disease in AD. 14 , 15 , 31 , 32 Similarly, full‐field ERG studies have demonstrated inner retinal dysfunction in MCI and AD, including reduced phNR amplitudes and prolonged implicit times. 20 , 33 , 34 , 35 Together, these findings support the presence of widespread retinal neurodegeneration and microangiopathy in AD. However, direct links between such retinal alterations and peripheral AD biomarkers have remained limited.

Recent reviews and experimental studies have begun to bridge this gap by examining associations between systemic AD biomarkers, brain pathology, and retinal changes. 36 , 37 , 38 Plasma p‐tau levels have been correlated with ERG measures such as phNR, suggesting that tau pathology may directly impair retinal ganglion cell function. 20 , 34 Postmortem analyses by Hart de Ruyter et al. demonstrated the presence of hallmark AD proteins, including p‐tau and Aβ, in the retina, indicating that retinal pathology mirrors cerebral pathology in AD. 39 In addition, Kwapong et al. reported associations between retinal microvascular alterations on fundus imaging and plasma AD biomarkers, as well as markers of cerebral small‐vessel disease. 12 Within this framework, our study extends the literature by demonstrating robust associations between plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 and GFAP and multiple fovea‐centered retinal measures across OCT, OCT‐A, and ERG, reinforcing the rationale for integrating retinal imaging with blood‐based biomarkers in early AD detection strategies. A key contribution is the observation that AD‐related retinal changes appear predominantly localized to the fovea. Most prior studies relied on global or parafoveal averages and therefore did not detect region‐specific vulnerability. 8 , 40 , 41 , 42 This focused topographic approach likely enhanced sensitivity to early localized pathology diluted in broader analyses. These findings suggest that different retinal regions may capture complementary dimensions of disease expression, with the fovea reflecting biomarker‐coupled sensitivity and the parafovea capturing more overt phenotypic differences. To our knowledge, no previous OCT, OCT‐A, or ERG study has demonstrated a fovea‐centric relationship between retinal abnormalities and plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 or GFAP. The co‐localization of structural, vascular, and functional changes suggests that pAD affects the retinal neurovascular unit as an integrated system rather than targeting isolated components. These findings support a perspective in which the earliest retinal manifestations of AD are focal rather than diffuse, positioning the fovea as a strategic target for early biomarker development.

The observation that foveal GCIPL thickness was increased—rather than decreased—in association with higher plasma p‐tau217/Aβ42 and GFAP is particularly noteworthy. This pattern is consistent with an early neuroinflammatory or gliotic response preceding overt neurodegeneration. Experimental and pathological studies indicate that early tau accumulation can induce axonal and dendritic swelling, synaptic dysfunction, and microglial activation, resulting in transient thickening of inner retinal layers before subsequent atrophy. 36 , 43 , 44 , 45 In parallel, Müller glial activation, reflected by elevated GFAP, may contribute to GCIPL swelling. We also observed increased foveal microvascular metrics, which likely reflect compensatory vascular remodeling in response to localized metabolic stress or inflammation. The fovea's high metabolic demand, reliance on tight neurovascular coupling, 46 and distinctive Müller cell architecture 47 may render this region particularly susceptible to subtle disruptions in neuronal–glial–vascular interactions. Our mediation analyses are consistent with a hierarchical model in which early tau‐related neuronal stress may induce foveal GCIPL changes that subsequently drive vascular and electrophysiological alterations.

As this study is cross‐sectional, we cannot determine the temporal dynamics of foveal alterations. Our findings show an association between higher plasma p‐tau217 and GFAP levels and increased foveal GCIPL thickness, which may reflect an early, transient response to neuronal stress or localized glial/vascular activation. As these processes plateau, the relationship with plasma biomarkers may attenuate, potentially preceding parafoveal thinning and retinal atrophy, as observed in longitudinal Parkinson's studies. 48 Future longitudinal analyses in the BegiAlz cohort are essential to clarify the trajectory of these retinal changes.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the large number of biomarker–retina comparisons increases the risk of type I error. Exploratory correlations here were intended to identify spatial patterns rather than provide confirmatory inference. Main findings were supported by multivariable models focused on specific biomarkers and retinal regions, reducing multiple‐comparison concerns. Although strict correction attenuated individual statistical significance, the convergence of findings across correlation patterns, adjusted regression models, and mediation analyses—together with the stability of estimates confirmed by bootstrapping, indicating that results are not driven by sampling variability—supports a biologically coherent, fovea‐centered retinal pattern linked to early AD pathology. Despite formal correction for multiple comparisons, the spatial consistency and cross‐modal convergence of effects remained preserved. Second, the modest sample size reflects strict inclusion criteria for biomarker‐confirmed pAD and high‐quality multimodal imaging. Although comparable to similar studies with deeply phenotyped populations, 49 , 50 this limits generalizability. Finally, the lack of CSF biomarker confirmation in controls raises the possibility of undetected preclinical AD. Future work with larger prospective cohorts and longitudinal follow‐up of biomarker‐confirmed control groups will be important to confirm the reproducibility and specificity of the observed foveal retinal signatures and explore their temporal progression across disease stages.

Notwithstanding these limitations, strengths include a comprehensive multimodal retinal approach (OCT, OCT‐A, ERG) assessing structural, vascular, and functional changes, and the use of state‐of‐the‐art plasma biomarkers, particularly p‐tau217, linking retinal measures to systemic AD pathology. Adjustment for key confounders and bootstrapping supports the robustness of observed associations.

In summary, pAD appears to be characterized by anatomically focal, fovea‐centered alterations in retinal structure, microvasculature, and function that closely track plasma markers of tau pathology and glial activation. These findings are consistent with shared neurovascular and neuroinflammatory mechanisms linking the eye and brain in early AD and highlight multimodal retinal assessment as a promising, non‐invasive complement to emerging blood‐based biomarker strategies. If validated, this approach may support the use of targeted, topography‐driven retinal assessment as a scalable complement to blood‐based biomarker strategies in early AD.
