Decoding the ankle–brachial index in peripheral artery disease: coronary versus valvular calcification
Authors: Marcelo Heron Petri, Payam Haftbaradaran Esfahani, Oscar Plunde, Magnus Bäck
Published: 2025

This editorial refers to ‘Association of ankle-brachial index with coronary and extracoronary calcification in older adults: ARIC study’, by E.C. Bansah et al., https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjopen/oeaf133.

Atherosclerosis in peripheral artery disease (PAD) frequently overlaps with coronary and cerebrovascular disease, although isolated PAD also occurs. The ankle–brachial index (ABI) is the most widely used non-invasive diagnostic tool, yet interpretation requires caution: the comparison may involve diseased vessels at both the ankle and the arm. Still, ABI remains clinically central; values below 0.90 confirm PAD,1 whereas values above 1.40 generally indicate noncompressible, calcified arteries and necessitate adjunctive measures such as toe pressure.1 Circulating proteins have also been identified as markers of PAD.2

Vascular calcification, defined by the deposition of calcium phosphate within the arterial wall, reduces vascular elasticity and impairs compliance to pulsatile blood flow. This process is not confined to peripheral arteries but also affects the coronary circulation and cardiac valves. Importantly, these sites may be affected both independently and in parallel, which suggests that calcification processes in different vascular beds and valvular calcification are not governed by a single unifying process.

In this issue of European Heart Journal Open, Bansaha and colleagues3 report associations between ABI and both coronary and extracoronary calcification. A low ABI (<0.90) was strongly associated with coronary artery calcification (CAC), whereas a high ABI (>1.30) was associated with aortic valve calcification (AVC) (Figure 1). These findings support the concept that a low ABI reflects atherosclerosis of the lower extremity arteries, which shares pathophysiologic features with coronary artery disease. In contrast, the marked calcification that reduces arterial compressibility in PAD may represent a distinct process that parallels the development of calcific valvular heart disease (Figure 1). These observations, however, are derived from cross-sectional data, and causal relationships between atherosclerosis in the lower extremities and calcification in the coronary arteries and valves remain to be established. Notably, CAC has been shown to predict both abnormal ABI and clinical PAD independently of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and baseline ABI,4 suggesting that shared risk factors may not fully account for the overlap of these disease processes.

The divergent associations of low ABI with CAC and high ABI with AVC3 (Figure 1) may also reflect different stages in the trajectory of PAD, with later disease marked by systemic calcification. Reduced compressibility of lower extremity arteries contributes to vascular stiffness, which can be assessed by, for example, pulse wave velocity (PWV) and the cardio–ankle vascular index (CAVI).5 The relationship between vascular stiffness and valvular calcification has been examined in several studies, some of which have demonstrated worsened stiffness after transcatheter or surgical aortic valve replacement.6 One of the latter studies, however, reported higher ABI values in patients with aortic stenosis compared with those with aortic regurgitation.7 Calcific aortic valve stenosis may hence underestimate PWV and CAVI, but that underlying arterial stiffness is unmasked after removal of the aortic valve obstruction, underscoring the complex interplay between vascular stiffness, valvular disease, and ABI interpretation.

Taken together, these observations highlight the heterogeneity of cardiovascular calcification. Low ABI aligns with coronary artery atherosclerosis, whereas high ABI points towards valve calcification. These differential patterns provide support for distinct site-specific mechanisms, albeit partly overlapping, rather than a uniform cardiovascular calcification pathway.
