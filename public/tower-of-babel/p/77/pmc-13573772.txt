Molecules to medicine: advances in metabolomics for natural product drug discovery
Authors: Victoria Deleray, Pieter C Dorrestein, Nicole E Avalon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in metabolomics are accelerating natural product (NP) drug discovery. NPs possess diverse biological relevance and comprise a significant portion of our modern pharmacopeia. We highlight studies from the past two years with innovative discovery techniques, ranging from small sample analyses to large-scale data-driven approaches. We focus on nuclear magnetic resonance- and mass spectrometry-based metabolomics for their broad use and greatest advancements in the field. We highlight strategies that utilize computational tools to enable prioritization of samples based on structural novelty, cross-referencing structural data with bioactivity, and the development of innovative annotation techniques that surpass common library matching methods. We also look at the trajectory of metabolomic discovery of NPs over the last decade to inform how these platforms may further evolve. The goal is to enhance the likelihood and improve the efficiency of discovering NPs with pharmaceutical potential, while strategically harnessing data in order to reduce rediscovery and methodological redundancy.

Introduction

Natural products (NPs) are chemically diverse molecules found in all kingdoms of life that confer a competitive advantage to the producing organism and often have biological activity outside of the organism’s environmental milieu. Outside of their ecosystem, these molecules are repurposed to benefit human health and other biotechnological applications. NPs have played a critical role in the modern pharmacopeia, comprising 50–60% of FDA-approved medications [1]. Many NPs remain uncharacterized, however, and are without known medicinal functions or biotechnological applications. Additionally, the 2011 Roche ruling has made establishing the biological activity of NPs more relevant for drug companies and filing patents [2]. For these reasons, drug discovery from natural sources continues with an ongoing need for streamlined structural characterization and efficient biological profiling to discover novel therapeutics.

The emergence of metabolomics, the study of all molecules in a biological system utilizing a range of analytical and computational tools, is enabling researchers to expand the breadth of what is identified in biological samples. Analytical tools have been used since the early 20th century, with instrumentation including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), circular dichroism, single-molecule atomic force microscopy, various types of X-ray crystallography, and mass spectrometry (MS) [3].

NMR remains critical in formal structure elucidation of NPs, though NMR analysis requires a unique and increasingly rare skill set. Over the last 15 years, MS methods have become one of the leading technologies in NP discovery workflows in ways that reach well beyond the traditional use of MS for molecular formula generation. The number of MS-based workflows to accompany traditional NMR-based approaches is growing (Figure 1), primarily from high-resolution LC-MS (and LC-MS/MS) analysis.

The evolution of analytical instrumentation and the creation of public tools, data repositories, and analysis platforms, covered in depth previously [4], have accelerated the growth of the metabolomics field and aided in dereplication, a critical facet in the field of NPs in order to minimize re-isolation and reduce rediscovery [5].

With the shift from using instruments to characterize known structures to using a suite of technologies in the initial discovery phase of unknown NPs, methodologies now focus on the creation and utility of discovery tools for structure annotation, biological activity screening, and more. In this review, we identify case studies that have and continue to inform metabolomics tools of tomorrow while highlighting tools that have followed the needs of discovery-based science (Figure 2). These studies highlight the use of various analytical techniques, proficiency in computational tools for efficiency and data interpretation, and the critical nature of data availability based upon the FAIR data initiative, which follows the principles of findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability (FAIR) [6].

Metabolomic approaches for natural product drug discovery: small-scale and large-scale

NMR continues to be one of the most powerful tools for structure elucidation of small molecules, a quality that has only increased with hardware enhancements (e.g. cryoprobes, increased magnet strength) and the development of novel analysis methods (e.g. ultra-high resolution band-selective HSQC, LC-coupled NMR, and nano-scale identification) [7,8], which have resulted in substantial improvements in sensitivity. Because NMR spectra are highly reproducible, repositories of experimental NMR spectra and chemical shift data have been created in the last 10 years [9]. The most recent of these repositories is NP-MRD, which includes > 200,000 spectra and is open access, positioning it as a key companion to established MS databases [10].

There is, however, an increasing reliance of MS in workflows for first pass discovery to identify undiscovered small molecules and enhance efficiency of structure elucidation (Figure 1). This growth is partly due to high-resolution mass spectrometers becoming more accessible. Fifteen years ago, most NP scientists did not have routine access to high-resolution MS instrumentation such as Q-TOFs, FT-ICRs, or Orbitraps. Since then, high-resolution instruments have much lower acquisition and operational costs, and their use is becoming routine. Globally, only a few NMR vendors remain, while an increasing while an increasing number of companies are developing new instrumentation for MS. This facilitated the growth of MS as the starting point for many metabolomic workflows by broadening availability and support of the instrumentation [4,9].

Several additional factors have contributed to the increasing reliance on MS for compound discovery, including technological and engineering advancements that led to increased sensitivity and capacity of spectroscopic and MS analyses [11]. Once serving as a method to determine the chemical formula of a given analyte, MS is now utilized to prioritize compounds, automatically dereplicate previously discovered metabolites, and triangulate with other metrics, including biosynthesis and bioactivity. Even with access to high-resolution instrumentation and ancillary tools in metabolomic evaluation, there are cases where specialized ultra-high-resolution instruments are still needed. Ultra-high resolution MS, which can achieve 10,000,000 resolution, such as 21Tesla FT-ICR instruments, has been used to identify critical isotopic patterns and hyperfine splitting of those patterns (e.g. separation of the ion signals of the 34S, 2*13C, and +2 isotope of bromines) and refine the molecular formula for newly characterized compounds (e.g. leptochelin A) [28]. This was critical in this case, as the molecular formula had escaped accurate molecular formula predictions from high-resolution Orbitrap and Q-TOF-based systems.

Many advances in MS rely on an instrumental property named tandem mass spectrometry (known as MS/MS), a coupled m/z analysis of a measured ion resulting from a fragmentation of ions within the mass spectrometer. Collision-induced dissociation is a common fragmentation method, creating a molecular fingerprint. This, in turn, enables the widespread use of data libraries, allowing for matching of experimental spectra to spectra of known compounds [5,9]. Spectral matching has been instrumental for NP dereplication and profiling of untargeted metabolomics data, as the experimentalist can reliably identify thousands of detected compounds within minutes without needing to manually assign fragment peaks or perform extensive analytical methods to assign planar structure [5]. Engineering of high-throughput (HT) mechanisms, such as automation and multiplexing have further extended the impact of metabolomics [12,13]. While many discovery-based approaches have utilized LC-MS for its ability to separate a diversity of metabolomic features and for its translatability to downstream LC-based isolation, alternative methods exist and have been covered previously [14].

Some software incorporates machine learning (ML) into discovery workflows, most notably used in NMR-based approaches [15,16]. However, computational solutions that give an accurate structure and/or predicted activity from MS are few and far between. Nearly every computational solution in MS requires additional experimental analysis, including formal NMR-based structure elucidation. A critical component to these advancements has been data availability and community curation efforts [5]. Repositories for NMR and MS data have been developed [14,17–19], and the deposition of analytical data is increasingly required by journals. This is beneficial for peer-reviewed validation and future use in big-data discovery approaches, including ML. This parallels the efforts used for decades in the field of genomics. Tools analogous to Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) for genomic data, such as MASST (Table 1) for mass spectral data, were developed to enable queries into the presence of molecules or their analogs across publicly available datasets [20]. No such search engine for NMR has been released at the time of this publication.

As a result of these major factors, metabolomics now has a prominent role in NP drug discovery, and the expansion of its utility only continues. Computational strategies have been developed over the last 15 years, with tools building upon and informing each other. Strategies for elucidation of novel structures, determination of origin, or assessment of bioactivity span from cases with single samples to pan-repository analyses.

Case studies utilizing metabolomic approaches for drug discovery that are based on a relatively small number of samples

Metabolomics can be used with a single or small number of samples to strategically identify new NPs, analogues of known NPs, and biologically active NPs (Figure 2a–d). Many case studies demonstrate that molecular networking (MN) with untargeted metabolomics data has accelerated discovery of new biologically active NPs in both terrestrial and marine environments. Global Natural Products Social Networking (GNPS) based MN harnesses MS/MS patterns to visualize the metabolomic chemical space with ‘nodes’ from consensus spectra that represent the ions of a single compound and ‘edges’ that connect those nodes based upon a certain number of matching fragments (a threshold which is set by the user) to create clusters that contain mass spectral, and therefore, structural similarity [21] (Figure 2b). Annotations of known compounds can be visualized within the network and used for dereplication or as a starting point for analogue identification [5,21]. A variety of strategies using MN for data-informed discovery as well as NMR-based tools to reduce the bottleneck for formal structure elucidation have been used since their introduction to the field of metabolomics. Here, we highlight a few of these strategies used in the last two years.

Molecular networking-based dereplication for discovery

Many of the initial use cases of NP discovery using GNPS molecular networking included the value of rapid cheminformatic dereplication using the community-curated and ever-expanding mass spectral libraries. This component of the GNPS ecosystem is still widely used to help reduce rediscovery and enhance the likelihood of targeting structural novelty and is currently used for discovery of NPs [17–19] (Figure 2a,b). The libraries are dependent upon community efforts to deposit data into the public domain, and therefore, innately have some limitations due to data availability. This being said, the rates of deposition of structural data have increased substantially, which, in turn, adds to the utility of tools such as the GNPS libraries.

Analogue identification through propagating mass spectrometry annotations

Once a node within the cluster is annotated as a library hit, the delta m/z calculated between nodes can be used in concert with the fragmentation pattern similarities to predict structure (Figure 2b). For example, avellanins D-O were discovered from host-associated fungus isolated from an anemone from the Mariana Trench using this GNPS-based approach. Untargeted MS/MS data from MALDI-TOF were analyzed with classical MN, and a library match to avellanin C was identified. Nodes within one cluster were prioritized, and 12 new pentapeptides were isolated and fully characterized. Many demonstrate antimalarial activity with no accompanying cytotoxicity with avellanin I standing out as a compound with pharmacological promise with an IC50 of 190 nM [22]. Manual inspection and annotation of propagated nodes can be used in such strategies, though tools such as the repository-scale propagated nearest neighbor suspect spectral library can be employed to semi-automate the approach [23].

Chemigeographic approach for discovery

Association of chemical profiles to geographic locations can be used to leverage chemical ecology to discover novel NPs and prioritize bioactivity assessment. This method has been augmented by the GNPS ecosystem through visualization of different groups of samples stratified in a single node within a molecular network, with the relative abundance of an ion contributed from each group signified by distinct colors in a pie chart (Figure 2c). In the case of the kavaratamides, a chemigeographic approach guided by GNPS Classical Molecular Networking led to the prioritization of new metabolites from the well-studied species of filamentous cyanobacterium, Moorena bouillonii. Nodes unique to samples from one geographic location, Kavaratti, India, were in a unique cluster, free from library annotations, and therefore prioritized. This MS-based strategy was complemented by NMR-based approaches SMART 2.0 and DeepSAT by providing valuable structural insights through similarity scoring through ML to accelerate subsequent rigorous structure elucidation of three new lipodepsipeptides [24] (Figure 2d).

Bioactivity-based approaches for discovery

Bioactivity-guided isolation of NPs is a long-standing approach to drug discovery. Modern metabolomic workflows have harnessed this approach to streamline the process of identifying compounds with pharmaceutical promise. Whether for compound prioritization [25] or to identify NP synergists [26], metabolomic strategies over the last couple of years have been productive. For example, vernoliganans A-C were isolated using a traditional bioactivity-guided fractionation approach; then, advanced metabolomic methods accelerated structure elucidation with DeepSAT (Figure 2d). The mechanism of action was also hypothesized based on a computational approach with molecular docking, which suggested BRAF, a protein kinase that directs cellular growth, as the biological target [27]. This synergy of tools is another excellent example of the current metabolomic and computational strategies enhancing drug discovery, and represents a future opportunity for the development of streamlined tools to automate such processes and further improve efficiency.

Convergent omics strategies for discovery

As multi-omics approaches are used on a single sample or set of samples, convergent strategies can link NP scaffolds with their biosynthetic genes and inform structure as well as function of the metabolites. This approach was used in identifying leptochelins as promiscuous metallophores using MS with post-column infusion of metals and utilizing enzymatic insights from the biosynthetic cluster to aid in stereochemical determination [28]. The ecteinamines, diasperonins, fradiamines, and lagriamides represent additional examples of convergent use of metabolomics data with other omics technologies to accelerate NP drug discovery [29–32]. Workflows that combine the big data resulting from metabolomics, transcriptomics, and genomics, even on a single-sample scale, will continue to be used to strategically discover novel chemistry with pharmaceutical promise. The development of tools to aid in big-data integration is nicely covered in several recent reviews [33–35].

High-throughput identification of potential therapeutics in mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance

Finding new compounds/drug targets at a large scale proves an effective method of prioritization and quick or partial discovery (Figure 2e–i). Big data strategies have led to advances in HT experiments and reusing public NMR/MS data. Based on the current needs of the field, we showcase studies that have utilized such tools outstandingly. We acknowledge the breadth of such tools and use-cases in more comprehensive reviews [8,15,36–38].

Search tools and substructure annotation

The ability to annotate data computationally has increased substantially in the last two years. For LC-MS data, a search engine utilizes matching of indexed spectra against over 19,000 plant MS/MS with species-level metadata, allowing for annotations against biosynthetic origin or isolation prospects beyond structure-level information [20] (Figure 2g). Although typically dereplication is performed using spectral library matching, this strategy can also be utilized to identify compounds found in previous datasets, even if the annotated structure has not been uploaded to public spectral libraries, saving time and resources before the lengthy compound isolation process [39]. A new programming language allows queries of MS data patterns, including m/z, retention time, isotopic signatures, and more within MS data [40] (Figure 2f). This mass query language termed MassQL has led to the discovery of 3900 MS features explored to be siderophores through Fe3+ isotopic searching in public metabolomics data.

For discovery of novel structures, MS-based search tools have proved fruitful. MassQL led to the discovery of over 21,000 MS/MS of bile acids in raw untargeted metabolomics data within GNPS/MassIVE, resulting in the discovery and synthetic confirmation of 13 new polyamine conjugated bile amidates and five isomers [41]. Currently, ∼8500 have been verified with synthetic standards and are potential regulators of metabolic diseases [41,42], highlighting how large repository-scale resources can be leveraged to discover many new molecules. Three substructures were used in a similar strategy to characterize 250 novel monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs), molecules with potential antibacterial or anti-inflammatory properties [43,44]. These large-scale references guide smaller-scale isolation efforts, such as the MN-driven discovery of pyrrovobasine, an MIA [45]. Although MassQL has been used in multiple use-cases for pattern searching, there is no web-based functionality for conditional searches, tree-based searching, or overlapping queries without some computational expertise. The MassQL chatbot represents a frontier in translating human language into MS data, simplifying their creation [40] (Figure 2f). Despite this, effective query design still requires domain expertise in chemistry, MS, and data science.

MotifDB is a publicly available database with ever-growing substructures named Mass2Motifs, including 2012 expertly curated and labeled MS/MS substructures, which have also been converted into easily searchable mass query language [46]. Searching the MotifDB against untargeted metabolomics LC-MS/MS data of bioactive extracts has led to increased metabolite annotation and visualization of structural diversity (Figure 2e). Furthermore, recent development of Mass2Motif annotation guidance has enabled ML trained on public library spectra to classify unknown substructures (known as motifs). MS2LDA has an unsupervised approach, so discovery with this method will target more common motifs or higher abundance features, rather than unique, underrepresented substructures of which many NP researchers target. Other ML methods, including in silico generation of reference mass spectra has been done, and is improving [47], but it is often unused in the field due to difficulty with interpretability [48]. ML has been used more widely in NMR metabolomics for spectrum prediction [15,16], and recently an in silico library generation of over 600,000 NMR spectra for NPs was generated [49] (Figure 2i). Additionally, many new in silico NMR annotation methods are being developed to characterize structures and substructures of NPs [16,49,50], though these methods are best used on pure standards rather than complex mixtures.

High-throughput methods guided by bioactivity

Combinatorial synthesis of drug prospects has huge potential in ML, HT screening, and pharmacophore-based strategies [12,13,51,52], with miniaturization advancing to a nanoliter scale [51]. Combinatorial synthesis of large-scale drug libraries have been validated by both MS and NMR [12,52], but NMR which is also possible at nano-scale [7,53] may be more reliable in determining the absolute structures of such compounds. These compounds can be tested against protein interactions [54] or antibacterial properties [52], for example. Additionally, protein-based NMR serves as a promising HTS technique as it detects when specific binding interactions are present within a binding site, a method that enhances identification of target-specific activity [13,54]. Fragment-based drug design is a popular method to identify weakly bound ligand cores as a starting point to then guide structural modifications to a compound or series of compounds and achieve high-affinity [54,55].

Computational methods have been used to classify bioactivity of novel NPs based on their substructure. ML methods can be applied to classify the bioactivity of unannotated MS/MS and NMR spectra, trained on spectra of molecules containing specific pharmacophores [56,57]. Methods including integration studies which measure co-occurrences or similarity scores of abundances of untargeted metabolomics data and other data types such as imaging, gene expression, or transcriptomics [58,59], termed Compound Activity Mapping (CAM), provide a unique advantage as the analysis does not rely on knowing the structure of the molecule, just the abundance through MS1 analysis (Figure 2h). More recently, a platform called NP Analyst has been developed as an open-source platform allowing CAM in a web interface, utilizing the same similarity network fusion method [60]. Other methods like bioactivity-MN have utilized co-occurrence Pearson correlation scores between selected bioactivities and intensities to visualize fractions of interest [25]. Integration studies in metabolomics continue to rely on co-occurrence metrics, whose results do not interpret mechanistic truth, but are a prioritization technique or hypothesis for validation studies.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Here, we discussed a selection of tools and studies in the discovery of NPs for drug discovery (Table 1). The use of small-scale tools, including MN to find structurally similar metabolites in a biological system, or utilizing it to prioritize untapped biological samples, can inform how big data science is leveraged for discovery. As highlighted here, MN has been used extensively to identify novel metabolites from known NPs or drug molecules. This study and others like it demonstrate the significance of structural propagation, a concept that led to the creation of MassQL, which enables MS data searches within large datasets or entire repositories. These methods can be used in series to amplify discovery, as seen in the case of bile acids. MN was used to discover three new microbial conjugates [61] leading to the creation of the MassQL bile acid conjugate library [41], then biological testing revealed binding to Mas-related G protein-coupled receptor family member E and anti-obesity functions [41,42].

At the time of this publication, 1,428,956 library spectra are available on GNPS, and there are potentially millions more to be discovered [5]. In addition to uploading elucidated structures to libraries such as GNPS or others, we urge researchers to follow FAIR data guidelines [6], including raw data availability, aiding in computational discovery and dereplication. Many public databases exist, and we call for further integration of such databases for increased accessibility and redundancy, as in genomics [33] or proteomics [62], for example.

Directions for future advancements of metabolomics data for NP drug discovery have been identified here, providing opportunities for the community of NP researchers to address. These include developing tools that broadly and efficiently link bioactivity profiles with metabolites, integrating metadata that shares common ontology across multiple platforms, and subsequently, improving crosstalk between tools. Metabolomics has taken and should continue to take inspiration from the genomics field in tool building and statistical analysis of compositional data. For example, in the case of data-driven ontologies, microbial phylogeny from kingdom to species level is interpreted based on the level of information that is captured in an analysis, and differences in instrumentation impact this (e.g. PacBio long-read sequencing vs. Illumina short read sequencing). This presents an opportunity to create similar ontologies from chemical data, such as MS/MS or NMR spectra, which describe all of what is known, even if not a full characterization. Many hypothesis-driving tools exist separately (e.g. in silico predictions, substructure classifications, bioactivity results) and can be used in series. An integrated network of tools using a common language and community-agreed standards could augment these stand-alone tools, create opportunities for parallel analyses, unify the field, and add nuance to discovery approaches.

NP drug discovery is a multi-faceted field at the interface of chemistry, biology, analytical methodology, engineering, instrumentation, and computational tool development. Each subdiscipline informs the next, and advancing the field will require ongoing technological advances, multi-institutional collaborations, and creative application to discovery.
