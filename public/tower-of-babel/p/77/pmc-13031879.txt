Accelerating a coherent Ising machine by XY-Ising spin transition
Authors: Kyungduk Kim, Yoshihisa Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We numerically demonstrate that introducing XY-spin dynamics enabled by phase-insensitive optical parametric gain enhances the performance of coherent Ising machines (CIMs). Whereas conventional CIMs based on phase-sensitive optical parametric gain confine spins to two discrete Ising states, nondegenerate optical parametric oscillators with phase-insensitive gain support continuous-phase XY spins, facilitating spin flips that enable the system to escape local minima. Benchmarking on Wishart-planted problem instances shows that gradually transitioning from XY to Ising spins reduces the time-to-solution by roughly an order of magnitude relative to purely Ising-based dynamics. Furthermore, tailoring the transitions between XY-like and Ising-like regimes yields additional improvements beyond a simple unidirectional XY-to-Ising transition. Our results establish a new framework for engineering CIM dynamics in full phase–quadrature space and point toward fully optical architectures for efficient combinatorial optimization.

Introduction

Combinatorial optimization underpins a broad range of scientific and technological domains by finding optimal configurations in complex systems. Solving these problems involves exploring a vast solution space, making exhaustive search intractable for conventional digital hardware operating under finite time and energy constraints. These limitations have motivated the pursuit of unconventional computing paradigms that exploit the intrinsic dynamics of physical systems to perform optimization more efficiently. A class of combinatorial optimization problems can be reformulated as the task of identifying the ground state of the Ising model1, where binary variables are encoded as spins \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i = \pm 1$$\end{document}, and the objective function is represented by the energy landscape \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\sum _{i, j\ne i} J_{ij} s_i s_j$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{ij}$$\end{document} denoting the coupling between spins. This mapping has led to the development of specialized physical platforms–collectively termed Ising machines–that are designed to naturally settle toward low-energy states2, which reveals solutions to the original problem.

Optical Ising machines constitute a particularly promising class of such platforms thanks to their ultrafast temporal dynamics, massive parallelism, high connectivity, and robustness to thermal and electronic noise3,4. Various photonic architectures have been explored, including injection-locked laser arrays5,6, spatial light modulator–based analog optimizers7,8, and hybrid optoelectronic oscillators9–13. Among these approaches, the Coherent Ising Machine (CIM)14–17 is a prominent form of such optical implementations. The CIM comprises a network of time-multiplexed degenerate optical parametric oscillators (DOPO)–circulating optical pulses in a ring cavity sustained by phase-sensitive parametric gain–that interact through a coupling network implementing the Ising couplings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{ij}$$\end{document}. Each DOPO exhibits bistable phase states, 0 or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}, naturally encoding the two Ising spin values. The nonlinear optical parametric gain and engineered coupling collectively guide the system toward low-energy configurations corresponding to exact or approximate solutions of the encoded optimization problem.

In oscillator-based analog systems such as DOPO networks, each oscillator begins in a soft-spin regime at below the self-oscillation threshold, in which its amplitude and phase vary continuously. As the parametric gain is increased and exceeds the self-oscillation threshold, the DOPO undergoes a pitchfork bifurcation that drives it into a hard-spin regime, where the phase becomes discretized and maps onto binary Ising spin states18,19. However, during this soft-to-hard transition, the network can become transiently trapped in local minima of the energy landscape, resulting in suboptimal spin configurations. Once the bifurcation completes, the oscillator phases are effectively fixed. If the resulting configuration is not the ground state, additional mechanisms–such as controlled destabilization via feedback-induced spin flips20–22 or stochastic perturbations introduced through quantum noise23–are required to facilitate further exploration. Consequently, precise control and understanding of the spin dynamics during bifurcation are crucial for improving the computational performance of coherent Ising machines24–26.

These limitations can be mitigated by relaxing the binary spin representation into higher-dimensional spaces–such as XY spins in two dimensions27–31 or Heisenberg spins in three dimensions32. A prominent performance-enhancement strategy for CIMs is the use of hyperspins33–36, which allow the system to explore an expanded configuration space before ultimately being projected back onto the Ising spins. In such a scheme, higher-dimensional spin representations can be implemented using synthetic optical parametric oscillators, where multiple coupled OPOs jointly encode a hyperspin. Multidimensional spin variables have also been demonstrated in gain-based optical computing architectures37 and in Poincaré-space implementations38, the latter exploiting the polarization state of light to naturally support three-component spin vectors. For CIMs, replacing DOPOs with nondegenerate optical parametric oscillators (NOPO) offers a direct physical realization of two-dimensional XY spins, where a single NOPO represents a single XY spin. In such systems, the phase-insensitive gain provides the core mechanism underlying coherent XY machines (CXYM)39–42. Phase-insensitive amplification has further been proposed as a means of modifying the bifurcation dynamics that enhances correction of phases to guide the system into the ground state solution43,44. Yet, previous numerical studies of phase-insensitive gain have been limited to small systems–fewer than five spins–and lack systematic performance benchmarks, leaving the open question of whether these advantages scale to larger problem instances.

In this work, we numerically investigate CIMs incorporating phase-insensitive gain and systematically benchmark their performance. We show that enabling XY spin dynamics accelerates finding the ground state by approximately an order of magnitude compared to systems restricted to purely Ising spins, for a system size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 60$$\end{document}. Moreover, instead of relying entirely on fully XY spins or a simple unidirectional transition from XY to Ising behavior, we dynamically engineer the transition between the XY and Ising regimes to further boost the performance. This control of transition between one- and two-dimensional spin representations allows the system to enhance the computation performance afforded by full access to the phase–quadrature space in CIMs.

Figure 1a illustrates a schematic of the CIM (or CXYM), which encodes an N-spin Ising problem using a set of time-multiplexed optical pulses circulating in a fiber ring cavity. A coherent light source generates a pulse train with repetition period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} and injects it into the ring cavity. For a problem of size N, the cavity stores N pulses, and accordingly the cavity round-trip time matches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N\Delta t$$\end{document}. The intracavity pulses represent spin variables through their optical phase. To implement interactions between these pulses, a portion of the intracavity field is tapped out and routed through optical delay lines45–47. Each delay line introduces a delay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\Delta t$$\end{document}, where k is an integer between 1 and N. These delayed pulses are coherently recombined with the circulating internal pulses, enabling interference that realizes the Ising coupling matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{ij}$$\end{document}, which couples spin i and spin j. Modulators placed within the delay lines control the coupling amplitudes. Implementing full all-to-all connectivity requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N-1$$\end{document} delay lines that each introduce a distinct delay, with the modulators updating their coupling weights every \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. An additional N-th delay line introduces a delay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N\Delta t$$\end{document}, creating self-feedback for each pulse. This self-feedback acts as a momentum-like term that can accelerate the convergence toward a solution48.

After the recombination of the delayed fields, the combined feedback signal enters the optical parametric amplifier (OPA), the key component of the CIM. When its parametric gain exceeds the cavity loss, the system transitions into parametric oscillation, forming a set of time-multiplexed OPOs, which are mutually coupled to each other by the feedback field. Gain saturation in the OPA maintains the energy of each pulse at an approximately constant level, helping suppress amplitude nonuniformities that is a main challenge in finding the ground state solution2,49. Lastly, for readout, a small fraction of the intracavity field is extracted and measured using homodyne detection with a local oscillator. The in-phase quadrature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} determines the value of each spin i: if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} is positive, the corresponding spin is assigned \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i = +1$$\end{document}, and if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} is negative, it is assigned \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i = -1$$\end{document}.

In CIMs, OPAs rely on parametric down-conversion via a second-order nonlinear interaction, in which a pump field is converted into lower-energy signal and idler fields, providing parametric gain to the signal field. Traditional CIMs employ degenerate optical parametric amplifiers (DOPAs), in which the signal and idler have identical frequencies, each corresponding to half the pump frequency. DOPAs are phase-sensitive amplifiers50,51, selectively amplifying the in-phase quadrature x that aligns with the pump phase, while deamplifying the orthogonal quadrature p [Fig. 1b]. When the parametric gain exceeds the cavity loss, the system reaches the self-oscillation threshold and forms a DOPO. Above this threshold, fluctuations along p are strongly suppressed, forcing the field into one of two stable phase states, with x taking values corresponding to phases 0 or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, each spin exhibits a bistable potential along x, with two wells representing the two spin states. To switch from one spin to the other–i.e., to move to a lower-energy configuration in the Ising energy landscape–a spin must overcome the barrier separating these wells. After the system undergoes bifurcation and the DOPO amplitudes become fully binarized, the barrier becomes increasingly difficult to cross, limiting the system’s ability to explore the energy landscape.

In contrast, non-degenerate optical parametric amplifiers (NOPAs) generate signal and idler fields at different wavelengths. When no idler field is injected, the amplifier exhibits phase-insensitive amplification50,51, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. Because the amplification does not distinguish between the two phase quadratures, both quadratures experience the same amplification gain. As a result, when the parametric gain exceeds the self-oscillation threshold and each spin becomes a NOPO, there is no mechanism that enforces the optical phase to lock to 0 or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}. Instead, the phase can take on any continuous value, allowing the optical field to naturally represent XY spins39–42. The corresponding energy landscape for a spin, shown in Fig. 1e, reflects this additional degree of freedom: rather than being confined to two discrete minima, the phase can move continuously around the circle. This continuous landscape provides additional pathways for escaping local minima, reducing the likelihood of the system becoming trapped and potentially improving its ability to explore the energy landscape and reach the ground-state solution.

We achieve the crossover between the two regimes by cascading the NOPA and DOPA43,44, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The pump on these OPAs are independently controlled, enabling tuning of the relative contributions of phase-insensitive and phase-sensitive gain. For coherent Ising machine (CIM) operation, only the DOPA is pumped while the NOPA remains unpumped; in this configuration, the signal propagates through the unpumped NOPA without experiencing phase-insensitive amplification, thereby allowing purely phase-sensitive gain in the DOPA. In contrast, for coherent XY machine (CXYM) operation, only the NOPA is pumped, resulting in phase-insensitive gain. Continuously adjusting the relative pump powers allows us to achieve a smooth transition between XY and Ising dynamics. This scheme enables an effective implementation of dynamically controlling the spin dimensionality, a core principle of dimensional annealing proposed earlier33,34, through the phase-sensitive or phase-insensitive nature of the internal amplifier of the CIM.

Here, the discrete spin state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document} and the associated cost function are determined exclusively by the in-phase amplitudes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} through the mapping \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i = x_i / |x_i|$$\end{document}, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i \in \{+1, -1\}$$\end{document}. Although XY spins with a NOPA may possess non-zero out-of-phase components \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document}, we preserve the binary nature of the spin state in the cost function by projecting the dynamics onto the in-phase component.

To investigate the impact of XY spins on solving Ising problems, we benchmark our approach using Wishart-planted instances53,54. These instances correspond to fully connected Ising models whose coupling coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{ij}$$\end{document} appear quasi-random and are drawn from a Wishart distribution. An advantage of this class of problems is that a ground-state configuration can be explicitly planted, enabling the exact solution to be known a priori, which is particularly useful for benchmarking. Moreover, the computational hardness of these instances can be tuned via the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}. For large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, the resulting energy landscape is nearly convex, and gradient descent readily identifies the solution. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} decreases, numerous local minima near the planted ground state are developed, creating rugged energy landscape that increase problem difficulty. The hardness reaches a maximum at a critical value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and subsequently decreases, exhibiting the characteristic easy–hard–easy transition.

We first consider a Wishart-planted instance with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 60$$\end{document} spins and hardness parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.8$$\end{document}, where the ground state corresponds to all spins aligned in the same direction (all +1 or all -1) (see Methods for details). For a conventional CIM with a DOPA by setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(t) = 1$$\end{document}, the spins are restricted along the x-quadrature, representing classical Ising spins (Left panels of Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2b, starting from small initial amplitudes, the spin amplitudes bifurcate into positive or negative values along x and remain fixed after a normalized run time–the number of round trips–of about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 10^2$$\end{document}. However, some spins become trapped on the opposite side of the x-amplitude relative to the others. Consequently, as shown in Fig. 2c, the cost function in Eq. 7 does not reach the global minimum of the Ising problem.

To overcome this limitation, we initialize the optical parametric amplifier as a NOPA and gradually transition to a DOPA. This transition is controlled by a monotonically increasing parameter c(t), which describes the crossover between phase-insensitive and phase-sensitive gain. We choose a hyperbolic tangent form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(t)=\tanh (\epsilon t/\Delta t)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon =10^{-3}$$\end{document} specifies the transition rate and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t/\Delta t$$\end{document} is the cavity round trip number. This function monotonically increases from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(t)=0$$\end{document} to 1, thereby implementing a continuous evolution from XY-like to Ising-like spin dynamics.

During the early stage of computation, this scheme provides phase-insensitive gain, allowing the spins to explore both the x- and p-quadratures and realize the XY-spin dynamics. In this regime, the spins are distributed across the two-dimensional plane rather than being confined to the x-axis (Fig. 2a-ii). As the phase-sensitive gain of the DOPA dominates, the spins become confined to the x-coordinate (Fig. 2a-iv). As shown in Fig. 2b, starting from the same initial conditions as the DOPA-only case, this transition scheme enables a spin flip that allows all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document} to be +1. Consequently, the cost function in Eq. 7 continues to decrease and converges to the ground state (Fig. 2c). These results demonstrate that exploration of the full phase-quadrature space via phase-insensitive gain enables the system to find the correct solution.

To further assess performance, we evaluate the success probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_s$$\end{document} of obtaining the ground state. We solve an ensemble of ten problem instances (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 60, \alpha = 0.8$$\end{document}), and for each instance the solver is run \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^4$$\end{document} times with random initial phases. Success is defined as the solver reaching the ground state before the specified run time. In other words, a run is considered successful if all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i(t)$$\end{document} become either \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document}; equivalently, if the in-phase amplitudes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} of all spins become either all positive or all negative at any time t before the end of the computation. The success probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_s$$\end{document} is then computed as the fraction of successful runs out of the total number of runs.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the success probability increases with run time and eventually saturates. Compared with the pure Ising case [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(t)=1$$\end{document}], the XY-spin case [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(t)=0$$\end{document}] using a NOPA achieves a higher success probability, demonstrating the benefit of phase-insensitive gain. A NOPA alone results in an arbitrary global phase of the spins and provides no mechanism to lock their phase to 0 or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}. Therefore, transitioning from a NOPA to a DOPA is necessary to constrain the spins to the x-axis. This transition yields a 4.8-fold increase in success probability at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^3$$\end{document} round trips compared with the pure Ising configuration, demonstrating the advantage of the XY-to-Ising transition scheme.

As the success probability depends on the choice of run time, we adopt the time-to-solution as our primary metric, which removes the ambiguity associated with selecting a run time2. This quantity represents the total number of time steps required to achieve a target success probability. Because short-time success probabilities are typically less than unity, multiple independent runs may be required to reach the ground state. If a single run of duration T yields a success probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_s$$\end{document}, and we desire a 99% overall success probability, then the probability of obtaining the correct solution at least once in r repetitions is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-(1-p_s)^r$$\end{document}. The total run time is thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_s = rT$$\end{document}, or8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_s = T\frac{\log (1-0.99)}{\log (1-p_s)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which specifies the expected total run time needed to reach 99% success. We then identify the time-to-solution as9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} (\textrm{TTS}) = \min _T T_s, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}by selecting the run time T that minimizes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_s$$\end{document}.

Figure 3b shows that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_s$$\end{document} typically decreases with increasing normalized run time T, reaches a minimum, and then increases again. For the pure Ising case, the median TTS across ten problem instances is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.2 \times 10^3$$\end{document} round trips. Employing XY spins lowers the median TTS to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3.5 \times 10^3$$\end{document} round trips. Applying the XY-to-Ising transition scheme reduces it further–to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.0 \times 10^3$$\end{document} round trips–representing a reduction in the time-to-solution by a factor of 2.6 compared with the purely Ising-based approach.

We optimize this transition scheme by fine-tuning the transition rate. Figure 3c illustrates the dependence of the TTS on the transition rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}. For large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}, the transition is rapid and the dynamics approach those of a pure Ising machine. Conversely, for small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}, the transition is sufficiently slow that the system effectively remains in the XY regime, which exhibits the reduced TTS compared to the Ising regime. Notably, optimal performance occurs at an intermediate rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon = 5.6\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, demonstrating that a gradual shift from XY to Ising spins is most effective.

Lastly, we investigate a dynamic control of the XY–Ising transition. Rather than employing a single monotonic transition from XY to Ising spins, we consider schedules in which the system is returned to the XY regime. More specifically, the system initially evolves in the XY phase, gradually transitions to the Ising phase, and, upon encountering local traps, is reset to the XY phase before resuming its progression toward the Ising regime. This process may be repeated multiple times. A key challenge is the identification of optimal schedule that exhibit the best performance.

To address this problem, we employ the Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) algorithm55 (see Methods for details) on the transition schedule c(t). SPSA is particularly well suited for optimizing noisy objective functions in scenarios where gradients are unavailable. By applying SPSA and setting the cost function as the TTS, we aim to determine adaptive switching schedules that minimize the TTS.

Discussion

In summary, we numerically demonstrate a mechanism for accelerating CIM computation by introducing phase-insensitive gain into benchmark Ising problems. Whereas the phase-sensitive gain of a DOPA confines spins in coherent Ising machines to a single dimension in phase-quadrature space, a NOPA provides phase-insensitive gain that supports continuous-phase dynamics in full phase-quadrature space. Incorporating a NOPA thus expands the accessible phase space, enabling continuous exploration during binarization onto the discrete Ising spins. Our numerical experiments can be considered as one simple physical implementation of the dimensional annealing concept proposed earlier33,34, allowing continuous tuning of the effective spin dimensionality between one and two.

Using Wishart-planted instances with tunable hardness, we numerically show that initiating the dynamics in the XY regime and subsequently transitioning to Ising spins yields roughly an order-of-magnitude reduction in time to solution for Wishart-planted problems with a problem size of 60. Additionally, allowing the system to temporarily revert to the XY regime when trapped in local minima provides an effective escape mechanism, thereby reducing the time required to reach the planted ground state. Our approach offers a new mechanism for engineering transitions between Ising and XY spin representations, enabling more flexible control schemes that can further enhance CIM performance.

A promising avenue for future work is the development of amplitude-correction schemes tailored to XY spins. In the current framework, amplitude stabilization is achieved solely through the nonlinear filtering of gain saturation. Additional amplitude-control mechanisms–such as chaotic amplitude control20,21 or chaotic feedback control22, which enables chaotic search with spin flips and has been demonstrated previously for Ising spins56–58–could offer similar advantages for XY-spin dynamics. More broadly, constructing dedicated amplitude-correction strategies for high-dimensional spin systems is likely to enhance the overall performance of Ising machines35,59.

From a physical-implementation standpoint, the proposed scheme can be realized entirely in the optical domain. Nondegenerate or degenerate parametric amplification intrinsically realizes XY or Ising spin representations, and each optical pulse serves as a single spin degree of freedom without requiring auxiliary encoding layers, thereby reducing architectural complexity and associated overhead. The primary experimental challenge is the realization of a hybrid parametric amplification stage capable of delivering both degenerate and nondegenerate gain with continuously adjustable weighting. In addition to the cascaded DOPA and NOPA43,44, one may alternatively tune the wavelength to modify the degeneracy condition. Since the degeneracy is determined by the wavelength relationships among the pump, signal, and idler fields, the XY–Ising transition can be controlled by tuning the pump wavelength–for example, using a swept source–or by applying spectral shearing via electro-optic modulation. This approach, involving the tuning of the internal degrees of freedom of oscillators, potentially eliminates the requirement for synthetic OPOs that employ multi-pulse sequences to represent high-dimensional spins. Achieving such hybrid amplification with the required phase stability, gain precision, and low-noise performance remains an open technical challenge and a critical avenue for future development.

Once the gain exceeds the oscillation threshold, the system enters an oscillatory regime and the spin amplitude begins to grow. In this regime, the solver increasingly identifies solutions, resulting in a rapid increase in success probability. This transition is particularly pronounced for easy instances (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} = 0.95), where the energy landscape is less rugged. Furthermore, the success probability decreases monotonically with decreasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, due to the increased level of problem hardness.

Optimization algorithm

The Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) algorithm is an optimization method that is particularly useful when the gradient of the objective function is not readily available55. At each iteration, SPSA approximates the gradient using measurements of the objective function at only two perturbed points, enabling efficient optimization even in high-dimensional problems.

We employ the SPSA algorithm to find the optimal transition schedule c(t) that minimizes the TTS. The loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\cdot )$$\end{document} is equal to TTS and depends on the transition schedule. The schedule c(t) is parametrized by a vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\theta }= [\theta _1, \cdots , \theta _P]^\textrm{T}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _p$$\end{document} determines the timing at which c(t) becomes zero. With P parameters, where P is an even integer, the schedule is13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c(t) = \tanh (\epsilon t/\Delta t) \left[ 1 - \sum ^P_{p=1} (-1)^{p+1} H(t/\Delta t - \theta _p) \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(\cdot )$$\end{document} is the Heaviside step function. The function is zero whenever \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t/\Delta t$$\end{document} lies in the interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\theta _p, \theta _{p+1})$$\end{document} with p taking an odd index; otherwise, it follows the hyperbolic tangent. This form allows us to describe the temporary reversion to XY spins during the gradual transition to Ising spins.

The algorithm starts with an initial parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\theta }^{(1)}$$\end{document}. It also requires gain parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_k = b/(B+k)^{\beta }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_k = d/k^\gamma$$\end{document}, which determine the step size at each iteration, where b, B, and d are constants. At iteration k, the gradient vector of the loss function is approximated by14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{g}^{(k)} (\hat{\theta }^{(k)}) = \frac{L(\hat{\theta }^{(k)} + d_k \Delta ^{(k)}) - L(\hat{\theta }^{(k)} - d_k \Delta ^{(k)})}{2 d_k} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\Delta ^{(k)}_1, \cdots , 1/\Delta ^{(k)}_P \end{bmatrix}^\textrm{T} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta ^{(k)} = [\Delta ^{(k)}_1, \cdots , \Delta ^{(k)}_P]^\textrm{T}$$\end{document} is a random perturbation vector following the Bernoulli distribution, i.e., each element is either +1 or -1 with equal probability of 0.5. The parameters for the next iteration are then updated according to15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\theta }^{(k+1)} = \hat{\theta }^{(k)} - b_k \hat{g}^{(k)} (\hat{\theta }^{(k)}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For the optimization illustrated in Fig. 5, we set the number of parameters P to 6, resulting in the schedule c(t) reverting to zero three times. The gain parameters are chosen as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta = 0.602$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma = 0.101$$\end{document}, consistent with recommended values61. The initial parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\theta }^{(1)}_p$$\end{document}, are sampled uniformly from the interval (0, 300). At each iteration, the TTS is computed by running the simulation for 300 round trips and averaging over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^3$$\end{document} random initial conditions. After fine-tuning, we set the gain parameters to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b = 1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d = 100$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = 10$$\end{document}. The SPSA algorithm is executed for 100 iterations, by which point the loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\cdot )$$\end{document}, or the TTS, is saturated and no longer decreases.
