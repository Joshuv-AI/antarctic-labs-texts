An explainable geospatial framework for fine-scale Fatty Liver Disease (FLD) risk assessment: roles of social economy, built environment, and psychology
Authors: Qiushi Hu, Huiheng Wang, Chunmei Yang, Jiaxin Wang, Tongxin Xia, Xinmin Chu, Xuehan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFatty Liver Disease (FLD) has become the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, posing a great threat to public health. The existing methods are primarily based on clinical patient cohorts, supplemented by urban environmental factors, to elucidate biological mechanisms. However, insufficient geographic information and spatial resolution limit the public risk identification. This study integrates real and virtual multi-source data and proposes an explainable geospatial framework for street-level HS risk assessment.MethodsFirstly, based on prior medical knowledge, we construct an indicator system comprising three dimensions: social economy, built environment, and psychology (SBP). Then, the multi-model generalization and contribution voting strategy are used for preliminary analysis and optimization to identify key factors. Finally, based on ablation, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and spatial analysis, the independent and synergistic effects of different geographical features are revealed. This study takes 90 streets in Hefei, China, as the research area, and combines multi-regional and dynamic prediction (2023–2024) for verification.ResultsThe results show that the SBP framework achieves good performance in both multi-regional (R2 = 0.765; MAPE = 0.273) and dynamic prediction (R2 = 0.75; MAPE = 0.328) scenarios. The analysis shows that socio-economic features such as age structure and medical care are the most fundamental factors. Further, the superposition of built environmental features, such as health space accessibility and traffic conditions, related to the difference in spatial distribution of disease and the role of psychology.ConclusionsThis study focuses on the challenge of unknown the geospatial factors for HS risk in the context of urbanization, aiming to solve three core issues: insufficient spatial resolution, indicator framework design, and interpretability. The empirical results demonstrate the effectiveness of the SBP framework in identifying spatial heterogeneity of FLD, emphasizes its potential to identify regional differences and inform policy design. The method also provides a quantifiable scientific basis for precise FLD prevention and refined urban health management.

Introduction

With the acceleration of urbanization, the health environment has been profoundly reshaped as the population has increased [1]. Then there is the phenomenon of urban disease, or urban health penalty [2]. Among them, Fatty Liver Disease (FLD) has become the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, with a range of manifestations, such as hepatic steatosis (HS) [3]. The prevalence rate is gradually increasing in most countries and has become one of the leading causes of death from liver disease, posing a great threat to public health [4]. In traditional studies, the incidence or risk assessment of FLD is mainly based on clinical characteristics, such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and obesity [5]. But an individual’s health condition does not exist alone. It is the result of long-term interaction with the daily environment [6]. Especially for people in meso- or micro-areas, their daily activity patterns are closely related to the geographical space [7]. Due to the increasingly prominent independence of urban functions, these people have long been in a differentiated regional environment, which has a profound impact on health [8]. Therefore, extending the research perspective from the individual clinical level to the geospatial environment is crucial for exploring group-level exposure factors that contribute to the risk of FLD and understanding the role of population-urban spatial interaction in public health [9]. In recent years, some studies have begun to explore the relationship between FLD and the spatial environment. For example, in areas with low levels of development are associated with a higher risk [10]. Urban construction factor can affect the obesity rate, and metabolic status of population, thereby indirectly influencing the risk [11]. In addition, it is worth noting that negative psychological factors can lead to a decrease in individual health scores, which develops into a unique risk pathway for FLD [12]. However, these studies focus more on macro spatial scales, and pay less attention to differences inner urban [13]. Secondly, existing studies usually examine the effects of different factors independently, as an additional supplement to the clinical cohort [14]. The lack representation of a geospatial perspective fails to fully reveal the fusion effect of these factors, which makes it difficult to explain the relationship between geospatial features and the risk of FLD, and cannot support refined public health risk discovery and management strategy configuration.

Therefore, guided by medical prior knowledge, this study proposes an explainable geospatial feature description framework called SBP (Social Economy-Built Environment-Psychology). We hope to assess the risk of FLD in a street area and analysis the independent and synergistic effects of various factors. As shown in Fig. 1, the research framework mainly includes indicator design, modeling, and result analysis. We selected 20 independent variables that may be related to FLD within the SBP and used the actual patient counts reported by large hospitals as the verification source. The experiments were conducted across 90 streets in the main built-up areas of Hefei, China, using five machine learning models. The results show that our method achieves good performance in both multi-regional (R2 = 0.765; MAPE = 0.273) and dynamic prediction (R2 = 0.75; MAPE = 0.328) scenarios. Furthermore, this study revealed the independent and synergistic effects of each dimension in SBP on the risk of FLD through feature ablation, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and spatial analysis. These observations highlight the stability and potential of our approach in identifying FLD risks and provide explainable decision support for refined urban health management and resource allocation.

Related work

Socio-economic factors

The social economy is generally considered the fundamental driver of health inequalities or differences in disease distribution [15], which the role in the risk of FLD has been widely accepted in the academic community. Among them, population age is a key factor in distinguishing chronic diseases [16]. Existing studies have shown that, driven by the global aging trend, the elderly group had the heaviest burden of FLD death [17]. In the past five years, the age distribution of FLD has gradually changed, with the young group accounting for the majority of patients [18]. Similarly, economic inequality is a key indicator of public health risks, such as poverty rates and average incomes [19]. For FLD, economic differences may be associated with distinct pathways. For example, low economic levels in developing countries may be associated with unhealthy diets or a lack of medical resources [20], while in high-income countries, the upward trend in Body Mass Index (BMI) of the population will be maintained at a high level [21]. Further, relevant studies calculated the socio-demographic index (SDI) for different regions based on multiple indicators, which have shown that a low-to-medium SDI index is significantly correlated with FLD burden [22].

In summary, existing research generally focuses on large-scale areas such as cities, provinces, countries, and pays less attention to differences within urban internal spaces.

Built environment factors

The built environment is an important channel for connecting social economy and population activities [23] and directly shapes public health outcomes [24]. Studies have shown that the assessment of FLD is usually based on clinical indications such as type 2 diabetes, and obesity [5]. These indicators are derived from the specific activities of the population, including eating habits, healthy living, etc., and are constrained by the built environment [25]. In terms of diet, such as the number of fast-food restaurants [26], convenience stores [27], large supermarkets [28], etc. In terms of health activities, such as road connectivity [29], sports facilities [30], high-quality parks [31], public transportation [32], building density [29], etc. Meanwhile, At the same time, the study of urban ecological environment is also a supplement to the potential risk factors of FLD, such as the lack of vegetation, long-term serious air pollution areas, which will increase the possibility of non-communicable diseases [33, 34]. In addition, some studies comprehensively quantify regional disease risk by socio-economic status (SES) [35].

In general, current medical research follows a similar methodology: identifying the correlation between environmental factors and FLD-related parameters using clinical cohorts, which lacks the information of geospatial distribution.

Psychological factors

Psychological factors, especially chronic stress and negative emotions in the context of urban development, have been considered as unique risk factors for FLD, and are key mediators connecting built environment, and individual physiology. Frontier mechanism studies have revealed that chronic stress and depression often coexist with a variety of chronic diseases [12], which directly promote liver steatosis [36] by causing long-term imbalance of individual hormone levels [37]. Multiple studies based on population cohorts have also confirmed that individual self-reported high stress, anxiety, and work fatigue are significantly associated with the severity of FLD [38]. However, most medical studies still remain at the level of individual psychology, rely on questionnaires to collect data, and fail to convert negative emotions into quantifiable regional exposure factors.

In summary, psychological factors should be considered to better understand the risk of FLD. At present, based on natural language processing (NLP) methods, combined with social media texts to analyze emotional scores has become a popular approach [39], which provides a feasible means for extracting psychological characteristics at the geospatial level.

Study area and data

Study area

The research area for this paper was Hefei, the capital city of Anhui Province, China. Hefei is a sub-center of the Yangtze River Delta urban agglomeration in China, a comprehensive transportation hub city, and a strategic double-node city for the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and the Yangtze River Economic Belt [40]. As of 2020, the permanent population of Hefei was about 9.37 million, accounting for 15.36% of Anhui Province’s total population. As shown in Fig. 2, Hefei has 145 streets across 9 districts, of which the main urban planning area comprises about 90 streets, accounting for 79% of the total population. In recent years, the population of Hefei has continued to grow, regional development has shown significant differences, and the incidence of non-communicable diseases has increased year by year [41]. Given its typical regional characteristics, Hefei can serve as a case study for street-level FLD risk prediction.

FLD risk information originated from the electronic health records of The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). This is a provincial-level large-scale tertiary general hospital, and the only direct affiliated hospital of USTC, with an annual outpatient volume of 7 million people, integrating medical, teaching, scientific research, prevention, health care, rehabilitation, first aid and other services. Considering that the electronic health record is a positive patient, it can only be used as a partial sample reflecting the risk of illness, in order to improve the reliability of modeling. We consider calculating the cumulative number of patients in each street within 1 year as an index of observed burden, while address text was geocoded to street units. This study was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul, and was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of exclusively de-identified data obtained from routine health checkups. FLD evaluates hepatic steatosis by transient elastography of the liver, rather than histopathological confirmation, rather than histopathological confirmation. Hepatic steatosis is identified by the ultrasound attenuation parameter (UAP) from the FibroTouch system. According to the standard parameters of the machine, individuals with UAP values > 244 dB/m at their first examination were classified as ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis. Although UAP-based assessment may result in misclassification at the individual level, due to this method is non-invasive and fast, it is suitable for large-scale screening, and it is a widely used non-invasive proxy in large-scale epidemiological studies [42]. In addition, to reduce the possibility of purely contemporaneous matching and to evaluate the predictive utility of street-level environmental characteristics [43, 44], considering the frequency of change for each data source, we used SBP indicators derived from 2022 to 2023 to predict HS burden from 2023 to 2024. This design should be interpreted as a temporally separated area-level prediction analysis rather than an analysis of individual-level exposure latency or causal lag effects.

Methodology

The research method is shown in Fig. 3, including index framework design, feature extraction and calculation, FLD modeling evaluation, feature identification, and analysis. We elaborate in Sect. 4.1 to 4.4.

Fig. 3Method framework

Method framework

First, we designed the SBP description framework from the three dimensions of social economy, built environment, and psychology under the guidance of medical prior knowledge. Secondly, this study collected the relevant data (Table 1). Then, invalid and illegal data are removed through data preprocessing, and all geographic data are transformed to the WGS84 reference system. Thirdly, this study identified 20 independent variables from the SBP, and we selected five commonly used machine learning models. These models are trained on 90 streets and then predicted in different scenarios. Finally, we preliminarily verified the effects on FLD by feature ablation and model contribution voting. Based on the results, we selected critical features and tested their performance on the dynamic prediction task to assess their sensitivity. Further, this study uses the SHAP strategy combined with the spatial hotspot method to analyze the independent and synergistic effects.

Indicators framework design

The idea of SBP primarily stems from clinical and public health research on FLD. We use the biological mechanism discovered by the patient cohort as credible prior knowledge and combine the attribute expression method for spatiotemporal objects to design the feature framework [45]. Therefore, this study summarizes geospatial factors and characterizes regional FLD risk along three dimensions: social economy, built environment, and psychology (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4SBP Indicators Framework

SBP Indicators Framework

The social economy is the fundamental driver of differences in disease distribution [15]. Based on the SDI index, this study selected attributes such as population, development level, and medical conditions to assess socio-economic differences, emphasizing the role of inequality in population composition, life security, and medical availability in the risk of FLD. The built environment directly shapes the lifestyle of the urban population [24], and focus on intervening in the diet, physical activity, and other behaviors of the regional population, thereby affecting individual BMI and insulin levels [20]. Therefore, this study considers related variables such as dietary conditions, public transportation, buildings, facilities, and air quality to assess environmental health. Psychological factors are considered unique contributors to FLD [12]. This study characterizes the psychological status of the regional population using emotional anomaly analysis. In general, the social economy directly affects the built environment, which in turn affects the individual psychological level. Differences in psychological factors will lead to changes in the interaction mode between individuals and other dimensions [46]. Then, the three dimensions synergistically act on the risk of FLD. Given data availability and prior research, we initially selected 20 candidate variables (Table 2).

Features of the psychological

In recent years, the use of social media data analysis of mental state and mental health environment is very popular [47, 48]. To quantify psychological factors, this study calculated negative emotional intensity (Weibo) using mainstream social media data, which is used to describe the heterogeneity of negative sentiment proxy in the region. The data mainly comes from Sina Weibo, one of China ‘s largest social media, including Weibo tweets published within 24 hours every day from 2022 to 2023. We extract all text and location information published by non-repetitive users, use NLP tools to analyze sentiment, and summarize the number of negative users as the indicator value. This study mainly uses jieba and SnowNLP libraries in Python. The jieba is used to perform appropriate word segmentation on the text, and the results are input into the SnowNLP sentiment analysis module to obtain the sentiment score. The core of SnowNLP sentiment analysis uses a naive Bayesian classifier, and its data expression is shown in formula (4):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}P\left(c|d\right)=\frac{P\left(c\right)\bullet\:P\left(d\right|c)}{P\left(d\right)}\end{array}$$\end{document}

where c denotes the sentiment category (positive / negative), d is the input text, P(c|d) denotes the posterior probability of text d belonging to category c, P(c) denotes the posterior probability of category c, P(d|c) denotes the conditional probability of text d under category c, and P(d) represents the total probability of text d.

In addition, considering the textual features of social media, we summarize the network language involving negative emotions and update the dictionary (such as ‘rushing to work’/ ‘being cut’/ ‘emo’/ ‘pua’) to better identify negative emotions. The emotional score range is 0–1, according to the algorithm parameter dictionary provided by Snow NLP library, we set the threshold to 0.5 [49], that is, users with a score below 0.5 are identified as having negative emotions.

Modeling for FLD prediction

This section introduces the model construction method and evaluation scenario for FLD risk prediction. We made a retrospective analysis of the spatial distribution of FLD patients and their association with SBP feature sets from January 2023 to December 2024. The construction method includes model selection and training strategy, and the scenario includes two dimensions of space and time.

Model selection

Considering the generalization ability and interpretability, this study selected five machine learning models: Decision Tree (DT), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM). These models have strong nonlinear fitting capabilities and can handle complex feature interactions without feature scaling [50]. They all inherit the interpretability of the tree model and can provide a feature importance evaluation function to capture the mechanism of SBP [51].

Training strategy

The strategy of model training mainly includes three aspects: (1) For the model input, we use the above constructed indicators as independent variables, and the number of patients in each street as the dependent variable. Considering that population size related indicators such as the total population may have a collinearity relationship with the dependent variable, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients of five population-related indicators and real patients (Table 5).

(2) For parameter setting, considering that this study is to analyze the urban environmental indicators associated with FLD risk, in order to ensure consistency and comparability while minimizing the computational cost, the hyperparameters of each model are optimized based on its optimal variable subset during cross-validation through Bayesian tuning; (3) For the model training process, each model used a cross-validation strategy to evaluate the robustness of SBP. Since this study uses a small sample, we split the data into 80% for training and 20% for cross-validation. To reduce the influence of randomness, we run each model 100 times across different prediction scenarios and take the average as the final result for comparison.

Multi-regional prediction

Geographical distribution difference is a key dimension of public health research. Previous studies have shown that the spatial distribution of diseases is crucial for identifying high-risk areas and exploring environmental risk factors [52]. To verify the generalization ability of SBP across space, this study used 90 streets training models and additional selected 19 streets in 3 county-level cities for FLD prediction: Changfeng, Lujiang, and Chaohu (Fig. 5). These streets are located in the county-level city planning area around Hefei, accounting for about 12% of the population, and are suitable for assessing the robustness of the SBP. These 19 streets are only used to evaluate the performance of the model and do not participate in model training and feature optimization.

Dynamic prediction

Dynamic change is another important dimension, which can identify the long-term association between diseases and environmental factors [53]. To evaluate the sensitivity of the SBP framework, based on the 2023 FLD prevalence fit, this study identified key features from the results in Sect. 4.3.3 and predicted the distribution in 2024, further evaluating the effect of SBP and the association between FLD and single-feature distributions.

Feature identification and analysis strategy

In this study, four evaluation strategies were used to analyze the key influence and synergistic effect of SBP features, including model performance, feature evaluation, SHAP, and spatial visualization analysis.

Model evaluation

Considering the scientific of the comparison between models, we choose the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) to evaluate the absolute error of the model prediction, and the coefficient of determination(R2) to evaluate the model fitting degree, as shown in formula (5)(6):5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}MAPE=\frac{100\%}{N}{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{N}\left|\frac{{(yPre}_{i}-{yReal}_{i})}{{yReal}_{i}}\right|\end{array}$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{R}^{2}=1-\frac{{\sum\:}_{i}{\left({yReal}_{i}-{yPre}_{i}\right)}^{2}}{{\sum\:}_{i}{\left({yReal}_{i}-\overline{yReal}\right)}^{2}}\end{array}$$\end{document}

where N is the total number of streets, i is the street id, yReali is the real value in street i, yReal is the average value, and yPrei is the predicted value. On this basis, in order to evaluate the modeling effect of different feature combinations, this study used ablation strategies to carry out controlled experiments.

Feature evaluation

In order to identify the key factors to street-level risk stratification, rather than as independently causal environmental determinants, so as to improve the reliability of the index effect analysis, and reduce the influence of semantic homogeneity and noise characteristics, the combined results of collinearity analysis and contribution voting were used to preliminarily optimize the 20 candidate variables of SBP. These strategies are only based on the feature data of 90 training streets and the fitted model, and do not contain data sets for verification.

SHAP

To further explain the independent and synergistic effects of features, this study used SHAP strategy, which is mainly based on the Shapley value proposed in the cooperative game theory [54]. The value allocates and quantifies the marginal contribution of each input variable to the model output in the possible combination according to the total income obtained by each variable, as shown in formula (9):9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{\varnothing\:\left(f,x\right)}_{i}=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq\:M\backslash\:\left\{i\right\}}\frac{\left|S\right|!\left(\left|M\right|-\left|S\right|-1\right)!}{\left|M\right|!}\left[{f}_{x}\left(S\bigcup\:\left\{i\right\}\right)-{f}_{x}\left(S\right)\right]\end{array}$$\end{document}

Where i is the feature id, ∅(f, x)i represents the SHAP value of feature i to sample x prediction, M represents all feature sets, S represents any feature subset that does not contain feature i, and fx (S⋃{i})-fx (S) represents the additional benefit brought by feature i after joining the set S. This study uses the open source shap library in Python for calculation and visualization.

Spatial analysis

This strategy primarily displays the spatial distribution of modeling results intuitively using GIS mapping tools and further identifies the spatial pattern. In this study, prediction results from the two scenarios were used to produce thematic maps via hotspot analysis and gradient classification. The aim is to explain the spatial co-occurrence of the SBP feature set and FLD risk.

Discussion

Policy implication

Based on the model-driven insights described in Sect. 5, we found that the risk of regional FLD has a complex relationship with SBP feature set, and it is necessary to adopt a management strategy involving the whole society to reduce and prevent the risk.

The analysis results of Sect. 5.2.2 and 5.3.3 show that FLD mainly occurs in the elderly and spreads to young people, and the effect of urbanization health punishment is gradually increasing. For management, it is necessary to strengthen awareness of FLD among the 15–59 age group, and to foster social cognition of the harm [5]. In view of the low proportion of the elderly population in the streets with youth gathering, weak medical care and insufficient supporting facilities, priority should be given to sinking primary medical resources, adding regular screening services for hepatic steatosis [55].

Section 5.2.3 and 5.3 show that healthy food, access to exercise space, and improved vegetation cover can reduce the risk of FLD, while the convenience of traffic conditions has a negative feedback effect. Therefore, the ecological environment department can cooperate with the urban planning department to carry out the transformation of the street living environment and supplement the convenience sports and green food facilities [56]. At the same time, for the high-risk streets with dense buildings, scarce green space and depressed environment, the positive synergistic effect of green space and sports facilities is activated by adding pocket parks, micro-green spaces and vegetation, so as to improve the quality and relieve the negative emotions of residents [57].

The results in Sect. 5.2 and 5.3 also show that the intensity of negative emotions has a obvious influence on the risk, especially in the main urban area, where the spread of negative emotions has a stronger impact. From a management perspective, it is more sustainable than individual efforts by improving public health to prevent disease [58], . Therefore, the urban planning department should coordinate multiple departments to promote the refined update of traffic, focus on improving the pedestrian connection system between the living area and the subway and the main road, add pedestrian greenways and convenient crossing facilities to reduce the emotional anxiety and environmental pressure caused by congestion [59].

Limitations and future work

Although the method of this study has made significant progress in spatiotemporal generalization, and interpretability, there are still some limitations. Firstly, in the SBP framework’s construction stage, the default population object is static, which limits the ability to characterize population activity behavior the distribution of the importance of predictors [60]. For example, although the number of supermarkets reflects the health of diet to a certain extent, it lacks real population access information; the limited contribution of PM2.5 in the model may reflect the insufficient spatial variation of PM2.5 exposure at the street level in the study area, rather than the lack of biological correlation. In addition, in the time interval matching of modeling, we consider that the occurrence of non-communicable diseases has certain cumulative characteristics [61, 62]. In order to reduce the possibility of purely contemporaneous matching and show the numerical changes of each indicator, this study set a temporally separated area-level prediction analysis. However, there is a lack of accurate clinical demonstration of the average time for specific non-communicable diseases, which may affect the reliability of modeling. In future research, population sensing data with high spatiotemporal resolution can be introduced, and the indicator calculation method can be adjusted based on population interaction characteristics [63]. At the same time, the studies with repeated individual-level metabolic assessments and exposure histories based on long-term fine feature change series are needed to determine the true exposure duration, latency, the independent contribution of environmental exposures and causal temporal relationships.

Secondly, there are still lack of representativeness and spatial constraints in multi-source data. In the calculation of variables for psychological factors, we only use Weibo data to calculate a single indicator. Although the platform has a good network usage rate, there is a deviation in the age structure of social media user groups, usually dominated by active young groups, which may lead to insufficient data representation of middle-aged and elderly groups [64]. At the same time, social media data may lack sensitivity and pertinence for identifying psychological symptoms corresponding to specific diseases. Moreover, some data sources still have spatial bias. For example, the real patient data in this study only come from one hospital. This may be like what we stated in the analysis results, residents closer to the hospital may be more likely to generate medical data. Meanwhile, due to the lack of diagnostic criteria that have not been applied to metabolic dysfunction, this study should be understood as an assessment of the spatial heterogeneity for ultrasound defined hepatic steatosis. In addition, infrastructure indicators such as subway stations may not be representative of rural areas [65]. In the future, we consider introducing psychological assessment scale data in historical diagnosis and treatment or the network search data of psychological symptoms that may be related to FLD, so as to enrich the calculation source and index dimension of psychological factor variables. Meanwhile, we believe the framework can be expanded based on differences in rural development, such as education levels, land-use diversity, and water pollution, to enhance its universality, and combining with the real data of multiple hospitals to study the effectiveness and key factors of the new framework.

Finally, the machine learning model in Sect. 4.3.1 does not explicitly control spatial autocorrelation. Due to the occurrence of public health risks usually shows spillover effects and aggregation characteristics in geospatial space [66], ignoring spatial dependence may lead to bias in model evaluation. Meanwhile, the strategies used in this study such as feature contribution and SHAP analysis, are mainly oriented to the interpretation of modeling results, which are not equivalent to the causal reasoning in the real world. In essence, they also belong to the category of correlation analysis. In the future, we consider the joint causality diagram framework in modeling to quantify the real causal effects between variables. And the geographically weighted models will be introduced, such as GWXGB, GWRF, etc. to further reveal the geographical association between various environmental factors in different regions.

Conclusion

This study focuses on the challenge of unknown the geospatial factors for FLD risk in the context of urbanization, aiming to solve three core issues: insufficient spatial resolution, indicator framework design, and interpretability. Based on the guidance of medical prior knowledge, we propose the SBP framework for street-level FLD risk assessment, including three dimensions of social economy, built environment, and psychology. In this study, 90 streets in Hefei’s main planning areas were selected as the research subjects. Combined with multi-regional and dynamic prediction, the feasibility of the SBP indicator framework for detecting the spatial heterogeneity of FLD risk and predicting the risk trend was verified. At the same time, this study also uses multi-model generalization and a contribution voting strategy to identify more relevant factors affecting the risk of FLD. Based on feature ablation, SHAP and spatial distribution visualization, the independent and synergistic effect of different geospatial features are revealed. The above empirical results not only demonstrate the effectiveness of the SBP framework in identifying spatial heterogeneity of FLD risks but also emphasize its potential to identify regional factor differences and inform policy design, providing a quantifiable scientific basis for precise FLD prevention and refined urban health management.
