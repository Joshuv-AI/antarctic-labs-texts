Association of Inpatient Prescribing of First‐Generation Antihistamines With Delirium in Older Adults: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Alanna C Bridgman, Mohammad Arshad Imrit, Surain B Roberts, Mina Tadrous, Nathan M Stall, Michael Fralick, Jennifer Watt, Amol A Verma, Fahad Razak, Aaron M Drucker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSmall studies have reported associations between first‐generation antihistamines and delirium. It is unclear whether first‐generation antihistamines cause clinically important delirium among older adult inpatients.ObjectiveTo estimate the association between inpatient physician prescribing of first‐generation antihistamines and delirium among older general medicine inpatients.MethodsCross‐sectional study using the GEMINI database of inpatient admissions between April 1, 2015, and March 31, 2022, across 17 hospitals in Ontario, Canada, among people aged 65 years and older. The main exposure was the proportion of attending physicians' admissions by quartile that included a first‐generation antihistamine prescription. The primary outcome was inpatient delirium identified using a machine learning tool. We estimated the association between attending physicians' first‐generation antihistamine prescribing rate and individual inpatients' risk of delirium using multivariable mixed‐effects logistic regression.ResultsAmong 328,140 inpatient admissions to 755 physicians, 11,507 (3.5%) admissions included a first‐generation antihistamine prescription. Physicians in the lowest quartile prescribed a first‐generation antihistamine during 2.1% of admissions compared to 5.4% of physicians in the highest quartile. Delirium occurred in 32.3% of admissions to the lowest‐prescribing quartile and 36.6% of the highest‐prescribing quartile of physicians. In adjusted analyses, every 1% absolute increase in first‐generation antihistamine prescribing was associated with 8% increased odds of delirium (aOR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10). Patients admitted to physicians in the highest quartile had 41% increased odds of delirium compared to the lowest quartile (aOR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.28–1.56).ConclusionsOlder adults admitted to physicians who prescribe first‐generation antihistamines more commonly were more likely to experience delirium in the hospital.Keywords: delirium, drug safety, epidemiology, geriatrics, inpatient medicine

Introduction

First‐generation antihistamines, such as diphenhydramine, are among the leading causes of medication‐related harms in older adult outpatients [1], and are listed by groups including the American Geriatrics Society as medications to avoid in older adults [2, 3]. While there is significant potential for these medications to be inappropriately prescribed [4], there is limited data on inpatient prescribing and associated patient outcomes. First‐generation antihistamines are indicated for histamine‐mediated conditions such as urticaria and anaphylaxis but may be prescribed inappropriately for non‐histamine‐mediated pruritic conditions such as type‐IV hypersensitivity drug eruptions or non‐specific pruritus. Even when prescribed for histamine‐mediated conditions, first‐generation antihistamines do not have efficacy benefits over non‐sedating antihistamines, which are associated with fewer harms [5, 6, 7].

Delirium is a common, preventable medical condition that occurs in up to 50% of hospitalized older adults [8]. Delirium has been associated with major adverse outcomes in older adults, including increased mortality, institutionalization, and long‐term cognitive impairment [9]. Identifying and reducing the unnecessary use of deliriogenic medications in hospitals is an important tool to prevent delirium [10, 11, 12]. First‐generation antihistamines may contribute to delirium risk because they cross the blood–brain barrier and interact with other deliriogenic medications [13, 14]. A cohort study of 426 older medical inpatients found that exposure to diphenhydramine was associated with various delirium symptoms [15], but a systematic review found the evidence to be only suggestive of a possible association between first‐generation antihistamines and delirium [16]. Previous studies have relatively small sample sizes, which limit their power to detect small to moderate associations.

The objective of this study was to estimate the association between general medical hospitalist physician prescribing of first‐generation antihistamines and the risk of delirium in their older adult inpatients. We used the attending physician as the exposure because patients are quasirandomly allocated to specific hospitalists independent of patient characteristics.

Methods

We reported our manuscript using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Data Pharmacoepidemiology (RECORD‐PE) guidance [17, 18]. This study is approved by the Research Ethics Board (REB) at each participating hospital through Clinical Trials Ontario, with Unity Health Toronto as the REB of Record.

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This cross‐sectional study included older adults (≥ 65 years) admitted to hospitalist wards at 17 hospitals in Ontario, Canada (Table S1) contributing to the GEMINI database [19], between April 1, 2015, and March 31, 2022. We limited our study to people aged 65 years and older as delirium is more common among older adults admitted to hospital [8]. GEMINI is a large hospital clinical data research network that collects, standardizes, and analyzes data from over 30 acute care Ontario hospitals and currently contains more than 2.4 million hospitalizations, representing more than 50% of all medical/ICU admissions in Ontario [19]. The 17 hospitals included in this analysis were all of the hospitals that had validated pharmacy medication data for the study period.

GEMINI has established processes to collect detailed clinical data, with 98%–100% accuracy on a random sample of over 23,000 individual data points across key fields [20]. Data include patient demographics, diagnoses, vital signs, bloodwork, transfusions, medications, procedures, radiology reports, responsible physicians, ICU use, and hospital length‐of‐stay. GEMINI also captures variables (e.g., patient diagnoses) that are reported by hospitals to the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) Discharge Abstract Database [21] and National Ambulatory Care Reporting System [22].

Details of the hospitalist service model for hospitals included in GEMINI have been described elsewhere [19]. In brief, patients are admitted to the hospital and assigned to a most responsible physician (MRP) who is on call for admissions that day; within each hospital site over large samples of admissions, physician assignment occurs quasirandomly based on physician work schedule, and patient characteristics are balanced across attending physicians [19, 23, 24, 25, 26]. A large body of evidence has demonstrated that physician practice variation in this setting is not driven by differences in patient characteristics [19, 23, 24, 25, 26]; these physicians do not control who is admitted to their service, which reduces selection bias. In academic hospitals, care is provided by clinical teaching teams which include medical students, residents, and fellows under the supervision of the MRP. In community hospitals, care is provided by MRPs and allied health clinicians. In this study there are 8 community hospitals and 9 academic hospitals. Service blocks for the MRP are typically 2 weeks long but tend to vary from 1 to 4 weeks [19]. In this study, we take advantage of the quasirandomization of patients to specific hospitalists and use the hospitalist physician as the exposure to mitigate selection bias and confounding by indication [19, 23, 24, 25, 26].

Eligibility Criteria

We assessed all patients admitted to hospitalist wards between April 1, 2015, and March 31, 2022. We included older adults (≥ 65 years old) admitted to hospitalist wards under an internal medicine MRP (including physicians with subspecialty certification attending on a hospitalist ward). Patient‐days were attributed to MRPs using the CIHI definition—“responsible for the care and treatment of the patient for the majority of the visit to the health care facility.” [27] Therefore, each admission was associated with a single MRP. Patients were excluded if they were admitted to an MRP with fewer than 50 admissions to a hospitalist ward during the study period (to exclude physicians with little service time and therefore non‐representative prescribing). Encounters were excluded if a first‐generation antihistamine was prescribed during the first 36 h of the admission to avoid including reflex prescriptions by admitting teams and medications already used prior to admission.

Exposure

To avoid confounding by indication inherent in directly comparing patients who were prescribed or not prescribed antihistamines, the main exposure was defined as the proportion of admissions attributed to each MRP that included at least one first‐generation antihistamine prescription (standing or as needed).

We analyzed the exposure both as a continuous variable (primary analysis) and by quartiles of antihistamine prescribing (secondary analysis). We calculated antihistamine prescribing quartiles for the secondary analysis within each hospital, rather than across the entire sample, so that quartiles would not be driven by differences in hospital‐level prescribing patterns. Each admission was assigned a physician‐level antihistamine prescribing proportion (or quartile of prescribing proportion) based on the MRP hospitalist.

We included the following first‐generation antihistamines: diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, cyproheptadine, chlorpheniramine, doxylamine, meclizine, promethazine, brompheniramine, pheniramine, triprolidine, azatadine, diphenylpyraline, carbinoxamine, clemastine, antazoline, pyrilamine, mepyramine, tripelennamine, methdilazine, cyclizine, and buclizine. We did not include dimenhydrinate in the exposure definition because it is typically prescribed as an anti‐nauseant rather than for histamine‐mediated conditions, and it is sometimes included in generic as‐needed (PRN) order sets. Pharmacy data were extracted from each hospital's individual pharmacy system. GEMINI‐RxNorm [28] was used to identify antihistamines with each hospital's dataset, with review of each medication by a clinical subject matter expert to ensure accuracy.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was the occurrence of delirium (both incident and prevalent delirium) at any point during an inpatient admission identified using GEMINI's machine learning tool [29]. This tool retrospectively identifies patients with delirium after they have been discharged using data from imaging, labs, pharmacy, and patient touchpoints such as transfers and ICU (Table S2). The model was developed and validated against chart review in a randomly selected sample of 3862 general internal medicine hospital admissions from 6 hospitals in Ontario, Canada [29]. Delirium diagnoses in this dataset were determined through the “Chart‐DEL method” [30]. The model performed with 85% accuracy, 74% positive predictive value, 92% specificity, and 67% sensitivity against the Chart‐DEL method [29], and has higher sensitivity than ICD‐10 codes [31]. The original delirium prediction model included some of the first‐generation antihistamine medications included in this study, so we modified the model to exclude those inputs. We compared the receiver operator characteristics area under the curve (ROC AUC) scores of the original and our modified model and found no substantial degradation (ROC AUC Original Delirium Model: 0.884; ROC AUC Modified Delirium Model: 0.882).

Statistical Analysis

We report standardized mean differences for baseline characteristics between antihistamine prescribing quartiles [32]. We estimated the association between physician‐level first‐generation antihistamine prescribing and inpatient delirium using mixed‐effects logistic regression. The following patient characteristics were used as covariates in multivariable models, selected a priori based on clinical knowledge of their association with delirium: age, sex, year of admission, Charlson Comorbidity Index Score [33] (a predictor of inpatient mortality in Ontario, Canada), pre‐existing dementia (using codes from the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th Revision, Table S3), if they were admitted to hospital from a nursing home, and the modified laboratory‐based acute physiology score (mLAPS), which estimates in‐hospital mortality for general medicine inpatients [34, 35]. While it is unknown whether year of admission, admitted from nursing home, and mLAPS are associated with receiving antihistamine prescriptions, age [36, 37], dementia [37, 38], higher Charlson Comorbidity Index Score [39, 40], and male sex [39, 40] are associated with increased risk of inpatient delirium. We did not include covariates that occurred following our admission, such as co‐administration of other sedating medications. We included random intercepts at the physician level to account for the nesting of patients within physicians and physician‐level variability in delirium rates. We adjust for hospitals as fixed effects.

In our primary analyses, we operationalized our exposure (physician‐level rate of admissions associated with a first‐generation antihistamine prescription) as a continuous variable. We used a 4‐knot restricted cubic spline [41, 42] a priori to allow for a non‐linear relationship between physician‐level antihistamine prescribing rate and odds of delirium. We compared this model to an identical model with the exposure fit as a linear continuous term and proceeded with the linear model based on Akaike's Information Criteria. In a secondary analysis to aid with interpretability, we fit our exposure as within‐hospital physician‐level quartiles of first‐generation antihistamine prescribing and report the odds ratios of delirium when a patient is exposed to a physician in different quartiles.

We conducted subgroup analyses to assess whether the association between physician‐level prescribing and delirium was consistent between subgroups of patients who may be at higher risk of delirium: 10‐year age bands, sex, dementia, and admission from a nursing home. We stratified the subgroups of interest to encounters satisfying these criteria and replicated the primary analyses within strata of interest.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 [43] and models were fit using the glmmTMB package [44].

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted 7 sensitivity analyses using the same covariates: (1) using ICD‐10‐CA codes (Table S3) for delirium during admission (codes starting with “F05”: delirium, not induced by alcohol or other psychoactive substances) to identify the outcome instead of the AI machine learning tool, because ICD‐10 codes are more specific (though less sensitive) [31]; (2) using the ICD‐10 post‐admission diagnosis code for delirium to identify the outcome; (3) excluding prescriptions ordered within the first 36 h of hospitalization to avoid emergency physician prescriptions and/or reflex prescriptions (i.e., on admission order sets); (4) excluding admissions ≥ 4 weeks in duration as patients would likely have more than one MRP; (5) excluding patients receiving palliative care based on a most responsible ICD‐10‐CA discharge diagnosis code (Z51.5) to avoid confounding based on goals of care, as antihistamines may be used for comfort and/or sedation in this patient population; (6) adjusting for benzodiazepines prescribed during the admission, as these are known deliriogenic medications commonly prescribed in the inpatient setting [36]; and (7) assessing the association of first‐generation antihistamines versus no first‐generation antihistamine with delirium at the individual patient encounter level. Sensitivity analyses were adjusted for the same covariates as the primary analysis unless otherwise specified.

Results

There were 415,606 inpatient encounters at the 17 included hospital sites during the study period between April 1, 2015, and March 31, 2022. After exclusions, there were 328,140 inpatient admissions associated with 755 MRPs (Figure 1). There were no major differences in patient characteristics for those in whom we could and could not identify delirium using the AI modeling tool (Table S4).

Flow diagram of admissions included in the study.

The median inpatient age was 80 years (IQR: 73–87) and 48.2% (n = 158,045) of inpatients were male (Table 1). On admission, 35.2% (n = 115,608) of patients had a Charlson Comorbidity Score of ≥ 2, 11.8% (n = 38,736) had a pre‐existing diagnosis of dementia, and 13.5% (n = 44,334) of patients were from a nursing home. Patients treated by physicians in the highest quartile of antihistamine prescribing were younger, more likely to have high comorbidity, and had higher mLAPS scores.

The overall prevalence of delirium in our sample was 34.8% (n = 114,193). The median proportion of hospitalist admissions associated with a first‐generation antihistamine prescription was 3.3% (IQR: 2.9–4.1) (Figure S1). Physicians in the highest quartile prescribed first‐generation antihistamines during 5.4% of their inpatient encounters compared to 2.1% in the lowest quartile (standardized mean difference (SMD) 1.72). Delirium occurred in 36.6% of admissions to physicians in the highest quartile of antihistamine prescribing compared to 32.3% of admissions to physicians in the lowest quartile of antihistamine prescribing.

In adjusted analyses, admissions to higher first‐generation antihistamine prescribing physicians were associated with increased odds of delirium (Figure 2); every 1% absolute increase in physician‐level first‐generation antihistamine prescription proportion was associated with an 8% increased odds of delirium (aOR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10; Table S5). Compared to patients admitted to MRPs in the lowest quartile of first‐generation antihistamine prescribing, patients admitted to MRPs in the highest quartile had a 41% increased odds of delirium (aOR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.28–1.56; Table 2).

Results were consistent across subgroups limited to patients of different ages (65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years old), each of male and female sex, people with a diagnosis of dementia, and people admitted from nursing homes (Table S6).

The results of our sensitivity analyses were supportive of our main findings, other than the analysis excluding admissions longer than 4 weeks, in which the effect estimate moved toward the null (aOR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04) (Table S7). The association was particularly strong in the analysis of the association between first‐generation antihistamines and delirium at the individual encounter exposure level (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.38–1.51).

Discussion

In this study of 328,140 hospitalist inpatient admissions of older adults in Ontario, being admitted to a physician who more commonly prescribes first‐generation antihistamines was associated with increased occurrence of delirium. Results were consistent when adjusting for patient characteristics, applying different definitions of delirium, and across strata of important patient characteristics. Our findings suggest that first‐generation antihistamine prescribing may be a modifiable risk factor for delirium among older inpatients.

Delirium is a highly morbid and costly medical condition that has been associated with increased mortality, hospitalizations, and long‐term cognitive impairment in older adults [9]. The ideal method to study first‐generation antihistamines as a modifiable risk factor for delirium would be a prospective trial randomizing inpatients to receive or not receive a first‐generation antihistamine. Given the known harms associated with first‐generation antihistamines, that approach would be considered unethical, so observational studies are useful [1]. A recent systematic review found only two small single‐centre studies on inpatient antihistamine use and the risk of delirium: a cohort study of 426 older medical inpatients and a case–control study of 245 postoperative patients [15, 16, 45]. That systematic review concluded there was only enough evidence to suggest a possible association—our findings, which are consistent in direction with prior studies, add to the literature supporting first‐generation antihistamines as deliriogenic. Since patients admitted to hospitalist wards are quasirandomized to admitting MRPs, our approach may reduce confounding by indication, allowing us to evaluate the association between physician‐level variation in antihistamine prescribing and inpatient delirium. While we are unable to establish temporality, it is unlikely that a hospitalist physician would knowingly prescribe first‐generation antihistamines to a patient who is already delirious; however, there is evidence to suggest that inpatient providers often miss the diagnosis of delirium [46, 47].

We found significant variability in first‐generation antihistamine prescribing practices among inpatient hospitalist physicians. Physician‐level practice variation in the inpatient setting has been described in domains such as opioid prescribing [26], resource use, and mortality [19, 48, 49]. Future research is needed to understand variations in first‐generation antihistamine prescribing and to promote quality improvement initiatives to encourage appropriate prescribing and reduce variation. Attending physicians should be cautious when prescribing first‐generation antihistamines to older inpatients.

Limitations

While machine learning and artificial intelligence approaches have great potential to open new avenues of investigation for large database studies relying on electronic medical records and to improve clinical care through improved and earlier detection of conditions such as delirium, our approach to delirium detection has limitations. The diagnosis of delirium from both the machine learning tool and ICD‐10 codes is at the hospitalization level; values are not assigned a timestamp. Thus, we are unable to examine the direct temporal association between first‐generation antihistamine utilization among individual inpatients and their risk of subsequent delirium. Future observational studies could employ target trial methodologies to compare the risk of delirium associated with first‐generation antihistamines compared to a relevant comparator. We used a machine learning tool to identify delirium, but this is not the gold standard for delirium identification, which would be active case finding or a detailed retrospective chart review, but such methods are too labor‐intensive to be applied at large scale [30]. Efforts are underway to improve the clinical recognition and documentation of delirium [50], which may improve machine learning models in the future. Nevertheless, misclassification of delirium likely occurred to some extent in our study, which may have biased the effect estimates.

We used CIHI's definition of an MRP to assign a single attending physician to each admission [27]; however, if a patient has a long admission, more than one MRP would likely be involved in their care, and when we excluded admissions greater than 4 weeks in duration, the effect estimate was attenuated toward the null. Physicians who prescribe higher rates of first‐generation antihistamines may also be more likely to precipitate delirium in other ways, which we did not account for (i.e., higher rates of foley catheter use or other psychotropic medications). Importantly, our sensitivity analysis adjusting for benzodiazepine use yielded similar results to our primary analysis.

Conclusions

Among older general medicine inpatients, being admitted to high prescribers of first‐generation antihistamines was associated with increased odds of delirium. These findings suggest that inpatient first‐generation antihistamines may be associated with an increased risk of delirium, and their prescribing should be approached carefully for older adults.
