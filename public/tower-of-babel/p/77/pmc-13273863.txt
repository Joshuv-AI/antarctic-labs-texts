ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermination of suction stress characteristic curve is essential for understanding the behavior of unsaturated soils. Existing methods suffer from several shortcomings such as inability to capture suction stress at low water contents, non-uniform distribution of suction stress, and reliance on multiple fitting parameters that are difficult to calibrate. A shrinkage-based method is developed to estimate the suction stress characteristic curve from the soil shrinkage curve.•This paper develops a relationship between volumetric strain and suction stress.•This paper describes the inclusion of capillarity and absorption in the above relationship.•This paper validates the shrinkage-based method through comparison with established models.Keywords: Suction stress characteristic curve, Shrinkage curve, Unsaturated soils

Background

Suction stress (σs) is an independent stress state variable, which is derived from interparticle stresses present in soils [2]. Since this parameter cannot be measured directly using conventional laboratory methods, suction stress is indirectly determined from measurable data such as matric suction or tensile strength. Conceptually, such stresses result in two primary soil-water interaction mechanisms; adsorption of pore water on the surface of soil particles and capillarity due to the curvature of the air-water interface in soil pores [3]. The adsorptive mechanisms originate from physicochemical forces [4,5], van der Waals attraction (σvdr), double layer repulsion (σdlr), and particle cementation (σcem). In contrast, the capillary mechanisms originate from physical interactions [6]. pore water pressure (uw) related to matric suction and capillary pressure (σcap) arising from surface tension. With desaturation, the thickness of water film around particles decreases thereby bringing the particles closer together and, as such, increasing van der Waals attraction. The increased concentration of ions within the thinner water film decreases double layer repulsion among particles. Cementation is generally not affected because new chemical compounds, which can precipitate, are not formed by desaturation. Furthermore, the lower amount of water in the soil pores means that the pore water pressure decreases (matric suction increases) whereas the smaller pore sizes result in an increase in capillary pressure. Clearly, increase in van der Waals attraction, decrease in double layer repulsion, and increase in capillary pressure are related to soil shrinkage due to desaturation [2,7].

The suction stress characteristic curve (SSCC) plots the interparticle stress with respect to soil properties. Several methods have been developed to determine SSCC. The suction based method developed by Lu et al. [8] does not capture adsorptive suction stress at low water content. Although the unified effective stress based method incorporates both adsorptive and capillary suction stresses [2], it involves multiple fitting parameters which are difficult to calibrate. The Brazilian tensile strength method developed by Akin and Likos [9] is limited because tensile stresses are localized and do not represent the uniform distribution of suction stress in the soil. The drying cake method developed by Dong and Lu [1] uses the soil shrinkage curve along with the unconfined compression tests for elastic modulus determination. The use of compression tests is not adequate to capture tension and separate soil specimens for the two types of tests that result in fabric variations.

This paper develops a simple method to determine SSCC using shrinkage behavior of soils during desaturation, thereby providing a mechanistically consistent description of suction stress development. The novelty of this paper is: (i) development of a bilinear relationship that captures both capillary and adsorptive mechanisms – an improvement over the water retention-based method of Lu et al. [8]. (ii) use of the shrinkage curve only without requiring elastic modulus determination from unconfined compression test – an improvement over the drying cake method of Dong and Lu [1]; and (iii) preclusion of dependence on multiple fitting parameters required in the effective stress-based model of Zhang and Lu [2]. Using published data (soil shrinkage and suction stress) on ten different soils, a two-step equation was obtained to correlate suction stress with volumetric strain (ε). The equation was used to generate the SSCC from the shrinkage curve test data determined for a fine-grained clayey soil by Suchan and Azam [10]. The resulting SSCC was compared (trends and magnitudes) with established theoretical models, namely: water retention model by Lu et al. [8], effective stress model by Zhang and Lu [2] and tensile strength based model by Akin and Likos [9].

Method details

Fig. 1 gives the variation of shrinkage parameters with suctions stress. SSCC and shrinkage data of three silty soils and seven clayey soils reported by Lu and Dong [11], Dong and Lu [1] and Zhang and Lu [2] were used. The shrinkage curves (Fig. 1a) are J-shaped comprising two linear segments: proportional shrinkage and residual shrinkage [10,12]. Proportional shrinkage represents a saturated (S = 1) soil where there is no air in the voids (Va = 0) and the loss of water volume (Vw) equals the volume decrease of voids (Vv = Vw) resulting in an equivalent reduction in the overall soil volume (e = Vw/Vs). In contrast, residual shrinkage represents an unsaturated soil (S < 1), where air partially occupies the voids (Va > 0) causing capillary pressure to shrink void volume (Vv = Va + Vw), and producing a soil volume (e = (Va + Vw)/Vs) decrease that is smaller than the volume of water lost (Vw) [13]. Finally, Fig. 1c shows a bi-linear volumetric strain versus suction stress variation with an initial steep increase (capillarity) followed by a low increase (adsorption). In the capillary mechanism, larger pore spaces in the soil allows greater increase in volumetric strain, while the decrease in pore water pressure and increase in capillary forces cause moderate increase of suction stress [13]. In the adsorptive mechanism, the soil skeleton becomes stiffer to undergo further volumetric strain but the short interparticle distances cause high van der Waals force whereas double layer repulsion drops and cementation stays constant [11]. This behaviour is conceptually presented in Fig. 2, which is based on the analysis of ten soils reported by Lu and Dong [11], Dong and Lu [1] and Zhang and Lu [2] and analyzed in Appendix A. The capillary regime ranges between fully wet conditions (σswet, εwet) and the shrinkage limit (σsSL, εSL) whereas the adsorptive regime is from the shrinkage limit (σsSL, εSL) to the dry conditions (σsdry, εdry). The shrinkage curve exhibited the typical J-shape (Fig. 4a) and corresponding volumetric strain versus gravimetric water content, calculated based on Eq. (1), was found to have a reverse trend (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, the measured water retention curve (WRC) (Fig. 4c) closely followed the van Genuchten equation fitted [16]. In the volumetric strain versus suction stress plot (Fig. 4d), the various colored lines represent the suction stress characteristic curves predicted using different models: shrinkage-based method proposed in this study (black); effective stress-based model by Zhang and Lu [2] (blue); water retention-based model by Lu et al. [8] (green); and tensile strength-based model by Akin and Likos [9] (red). The proposed equation shows two linear components: an initial steep slope (capillary suction stress of up to 22.25 kPa with a volumetric strain of up to 0.136) followed by a flat slope (adsorptive suction stress of about 37.5 kPa with a volumetric strain of 0.144); the inflexion point pertains to the shrinkage limit.

The trend closely matches that by Zhang and Lu [2], which captures both adsorptive and capillary suction stress. Their model shows a smooth transition by using an error function utilizing the transitional water content (wtranss). This parameter is similar to the εSL in the proposed equation, which is treated as a singular number. Furthermore, the shrinkage curve is based on the tensile behavior of soils, which aligns with the development of suction stress. This is an improvement over the model by Zhang and Lu [2], which is based on compressive behavior of soils, which is opposite of the development of suction stress.

The Lu et al. [8] model shows continuously increasing suction stress with a relatively sharp initial increase in capillary suction stress up to 5 kPa followed by a low increase via adsorptive suction stress. The model is based on the air entry value of matric suction in WRC. The underestimated volumetric strain in adsorptive regime should be attributed to the exclusion of residual conditions in this model.

The Akin and Likos [9]. model follows a wetting path showing a constant adsorptive regime (no change in volumetric strain with decreasing suction stress) depicted by the first component of the equation, that is, oven dry conditions. The capillary regime is much smaller and shows almost a constant suction stress associated with most of the change in volumetric strain. This model does not capture the suction stress variation similar to other models and the proposed model.

Limitations

The proposed method was validated using an inert fine-grained soil exhibiting a J-shaped shrinkage curve. For clayey soils (pre-consolidated and compacted) with S-shaped curve, the model needs to be further calibrated and validated. Furthermore, the method does not capture osmotic suction stress, which may be critical for fine-grained clayey soils when infiltrated by saline water.
