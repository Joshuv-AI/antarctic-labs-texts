Programmable Artificial-Cellular Membrane Dynamics via Ring-Closing Metathesis
Authors: Rei Hamaguchi, Damian Alexander Graf, Kazushi Kinbara, Thomas R. Ward
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lateral phase-separated structures in cellular membranes serve as functional domains in various membrane-mediated biological processes. Controlling phase-separation dynamics through catalysis could enable the assembly of smart vesicles that exhibit life-like phenomena. Herein, we present the first example of dynamic control of lateral phase separation via a catalytic reaction: ring-closing olefin metathesis (RCM). This catalytic reaction occurs near the membrane by engineering the surface of lipid membranes with a biotin-streptavidin-based artificial metalloenzyme (ArM). We designed a substrate that, following RCM, releases decanoic acid, which could be spontaneously incorporated into the lipid bilayer, resulting in the disappearance of lipid domains within the membrane. Genetic optimization of the ArM led to a 3-fold increase in catalytic activity, ultimately leading to the budding of a larger lipid domain.

Introduction

Lateral phase separation of membranes is one of the most fundamental phase-transition phenomena, which is critical for cellular homeostasis − and for engineering smart nanomaterials. − Specifically, the dynamic control of phase-separated domains, known as lipid rafts, in cellular membranes is crucial for membrane-mediated biomolecular processes such as signal transduction − and exocytosis. , The formation of membrane domains in living cells is thought to be linked to the coexistence of liquid-ordered (Lo) and liquid-disordered (Ld) domains observed in model membranes composed of phospholipids and cholesterol. , Their dynamics are known to be influenced by temperature, , pH, osmotic pressure, , mechanical force, and spatial confinement (Figure a). In addition to physical stimuli, chemical reactions can influence the dynamics of lateral phase separation, as illustrated by observations of lipid modifications arising from a photooxidation reaction. This approach could enable the use of diverse chemical fuels to modulate the behavior of life-like nanomaterials, including protocells. We report an example in which phase-separated domains formed in a lipid bilayer membrane are dynamically controlled through a transition metal-catalyzed reaction. This reaction is catalyzed by an artificial metalloenzyme (ArM), a biohybrid catalyst composed of an abiotic metal cofactor anchored within a protein, which causes either the disappearance or the budding of lipid domains within the vesicles.

(c) Caged decanoic acid (CDA) releases decanoic acid (DA) and naphthalene as a result of RCM followed by a spontaneous 1,4-elimination.

Our system consists of phospholipid vesicles that harbor a ring-closing metathase (a Ru­(NHC)-containing ArM based on the biotin-streptavidin (Sav) technology) that catalyzes a ring-closing metathesis reaction (RCM) of a diolefin. This ArM is immobilized on the membrane’s surface via a biotinylated lipid, enabling localized RCM to occur in the vicinity of the membrane (Figure b). Relying on a bioorthogonal uncaging reaction that has been previously described, , we designed a substrate that releases decanoic acid (DA). The caged decanoic acid (CDA) first undergoes RCM to afford a 1,4-dihydronaphthalene intermediate, which spontaneously eliminates DA and naphthalene via a 1,4-elimination (Figure c). By applying this “close-to-release” approach, we harnessed a catalytic RCM to generate DA in close proximity to the phospholipid membrane of the protocell.

We surmised that, following uncaging triggered by RCM, the released DA may be incorporated into the lipid bilayer of the protocell, as reported for fatty acids. , Fatty acids contribute to critical membrane properties, including fluidity, elasticity, and curvature-strain. , The uncaging of DA at membrane interfaces could provide a programmable means of controlling the lipid domain organization via ring-closing metathesis. Building on this concept, we engineered a localized catalytic system to chemically control lipid domain dynamics on the surface of a protocell.

Results and Discussion

To anchor the ArM based on the biotin-Sav technology, we synthesized a biotinylated lipid, DPPE-PEG-Biot (DPPE is 1,2-di­pal­mito­yl-sn-glyc­ero-3-phos­pho­ethan­ola­mine, and Biot is biotin), enabling its immobilization on the surface of the lipid bilayer (Figure a). To confirm that Sav equipped with one equivalent of DPPE-PEG-Biot can be immobilized on the vesicle’s surface, we further functionalized Sav with a biotinylated fluorophore (Biot-Alexa488). The fluorescently labeled Sav was then incubated with giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) composed of 1,2-di­ole­oyl-sn-gly­cero-3-phos­pho­chol­ine (DOPC), which were prepared using the gentle hydration method (Figure b). Fluorescence microscopy revealed that GUVs containing 5 mol % DPPE-PEG-Biot (relative to DOPC) exhibited notably higher fluorescence intensity, caused by Biot-Alexa488, compared to GUVs lacking DPPE-PEG-Biot (Figure c). These results confirm that DPPE-PEG-Biot contributes to immobilizing Sav on the lipid bilayer surface.

Immobilization of the Metathase on the Lipid Membrane

Next, to anchor the artificial metathase based on the biotin-Sav technology (Ru-Biot@SavWT hereafter), we employed large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) composed of DOPC, which were prepared using the extrusion method. To evaluate the catalytic activity of the membrane-bound artificial metathase (Ru-Biot@SavWT·LUV hereafter), we purified the conjugated LUVs by size exclusion chromatography (SEC), enabling the removal of unbound Ru-Biot@SavWT (Figure a). To assess the RCM activity of Ru-Biot@SavWT·LUV, we subjected the caged umbelliferone substrate (CUMB) to RCM, resulting in the release of umbelliferone and naphthalene (Figure b). We observed that the yield of umbelliferone increased with the concentration of DPPE-PEG-Biot and thus with higher ArM loading on the LUVs (Figure c). The Stewart assay confirmed that the third SEC fraction contained the highest number of phospholipids, confirming that the majority of LUVs were eluted in this fraction (Figure S5b). These results suggest that a larger amount of the metathase eluted because of its immobilization on the LUV via DPPE-PEG-Biot. To examine potential changes in LUV size distribution upon Sav conjugation, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements before and after ArM assembly and immobilization on the LUV. Despite slight variations in distribution profiles, the LUVs remained monodisperse with a peak corresponding to a diameter of around 200 nm, independent of the DPPE-PEG-Biot concentration relative to DOPC (Figure d). Although the multiple biotin-binding sites on Sav could, in principle, induce cross-linking between biotinylated LUVs, we observed minimal aggregation under the conditions tested.

To determine whether the immobilization of Sav on the vesicle surface alters the morphology of the LUVs, we subjected them to cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM). The LUVs were rapidly frozen in vitreous ice without dehydration or staining, thereby preserving their native shapes. For LUVs without DPPE-PEG-Biot and LUVs containing 1 mol % DPPE-PEG-Biot relative to DOPC, the lipid bilayer membranes exhibited uniformly smooth surfaces. In contrast, LUVs containing 5 mol % DPPE-PEG-Biot relative to DOPC displayed multiple dense black spots near the membrane’s surface, presumably resulting from the surface-bound metathase (Figure e). These observations are consistent with experimental results confirming that Sav is immobilized on GUVs containing 5 mol % DPPE-PEG-Biot (Figure c). Importantly, the LUVs with the same composition exhibit the highest RCM activity using CUMB. Notably, the overall morphology of the LUVs remains intact, indicating that surface conjugation of the metathase does not compromise the vesicle integrity.

Influence of the Artificial Metathase on Membrane Dynamics

As described above, we confirmed that the metathase is indeed immobilized on the surface of the vesicles and that the resulting Ru-Biot@SavWT·LUV conjugate catalyzes RCM near the protocell’s lipid bilayer. We hypothesized that the localized ArM on the membrane may increase the local concentration of the product, resulting from its RCM activity. As a result, the DA produced through the RCM may be incorporated into the phospholipid membrane, thereby allowing metathase to regulate lateral phase separation. To monitor the progress of the uncaging reaction mediated by Ru-Biot@SavWT·LUV, we quantified the amount of naphthalene released after 1,4-elimination using GC-MS. Gratifyingly, GC-MS analysis confirmed the presence of naphthalene, supporting that CDA undergoes RCM followed by 1,4-elimination in the presence of Ru-Biot@SavWT·LUV (Figure S6c).

Lipid Domain Disappearance

To further investigate the effect of DA release on lateral phase separation, we selected the following lipid composition: DOPC/1,2-di­pal­mi­toyl-sn-gly­cero-3-phos­pho­cho­line (DPPC)/cholesterol = 35/35/30. With the aim of preparing vesicles containing raft domains, this ratio was selected based on previously reported ternary phase diagrams. We then prepared GUVs composed of DOPC, DPPC, and cholesterol, which exhibited Lo domains, and subsequently conjugated them with Ru-Biot@SavWT (Ru-Biot@SavWT·GUV hereafter). To visualize the Lo domains by fluorescence microscopy, a small amount of the fluorescent membrane dye 1,2-di­ole­oyl-sn-gly­cero-3-phos­pho­ethan­ola­mine-N-(liss­amine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (18:1 Liss-Rhod PE), which localizes in the phase enriched in unsaturated lipids, was added to the GUVs. The addition of caged decanoic acid (CDA) to the GUVs led to the following observations: (i) For GUVs without DPPE-PEG-Biot or Ru-Biot@SavWT, the Lo domains remained visible up to 40 min after CDA addition (Figure a). This suggests that CDA alone does not significantly affect the lateral phase separation of the GUVs. (ii) Similarly, for the GUVs without DPPE-PEG-Biot but with “free” Ru-Biot@SavWT, no noticeable change in the Lo domains was observed after the addition of CDA (Figure a). (iii) For the Ru-Biot@SavWT·GUV with 5 mol % DPPE-PEG-Biot, the number of lipid domains gradually decreased upon CDA addition. Strikingly, the large Lo domains initially present disappeared 40 min after CDA addition (Figure a). This suggests that the membrane-associated artificial metathase, which catalyzes the production of DA in the immediate vicinity of the lipid bilayer, likely plays a critical role in triggering the disappearance of the lipid domains.

Membrane Budding

We next hypothesized that a more active metathase conjugated to the protocell’s membrane might contribute to further increasing the local DA concentration, facilitating its partition into the outer leaflet of the lipid bilayer. This, in turn, might induce more pronounced changes in membrane properties, such as curvature, ultimately promoting membrane budding, as DA incorporation may enhance the membrane curvature. To test this idea, we screened Sav mutants, leading to the identification of Ru-Biot@SavK121R, which exhibited a 3-fold higher turnover number (TON) than Ru-Biot@SavWT for DA production via RCM of CDA (Figure S7).

Vesicles possessing larger raft domains have been reported to undergo domain budding facilitated by two key effects: (i) destabilization arising from hydrophobic mismatch caused by the difference in membrane thickness between the Lo and Ld domains and (ii) an increase in line tension caused by the extended domain boundaries. − It is also well established that Lo domains are enriched in saturated lipids compared to Ld domains. Therefore, we hypothesized that increasing the overall saturated lipid content would promote Lo domain expansion and fusion, thereby enhancing line tension and ultimately assisting the domain-budding process. Accordingly, we increased the proportion of DPPC in the lipid composition of the GUVs and found that at a DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol ratio of 20/50/30, vesicles reproducibly formed larger lipid domains, which were again labeled with Liss-Rhod PE. Upon treating Ru-Biot@SavK121R·GUV with CDA, we monitored morphological changes using fluorescence microscopy. After approximately 30 min, the budding of the red-labeled lipid domain was clearly observed (Figure b). Merged fluorescence images confirmed that the budded domain originated from the parent vesicle membrane. Initially, the red microdomain remained connected to the vesicle. Gratifyingly, after 30 min, it appeared as a fully spherical vesicle, confirming the completion of the budding process (Figure b).

MD Simulations Provide Atomic-Level Insight into the Effect of DA on Membrane Properties

Atomic-level insight into the partitioning of molecules into the lipid bilayer and its effect on membrane dynamics could lead to unraveling of the details of the mechanism behind the lipid domain disappearance and the membrane-budding phenomena. Molecular dynamics (MD) is a powerful tool for investigating the dynamic behavior of molecules in complex environments, such as a multicomponent lipid bilayer, which is challenging to probe experimentally.

To investigate whether the caged decanoic acid (CDA) or decanoic acid (DA) is spontaneously incorporated into a lipid bilayer, we performed a 100 ns MD simulation of a membrane–water system containing a single CDA or DA molecule dissolved in the aqueous phase. As the initial configuration, the center of mass (COM) of each molecule was placed 4 nm from the bilayer midplane along the membrane normal (Figure a,d). The results highlight that after 100 ns simulations, CDA is not incorporated into the bilayer, whereas DA is incorporated into one leaflet of the bilayer (Figure c,f). We also analyzed the z-coordinate of the COM of CDA and DA as a function of simulation time. These results suggest that CDA was not spontaneously incorporated into the membrane despite multiple contacts with the membrane surface (Figure b). In stark contrast, DA was spontaneously incorporated into the lipid bilayer roughly 20 ns after the start of the production run. Importantly, it remains incorporated within one of the leaflets after incorporation into the membrane (Figure e). This behavior highlights the propensity of DA to be incorporated into the lipid bilayer. This feature differs significantly from that of its precursor CDA, particularly in terms of its ability to partition into the membrane. These findings offer a possible rationale for the experimentally observed changes in membrane dynamics occurring only in vesicles conjugated with metathase, potentially due to the localized production of DA near the membrane.

To probe the effect of DA on lipid bilayer dynamics, we performed several MD simulations of membrane–water systems, which are composed of approximately the same number of DOPC, DPPC, and cholesterol as those used in the experiments where the lipid domain disappearance was observed, containing different concentrations of DA. Multiple molecular simulation studies of phase-separation phenomena in lipid bilayers have been reported. The lipid order parameter, such as the deuterium order parameter (S CD) of the phospholipid molecules, is strongly correlated with the phase-transition temperature of the lipid bilayer membrane. , To scrutinize the effect of DA on the physicochemical properties of the lipid bilayer, we calculated the absolute value of S CD for DPPC. The results suggest that these S CD values decrease with increasing DA concentration (Figure g). We surmised that DA contributes to the lowering of the phase-transition temperature of the lipid bilayer membrane. Experimental determination of the phase-transition temperature of DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol mixtures is challenging. However, prior reports have correlated the behavior in the ternary system with transition temperatures measured by differential scanning calorimetry of the binary DPPC/cholesterol mixture. , In the present work, the DPPC/cholesterol transition temperature decreased with increasing DA concentration (Figure S8). Moreover, fluorescence microscopy of DA-pretreated DOPC/DPPC/cholesterol GUVs revealed that the fraction of vesicles exhibiting Lo domains decreased as the DA concentration increased (Figure S9b). These observations are consistent with the MD simulations, which predict a DA-induced decrease in the bilayer phase-transition temperature. Based on the simulations and our experimental findings, we hypothesize that DA released via RCM and subsequently incorporated into the membrane lowers the bilayer phase-transition temperature, leading to the disappearance of Lo domains. The incorporation of DA into the lipid bilayer may contribute to increasing the surface-to-volume ratio and thus affect the curvature of the membrane, ultimately resulting in the budding of lipid domains. This is consistent with a previous study by Webb revealing that a vesicle-budding process occurs when the temperature of vesicles with vastly separated domains is increased. Furthermore, we hypothesize that the change in lipid packing associated with the incorporation of DA is involved in the membrane-budding process. It is known that the area per lipid parameter (APL) computed in the simulation is strongly correlated with the packing state of lipid molecules, which influences the membrane curvature and ultimately affects the self-assembled structure of the lipid molecules. We thus determined the APL for DOPC and DPPC from the MD simulations of membrane–water systems containing DA. We found that the APL increases with increasing DA concentration (Figure h). An increase in APL, combined with fatty acid asymmetry and domain line tension, likely contributes to the experimentally observed membrane budding.

Conclusion

In this study, we achieved dynamic control of lipid domain behavior by immobilizing an artificial metathase on lipid bilayers via biotin-Sav technology, enabling the localized release of DA through ring-closing metathesis. This chemically induced modulation triggered large-scale membrane responses, including lipid domain disappearance and domain budding. MD simulations highlighted that DA may be spontaneously incorporated into the lipid bilayer, thereby altering critical membrane properties, such as the phase-transition temperature and area per lipid. Taken together, these findings provide atomistic insight into how local catalytic activity can drive macroscopic changes in the membrane organization.

To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first demonstration of membrane dynamics regulated through a localized catalytic event. By combining metalloenzyme catalysis with supramolecular membrane immobilization, this approach establishes a molecular strategy for functionalizing lipid membranes and offers a blueprint for the development of life-like nanomaterials capable of mimicking membrane-mediated biological processes.
