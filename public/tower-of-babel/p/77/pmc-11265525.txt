Levopositioned caudal vena cava and circumcaval left ureter with subsequent hydronephrosis and hydroureter in a dog
Authors: Eleanor L. E. Woolley, Kathryn Hogan, Arthur K. House
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presentation and investigation of a levopositioned caudal vena cava, a rare congenital vascular abnormality seen mainly in Bernese Mountain Dogs, which resulted in ureteral compression, hydronephrosis and hydroureter. Surgical transection, transposition and anastomosis were performed to reposition the circumcaval ureter. A 19‐month‐old male neutered Bernese Mountain Dog was presented with a 13‐month history of vague signs including intermittent abdominal pain, inappetence and diarrhoea, which were poorly responsive to medical management. Abdominal ultrasound revealed left‐sided hydronephrosis and hydroureter. Further investigation included abdominal computed tomography and pre‐ and post‐intravenous iodinated contrast, which revealed a levopositioned caudal vena cava with associated ureteral displacement to a circumcaval position, resulting in ureteral compression and proximal dilation affecting both the proximal ureter and the left kidney. The patient was also found to have concomitant gall bladder agenesis. Surgery was performed to transect, transpose and anastomose the ureter in a normal anatomical position, and the patient made an excellent clinical recovery. All gastrointestinal signs resolved within 2 weeks of surgery and remained resolved 12 months later. Follow‐up ultrasound and pyelography were performed at 4‐month post‐surgery, revealing a mild improvement in the hydronephrosis and hydroureter. Although rare, circumcaval ureter should be considered a differential in dogs presenting with hydroureter and hydronephrosis, especially Bernese Mountain Dogs.

INTRODUCTION

More commonly seen as an incidental finding in cats, circumcaval (also referred to as retrocaval) ureters are a rare cause of hydroureter and hydronephrosis in dogs (Bélanger et al., 2014; Doust et al., 2006; Duconseille et al., 2010; Izquierdo Robert et al., 2021; Schwarz et al., 2020). They occur due to a congenital malformation of the caudal vena cava (CdVC) (Bass et al., 2000) and may be incidental findings or cause a clinically relevant compression of the ureter, resulting in partial or full obstruction. The purpose of this case report was to document the presentation, investigation and surgical management of a rare condition in full, the only canine case reported to date which includes caval malpositioning, documented with advanced imaging and addressed with surgical transection, transposition and anastomosis.

HISTORY

A 19‐month‐old neutered male Bernese Mountain Dog presented with a 13‐month history of chronic intermittent abdominal pain, inappetence and diarrhoea with occasional haematochezia, which probiotics and dietary modification had been only partially successful in managing. No abnormalities had been noted on a full physical examination. Routine urinalysis, haematological and biochemical screening (including blood urea nitrogen, creatinine and electrolytes; symmetrical dimethylarginine [SDMA] was not included) and paired pre‐ and post‐prandial bile acid testing had not found any significant abnormalities. Abdominal ultrasound, performed prior to referral for further investigation, had revealed left‐sided hydronephrosis and hydroureter, an absent gall bladder and an enlarged prostate.

DIAGNOSTIC FINDINGS AND INTERPRETATION

The patient was anaesthetised, and 1.25 mm transverse computed tomographic (CT) images of the thorax and abdomen were acquired in both pre‐and post‐ (seven series) intravenous contrast medium administration with soft tissue, lung and bone algorithms on a GE Optima CT520 CT scanner. A half‐volume iodinated intravenous contrast (iohexol 300 mg/mL, dosed at 300 mg/kg due to concern for possible reduced renal function) was administered. Specialist imaging reporting of the CT confirmed the findings of an enlarged left kidney (9 cm cranial to caudal) with severe hydronephrosis (renal pelvis 6 cm cranial to caudal), marked hydroureter causing a 4.5 cm dilation of the left proximal ureter and obstruction of the left ureter caused by a levopositioned CdVC (see Figures 1a–c and 2), enlarged tortuous bile duct with absent gall bladder and mild prostatomegaly consistent with recently‐neutered status. At the level of the left renal vein, the CdVC was noted to transition to the left and, from the level of the cranial aspect of the sixth lumbar vertebra caudally, it was at the left ventral aspect of the aorta. The distended proximal left ureter extended caudally, and, at the level of the caudal aspect of the fifth lumbar vertebra, it gradually attenuated to 4.5 mm as it deviated craniomedially, extended over the dorsal aspect of the abnormally located CdVC and ran between the CdVC and the aorta. The ureter then extended caudally along the ventral aspect of the CdVC and aorta and, at the level of the caudal aspect of the sixth lumbar vertebra, deviated ventrolaterally to enter the bladder. The distal ureter from the point of transition over the CdVC to the bladder was of normal size (2.3 mm). The hydronephrotic left kidney was noted to be excreting contrast medium, which settled to the gravity‐dependent portion of the distended renal pelvis and ureter. The right kidney and ureter were normal.

Routine biochemical (including blood urea nitrogen, creatinine and electrolytes; SDMA was not included), haematological and urinalysis (collected via ureterocentesis from the left hydroureter under ultrasound guidance) testing were repeated with no abnormalities detected, including negative bacterial and fungal culture.

TREATMENT AND OUTCOME

Given the residual function of the left kidney, with urine production noted on CT scan, the options of placement of a Subcutaneous Ureteral Bypass (SUB) device or performing surgery to reroute the circumcaval ureter were considered appropriate, with the salvage option of performing a left nephroureterectomy also discussed with the patient's owners. The decision was made to attempt to surgically reroute the ureter. The patient was anaesthetised (methadone 0.3 mg/kg intramuscular premedication, alfaxalone 2 mg/kg intravenous induction, isoflurane gaseous maintenance anaesthesia and transabdominal plane block under ultrasound guidance with 0.5% bupivacaine). A 100 mcg/h transdermal fentanyl patch was placed at induction, and a fentanyl constant rate infusion (CRI) administered intravenously at 3 mcg/kg/h throughout the procedure. This CRI was continued post‐operatively and then gradually tapered between 12 and 18 h post‐operatively in response to serial analgesia assessment using the Glasgow Composite Pain Scale (Short Form) (Reid et al., 2023). Perioperative prophylactic intravenous cephazolin was administered at induction and repeated every 2 h throughout surgery, and a ventral midline celiotomy was performed. Falciform fat was removed, and exploration of the abdominal cavity was performed, which confirmed the presence of a malpositioned CdVC, positioned to the left side of the aorta, causing circumcaval deviation of the left ureter. There was marked dilation of the portion of the left ureter that was proximal to the circumcaval segment, and the remaining (distal) left ureter was of normal size. The length of the left ureter was dissected free from the retroperitoneal fascia and subsequently transected approximately 1 cm from the ureterovesicular junction. The free proximal ureter was then passed from medial to lateral, ventral to the malpositioned CdVC, to restore normal ureteral positioning and relieve ureteric compression. Catheterisation of both portions of the transected ureter confirmed the absence of strictures. Urine flow was observed from the transected end of the left ureter, and a sample was collected for culture and sensitivity testing, which was negative. Fascial adhesions to the proximal ureter were bluntly dissected to ensure no ureteral compression remained. The transected ureter was anastomosed with 4/0 PDS simple interrupted sutures. Normal peristalsis was observed along the length of the left ureter following anastomosis. The abdomen was copiously lavaged with warmed sterile saline, and abdominal closure was performed in a routine manner.

The patient was discharged from the hospital after 24 h and continued to recover uneventfully. The patient was observed to urinate normally post‐operatively. A steady improvement in demeanour, stool consistency and appetite was noted by the owners post‐discharge. At a revisit 6 days post‐operatively, an abdominal ultrasound revealed no uroabdomen, and blood electrolytes were within normal limits. No further testing was performed at this time. Follow‐up abdominal ultrasonography performed 4 months post‐operatively revealed continued, though mildly improved, left hydronephrosis and hydroureter. The kidney had reduced in size from 9.0 to 7.9 cm in the long axis, and proximal ureteral dilation had reduced from 4.5 to 2.2 cm in the transverse axis. Ureteral peristalsis was visualised, although there was no evidence of a ureteral jet on Doppler imaging of the bladder trigone. Biochemical and haematological testing at this time were all within normal limits other than a mild creatinine elevation of 162 µmol/L (39–117 µmol/L) and a mild SDMA elevation of 18 (reference range <18 µg/dL), consistent with International Renal Interest Society (IRIS, iris‐kidney.com) Stage II chronic kidney disease (CKD). Urinalysis at this time on a sample collected via pyelocentesis revealed dilute urine (USG 1.006), normal dipstick parameters, normal sediment and no inflammatory or infectious agents. Culture and sensitivity testing was negative. Ultrasound‐guided percutaneous anterograde pyelography was performed on the left kidney with 10 mL of iodinated contrast diluted 50:50 in sterile saline. Serial abdominal radiographs taken over the following 30 min showed pooling of the contrast in the dilated renal pelvis, though no passage into the dilated ureter was noted in this time frame (Figure 3).

After 12 months post‐operatively, the patient continues to eat, drink and urinate normally. Owners report a dramatic improvement in the patient's demeanour since 2 weeks post‐surgery, with no further episodes of abdominal pain, diarrhoea or haematochezia and a consistent good appetite.

Despite the marked clinical improvements noted by the owners, imaging revealed only mild improvement in the gross appearance of the left kidney following surgical correction of the ureteral pathway. There was no evidence of pyelonephritis; however, the ureteral dilation and hydronephrosis do represent a risk of this developing in the future. Feeding of a renal diet, ongoing monitoring of renal parameters, urine protein levels and blood pressure (as recommended for IRIS stage II CKD; IRiS International Renal Interest Society, 2023) and continued regular monitoring for signs of pyelonephritis were advised.

DISCUSSION

The embryogenesis of the CdVC is complex, involving anastomosis and regression of three paired venous systems (the sub‐, supra‐ and posterior cardinal systems); hence, embryonic malformations can result in one or a combination of four anatomic variants, including transposition/left‐sided CdVC, duplicated CdVC, retroaortic renal vein and circumaortic left renal vein (Bass et al., 2000). The combination of left‐sided CdVC and circumcaval ureter as seen in this case occurs with two simultaneous caudal vena caval anomalies: the regression of the supracardinal system, which usually forms the infrarenal segment of the CdVC, and the persistence of the left subcardinal system, leading to the development of a left‐sided CdVC (Pierro et al., 1990). The supracardinal system would normally lie dorsal to the developing ureter; however, the ventral position of the subcardinal system that persists results in a circumcaval ureteral pathway, displacing the left ureter dorsally and medially. Although this displacement can be an incidental finding, compression and full or partial obstruction of the ureter may occur as it passes dorsal to the CdVC, resulting in hydroureter and hydronephrosis proximal to the obstruction, as occurred in this case.

Up to 21% of humans with circumcaval ureter are reported to have concomitant congenital abnormalities (Perimenis et al., 2002), which include not only vascular anomalies (Nuri Sener, 1993; Perimenis et al., 2002; Rao et al., 2008; Shindo et al., 1999) but also partial situs inversus (Watanabe et al., 1991), glandular hypospadias (Friebel et al., 2004; Perimenis et al., 2002), syndactylia (Perimenis et al., 2002), supernumerary lumbar vertebrae (Perimenis et al., 2002) and intestinal malrotation (Mitchell et al., 2003). A cadaveric study into feline anatomy in 2014 found that 30.6% of the 301 cadavers had a circumcaval ureter on the right side, 1.3% had a circumcaval on the left side, and 3.3% were bilaterally affected. In addition to these findings, 7.0% of cases were found to have double CdVCs (Bélanger et al., 2014). The majority of circumcaval ureters reported in humans and cats affect the right side, but when the left side is affected, concomitant vascular anomalies are more likely to be found (Bélanger et al., 2014; Perimenis et al., 2002). In contrast to feline and human studies, CdVC malformations are less common in dogs, with two post‐mortem studies finding 0.6%–2.8% of dogs having any kind of CdVC malformation (Cornillie & Simoens, 2005; Reis & Tepe, 1956). Our patient was also noted to have gall bladder agenesis. Although the gall bladder has a different embryological origin to the CdVC and ureter (endoderm and mesoderm, respectively), the high incidence of concomitant congenital abnormalities in animals with caval malformations highlights the need to screen patients thoroughly if any caval malformation is detected.

In cats and humans, circumcaval ureters are typically an incidental finding, or silent until adulthood; the late signs in people being hypothesised as due to local fibrotic reactions and worsening of ureteral luminal stricture over time (Bélanger et al., 2014; Steinhaus et al., 2015). Our case had abdominal imaging initially performed to investigate chronic intermittent low‐grade gastrointestinal signs of inappetence, diarrhoea with occasional haematochezia, which all resolved after this surgical treatment. How this condition was linked to the signs reported is not known. An undiagnosed gastrointestinal condition may have been responsible, though focal inflammation and pain due to ureteral and renal dilatation and local fibrotic reaction are also considered reasonable explanations.

There are various imaging modalities available for the investigation of vascular and ureteral anomalies. Although our patient had significant hydroureter and hydronephrosis appreciable on abdominal ultrasound, intravenous urography is an option to investigate ureteral anatomy, revealing a characteristic reverse‐J shape where the ureter gets compressed (Pey et al., 2015). However, advanced imaging may be required to identify a circumcaval ureter, with the gold standard being CT with intravenous contrast or magnetic resonance imaging. If concerns exist regarding possible reduced renal function, half‐dose iodinated contrast can be administered (as in this case) or gadolinium‐enhanced magnetic resonance urography used (Blandino et al., 2002; Leyendecker et al., 2008), which is considered safe in patients with moderately reduced excretory function. The use of gadolinium should, however, be avoided in patients with severe renal dysfunction due to risks of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (Kribben et al., 2009).

To the author's knowledge, there have only been four reported cases of circumcaval ureters in dogs, all of which were left‐sided, with two males and two females affected (Duconseille et al., 2010; Doust et al., 2006; Izquierdo Robert et al., 2021; Schwarz et al., 2020). Three of the four reported cases were in Bernese Mountain Dogs. Treatment options for circumcaval ureters include transection, repositioning and anastomosis of the malpositioned ureter, nephroureterectomy or placement of a SUB device. Only two of the reported cases of canine circumcaval ureters were addressed surgically. One was a 2‐year‐old Bernese Mountain Dog treated via nephroureterectomy (Izquierdo Robert et al., 2021). The other was a case of left circumcaval ureter treated with transposition and anastomosis of the entrapped ureter (Doust et al., 2006) reported in a 4‐month‐old Bernese Mountain Dog with concurrent extrahepatic portosystemic shunt. This latter case, however, had no advanced imaging or note of caudal vena caval malpositioning, making this the only reported canine case of levopositioned CdVC and circumcaval left ureter addressed with surgical transposition and anastomosis of the ureter.

Our case did not have biochemical evidence of renal dysfunction at presentation, and, with a previous report supporting the possibility of a good clinical outcome with relatively early intervention to reroute the compressed ureter prior to irreversible renal damage (Doust et al., 2006), the decision was made to proceed with surgical transposition and anastomosis in this case. Post‐operative assessment of the patient, however, revealed biochemical changes consistent with IRIS stage II CKD and ongoing urine pooling in the dilated kidney with no evidence of a ureteral jet seen on ultrasound. Whether this was due to ongoing abnormal ureteral motility or failure of the surgery to relieve the ureteral obstruction is not known. The hyposthenuria noted on the pyelocentesis sample collected under general anaesthesia 4 months post‐operatively most likely reflects the inclusion of medetomidine in his premedication, which was not utilised in the initial investigation. Medetomidine is known to significantly increase urine production, including suppressing the release of anti‐diuretic hormone, resulting in dilute urine (Saleh et al., 2005). Additionally, the effect of haemodilution due to perioperative intravenous fluid therapy and the previously noted IRIS stage II CKD could also represent contributory factors to this event of hyposthenuria. Along with the inherent retrospective nature of this case report, the lack of SDMA testing prior to surgery also limits our ability to fully assess the impact that our surgery had on the progression of this patient's renal function (Hall et al., 2016).

The challenges of an accurate assessment of each separate kidney's function are highlighted in this case. Renal function, including filtration and excretion abilities, is assessed through estimations of the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) (Mckenna et al., 2020). Commonly accessible surrogate markers of estimated GFR, such as creatinine and SDMA, remain insensitive to early declines in GFR. They are also affected by day‐to‐day and individual variations, limiting the utility of one‐off testing for the evaluation of estimated GFR (Kopke et al., 2018; Nabity et al., 2015). Although more direct estimates of GFR, including urinary clearance of inulin or, more practically, plasma excretion of a marker excreted only by the kidneys (such as iohexol, exogenous creatinine or radionuclides) provide a more accurate assessment of estimated GFR, they, like indirect measures, only evaluate total renal function, not each individual kidney's estimated GFR (Von Hendy‐Willson & Pressler, 2011). In cases of unilateral renal pathology, the ability of a normal contralateral kidney to hypertrophy and appropriately maintain renal function (Urie et al., 2007) limits the utility of these whole‐body tests to accurately evaluate the affected kidney's estimated GFR. Evaluation of an individual kidney's GFR requires quantitative renal scintigraphy (Von Hendy‐Willson & Pressler, 2011), a resource that is not routinely available in clinical practice and was not available for investigation of this case. In this situation, normal measures of total body markers of renal function (primarily creatinine and USG) with concurrent visualisation of urinary excretion of iohexol, although less accurate, at least confirm the ability of the atypical kidney to produce urine and suggest that the normal kidney is compensating adequately at that time. Given the absence of infection and appropriate urinary filtration of iohexol visualised in this case, the decision was made to attempt to preserve all remaining nephrons in the affected kidney rather than consider nephrectomy. Although monitoring of individual kidney GFRs, both pre‐ and post‐operatively, would have been ideal, the realistic long‐term monitoring regime for our patient will consist of less accurate total body assessments of GFR, with regular biochemical and urinary assessments, and ongoing minimally invasive imaging to assess the kidneys, ureter and ureteral movement with ultrasound.

CONCLUSION

Overall, although this is a rare presentation, if a dog, especially a Bernese Mountain Dog, is presented with hydroureter and hydronephrosis, circumcaval ureter should be considered a differential. Further investigation with excretory contrast radiography, CT scan or MRI can help identify caudal vena caval malformations causing ureteral compression.
