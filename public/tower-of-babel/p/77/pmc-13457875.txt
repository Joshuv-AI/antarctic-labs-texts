Quantitative assessment of occlusal function before and after extraction of unilateral impacted mandibular third molars using the TeeTester occlusal analyzer
Authors: Shengwen Liu, Haojie Chen, Mingle Wu, Xi Chen, Shan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImpacted teeth of mandibular third molars have been associated with disruptions in full-mouth occlusal force distribution, potentially affecting mandibular movement, masticatory muscle activity, and temporomandibular joint function. The present investigation employed the TeeTester occlusal analyzer to obtain quantitative data on the occlusal characteristics associated with impacted mandibular third molars (IM3Ms), both prior to and following surgical extraction. This approach enabled a dynamic assessment of occlusal function and provided clinical insights regarding functional changes post-intervention.MethodsA total of 54 individuals presenting with unilateral IM3M were included and stratified into three groups based on impaction type: vertical, mesioangular, and horizontal, with 18 participants allocated to each category. Occlusal parameters were measured using the TeeTester device before extraction and one week post-extraction.ResultsWithin the mesioangular impaction group, the mean occlusal contact time significantly decreased from 0.45 seconds pre-extraction to 0.27 seconds post-extraction (P<0.05). No statistically significant changes were detected in occlusal characteristics in either the vertical or horizontal impaction groups when comparing pre- and post-extraction measurements.ConclusionsAccording to the findings of this study, surgical removal of a unilaterally mesioangular IM3M resulted in a statistically significant reduction in occlusal contact time between the maxillary and mandibular dental arches on the affected side. This reduction may contribute to improved occlusal stability and function.

Introduction

Impacted mandibular third molars (IM3Ms) represent the most frequently observed form of dental impaction, predominantly resulting from delayed eruption and inadequate space within the mandibular arch. The prevalence of IM3Ms has been reported to reach up to 77% among individuals aged 20 years (1). Contributing factors include insufficient space between the anterior margin of the mandibular ramus and the distal aspect of the second molar, aberrant tooth angulation, discrepancies in tooth size, and altered eruption trajectories (2). These anatomical and developmental constraints may prevent complete eruption of the mandibular third molar (M3), increasing the risk of conditions such as pericoronitis, odontogenic infections, maxillofacial space infections, odontogenic tumors, and temporomandibular joint disorders (3,4).

Occlusion is a three-dimensional biomechanical construct encompassing vertical, buccolingual, and mesiodistal positional relationships of opposing teeth. Optimal occlusal contact plays a critical role in regulating mandibular movements and maintaining masticatory muscle coordination, with implications for orthodontic treatment planning and temporomandibular joint cartilage adaptation (5). Altered occlusal characteristics have been associated with a spectrum of clinical outcomes, ranging from aesthetic disturbances to temporomandibular disorders (TMDs), the latter often attributed to excessive joint loading (6).

Surgical removal of IM3Ms has been demonstrated to enhance the periodontal status of adjacent mandibular second molars and mitigate the progression of periodontitis, thereby contributing to improved occlusal function. Although IM3M extraction is a common clinical procedure, most existing studies have focused on surgical techniques and the prevention or management of associated complications such as infection and hemorrhage (7,8). There is limited evidence regarding the influence of IM3Ms on full-arch occlusal contact dynamics.

The TeeTester V3.2.0 occlusal analyzer—a pressure-sensitive system comprising a force-responsive sensor (TeePaper), a data acquisition handle (TeeTester), and accompanying analytical software—enables the objective assessment of occlusal parameters. First introduced and manufactured in China in 2013, the TeeTester is the first occlusal analysis system in the country with independent intellectual property rights (Figure 1). The sensor’s material converts occlusal contact into electrical signals, with resistance values at individual sensing points used to compute corresponding pressure data (6). With a resolution of up to 1 mm2, the system permits three-dimensional mapping and dynamic analysis of occlusal contacts, facilitating real-time, continuous monitoring of occlusal characteristics (Figure 2).

Individuals scheduled for unilateral mandibular M3 extraction were recruited, and the TeeTester occlusal analysis system was utilized to quantify pre- and post-extraction changes in occlusal characteristics. The present study aimed to quantify alterations in occlusal contact parameters that are a direct consequence of anatomical changes in the early postoperative period (1 week) post-extraction of IM3Ms, with a view to elucidating how these changes immediately affect the initial occlusal state. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-2078/rc).

Methods

Study sample

This is an observational study. A total of 54 individuals who were at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Jinan University (Daliang Hospital Shunde District Foshan City) between January and August 2024 for unilateral mandibular M3 extraction were included. Occlusal evaluation was performed using the TeeTester occlusal force analysis system before and one week after extraction. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Jinan University (Daliang Hospital Shunde District Foshan City) (No. 2024-40). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants following detailed explanation of the procedures.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were included if they exhibited unilateral vertical, mesioangular, or horizontal impaction of mandibular third molars (M3s), as confirmed by panoramic radiography, and presented without evidence of mandibular fractures, cysts, tumors, or other pathologies in the molar region. Absence of neurosensory impairment in the distribution of the inferior alveolar nerve was required. Additional eligibility criteria included no known drug allergies, the ability to maintain oral hygiene, a willingness to comply with the study protocol, and availability for postoperative follow-up.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria comprised buccolingual, distal, or inverted third molar orientations; the presence of clinical symptoms suggestive of temporomandibular joint dysfunction; systemic diseases; active periodontal disease; bruxism; unilateral mastication habits; or a history of orthodontic treatment.

Occlusal recording using the TeeTester system

Before the extraction, a comprehensive evaluation of the participant’s primary symptoms and overall health status was conducted. A thorough examination of the oral cavity and impacted teeth was performed. A panoramic radiograph was taken to record the participant’s demographic information, including gender and age. Participants were then assigned to one of three groups based on the type of impaction: vertical impaction (group A), mesioangular impaction (group B), or horizontal impaction (group C), with 18 participants in each group. To ensure accurate occlusal measurements, the procedure was conducted in a controlled, distraction-free environment. Each participant was seated in a stable position, with the head maintained in a neutral posture and the eyes directed forward. Proper alignment of the orbitomeatal line with the horizontal plane was confirmed to minimize head tilt and prevent measurement error. Standardized instructions and demonstrations regarding centric occlusion were provided prior to testing to facilitate consistent execution of mandibular movements.

Participants were observed until they demonstrated independent and accurate performance of the required occlusal actions. The TeeTester software system was initialized, and participant data—including name, gender, and age—were entered into the database. A simulated two-dimensional dental arch model was generated using the default mesiodistal width of the maxillary central incisors. For occlusal force measurement, a film-based pressure sensor was connected to the handle and computer, then affixed to the jaw fork and positioned to maintain close contact with the occlusal surfaces of the maxillary and mandibular dentition. Each participant performed three occlusal closures (Figure 3), which facilitated calibration of sensor sensitivity based on the captured images. To ensure measurement stability, a calibration process is carried out before the formal data collection at each time point for each patient: the patient is required to perform three consecutive bites with a natural and comfortable force, and then open their mouth and rest for 5 seconds after each bite. This process aims to familiarize the patient with the equipment and instructions, and to confirm the stability of the sensor signal. During the formal measurement, the patient is instructed to perform three bites with the maximum tolerable force, with an interval of 30 seconds between each bite. The highest bite force (N), the corresponding bite contact time [occlusion time (OT)], and the contact area (mm2) of each bite are recorded. The median of the three measurement results is taken as the final value for this record and used for statistical analysis. Take the median of the three recorded values as the final analysis value at this time point. This method is designed to buffer the accidental fluctuations that may exist in a single bite.

Consistent participant cooperation was required to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the recorded data. Participants were guided to transition from the mandibular postural position (MPP) to the intercuspal position (ICP) in a natural and rapid manner. During this process, occlusal alignment was verified using articulating paper to enhance accuracy. This procedure is presented in Figure 4. The system subsequently recorded the total occlusal force (TOF), total contact area (TCA), asymmetry index of occlusal force (AOF), OT, and disclusion time (DT) both prior to and one week following extraction for further analysis.

Flowchart of patient allocation.

Operational instructions: instruct the patient: “Please relax your lower jaw and gently separate your teeth (MPP). Then, with the same natural speed and manner as you usually chew food, close your teeth all at once and smoothly until you feel that all your teeth are in the tightest and most comfortable position (ICP), and hold this position for 2 seconds.” “Natural” is to avoid the patient deliberately making slow or rapid, non-physiological impact-like bites. If the movement is obviously not continuous (such as pausing halfway) or shows an impact-like behavior, this recording is invalid. Rest and re-perform the procedure. We did not conduct electronic measurement of the speed, but through the above standardized training and quality control, we strive to ensure that all patients complete the physiological closing movement under similar instructions.

All surgeries were performed by the same experienced team, following exactly the same clinical operation protocols and perioperative management plans. All patients received exactly the same postoperative instructions and standardized medication regimens. Before the measurement one week after the surgery, the operating physicians would verbally confirm that the patients had taken their medications on time and that there was no unbearable pain. All measurements were conducted under the condition that the patients had rested adequately and were emotionally stable.

All TeeTester measurements were independently completed by the same attending physician in oral and maxillofacial surgery with over ten years of clinical experience. The physician received unified equipment operation training before data collection and strictly followed the pre-established standardized procedures to minimize internal variations among operators to the greatest extent. The entire data collection process was completed in the same clinical environment using the same equipment parameter Settings. Due to the prospective design of the study, all measurements were conducted by a single experienced physician. Therefore, another measurement-taker was not included in the study process for a formal inter-measurement-taker reliability assessment.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the experimental research methodology outlined in, with the primary outcome being the alteration in occlusal force distribution across the anterior and posterior dental arches following the extraction of unilaterally impacted mandibular M3s (7). Assuming a standard deviation (σ) of 3 units for the occlusal force difference, a minimum clinically significant change of 2 units in occlusal force post-extraction was established. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05, and the statistical power (1 − β) at 0.90. The sample’s standard deviation for occlusal force was also σ=3 units, with an expected change in occlusal force (δ) of 2 units. The Z values were set as follows: α=0.05, Zα/2=1.96, β=0.10, and Zβ=1.282. The calculation was as follows: n=[(Zα/2+Zβ)Sδ]2

The calculated sample size was 23.65, indicating that at least 24 participants were required to ensure a valid comparison before and after the intervention.

Statistical analysis

Occlusal data collected from participants prior to and following extraction were analyzed using IBM SPSS 29.0 software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the data. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-extraction values. Categorical variables are reported as frequency (percentage) [n (%)], with the McNemar test applied for pre- and post-extraction comparisons. A significance level of α=0.05 was adopted, with P<0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Characterization of occlusal contacts

The TOF represents the peak occlusal force value recorded by the occlusal analysis software when the occlusal force reaches 100% during centric occlusal movement from the MPP to the ICP. Expressed in kilograms (kg), this value indicates the maximal force exerted by the entire dentition during occlusion, serving as a critical parameter for assessing oral function and occlusal stability. The TCA refers to the maximum occlusal contact area detected when the occlusal force reaches 100% during the same centric occlusal movement, measured in square millimeters (mm2). This parameter reflects the overall tooth contact area during occlusion and serves as an important indicator of contact quality. A larger TCA typically correlates with a more even occlusal force distribution, which contributes to improved occlusal stability and comfort (8). The AOF quantifies the absolute difference in occlusal force between the left and right sides, expressed as a percentage of the TOF when the force reaches 100% during the same centric occlusal movement. This index evaluates the degree of occlusal balance and is critical for assessing occlusal symmetry. A higher AOF indicates the presence of high occlusal points or interferences, which may require adjustment to restore occlusal balance (9).

No statistically significant differences were observed in the AOF, TCA, and TOF measured by the TeeTester occlusal analyzer before and after extraction (P>0.05), as presented in Table 2. The analysis revealed that both TCA and occlusal contact quality remained stable across all three impaction groups before and after extraction, with no alterations in the distribution of occlusal force. TOF measurements remained consistent, indicating no impact on oral function or occlusal stability.

The occlusal force percentage on the extracted and non-extracted sides refers to the proportion of the TOF exerted on each side during centric occlusal movement, from the MPP to the ICP, when the occlusal force reaches 100%. Ideally, occlusal force should be evenly distributed on both sides to ensure occlusal stability and comfort (10,11). No significant differences were observed in the occlusal force ratio or the balance of occlusal force before and after extraction across all three groups. Changes in the position and deviation direction of the center of occlusal force (COF) pre- and post-extraction

The position and deviation direction of the COF, as measured by the TeeTester occlusal analyzer, did not demonstrate statistically significant differences pre- and post-extraction across the three impacted tooth groups (P>0.05) (Table 4). We define the COF located within the stable zone (the ‘gray area’) as the ‘ideal’ distribution of occlusal load, indicating that the center of force is within the supporting area, which may help reduce local periodontal stress and promote occlusal stability. However, if the COF is located outside this area, it suggests that the occlusal load may be eccentric. This is theoretically related to premature contact of a specific tooth position or non-uniform stress distribution of the supporting tissues. Further analysis of the direction of COF deviation (such as mesial, distal, buccal, or lingual) is helpful in identifying the possible source of occlusal interference. For example, a significant deviation of the postoperative COF towards the mesial direction may indicate that due to the extraction of the third molar, the distal second molar loses its adjacent tooth support, resulting in an abnormal load being borne by it. The COF is ideally located at the intersection of the dental arch midline and the distal margin of the second premolar. Within the software, the COF position is defined relative to the gray area; optimal positioning is within the white elliptical area inside the gray zone. When the COF is within the gray area but outside the white elliptical region, occlusal contact remains relatively stable. However, if the COF falls outside the gray area, it may indicate the presence of high occlusal points or interferences (12). Analysis of COF positioning revealed no significant changes in the occlusal force trajectory before and after extraction across all groups, with no disruption in the balance of occlusal forces between the left and right sides. Occlusal contact remained stable, and no new high occlusal points or interferences were identified. Additionally, the direction of COF deviation demonstrated no significant changes in the force distribution or movement path. Even with potential shifts in tooth occlusion, the occlusal force dynamics remained consistent without significant deviation or adjustment.

Discussion

M3s are commonly associated with various oral pathologies, including pain, swelling, food impaction, caries, cysts, tumors, and damage to adjacent teeth. Even when M3s erupt in a normal position without symptoms or discomfort, the absence of apparent clinical signs does not rule out the possibility of underlying issues (13). Management strategies for M3s may include observation, exposure, transplantation, or extraction, depending on the patient’s chief complaint, clinical presentation, oral examination, radiographic findings, and the clinician’s diagnostic and prognostic evaluation. Symptomatic M3s with associated lesions generally necessitate extraction; however, the need for extracting asymptomatic M3s remains a subject of ongoing debate (14). Additional research is needed to better inform clinical decision-making.

This study observed that for patients in group B (mesially inclined impacted teeth), the OT (time of tooth contact) significantly shortened from 0.45 seconds to 0.27 seconds. Although the postoperative average value was still higher than the ‘efficient’ ideal threshold of <0.2 seconds proposed by some studies, this change represents a clear and substantial approach towards the ideal functional state. This suggests that for patients with impacted wisdom teeth causing clear occlusal interference, extraction surgery can significantly improve their occlusal efficiency, but the immediate postoperative period (the observation endpoint of this study) may not yet reach the most ideal state for some healthy individuals or those who have undergone long-term adaptive training. The complete optimization of function may be a gradual process that continues for several months as the neuromuscular system further adapts. From both absolute and relative values, a reduction of 0.18 seconds (equivalent to a 40% decrease) represents a substantial improvement in the closure efficiency. Over thousands of chewing cycles each day, this saved time could potentially reduce the fatigue of the chewing muscles.

The specific effects of group B can be explained from a biomechanical perspective. For mandibular M3s with mesially inclined impaction, the tooth crown often directly contacts the distal neck of the second molar, forming a stable, anteriorly inclined mechanical barrier point. At the end of the closing cycle, when the mandible rotates and closes around the temporomandibular joint, this barrier point will force the mandible to undergo a small, non-physiological adaptive deflection or 'jump' to achieve the final maximum intercuspation position. This process prolongs the time required from the initial contact to complete occlusion (i.e., OT). After the impacted tooth is extracted, this abnormal biomechanical barrier is removed, and the closing trajectory becomes smoother and more direct, thereby significantly shortening OT. In contrast, vertical impaction (group A) or mesially inclined impaction (group C) may not form such a direct and stable anterior barrier, so the interference on the closing path is relatively smaller, resulting in the improvement of OT not reaching a statistically significant level.

This study focuses on the effects of mandibular impacted M3 extraction on occlusal relationships, temporomandibular joint function, and muscle fatigue. A self-controlled clinical trial was conducted to evaluate changes in occlusal characteristics in patients before and after unilateral mandibular M3 extraction. The extension of M3s to contact the opposing second molar generates abnormal occlusal forces and creates mesioangular impact forces on adjacent teeth, which may disrupt their occlusal positioning (15). Although it did not reach statistical significance after strict correction, the observed trend of OT reduction in group B may be related to the specific biomechanical environment changes after the extraction of mesiodistally inclined impacted wisdom teeth. This finding merits further verification in larger sample size studies.

Traditional occlusal examinations typically capture only static contact, which does not reflect the dynamic interactions of tooth contact and force distribution during occlusion. In contrast, the TeeTester allows for real-time dynamic analysis, recording both variations in force and the trajectory and pattern of occlusal movement. This tool provides a comprehensive platform for evaluating occlusion, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy. Its integration into both clinical and research environments has significantly advanced occlusal assessment, leading to improved patient outcomes and oral health.

Consequently, thorough occlusal analysis during orthodontic procedures is essential for identifying and correcting occlusal discrepancies. Reducing OT through these modifications contributes to greater post-treatment occlusal stability and fosters uniform occlusal contact, thereby minimizing risks such as temporomandibular joint dysfunction, tooth fractures, wear, and trauma due to premature contacts (16). Moreover, early extraction of mesioangularly impacted M3s, which do not support orthodontic correction, is recommended to prevent adverse effects on dental alignment and occlusal balance (17). The primary goal of retention is to preserve optimal occlusion and function following orthodontic treatment; however, deviations in occlusion may result in treatment relapse or failure (18).

The relationship between occlusion and TMD remains a key issue in oral medicine. Previous studies have not consistently defined this relationship, with discrepancies in results often attributed to differences in occlusal evaluation methodologies (19). OT refers to the interval between initial and final tooth contact during occlusion, which should ideally be under 0.2 seconds in healthy individuals (20,21). OT reflects the distribution of occlusal forces on both sides of the dental arches and the balance of force on the masticatory muscles, acting as an indicator of occlusal health (22). Some studies emphasize that OT is a critical parameter for assessing the alignment and stability of tooth and muscle positions, as well as the balance of denture occlusion (23). The elimination of premature contacts and occlusal interferences, coupled with an increase in chewing cycles, allows patients to progressively adapt to the reconstructed occlusion, resulting in a balanced state and a significant reduction in OT.

Several studies using computerized occlusal analysis systems have further validated the clinical value of dynamic occlusal parameters. Kerstein and colleagues systematically confirmed that prolonged OT and DT were closely associated with masticatory muscle hyperactivity and TMD-related pain, highlighting OT as an objective and reproducible marker for functional evaluation (24). Similarly, Soldatos et al. reported that dynamic occlusal parameters detected by digital devices were more sensitive than conventional static methods in identifying minor occlusal disturbances related to TMD (25). Digital occlusal systems such as TScan and TeeTester have also been widely applied to evaluate occlusal changes after various interventions, including prosthodontic treatment, orthodontic therapy, and denture rehabilitation, all of which demonstrated improved occlusal stability and reduced OT after interference removal (26).

Baldini et al. stratified participants into a TMD cohort and a control group, performing eight mandibular opening and closing cycles, and recording OT (27). The mean OT for the TMD group was significantly prolonged compared to the control group, suggesting a correlation between TMD, early occlusal contact, and occlusal instability. This instability is directly linked to masticatory muscle pain and mandibular condylar dysfunction. Additionally, following the extraction of unilateral mandibular mesioangularly impacted M3s, OT in the dental arch decreased substantially, thereby promoting enhanced occlusal stability and suggesting that M3 extraction plays a critical role in improving joint and muscle coordination. Zhang et al. utilized the TeeTester digital occlusal system to assess occlusal conditions in unilateral masticators with TMD symptoms and those without (28). Their analysis revealed distinct occlusal characteristics between the two groups, with the digital system offering objective parameters for dynamic evaluation.

Among the parameters analyzed, TOF, TCA, and COF on the habitual side demonstrated a positive correlation with unilateral chewing, while abnormal occlusion resulting from unilateral chewing was inversely associated with TMD. Abdelnabi et al. demonstrated that OT serves as a significant indicator of occlusal stability (29). A shorter OT correlates with a lower likelihood of premature occlusal contact, reflecting high alignment between tooth and muscle positions, thus promoting rapid occlusal stability. Conversely, a prolonged OT increases the probability of premature contact, compromising occlusal stability, due to misalignment between muscle and tooth positions during occlusion (30). This misalignment can establish a prolonged cycle, increasing muscular burden and leading to subjective symptoms such as muscle soreness and discomfort (24).

Extraction of mesioangularly impacted M3s has been demonstrated to effectively reduce OT, thereby mitigating risks such as premature contact, cracked tooth, and wear, while enhancing overall occlusal stability. Over time, this intervention may contribute to the alleviation of facial pain, hearing impairment, unilateral mastication, and symptoms associated with TMD (31). In contrast, extractions of vertically and horizontally impacted M3s appear to yield less pronounced effects, indicating the need for larger sample sizes in future studies to support more robust statistical analysis. Subsequent research on the impact of M3 extraction on TMD is recommended to focus on the development of refined diagnostic criteria and risk assessment frameworks. For example, the application of big data analytics, incorporating clinical manifestations, imaging data, and genetic factors, may facilitate the development of a comprehensive predictive model capable of more accurately evaluating the potential influence of M3 extraction on TMD.

This study has some limitations. A major limitation of the study is the lack of long-term follow-up data. This study set the observation time point at one week postoperatively, focusing primarily on evaluating the direct effects of acute anatomical changes from tooth extraction on occlusal status—a design distinct from studies targeting long-term functional adaptation as the endpoint. The one-week time frame was selected because postoperative acute inflammation largely resolves with medication by this stage, while large-scale biological remodeling of the alveolar bone and neuromuscular system remains uninitiated. However, a core limitation is that although significant changes in occlusal parameters were detected one week after surgery, the study design did not systematically measure and control confounding factors affecting early function, making it impossible to strictly distinguish whether observed changes stem from tooth extraction-induced anatomical changes or temporary interference from these acute-phase factors. As a preliminary exploratory observational study, its main purpose is to describe the general state of patients’ occlusal function one week postoperatively under conventional clinical pathways; clinically, such patients usually have well-controlled pain/swelling via standardized medications and can cooperate with basic oral function tests. Notably, the one-week changes only reflect the onset of long-term functional adaptation, whose ultimate direction requires clarification through extended follow-up (3 months, 6 months, or longer).

Additionally, this study is prospective, with volunteers selected from patients visiting the clinic within a specific period who meet all inclusion and exclusion criteria. Some eligible patients declined participation due to concerns about the research process or time constraints, leading to a ~25% reduction in sample size compared to the original plan. This may reduce statistical power, increase the risk of type II error (false negative), and prevent detection of actual intergroup differences, so future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these negative findings.

Notably, patients with existing TMD symptoms were excluded to control key confounding factors, so results cannot be directly extrapolated to the TMD population nor used to prove tooth extraction’s efficacy for TMD. This study only reveals the association between impacted wisdom teeth and occlusal parameter changes, serving as a foundational step in exploring the “occlusal factors-TMD” relationship chain.

Finally, the absence of a non-extraction control group makes it difficult to distinguish occlusion changes caused by tooth extraction itself from those resulting from natural processes.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that extraction of a unilateral mesioangularly impacted M3 significantly reduces the OT between the upper and lower mandibular arches on the affected side, thereby enhancing occlusal stability. These changes hold notable clinical relevance across orthodontics, restorative dentistry, and periodontal therapy. Due to the limited duration of case collection, future investigations are planned to increase the sample size and extend the follow-up period in order to further assess occlusal relationships and changes within the same cohort before and after extraction. The aim of these studies will be to establish personalized reference values for occlusal contact. The TeeTester occlusal analysis system, which allows for intuitive and efficient recording of the dynamic occlusal contact process in real-time, provides three-dimensional contact maps and dynamic occlusal analysis diagrams. This system facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of occlusal status from multiple dimensions, offering valuable clinical insights to inform diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.
