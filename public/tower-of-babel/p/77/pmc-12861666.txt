The Role of Anticoagulants After Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion
Authors: Maria Segarra-Queralt, Marc Molla, Manal Barrouhou, Andy L. Olivares, Carlos Albors, Gabriel Bernardino, Ander Regueiro, Jordi Mill, Òscar Càmara, Xavier Freixa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial appendage occlusion prevents thromboembolic events in atrial fibrillation patients with bleeding risk, but device-related thrombosis (DRT) remains a challenge.Case SummaryA 78-year-old man with atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2-VASc score = 3) and prior intracranial hemorrhage underwent successful left atrial appendage occlusion with a 22-mm Amplatzer Amulet. After stepping down from apixaban to aspirin, DRT developed. Thrombi resolved with apixaban but recurred on its discontinuation. A multiscale model integrating hemodynamics, thrombus formation events, and apixaban revealed fibrin buildup beneath the pulmonary ridge, matching clinical thrombus. Optimized placement alone prevented fibrin formation in the virtual model.DiscussionPulmonary ridge coverage and low-dose direct oral anticoagulants have emerged as effective strategies to reduce DRT. This case links both mechanisms, showing recurrence due to incomplete ridge coverage and antithrombotic decision.Take-Home MessagesOur high-fidelity model offers mechanistic insight about antithrombotic therapy, which cannot be assessed with flow-based simulations alone. The results presented provide proof-of-concept evidence of the model's capabilities.

Past Medical History

A 78-year-old man with atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2-VASc score = 3) and a contraindication to oral anticoagulation due to a history of intracranial hemorrhage underwent a successful left atrial appendage occlusion with a 22-mm Amplatzer Amulet device (Abbott Laboratories). The patient was discharged on apixaban 2.5 mg every 12 hours, with no adverse events. Three months later, after ruling out device-related thrombosis (DRT) using transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), apixaban was discontinued and aspirin 100 mg every day was initiated.Take-Home Messages•Post-anticoagulation therapy after left atrial appendage occlusion must be tailored regarding the final position of the device position.•This case report provides proof-of-concept evidence for the model's potential.•From a clinical perspective, this is the first medical publication of the present model, so it will have to be tested in clinical models in further studies.

Differential Diagnosis

A follow-up TEE at 6 months (ie, 3 months after switching from apixaban to aspirin) revealed the presence of DRT beneath the pulmonary ridge.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

The thrombus was successfully treated with apixaban 2.5 mg every 12 hours for 3 months, after which aspirin was resumed. Unfortunately, 3 months later, while on aspirin, TEE showed a recurrence of DRT at the same location. This time, apixaban every 12 hours was maintained for 6 months. Follow-up TEE revealed no thrombus, and aspirin was reintroduced.

Outcome and Follow-Up

A 6-month control with TEE and angiographic computed tomography showed flat tissue coverage in the “triangle area” below the pulmonary ridge, with no visible thrombus, as it can be seen in the Supplemental Appendix.

Investigations

This case of recurrent DRT was considered optimal for evaluating whether a high-fidelity model, integrating thrombus formation dynamics, the pharmacologic effect of apixaban, and flow hemodynamics could help explain the mechanisms behind DRT formation. To investigate the interplay of these factors after left atrial appendage occlusion, left atrial geometry was extracted from the angiographic computed tomography. Building on hemodynamic simulations using a previously reported pipeline,1 thrombus formation events at the coagulation cascade level and apixaban pharmacodynamics2 were incorporated. The coagulation model is explained and validated against thromboplastin activated plasma in the paper by Willems et al.3 Detailed model assumptions and analysis of their variation can be found in the Supplemental Appendix.

The novel multitherapeutic framework is designed to explore the interactions between device and anticoagulant therapy mechanistically. Specifically, 3 scenarios were modeled: 1) actual device position with apixaban every 12 hours 2.5 mg; 2) actual device position without apixaban; and 3) an optimized device position without pharmacologic therapy.

In the first scenario, simulation regions with fibrin concentration >50 nM were not detected near the device nor beneath the pulmonary ridge (Figure 1). In the second scenario, fibrin accumulation above 50 nM (ie, fibrin levels above 50 nM significantly impact thrombus behavior by increasing resistance to blood flow4) below the pulmonary ridge (Figure 2), consistent with the thrombus location seen in the patient. Discussion

The presented model provides a mechanistic explanation for the absence of DRT in the patient's later follow-up, which could not be captured by the computational fluid dynamics pipeline in the paper by Mill et al5 because it lacked the biological details needed to simulate thrombus formation events alongside anticoagulant therapy. Moreover, the results support the hypothesis that a more proximal device placement can prevent DRT6 and potentially reduce the need for prolonged anticoagulation, as shown in the third scenario, where no significant fibrin accumulation was observed without simulating the intake of apixaban.

Conclusions

In the case tested, the model successfully reproduces high-fibrin accumulation in regions where thrombus formation was observed, offering physiological plausibility even in the absence of a full validation protocol (Supplemental Appendix). However, despite that no extensive validation has yet been conducted for the presented advection-reaction-diffusion framework, this case report, because of its highly controlled clinical conditions, provides valuable proof-of-concept evidence.
