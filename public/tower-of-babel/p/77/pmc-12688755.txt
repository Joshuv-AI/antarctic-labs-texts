From surgery to immunotherapy: Papzimeos as the first FDA-approved therapy for recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP)
Authors: Haris Afridi, Khola Qazi, Mariam Adnan Rizvi, Rida Nasir, Raghabendra Kumar Mahato
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

HPV type 6 or 11 can cause the uncommon, benign neoplasm known as recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP), which can affect both adults and children. Typical symptoms in the larynx include hoarseness, stridor, recurrent infections, and airway compromise. Its clinical burden is high because of its chronicity, continuous recurrence, and potential for complications like airway obstruction, pulmonary spread, voice deterioration, and, in rare cases, malignant transformation despite its incidence being only two to four cases per 100 000[1]. Management is challenging, as there is no cure; patients often require multiple surgeries to maintain airway patency, which significantly affects quality of life. In line with TITAN 2025 recommendations, outcomes and reporting of RRP management should be presented in a transparent and structured manner to allow accurate assessment of treatment effectiveness and safety[2].

Although cidofovir, interferon-α, and bevacizumab are examples of adjuvant therapies that have been tried, their use is limited due to their inconsistent effectiveness and side effects[3]. Even though HPV vaccination has reduced incidence in nations like the US and Australia, the global burden still exists, especially in areas with poor public health systems and low vaccination rates.

To address this unmet need, the FDA has approved Papzimeos (zopapogene imadenovec-drba/PRGN-2012), the first immunotherapy for adult RRP[4]. Papzimeos is a novel treatment that uses the “AdenoVerse” adenoviral vector, which encodes HPV-6/-11 fusion antigens to elicit type-specific T cell responses, to target the root cause of RRP. An open-label, single-arm trial in adults who needed at least three surgeries a year served as the basis for its approval. After surgical debulking, 51.4% (18/35) of patients experienced complete responses after four injections over 12 weeks, with the majority still in remission at 24 months. With only minor to moderate adverse events, safety was deemed acceptable. However, Papzimeos is still used as an adjuvant rather than a surgical substitute, and concerns regarding cost/accessibility, pediatric use, and long-term durability still exist[5].

Papzimeos thus represents a landmark advance for RRP: the first therapy to actively target HPV-6/11 via immunization, reducing dependence on repeated surgeries and providing durable benefit for many patients. Adult RRP care is no longer dependent on CO2 lasers, microdebriders, and photoangiolytic lasers, which only provide expensive temporary relief without changing the course of the disease. Although surgical excision was still the most common method for maintaining the airway and voice, it came with risks like fistulae, stenosis, and scarring[6]. Many patients needed several surgeries annually due to the frequent recurrences, which resulted in a lifelong reliance on excision. To lessen the surgical burden, off-label medications like cidofovir, 5-FU, bevacizumab, and interferons were investigated; however, none received FDA approval, and the available data were constrained by toxicity, inconsistent efficacy, and a dearth of extensive studies[7]. In stark contrast, Papzimeos advances therapy by being the first to target the immune control of HPV-6/-11 through viral elimination as the key pathophysiology of RRP, rather than just removing papillomas[5]. This novel immunotherapy aims to re-ignite the immune system against HPV oncoproteins, thus avoiding papilloma regrowth following initial debulking. Notably, immunologic assessments confirmed the mechanism of action, including HPV-specific expansion of T cells and favorable changes in the tumor microenvironment in responders[8]. These results provide the first strong biological proof that an immune-guided approach has the capability to modify the course of RRP disease.

In summary, by addressing the viral cause of the disease rather than just repeating surgery, Papzimeos represents a significant advancement in the management of adult RRP. Numerous patients exhibit long-lasting responses, lowering surgical dependence and enhancing quality of life, according to solid trial data. Future research should address access and cost barriers, extend evaluation to children, and validate durability in the real world. Notwithstanding these difficulties, Papzimeos emphasizes the promise of immunotherapies based on adenoviral vectors, as well as the necessity of continued study and monitoring.
