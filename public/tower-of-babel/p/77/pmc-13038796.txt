Corilagin induces apoptosis in glioblastoma cells by disrupting metabolic homeostasis: a metabolomics perspective
Authors: Fei Liu, Miaomiao Zhang, Qipeng Long, Yinuo Xiao, Qunfei Ma, Bing Han, Yuzhen Gao, Zhiqiang Liang, Yan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is a highly aggressive brain tumor with limited therapeutic potions. Corilagin, a natural polyphenol, exhibits anti-tumor activity, but its systemic metabolic mechanisms in GBM remain uncharacterized. We treated human glioblastoma LN229 and T98G cells with corilagin and evaluated its effects on cell viability, apoptosis, and migration. An untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics approach was performed to comprehensively profile global metabolic alterations in LN229 cells, followed by multivariate statistical analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment. Corilagin significantly inhibited proliferation, induced apoptotic cell death, and suppressed cell migration in a dose-dependent manner in LN229 and T98G cells. Moreover, principal component analysis and partial least squares-discriminate analysis clearly demonstrated that corilagin disrupted the cell metabolic profiles. Forty differential metabolites in LN229 cells responsible for the intervention effect of corilagin were screened and annotated. These metabolites corresponded to thirteen metabolic pathways. Integrated pathway analysis identified profound disruptions in nucleotide metabolism (notably purine and pyrimidine depletion), glycerophospholipid homeostasis (reduced phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine species), and the TCA cycle. The multi-pathway metabolic collapse was associated with the induction of apoptosis. Our study demonstrates that corilagin exerts its potent anti-glioma effects by inducing a coordinated disruption of core metabolic networks essential for GBM cell survival. These findings establish a strong association between corilagin-induced metabolic reprogramming and apoptotic cell death, highlighting its promise as a therapeutic agent targeting the metabolic vulnerabilities of glioblastoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00114-026-02097-0.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Corilagin, Cancer metabolism, Apoptosis, LC-MS, Omics

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive and prevalent primary malignancy of the central nervous system, remains a formidable clinical challenge characterized by relentless proliferation, diffuse infiltration, and near-universal recurrence(Ostrom et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2020). Despite significant advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and the standard-of-care alkylating agent temozolomide, the prognosis for GBM patients remains dismal, with a median survival of merely 15 months(Stupp et al. 2017, Weller et al. 2017). This therapeutic impasse underscores a necessity need to identify novel agents that can effectively target the unique biological vulnerabilities of glioma cells.

One such vulnerability is the profound metabolic reprogramming underpins tumorigenesis and progression. Cancer cells, including those of GBM, rewire their metabolic networks to meet the heightened demands for energy, biosynthetic precursors, and redox homeostasis required for rapid growth and survival in a hostile microenvironment(Pavlova Natalya and Thompson Craig 2016). This dependency on altered metabolism has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue, as healthy cells with their greater metabolic flexibility can better withstand (Luo et al. 2009, Liberti and Locasale 2016). Indeed, the convergence of multiple metabolic disruptions, such as nucleotide scarcity, mitochondrial stress, redox imbalance, and membrane lipid remodeling, can overwhelm the adaptive capacity of tumor cells, culminating in irreversible commitment to apoptosis (Preiser et al. 2014, Muluh et al. 2023).

Corilagin, a bioactive hydrolysable tannin found in various medicinal plants like Phyllanthus species, has garnered attention for its potent anti-cancer properties against a range of malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, ovarian cancer, and notably, glioma (Hwang et al. 2020, Zhao et al. 2008, Attar et al. 2017, Gu et al. 2016, Ming et al. 2013, Rpberta et al. 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated that corilagin can inhibit glioblastoma cell proliferation and invasion and induce apoptosis; however, the precise molecular mechanisms, particularly from a system-level metabolic perspective, remain largely enigmatic (Hwang et al. 2020). Given the intricate metabolic complexity of GBM, analyses focused solely on genetic or proteomic alterations are insufficient to capture the dynamic functional state of the cell. Metabolomics, as the most downstream “omics” layer, provides a direct readout of cellular phenotype and is thus ideally suited to elucidate the functional metabolic perturbations driven by therapeutic agents like corilagin (Johnson et al. 2016).

In this study, we hypothesized that corilagin exerts its anti-glioma effects by inducing a coordinated and catastrophic disruption of core metabolic pathways essential for GBM cell survival. To test this, we employed an integrated approach combining functional assays with untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics in human glioblastoma LN229 and T98G cell lines. Our findings reveal that corilagin treatment triggers a multi-faceted metabolic collapse, significantly perturbing pathways governing nucleotide metabolism, purine salvage, glycerophospholipid homeostasis, and TCA cycle. The depletion of key phospholipids and the imbalance in nucleotide pools, in concert with other metabolic stresses, create a lethal intracellular environment that drives apoptotic cell death. This work not only provides the first comprehensive map of the metabolic landscape reshaped by corilagin in glioma but also establishes metabolic reprogramming as a central mechanism of its anti-tumor activity, thereby positioning corilagin as a compelling candidate for further preclinical development in GBM therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human glioblastoma cell lines LN229 and T98G were obtained from the Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in a high glucose Dulbecco’s Mpdified Eagle Medium (Gibco BRL, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco BRL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco BRL) in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃. Corilagin was purchased from Yuanye Tech. Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). We used water purified from the Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) for our experiments. All other chemicals used in the experiments were of analytical grade. All experiments were performed in accordance with institutional biosafety guidelines.

Cell proliferation assay

We evaluated the proliferative effect of corilagin on glioblastoma cells by CCK8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 104 of LN229 and T98G cells/mL were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in 5% CO2 for 12 h at 37℃. Subsequently, they were treated with varying concentrations of corilagin (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL) for 24 h. Thereafter, we incubated the cells with 10 µL/well of CCK8 for 2 h at 37℃. The absorbance values of each well were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicates. Cell apoptosis analysis

The apoptosis in glioblastoma cells was evaluated using Annexin V-FITC / 7-AAD dual staining apoptosis detection kit (Yesen, Shanghai) according to manufacturer’s instructions. LN229 and T98G cells (1 × 106) were seeded in 6 well plates and incubated in 5% CO2 for 12 h at 37℃. Post corilagin (0, 100, and 300 µg/mL) treatment for 24 h, the supernatant was removed, digested with trypsin, and the cell pellt was collected by centrifugation at 300 g 4 ℃ for 5 min. The pellet was then washed twice with pre cooled PBS to collect the pellet. Then, 1 × Binding Buffer was added to resuspend the cells, and 100 µL of cell suspension was taken and added to 5 µL Annexin V-FITC. After mixing well, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Finally, 10 µL of 7-AAD and 400 µLof PBS were added and mixed well. The apoptosis analysis was performed after 1 h using flow cytometry.

Cell migration assay

We used transwell assay to evaluate the effect of corilagin on glioblastoma cells migration (Corning, USA). LN229 and T98G cells were seeded 6 well plates and incubated in 5% CO2 for 12 h at 37℃. Then, cells were subjected to treatment by corilagin (0, 100, and 300 µg/mL) for 24 h. The cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, and cell precipitates were collected and resuspended. Serum-free cell suspension (100 µL, 1 × 104) was added to each migration chamber, and serum containing culture medium (600 µL) was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of cell migration, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10–15 min, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and finally photographed under an optical microscope.

Metabolomics sample preparation

We collected LN229 cells (1 × 107) treated with corilagin (0, and 256.8 µg/mL) for 6 h, with six replicates per group, named as Control group and Corilagin group, respectively. After rapid freezing with liquid nitrogen, we added 400 µL of L-2-chlorophenylalanine to the powder and vortexed the mixture for 30 s and subjected it to ultrasonication at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5℃. Thereafter, we kept the samples at -20℃ for 30 min to precipitate cell proteins. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 g at 4 ℃ for 15 min, and the supernatant was carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS analysis. Additionally, we designated 20 µL of the supernatant from each sample as the quality control sample.

LC-MS analysis

We performed the chromatographic separation of the metabolites using a UHPLC-TripleTOF system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). We injected 10 µL per sample into the analytical column at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40℃. The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B: containing 0.1% formic acid). The mobile phase gradients were as follows: from 0 to 3 min, 95%(A): 5%(B) to 80%(A): 20%(B); from 3 to 9 min, 80%(A): 20%(B) to 5%(A): 95%(B); from 9 to 13 min, 5%(A): 95%(B) to 5%(A): 95%(B); from 13 to 13.1 min, 5%(A): 95%(B) to 95%(A): 5%(B); from 13.1 to 16 min, 95%(A): 5%(B) to 95%(A): 5%(B).

The sample quality spectrum was collected in the positive and negative modes for a full-scan mass range of m/z 50-1000. The heating temperature of the ion source was set at 500℃, the capillary voltage was set at 4000 V in the negative mode and at 5000 V in the positive mode, and the normalized collision energy was set at 20–60 V. Data acquisition was performed in the Data Dependent Acquisition mode.

Data processing and multivariate data analysis

We analyzed the raw data files obtained from LC-MS using Progenesis QI 2.3 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, USA). Initially, we pretreated the data by performing procedures such as peak detection, denoising, normalization, alignment, and deconvolution. Additionally, we obtained a list of mass and retention times corresponding to all the detected peaks from each data file in the data set. We used the ropls package (R packages, Version 1.6.2) for pattern recognition multivariate analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) to display the distribution of comparable groups. Moreover, we also used it to perform partial least squares-discriminate analysis (PLS-DA) to check the validity of model’s, and orthogonal partial least squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) to determine the global metabolic changes between the comparable groups. We also calculated the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores in the OPLS-DA model. We performed a one-dimensional statistical analysis; we analyzed the data by the Student’s t-test and obtained the corresponding p values. Firstly, we selected metabolites with VIP scores > 1.0 and P-value < 0.05 as altered metabolites. The fold change of each metabolite was calculated by comparing mean value of the peak area of the control group with that of the Corilagin group. We then summarized the metabolites that differed among the two groups, and mapped their biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), respectively. Each differential metabolite was correlated with the pathways in the KEGG database. We identified the altered pathways and eventually constructed in the light of their potential function.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Significant values were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and P-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Corilagin inhibits glioblastoma cell proliferation, migration and induces apoptosis

We treated LN229 and T98G glioblastoma cells with varying concentrations of corilagin (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL) for 24 h and determine the effect of corilagin on cell proliferation by CCK8 assay. As shown in Fig. 1A, corilagin significantly inhibited LN229 and T98G cell proliferation, with IC50 values of 256.8 µg/mL and 312.8 µg/mL, respectively. Therefore, the concentration at 50% proliferation rate was selected for subsequent mebabolomic and proteomic experiments. Then, we used flow cytometry technology to evaluate the apoptosis of cells by corilagin (0, 100, 300 µg/mL) for 24 h, and the results showed that when the concentration of corilagin was 300 µg/mL, the apoptosis rates of LN229 and T98G cells reached 22.95% and 31.21%, respectively (Fig. 1B). Finally, we evaluated the effect of corilgin on cell migration and found that corilagin can inhibit LN229 and T98G cell migration in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that corilagin can significantly inhibit the proliferation and migration, and induce cell apoptosis in LN229 and T98G glioblastoma cells.

The representative typical LC-MS total ion chromatogram (TICs) of cell precipitation samples are shown in Fig. 2. In raw data processing, most peaks in the TIC spectrum will be identified through retention time, mass spectrometry features, and LECO Fiehn Metabolomics Library. Metabolic profiles

Multivariate statistical analyses were performed to visualize the metabolic profiling. We observed a clear distinction between the PCA values of the Corilagin group (300 µg/mL) and the Control group in both the positive and negative ion modes (Fig. 3A, Fig. S1A). This suggested that corilagin significantly altered the cell metabolome after 24 h of treatment. To identify the sample groups, we performed PLS-DA and OPLS-DA to establish the cell metabolome of each sample. Notably, the positive ionization mode yielded robust PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA models with PC1 variance values below 80%, indicating a well-distributed multidimensional separation between groups (Fig. 3). In constrast, the negative ionization mode PLS-DA model exhibited a dominant first component (PC1 = 80.3%) (Fig. S1), suggesting that the positive mode provides a more balanced representation of the metabolic variance. The PLS-DA (Fig. 3B) and OPLS-DA (Fig. 3C) score plot revealed that the metabolic profiles of the Corilagin group deviated from those of the Control groups, indicating that corilagin induced significant biochemical changes in the LN229 cells. The R2X, and R2Y values ranged from 0.6 to 1.0, and Q2 values > 0.5, indicating that the mathematical model we established in this study have good reliability and high quality of data to identify differences among the two groups. These values are listed in Table 1 and Table S2, demonstrating good prediction.

Classification of differential metabolites reveals that corilagin results in severe interference with metabolites related to lipids metabolism and nucleic acid metabolism (Fig. 5A). To achieve better resolution and clarify the ionization mode-specific effects, we generated separate heatmaps for metabolites detected in positive and negative ion modes (Fig. 5B). This stratified visualization underscores the comprehensive metabolic reprogramming induced by corilagin across different detection modes, providing a clearer basis for identifying crucial metabolic targets for further studies.

Thus, we also identified the altered metabolic pathway profiles (P-value < 0.05) based on the already identified altered metabolites involved in corilagin-induced apoptosis of LN229 cells (Table 3; Fig. 6). We identified 13 metabolic pathways: nucleotide metabolism, purine metabolism, biosynthesis of cofactors, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, lipoic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, and beta-Alanine metabolism.

The function mechanism analysis corilagin on LN229 cells

In order to better understand the mechanism of action of corilagin on glioma, we annotated different metabolites using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases, and then mapped them to the KEGG pathway. The interactions between different metabolites in the organism will form different metabolic network pathways. Based on this annotation result, we constructed a metabolic pathway map to reflect the effect of corilagin on the overall metabolic network of glioma LN229 cells (Fig. 7). Our results provide a global overview of the metabolic changes caused by corilagin therapy, and mainly affect the nucleic acid metabolism, energy metabolism, and biosynthesis of cofactors in glioblastoma, which may be valuable for the treatment of glioblastoma.

Disccussion

Glioblastoma (GBM) remains one of the most aggressive and therapeutically refractory malignancies of the central nervous system, characterized by rapid proliferation, diffuse infiltration, and high rates of recurrence(Ostrom et al. 2022). Despite advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, particularly with temozolomide, the prognosis for GBM patients remains dismal(Frank et al. 2010), underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents with distinct mechanisms of action. In this study, we demonstrate that corilagin, a natural polyphenolic compound derived from several medicinal plants, exerts potent anti-tumor effects against human glioblastoma cell lines LN229 and T98G by inhibiting proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner. More importantly, through untargeted LC-MS-based metabolomics, we provide the first comprehensive evidence that corilagin’s anti-glioma activity is intimately linked to its ability to induce profound metabolic reprogramming, thereby disrupting core biochemical pathways essential for tumor cell survival and growth(Pavlova Natalya and Thompson Craig 2016).

Our metabolomic analysis revealed 40 significantly altered metabolites in LN229 cells following corilagin treatment, which mapped onto multiple interconnected metabolic pathways. Notably, nucleotide metabolism and purine metabolism emerged as the most significantly perturbed pathways (P < 0.001), highlighting a critical vulnerability of glioma cells to corilagin-induced nucleotide pool imbalance(Marco and Christian 2017). We observed a marked upregulation of key purine intermediates such as adenosine, inosine, guanosine, and adenylsuccinic acid, alongside a downregulation of deoxyadenosine monophosphate. While elevated extracellular adenosine is often immunosuppressive in the tumor microenvironment, intracellular imbalance of purine nucleoside homeostasis, such as imbalanced nucleotide pools or accumulation of metabolic intermediates, can trigger metabolic stress, DNA damage, and activation of pro-apoptotic signaling(Milne and Palmer 2011, Vijayan et al. 2017, Maddocks et al. 2012, Bester Assaf et al. 2011). The accumulation of purine pathway intermediates, such as adenylsuccinic acid, may reflect a blockade of imbalance in nucleotide biosynthesis, which can lead to replication stress and activation of tumor-suppressive pathways(Bester Assaf et al. 2011). Similarly, increased inosine levels may perturb the purine nucleotide pool, potentially limiting GTP availability and thereby interfering with GTP-dependent processes crucial for Ras/MAPK signaling and protein synthesis(Pavlova Natalya and Thompson Craig 2016, Hobbs et al. 2016, Wang et al. 2020). Conversely, the reduction in glutamine, a major nitrogen donor for de novo purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis, further compromises the anabolic capacity of tumor cells(Pavlova Natalya and Thompson Craig 2016, DeBerardinis and Cheng 2009). Collectively, these alterations suggest that corilagin cripples the nucleotide biosynthetic machinery, thereby halting cell cycle progression and promoting apoptotic cell death.

In parallel, corilagin profoundly impacted pathways related to cofactor and vitamin biosynthesis, specifically nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and pantothenate/CoA biosynthesis. Our data revealed a significant up-regulation of nicotinamide and riboflavin, along with their phosphorylated derivatives, such as β-nicotinate D-ribonucleotide and flavin mononucleotide precursors, indicating a robust alteration in redox homeostasis and energy metabolism. Notably, while nicotinamide serves as a vital precursor for NAD+, a coenzyme essential for glycolysis, mitochondrial function, and PARP-mediated DNA repair, its marked accumulation suggests a distinct pharmacological mechanism(Surjana et al. 2010). At elevated concentrations, nicotinamide acts as a potent feedback inhibitor of both PARP and sirtuin enzymes. This inhibition can impair DNA damage repair mechanisms, promot genomic instability, and ultimately trigger apoptotic cell death in tumor cells(Surjana et al. 2010). Thus, the corilagin-induced elevation of nicotinamide likely contributes to its anti-tumor efficacy by disrupting these critical survival pathways. Furthermore, the enrichment of the TCA cycle pathway, evidenced by increased citric and isocitric acid levels, suggests that corilagin may initially enhance mitochondrial substrate flux but ultimately overwhelm the electron transport chain or divert intermediates toward biosynthetic or oxidative stress pathways(Frank et al. 2010, Sciacovelli and Frezza 2017, Sullivan Lucas et al. 2015). The concurrent upregulation of lipoic acid and its reduced form, dihydrolipoate, essential cofactors for pyruvate and α–ketoglutarate dehydrogenases complexes(Lester 1998), further implicates mitochondrial dysfunction as a downstream consequence of corilagin exposure(Shay et al. 2009, Deng et al. 2018). This accumulation may reflect either a compensatory antioxidant response or the release of cofactors due to enzyme complex destabilization.

Another pivotal finding is the significant modulation of glycerophospholipid metabolism. Corilagin treatment resulted in a significant down-regulation of multiple phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) species. Specifically, lipids such as GPCho(20:1/18:4), GPCho(18:1/18:0), and PE(36:4) showed markedly decreased abundance in the treated group. These phospholipids are not only structural components of cellular membranes but also serve as reservoirs for lipid second messengers involved in proliferation and survival signaling, such as via PI3K/Akt(Vance and Vance 2004, Wenk 2005). Their depletion may compromise membrane integrity, alter lipid raft dynamics, and disrupt oncogenic signal transduction(Kai and Elina 1997, Delmas et al. 2025, Foster 2009). Moreover, the reduction in arachidonic acid, a precursor for pro-inflammatory eicosanoids, may contribute to an anti-tumorigenic microenvironment by dampening inflammation-driven tumor progression(Wang and DuBois 2010, Dietze et al. 2021, Netea-Maier et al. 2018).

The convergence of these metabolic disruptions (nucleotide scarcity, redox imbalance, mitochondrial stress, and membrane lipid remodeling) creates a state of irreversible metabolic crisis that culminates in apoptosis(Ralph and Navdeep 2016, Lyu et al. 2025). This mechanism aligns with the emerging paradigm that targeting cancer-specific metabolic dependencies represents a promising therapeutic strategy(Vendramin et al. 2021, Benede-Ubieto et al. 2024). Unlike conventional cytotoxic agents, corilagin appears to exploit the inherent metabolic plasticity of glioma cells by simultaneously attacking multiple anabolic and energetic nodes, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations in this work should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the statistical models. This was particularly evident in the negative ionization mode, where the PLS-DA model showed a high PC1 variance (80.3%), prompting our primary reliance on the more robust positive mode data. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these metabolic signatures. Second, while we achieved high-confidence identification for most metabolites using MS2 spectral matching, some compounds were putatively identified based on library similarity without authentic chemical standards; thus, their absolute quantification was not performed. Third, our study focused on the early metabolic responses (24 h post-treatment) in the LN229 cell line. Future work should validate these findings in additional GBM models (including patient-derived cells), in vivo xenografts, and explore potential synergies with standard therapies like temozolomide or radiation. Additionally, integrating our metabolomic data with transcriptomic and proteomic analyses would provide a more holistic view of the regulatory networks underlying corilagin’s effects.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the potent anti-glioblastoma mechanism of corilagin through an integrated metabolomics and functional approach. We demonstrate that corilagin does not merely inhibit cell growth but orchestrates a catastrophic metabolic collapse by simultaneously targeting multiple essential nodes: (1) disrupting nucleotide homeostasis, leading to purine/pyrimidine imbalance and replication stress; (2) inducing the accumulation of nicotinamide, which likely triggers feedback inhibition of PARP and sirtuins to impair DNA repair; (3) depleting critical glycerophospholipids (PC and PE species), thereby compromising membrane integrity and oncogenic signaling. This convergence of nucleotide scarcity, redox dysregulation, and lipid remodeling creates an irreversible metabolic crisis that drives apoptotic cell death in GBM cells. Our findings not only provide the first comprehensive map of corilagin-induced metabolic reprogramming but also validate metabolic vulnerability as a key therapeutic entry point for glioblastoma. Future research will focus on translating these insights into in vivo models and exploring synergistic strategies combining corilagin with standard-of-care therapies like temozolomide to overcome therapeutic resistance.
