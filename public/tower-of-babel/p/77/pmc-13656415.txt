Genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships in white lupin
Authors: Paolo Annicchiarico, Bruno Nevado, Nelson Nazzicari, Margherita Crosta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInformation on the genetic variation and phylogenetic relationships of white lupin (Lupinus albus L.) can support the increasing plant breeding effort on this crop. This study aimed to: (a) assess the genetic variation relative to germplasm type (landrace, modern cultivar or wild type), landrace region of origin, landrace accession within region, and genotype within landrace accession; (b) explore landrace phylogenetic relationships; and (c) assess the consistency between molecular and morphophysiological diversity.ResultsA landrace collection of 112 accessions classified into 11 regional pools and a set of modern cultivars underwent genotyping-by-sequencing characterization (genotyping four plants per landrace) and evaluation of 11 morphophysiological traits. Molecular data of four wild-type accessions were added. Multi-dimensional scaling indicated a sharp genetic distinctness of wild-type material and a fairly modest diversity of modern cultivars relative to landraces. Mantel’s test revealed low consistency between molecular and morphophysiological diversity. Overall, landrace germplasm exhibited high genetic differentiation. The size of variance components in an analysis of molecular variance ranked as: genotype within landrace > landrace within regional pool > regional pool. Cluster analysis, nucleotide diversity, number of private alleles, phylogeny and migration analyses suggested a dual or divergent crop domestication in Greece and Italy, followed by migration of Greek germplasm into North-Eastern Africa and the Near East, extensive migration of Italian germplasm in the Mediterranean basin, and hybridization of the two genetic pools in various regions.ConclusionsOur results increase our knowledge of white lupin diversity and phylogeny and can help optimize the exploitation of genetic resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09478-7.Keywords: Lupinus albus, Crop domestication, Crop evolution, Genetic resources, Genetic structure, Landrace, Modern germplasm, Phylogeny, Wild type

Background

The genus Lupinus is characterized by remarkable diversification and geographical distribution, encompassing about 270–280 annual or perennial species that are native to North or South America, Europe, Africa, and the Near East [32, 82]. While most species evolved in the Americas, their ancestors seemingly evolved in the Old World and reached the New World through a few long-distance dispersal events [32, 40]. An alternative hypothesis put forward by Kurlovich et al. [60] is the existence of Lupinus ancestors before the separation of the Americas from Europe and Africa.

White lupin (Lupinus albus L.) has been the main cultivated lupin species in the Old World. It includes two wild types, L. albus var. graecus and var. termis, which are very close genetically [76]. The former occurs in continental Greece, the Aegean islands and the Western part of Turkey, whereas the latter is present in Egypt and the Near East [57]. Most likely, white lupin was domesticated from var. graecus in the Aegean basin [93]. However, an alternative or concurrent domestication from var. termis in Egypt cannot be ruled out [46]. In addition, Kurlovich [57, 59] described a third wild type, var. albus, which occurs mainly in the Apennines of Southern Italy and may have been the source of an independent crop domestication in Italy. White lupin was an important crop in Ancient Rome, where it was grown for food, feed, pharmaceutical, and green manure purposes [58]. Its cultivation progressively spread along the two shores of the Mediterranean basin, reaching Eastern Africa and the Atlantic islands as well [58, 93].

White lupin has lost much of its historical importance, despite the selection of low-alkaloid genotypes around 1930 [24] and the identification of innovative plant types with dwarf stature and/or determinate growth [50]. This crop has recently gained new interest as a component of functional, nutraceutical, healthy or vegan food, owing to its high seed protein content (34–45%) with a good amount of essential amino acids and several useful techno-functional properties [18, 30] and the positive effects on human health that it can exert [71, 75]. In addition, white lupin has high potential interest as a high-protein feed crop for rain-fed environments of Southern Europe [1], where it displayed higher protein yield per unit area than other cool-season grain legumes such as pea, faba bean, and narrow-leafed lupin [5]. However, a key hindrance to the boost of this crop is its economically insufficient grain-yielding ability [42].

White lupin genetic resources are limited to the primary gene pool because of effective genetic barriers to interspecific hybridization [12] and are largely represented by cultivated landraces because of the limited distribution of wild types [40]. There is a paucity of molecular studies providing information on the genetic structure and phylogenetic relationships of white lupin landraces, the amount of landrace genetic variation exploited by plant breeding, or the consistency between molecular marker-based and morphophysiological trait-based diversity patterns. Some of this information could shed light on crop domestication routes or help optimize the conservation and exploitation of genetic resources. For example, a consistency between molecular and phenotypic variation patterns is a prerequisite for the use of the former variation (which is increasingly inexpensive to assess) to establish core collections and/or select germplasm sets that could maximize the latter variation. Atnaf et al. [13] reported much larger within-landrace variation than among-landrace variation in Ethiopian white lupin accessions characterized by 15 SSR markers. Ethiopian landraces proved remarkably distinct from other material in a study by Raman et al. [77] based on 315 molecular markers, which also showed some differentiation between modern Australian germplasm and European landraces. Greek white lupin landraces tended to differ from modern cultivars of different origin in a molecular study by Zafeiriou et al. [91] based on six SSR markers. Next-generation sequencing techniques, such as genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS), have dramatically increased the number of molecular markers that could be available for white lupin studies [56]. These techniques have already been applied to the study of wild and domesticated accessions of narrow-leafed lupin, where they revealed a strong longitudinal pattern of genetic differentiation for wild Mediterranean accessions along with evidence for an historical eastward migration [64] that was confirmed by the identification of Western Mediterranean germplasm as the founder for domestication of this crop [65]. In white lupin, a pangenome study including a fairly low number of accessions [49] and a few studies mainly aimed to assess trait-marker associations that included larger landrace accession numbers [4, 10, 79] indicated a weak population structure, a modest relationship of genetic diversity with geographical origin, and some impact of the phenological type (winter or spring) on the genetic diversity.

The present study is based on the GBS characterization of a global landrace collection that encompasses all historical regions of white lupin cultivation and a set of modern cultivars of different geographic origins. The study, which aimed to increase our knowledge on white lupin diversity and domestication and to support the exploitation of the crop genetic resources by breeding programs, had the following objectives: (a) investigating the structure of genetic variation with respect to diversity layers relative to germplasm type (landrace, modern cultivar or wild type), landrace region of origin, landrace accession within region, and genotype within landrace; (b) assessing molecular and morphophysiological patterns of diversity and their consistency; (c) exploring landrace phylogenetic relationships, and tracing migration events.

Materials and methods

Plant material

In this study, the term cultivar designates an inbred line produced by modern/commercial plant breeding; landrace (or landrace accession) indicates a locally adapted, domesticated variety developed through natural selection and traditional farming; and wild-type accession indicates non-domesticated material of L. albus. The term genotype indicates an individual plant sorted out of a landrace (or a cultivar) and subjected to genetic analysis. The core set of plant material used for this study comprised genotypes sorted out of 112 landrace accessions and eight commercial cultivars that were evaluated for 11 morphophysiological traits [7]. The landrace accessions were classified into 11 regional landrace germplasm pools, each of them represented by at least eight accessions. Seven regional pools were relative to individual countries such as Greece, Italy, Egypt, Spain, Portugal, and Turkey, and to the Azores. Four regional landrace pools were transnational and pooled regions of different countries that featured relatively modest historical crop cultivation, namely: (a) Maghreb (with five accessions each from Algeria and Morocco); (b) East Africa (with four accessions from Ethiopia, and three accessions each from Kenya and Sudan); (c) West Asia (with three accessions each from Syria and Lebanon, and two accessions each from Israel and Jordan); and (d) mid North Atlantic islands (with six accessions from Canaries, and two from Madeira). The definition of these transnational landrace pools is also supported by Kurlovich’s [59] ecogeographical classification of white lupins, which distinguishes an Algerian geotype grown in Algeria, Morocco and Libya, a Sudanese ecotype grown in Ethiopia and Sudan, and a Palestinian geotype grown in Syria, Lebanon, Israel and Jordan. Landrace accessions were randomly chosen from INRA’s global white lupin collection at Lusignan (France), ensuring that they sampled adequately the within-pool morphophysiological variation as emerged from collection data and data of previous preliminary characterization. Some landrace accessions recently collected in Italy were also included. The eight cultivars were all of French origin and, with respect to the phenological classification into spring type, winter type and Mediterranean type that can be envisaged for white lupin and other cool-season grain legumes [84], included four spring-type cultivars that had tall stature and indeterminate growth (Amiga, Arès, Energy, and Lublanc), and four winter-type cultivars (Adam, tall indeterminate; Ludet, tall determinate; Aster and Luxe, dwarf indeterminate).

In addition, the study included 10 commercial cultivars and four accessions representative of var. graecus wild-type material, which possessed molecular data but did not undergo the morphophysiological characterization. The commercial cultivars included three spring types (Feodora, from France; Frieda, from Germany; Dieta, from UK) and seven cultivars of Mediterranean phenological type (Rumbo, Alboroto, Pecosa, Typtop, and Rex, from Chile; Multitalia, from Italy; Murringo, from Australia). The accessions of var. graecus were those reported in the study by Hufnagel et al. [49], namely, Batsi Wild, GR38-Megalopolis, and GRC526B from Greece, and Graecus obtained from Hungary.

Molecular data were generated for each of four randomly chosen plants per landrace accession, owing to the expected within-accession genetic diversity. For cultivars, which were expected to be genetically homogeneous, we mostly genotyped two plants per cultivar. The complete list of genotyped genotypes is given in Supplementary Table S1 along with the names of the institutions that provided seed samples of landrace accessions or cultivars. INRAE Poitou–Charentes of Lusignan provided seed of 109 landraces and two cultivars in 2003, whereas three Italian landraces were collected by CREA. Each commercial cultivar was obtained from its breeding or marketing company, except for three cultivars that were provided by FiBL Switzerland.

Morphophysiological data

Eight cultivars and 112 landrace accessions were evaluated in Sanluri (Sardinia, Italy, 39°30’ N, 8°50’ E) in a randomized complete block design with three replications. Each plot was sown with 36 seeds whose plants were different from those used for genotyping. The evaluation concerned the following 11 morphophysiological traits: onset of flowering, plant height at onset of flowering, number of leaves on the main stem, leaf size (as leaflet length × number of leaflets), extent of winter growth (5-level visual score from 1 = low to 5 = high), harvest index, proportion of pod wall, proportion of seed on the main stem, number of pods per plant, number of seeds per pod, and individual seed weight. Details of this experiment are reported by Annicchiarico et al. [7]. Their evaluation work included three evaluation sites. We currently focused only on the Sardinian site because it provided a complete data set, unlike an autumn-sown site of Northern Italy (subjected to mortality of early-flowering material) and a spring-sown site of Western France (where just a subset of traits were recorded, and late-flowering material hardly reached maturity).

Genotyping

Genotypes extracted from original seed lots were subdivided into two groups for DNA extraction and genotyping, which took place using identical procedures for the two groups. One genotype group was sown in autumn 2015, and the other group was sown in autumn 2016, growing the plants in a heated greenhouse in Lodi. The four genotypes of each landrace accession derived from randomly chosen plants. Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Milan, Italy). Nucleic acid was quantified by a Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit (P7589, Life Technologies Italia, Segrate, Italy), checking its quality by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. A trial digestion was carried out on 10% of the DNA samples using the Optizyme EcoRI restriction enzyme (25,000 U, Fisher BioReagents), to compare bands of cut and uncut DNA. The reaction was performed at 37 °C for one hour and the enzyme was deactivated at 65 °C for 20 min.

GBS data were generated according to Elshire et al.’s [33] method with the following modification: we used 100 ng of genomic DNA and 3.6 ng of total adapters, and restricted the genomic DNA with ApeKI enzyme (NEB New England Biolabs, R0643L); and the library was amplified with Kapa Taq polymerase Alpha (KAPA Library Amplification Readymix, Kapa Biosystems KK2611) by 14 PCR cycles. Sequencing was performed on a single Illumina HiSeq X Lane. Outsourced library preparation and sequencing of the DNA samples was performed by the Genomics Core of the University of Texas (Dallas, TX, USA) for the first group of DNA samples, and by The Elshire Group Ltd. laboratory (Palmerston North, New Zealand) for the second group of samples. The joint SNP calling of GBS data of the two groups of samples was performed using Legpipe2 pipeline [66] with default settings for diploid species. For alignment, we used the Lupinus albus genome version 1.0 [48] downloaded from https://www.whitelupin.fr/.

Genotyping data of the four var. graecus accessions were obtained from whole genome sequencing data reported in Hufnagel et al. [49]. We used the same SNP calling procedure and merged the resulting VCF files using the bcftools software [26]. The resulting single VCF file went through an initial filtering step, removing markers with poor mapping quality (MQ < 40).

Data analysis

The genotyping data used for analyses of molecular diversity and genetic structure was filtered as follows: 1) removing loci with a minor allele frequency (MAF) lower than 5%; 2) removing loci with a missing rate greater than 1%; 3) removing loci with a SNP heterozygosity greater than 30%; 4) removing genotypes with a missing rate greater than 10%. A MAF threshold of 5% is widely used in genetic diversity and genomic studies as a compromise between retaining informative variation and excluding low-frequency variants that may introduce noise and may bias diversity patterns based on allele frequencies [27, 67, 92]. We adopted a random forest imputation of missing data [19] by the R package MissForest [83] with the configuration ntree = 100 and maxiter = 10, encoding genotypes as categorical data. Data were then pruned for excess of linkage disequilibrium by the snp.pruning() function of the R package ASRgenomics [39], as suggested by Laurie et al. [61] to avoid the strong influence of SNP clusters when estimating genetic relatedness. A maximum r2 threshold of 0.2, a window size of 50 SNPs, and an overlap of 5 SNPs between consecutive windows were employed on the dataset formed by SNPs of known genomic position. This process generated 31,144 SNPs used for the analyses.

Patterns of molecular and morphophysiological diversity relative to cultivar, landrace, and wild-type accessions were investigated by metric multi-dimensional scaling (MDS; [86]) using the R software to provide a concise representation of the diversity patterns and assess their relationship with germplasm types and regional germplasm pools. MDS was based on allele frequency data of each accession computed after pooling the marker data of its relevant genotype sample (e.g., four genotypes per landrace accession) to properly represent the accession variation. The adoption of the Euclidean distance for this analysis was justified by its suitability for allele frequency data of codominant markers [78]. One MDS analysis was based on SNP marker data of all entries (landrace and wild-type accessions and the 18 commercial cultivars). Two additional MDS analyses were based on SNP data and morphophysiological data, respectively, for all landrace accessions and the eight cultivars that underwent phenotypic characterization. Pearson’s correlations of the entry scores on the first two axes (D 1 and D 2) of the MDS for morphophysiological diversity with entry values of the morphophysiological traits were computed to enhance the interpretability of MDS results. The consistency between phenotypic and genetic dissimilarity matrices was assessed by Mantel’s test [63] using the mantel() function of the R package vegan [29].

We performed a hierarchical cluster analysis on a distance matrix of squared Euclidean distances based on allele frequency data of each landrace accession (computed after pooling the marker data of its relevant genotype sample) to further investigate the genetic diversity patterns of landrace germplasm. We initially envisaged various clustering methods (complete linkage; single linkage; average linkage; centroid linkage; Ward’s method) and selected the method by Ward [87] based on the maximization of the agglomerative coefficient [54] using the agnes() function of the R package cluster [62].

We applied the analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) described by Excoffier et al. [34] to landrace molecular data in two contexts. The first was assessing the overall structure of landrace genetic variation by estimating the size of the components of variance relative to regional germplasm pool, accession within germplasm pool, and genotype within accession. The analysis was performed on SNP data of all individual genotypes of the 112 landrace accessions using the poppr.amova() function from the R package poppr [53]. The AMOVA, which was based on Euclidean distance values, also included the source of variation relative to alleles within individuals as described in Cockerham [23], not to inflate the variation of the component of variance relative to genotype within accession. We estimated P values in the AMOVA by a permutation test using the randtest() function from ade4 R package [17]. The second context for application of AMOVA was the estimation, separately for each regional landrace pool, of the proportion of total genetic diversity due to differences among accessions by the θ value proposed by Weir and Cockerham [89], which is largely used as a robust estimator of the fixation index FST [88]. We also computed the θ value for the entire set of landrace accessions. An additional measure of the accession diversity within each regional landrace pool was provided by the average Euclidean distance between accessions based on allele frequency data.

An analysis of population genetic structure was performed on SNP data of all genotypes using the snmf() and Q() functions of the R package LEA [37]. This package relies on different algorithms compared to STRUCTURE [74] but gives similar outputs and is considered more accurate for self-pollinating species [38]. As it is not possible to use allele frequency data for this analysis, we randomly selected one genotype for each cultivar, while keeping all individual genotypes for each landrace in view of the expected within-accession genetic diversity of this material. We selected the optimal number of clusters K within a range of 1 to 25 according to the plot of the cross-entropy parameter estimated by cross-validation [3, 38].

The occurrence of migration events between historical lupin growing regions was inferred by the software Treemix v1.13 [73] on landrace molecular data. For this analysis we used a SNP filtering strategy aimed at providing a better representation of rare alleles. These alleles contain information of particular importance with respect to recent migration events (e.g., [47, 69]), and their removal could affect substantially the results. This limitation applies as well to other methods that infer migration events based on the covariance in allele frequencies [43]. We applied samtools v1.17 [26] to sequencing data to keep SNP markers with MAF > 1% and at least two reads per allele and considered only SNP markers within the largest scaffolds of the genome assembly (i.e. its 24 chromosomes). This filtering strategy was expected to remove most sequencing errors while retaining low frequency variants containing key information on recent migration events. We further removed SNP markers in linkage disequilibrium using plink v1.9 [22], by screening 50-SNP windows along the genome (10-SNP steps) and removing for each window the pairs of SNPs with a correlation between genotype allele counts above 0.3. The number of private alleles and the nucleotide diversity (π) value estimated by the software Stacks v2.68 [21] provided additional measures of genetic diversity for each regional landrace pool. Finally, prior to inferring migration events between regional pools, we removed the SNPs that were not sequenced in at least 10 genotypes of each pool using the software Stacks. These filtering steps retained about 48,000 SNPs for the analysis by Treemix. We adopted the procedure described by Fitak [35] to infer the best number of migration events (m). In particular, we ran 10 replicate datasets through Treemix for each number of migration events between 1 and 20, choosing the m value for which the composite likelihood had reached a plateau and the proportion of the variance explained by the model was above 0.99 (this occurred at m = 7). We summarized the migration events observed between present-day populations across the 10 replicates with m = 7, by noting the number of replicates for which each migration event was detected and its average migration value in either direction. Then, we performed a final analysis to estimate the phylogenetic relationships between present-day populations, taking into account the migration events that were widely supported across all 10 replicates with m = 7.

Results

Patterns of molecular and morphophysiological diversity

The two-dimensional ordination of all entries (112 landrace accessions; 18 commercial cultivars; four wild-type accessions) in the MDS analysis based on genomic data highlighted the sharp genetic distinctness of wild-type material relative to any landrace accession, owing to very low values along the first MDS dimension (D 1) (Fig. 1A). The landrace accessions from Greece, which is the most likely region of white lupin domestication from wild-type material, featured low D 1 values, but a few other regional landrace pools (e.g., the Azores) showed the same characteristic (Fig. 1A). In the same analysis, the three phenological types (winter; Mediterranean; spring) of modern germplasm were distinct, especially the spring type (featuring high D 1 and D 2 values) and the Mediterranean type (featuring low D 2 values) (Fig. 1A).

Classical multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) analysis for four wild-type (var. graecus) accessions, 18 commercial cultivars of spring, winter or Mediterranean phenological type, and 112 landrace accessions of white lupin based on 31,144 SNP markers (A) and a subset of eight cultivars of spring or winter type and 112 landrace accessions based on 31,144 SNP markers (B) or 11 morphophysiological traits (C)

The MDS ordination of the entries that underwent both molecular and morphophysiological characterization (112 landrace accessions and eight spring-type or winter-type cultivars) is reported in Fig. 1B for molecular data and in Fig. 1C for morphophysiological data. In both analyses, the relationship of diversity patterns with germplasm type (cultivar or landrace) or regional landrace germplasm pool was fairly loose. However, the analysis of molecular diversity revealed a trend of the commercial cultivars towards high values of D 1 and, for spring-type material, low D 2 values (Fig. 1B). The same analysis indicated a trend of landraces from West Asia and Italy towards high D 1 values, along with remarkable diversity along the D 2 axis for West Asian material. In contrast, low D 1 values were a feature of landraces from Azores, Greece, and some Turkish and Iberian accessions. The two regions of Atlantic islands (Azores; Madeira & Canaries) were genetically distinct from each other.

In the entry ordination based on morphophysiological data (Fig. 1C), the correlations of D 1 and D 2 values with original variables of the entries (Supplementary Table S2) indicated that D 1 was a close indicator of early flowering (r = − 0.96 for onset of flowering) and, to a lesser extent, less leaves on main stem, lower proportion of seed on the main stem, shorter plant height at flowering, greater pod fertility, faster winter growth, and smaller leaves. D 2 was a positive indicator of slower winter growth, a greater proportion of pod wall and, to a lesser extent, fewer pods per plant and shorter plant. Winter-type commercial cultivars were concentrated in the left upper corner of Fig. 1C (with low D 1 and high D 2 values), whereas spring-type cultivars featured high D 1 values, just like the landraces from Turkey and Egypt. The landraces from the Atlantic islands (Azores; Madeira & Canaries) tended to group in the left lower corner of Fig. 1C, whereas Greek landraces tended to be similar to other late-flowering germplasm pools such as winter-type cultivars and Azores. A low consistency between molecular and morphophysiological diversity patterns emerged according to Mantel’s test, with a correlation of r = 0.13 (P < 0.05) between Euclidean distance matrices based on these data layers.

The hierarchical cluster analysis of landrace accessions based on genomic data (Fig. 2) showed a greater impact of geographical origin on genetic diversity than what was suggested by MDS ordination results. The first clustering node separated all the accessions from Greece and Azores and some landraces from Turkey and Portugal from the other materials. The analysis highlighted the molecular distinctness of the landraces from Azores (all included in a monophyletic cluster) and, to a lesser extent, the Egyptian accessions (mostly grouped into a distinct cluster). Most other landrace pools tended to group their accessions into two separate groups, which, in the case of West Asia, were distinct from any other group. One cluster grouped all the landraces from Spain, some from Portugal, and some from the Canaries or Madeira.

Structure of genetic diversity

The results of the AMOVA are reported in Table 1. They confirmed that the regional landrace pool was a significant (P < 0.01) factor for the analysis of the overall variation between accessions, of which it accounted for 25% [= 654/(654 + 2010)] in terms of sum of squares. However, the average within-pool accession variance was larger than the between-pool variance, while the highest variance was exhibited by the variance component relative to plants within accession. The proportion of total genetic diversity due to differences among the entire set of 112 landrace accessions, as indicated by the θ value estimated from an AMOVA excluding the germplasm pool factor, was 0.176, which was indicative of high accession differentiation. Overall, these results confirmed the fairly loose relationship between accession genetic diversity and geographic origin that emerged from MDS and cluster analyses, while revealing the large genetic diversity occurring also within accessions.

Phylogeny and migration analyses

The phylogenetic and migration analysis performed by Treemix revealed that the second-order rate of change in likelihood (Supplementary Fig. S2) was greatest between a model with no migration (m = 0) and one migration event (m = 1). However, the percentage of variance explained assuming a single migration event was only about 94%. The second highest second-order rate of change in likelihood was observed at m = 7, with nearly 99% of explained variance.

Across the 10 replicates performed with m = 7, we found several well-supported migration events (as they were observed across most replicates), as well as several likely spurious results that were found in just one or a few replicates (Supplementary Table S3). Highly supported migration events included (a) the migration from the Madeira & Canaries pool into the Azores, which was found in all replicates and with an average migration weight of 0.22; (b) the migration from Greece into East Africa, found in nine replicates and with a migration weight of 0.14; (c) the migration from Madeira & Canaries into Egypt, found in eight replicates but with a low migration weight of 0.09; (d) the migration from Turkey into Greece, found in 7 replicates with a migration weight of 0.27, along with the migration from Greece to Turkey, found in two replicates with a migration weight of 0.33; (e) the migration from Turkey into Italy, found in five replicates with a migration weight of 0.20, along with the migration from Italy to Turkey, found in four replicates with a migration weight of 0.24. While the results for Turkey and Greece and for Turkey and Italy may well reflect major germplasm exchanges that occurred between these countries, it should be noted that a known limitation of Treemix in analyzing evolutionary histories involving many migration events is that the direction of migration can often be misinferred (e.g., [35, 73]). This might be the case, in particular, for the migration from Madeira & Canaries into Egypt—which is highly unlikely historically and had, anyway, low migration weight. Because of that, for the final analysis with Treemix we excluded this migration event and considered the remaining four migration events found in at least five replicates. The resulting phylogeny (Fig. 4A) indicated four main groups of regional pools: (a) Italy, Turkey, Maghreb, Egypt, East Africa, and West Asia; (b) Greece and Azores (which exhibited some similarity also in the MDS and cluster analyses); (c) Portugal and Spain (which were mostly close genetically also in the cluster analysis); and (d) Madeira & Canaries. The residuals from this modelled phylogeny (Fig. 4B) indicated a fairly modest model fitting only for the relationships between Turkey and Azores and between Egypt and Madeira & Canaries, which showed more similar allele frequencies than expected, and the relationships of Madeira & Canaries with Turkey and Greece, which showed more distinct allele frequencies than expected.

Discussion

Wild-type germplasm, in the MDS and admixture analyses in which it was included, exhibited a sharp genetic distinctness from any landrace germplasm pool or individual accession. Even the landrace pool from Greece, the most likely region of crop domestication [93], was quite distinct from wild-type material, albeit being slightly closer to it along the first dimension of the MDS ordination in comparison with several other regional pools. In the pangenome analysis by Hufnagel et al. [49], which included the same wild-type accessions but a different and much smaller set of landrace accessions, wild-type material showed some similarity with Ethiopian landraces that did not emerge here according to MDS ordination (where the Ethiopian germplasm was part of the East Africa germplasm pool, Fig. 1A). Anyway, such a similarity could hardly be indicative of crop domestication in Ethiopia, considering that this region lies outside the reported distribution of any wild type (var. graecus, termis, or albus) [57]. The sharp genetic distinctness of wild-type germplasm relative to cultivated materials reflected the old domestication history of white lupin. For example, wild populations and cultivars were distinct but genetically closer for a species of more recent domestication such as narrow-leafed lupin [65].

The three phenological types of modern germplasm (spring; winter; Mediterranean) occupied different positions in the MDS ordination for genetic diversity (Fig. 1A). The different country of origin (mostly Chile, for Mediterranean cultivars; mostly France, for spring- and winter-type cultivars) may have contributed to the distinct separation of the Mediterranean cultivars from spring-type and winter-type cultivars in this analysis. The fairly marginal position of modern cultivars in MDS ordinations for genetic diversity (Fig. 1A and B) suggests a modest exploitation of the crop genetic diversity by breeders, which was probably due to a definite preference for sweet-seed parent material to avoid the challenge of selecting for very low alkaloid content within lines having one landrace parent [44]. A strong preference for sweet-seed genetic resources was the main determinant of a domestication bottleneck revealed in narrow-leafed lupin by the small proportion of the species’ genetic diversity that is included in modern cultivars [16]. A narrow genetic diversity of modern germplasm relative to the overall crop diversity also emerged in pea [14]. Greater utilization of landrace genetic resources by white lupin breeders would be justified by the high agronomic value exhibited by some landrace accessions in terms of yielding ability in specific regions of Europe [7] or North America [68], adaptation to calcareous soils [6, 55], and tolerance to key stresses such as low winter temperatures [36, 51], drought [9], and anthracnose caused by Colletotrichum lupini [2, 4, 81]. The challenge of selecting for low alkaloid content can be facilitated by the simultaneous exploitation of different genes [70], recently developed selection tools based on near-infrared spectroscopy for quick identification of broadly sweet-seed material [15, 28], and genome-based prediction of material with very low alkaloid content within broadly sweet-seed germplasm possessing the pauper locus [10].

Core collections set up according to molecular marker data have been proposed for grain legume crops, e.g., Jing et al. [52] and Holdsworth et al. [45] for pea, but a prerequisite for their practical usefulness for breeders is a reasonable consistency between molecular and morphophysiological diversity. We observed a low correlation between these diversity layers according to Mantel’s test (r = 0.13). It should be noted that although our assessment of morphophysiological diversity relied on data collected only in one Sardinian site, accession data tended to be highly consistent with those observed in another evaluation site placed in Northern Italy (where the same traits were observed on the majority of the accessions), as shown by a high genetic correlation for accession trait values across locations (> 0.70, P < 0.01) for eight traits out of 11 and a significant genetic correlation for 10 traits [7]. The genotypes of each accession that provided morphophysiological data (originated from 108 sown seeds overall) were different from the four genotypes subjected to genotyping, but this difference is unlikely to have a large bearing on Mantel’s correlation results. The current low value of Mantel’s correlation agrees with correlation values below 0.18 reported in other studies performed on large international germplasm collections of other legume species, as in Perić et al. [72] for soybean, Crosta et al. [25] for pea, Schafleitner et al. [80] for mungbean, and Arcia-Ruiz et al. [11] for alfalfa. An exception to this trend was reported by Xiong et al. [90] for cowpea. The lack of association between genetic distances based on selectively neutral markers and phenotypic distances is a likely event according to Burstin and Charcosset [20]. On the whole, our results confirm the limited interest of defining core collections mainly on the grounds of molecular information and highlight the interest of setting up these collections according to morphophysiological diversity (possibly along with passport data) as recently envisaged for white lupin by Tanwar et al. [85].

The estimate of 0.176 for the fixation index FST that was provided by the θ value for the entire set of landrace accessions indicated high genetic differentiation of landrace germplasm. For example, much lower differentiation (θ = 0.061) was reported for soybean cultivars belonging to five global growing regions [72]. While the regional landrace pool factor accounted for a significant proportion of landrace genetic diversity in the AMOVA, the relationship between accession diversity and geographic origin was fairly loose according to MDS, cluster and admixture analyses and the high within-pool accession diversity in the AMOVA. These findings agree with earlier indications of substantial admixture and/or weak population structure that emerged in Hufnagel et al. [49] and in prior analyses of subsets of the current material [4, 10, 79]. A closer relationship between landrace diversity and area of origin may have emerged if environmental variables of landrace collecting sites had been largely available. However, a fairly weak population structure also emerged based on morphophysiological diversity in Tanwar et al. [85]. It also emerged in the current MDS analysis of phenotypic diversity in which, however, earliness of flowering had an overwhelming importance in shaping the diversity patterns. This trait also had crucial importance in a prior eco-geographic classification of white lupin landrace genetic resources [8]. Besides showing wide genetic variation between accessions within regional pools, the current AMOVA revealed over five-fold greater within-landrace genetic variance relative to within-regional pool accession variance, a result in substantial agreement with findings for Ethiopian landraces in Atnaf et al. [13] (in which the within-landrace variance actually was nearly 11-fold greater than the among-population variance). Such a large within-accession genetic variation may have been favored by the relatively high outcrossing rate of this species (8–10%) [41] relative to other inbred crops and by the historical crop cultivation in harsh environments—in which landrace genetic heterogeneity has a definite adaptive value [31]. Overall, our results have practical implications for conservation genetics and for exploitation of landrace genetic resources, pointing to the importance of thoroughly exploring the variation within regions and even within accessions for evaluation, selection and/or genomic prediction of useful traits. Additional indications for specific regional pools may derive from results in Table 2 (which highlight, for example, the large between-accession diversity featuring Turkey).

Cluster analysis results indicated two major groups of landraces: one including all the Greek and Azorean accessions along with some Turkish and Portuguese entries, and the other containing the remaining landrace accessions (Fig. 2). The much clearer geographical pattern observed in the former cluster suggests a history of relative geographic isolation, whereas the intermingling of accession from different regional pools in the latter cluster suggests either a more recent divergence or greater historical gene flow between regional pools. The Greek and the Italian regional pools were top-ranking for nucleotide diversity and exhibited a number of private alleles that was similar and much larger than that observed in any other regional pool (Table 2). All of these findings, along with the lack of substantial migration events between these pools according to the Treemix analysis, suggest that the domestication history of L. albus involved genetic contributions from two major sources that matched the dual or divergent domestication in Greece from var. graecus on the one hand, as largely acknowledged [93], and in Italy on the other hand. Kurlovich [57, 59] hypothesized an independent domestication in inland Southern Italy from a local wild type that he described as var. albus, but distinguishing this wild type from historical landraces that turned wild due to long-term crop abandonment is challenging [59]. A divergent domestication in Italy may have occurred from var. graecus material introduced very early in areas of Southern Italy belonging to Magna Graecia, considering the very ancient cultivation in this area reported by Marcus Terentius Varro in his opera De Rustica [59]. Our results did not support the presence of an additional center of domestication from var. termis in Egypt, as this regional gene pool showed modest nucleotide diversity and a very low number of private alleles.

The analysis of admixture (Fig. 3) and the inferred migration history (Fig. 4A) suggest that landrace gene pools across the Mediterranean region and Near East resulted from intermingling of material from the two putative centers of domestication (Greece and Italy) and human-mediated dispersal. Accordingly, an additional analysis of admixture at K = 2 excluding wild-type germplasm indicated that Greek and Italian regional pools are quite homogeneous, consisting almost exclusively of individuals with high ancestry in their respective clusters, whereas most other Mediterranean regional pools include material showing high ancestry with either Greek or Italian germplasm (Supplementary Fig. S3). The analysis of migration events supported the migration of Greek germplasm into North-Eastern African regions and the Near East, along with the extensive migration in Mediterranean regions of Italian germplasm likely driven by the expansion of the Roman Empire. The proposed scenario of dual or divergent domestication in Greece and Italy followed by hybridization of the two genetic pools in various regions is supported explicitly by migration analyses for Turkey, which highlighted substantial germplasm exchanges of this region with Greece and Italy (Supplementary Table S3), thereby resulting in high diversity between accessions for this region (based on θ and average Euclidean distance values). The same scenario is supported indirectly by various results for other regions such as Egypt, West Asia, and East Africa. Egypt might have been the object of germplasm introductions initially from Greece and then from Italy (to which its landraces display greater similarity: Supplementary Table S3). Two geographically vast regions such as West Asia and East Africa exhibited wide accession diversity along with relatively low nucleotide diversity and number of private alleles, suggesting that their landraces were founded by geographically different introduced populations with a fairly modest population size. For East Africa, an introduction of Greek material was supported by migration analyses (Supplementary Table S3), whereas an introduction from Egypt was suggested by the similarity with Egyptian material of a subset of its accessions in the cluster analysis (Fig. 2). The distinct genetic diversity of Ethiopian landraces reported by Raman et al. [77] and Atnaf et al. [13] did not emerge here for the East African germplasm pool that included them according to results of MDS, cluster analysis, nucleotide diversity, and number of private alleles. Only moderate distinctness of Ethiopian landraces was also reported in Alkemade et al. [4].

Relatively low within-regional pool diversity based on the θ value and the average between-accession Euclidean distance emerged for Spain, Portugal, and the Maghrebian region, where the crop might have been introduced later than in other regions. Somewhat unexpectedly, the two regional pools from the Atlantic islands differed sharply for genetic composition and diversity pattern, albeit in the presence of a migration event from Madeira & Canaries to the Azores (Supplementary Table S3). Madeira & Canaries displayed large variation between accessions and a very low number of private alleles; in contrast, the Azores showed lower genetic variation along with a much larger number of private alleles, which could result from genetic isolation and genetic drift within a small introduced population. Furthermore, the Azorean regional pool formed a monophyletic cluster close to Greek accessions assumed to derive from var. graecus, whereas the Madeira & Canaries pool consisted of different lineages within the broad cluster phylogenetically linked to the Italian gene pool (Fig. 2). These results suggest different colonization dynamics of the two island regions, likely due to the lower historical isolation and distance from Europe of Madeira & Canaries as well as colonization from partly different peoples.

Conclusions

This study, which included the main historical regions of origin of white lupin landraces along with modern cultivars of different origin and phenological type, generated information of interest to increase the efficiency of genetic resource utilization for crop improvement on the one hand and to improve our understanding of the species phylogeny and diversity patterns on the other. The fairly modest genetic diversity featuring modern cultivars relative to landrace germplasm encourages a more thorough exploitation of landrace parent material, also in view of mounting evidence for its high agronomic value and novel opportunities of selection for low content of seed alkaloids. The low consistency between molecular and phenotypic diversity supports the definition of landrace core collections mainly on the grounds of phenotypic diversity and passport data. Overall, landrace germplasm exhibited high genetic differentiation that was only partly related to its region of origin. The large genetic diversity observed within regional landrace pools and within landrace accessions calls for strategies of genetic resource evaluation (and prospectively genomic prediction) capable of fully exploiting these diversity layers. Diversity, phylogeny, and migration analyses supported the hypothesis of a dual or divergent domestication from var. graecus in Greece and from var. albus or from early-introduced var. graecus material in Southern Italy, followed by migration of Greek germplasm into North-Eastern African regions and the Near East, extensive migration of Italian germplasm in the Mediterranean basin, and hybridization of the two genetic pools in various regions. This hypothesis, however, requires verification through studies including a larger number of wild-type accessions and other landrace material. Although it was little represented, the wild-type material of var. graecus included in this study displayed a sharp genetic diversity relative to landrace germplasm that is consistent with the long history of domestication of this crop.
