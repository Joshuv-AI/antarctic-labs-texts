Bidirectional relationship between the systemic immune-inflammation index and post-autologous stem cell transplantation anemia in multiple myeloma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lin Liu, Min Ruan, Meng Wang, Lianfang Pu, Zihan Ling, Jiachang Bo, Kai Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) is a core consolidation therapy for multiple myeloma (MM). However, post-transplant anemia is a common complication that impairs recovery and prognosis. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), a convenient indicator of systemic inflammatory burden, is associated with prognosis in various cancers. This study aimed to investigate the bidirectional relationship between pre-ASCT SII and post-ASCT anemia in patients with MM, determine the predictive value of SII for anemia, and explore the effect of baseline anemia on inflammatory levels.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study recruited 200 patients with MM who underwent their first ASCT between January 2020 and December 2025. Patients were divided into high-SII and low-SII groups (n = 100 each) based on the optimal cutoff value, which was determined using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The primary outcome was the incidence of anemia within 30 days of ASCT. Multivariate logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the independent predictive value of SII for anemia occurrence and recovery time. The relationship between baseline anemia status and SII levels was also analyzed using multivariate linear regression.ResultsBaseline characteristics showed that the high SII group had a significantly higher prevalence of baseline anemia (57.0% vs. 42.0%, P = 0.034). Within 30 days post-transplant, the high SII group had a significantly higher incidence of anemia (64.0% vs. 36.0%, P < 0.001), more severe anemia (moderate-to-severe anemia: 39.0% vs. 18.0%, P = 0.019), and a longer recovery time (median duration: 34.0 days vs. 16.4 days, P < 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed that high SII was an independent risk factor for post-transplant anemia (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.52–5.38, P = 0.001) and was independently associated with delayed anemia recovery (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.50–0.85, P = 0.002). Reverse association analysis showed that after adjusting for relevant confounding factors, baseline anemia exhibited a trend toward association with increased log₁₀SII (β = 0.16, P = 0.085), and baseline hemoglobin level was negatively correlated with log₁₀SII (r=-0.22, P = 0.008). Subgroup analysis suggested that the predictive effect of SII on anemia was stronger in patients with ISS stage III and those who developed post-transplant infection (P for interaction < 0.05 for both).ConclusionIn patients with MM undergoing ASCT, a high pre-transplant SII is an independent predictor for the occurrence, severity, and delayed recovery of post-ASCT anemia. Baseline anemia status was associated with increased inflammatory burden. This suggests a potential bidirectional association between inflammation and anemia. SII is an easily accessible indicator that can be used to identify high-risk patients for anemia, providing new insights for optimizing peri-transplant management.Clinical trail numbernot applicable.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by abnormal proliferation of clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow, which may be accompanied by extramedullary infiltration. Recent therapeutic advances have contributed to the development of novel agent-based induction therapies, including proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, and monoclonal antibodies. However, high-dose chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) is the standard consolidation therapy for eligible, newly diagnosed patients, significantly improving responses and extending progression-free survival [1, 2]. The ASCT procedure, which involves high-dose conditioning chemotherapy (such as melphalan) and the subsequent hematopoietic reconstitution phase, presents a physiological challenge to patients and is associated with various complications. In particular, anemia adversely affects early post-transplant quality of life, recovery course, and long-term prognosis in patients with MM undergoing ASCT [3, 4].

The complex etiology of transplantation-related anemia has been attributed to the myelotoxicity of the conditioning regimen, the quality and quantity of stem cell infusion, and the delayed recovery of the post-transplant hematopoietic microenvironment [5, 6]. However, recent studies have revealed that systemic chronic inflammation mediates the pathophysiology and treatment-related complications of MM, a chronic inflammatory disease [7]. Tumor cells interact with the bone marrow microenvironment, promoting the production of several pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin (IL)-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)), establishing a microenvironment conducive to tumor growth, and suppressing hematopoietic function [7]. Furthermore, this inflammatory state may be further modulated or exacerbated during treatment.

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is a composite indicator derived from peripheral blood neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts. The levels of SII indicate the pro-inflammatory state (neutrophils), immune function (lymphocytes), and stress response (platelets). Recently, SII has been validated as a robust marker for assessing systemic inflammatory burden and predicting prognosis in various solid tumors and hematologic malignancies [8]. Inflammation and anemia exhibit bidirectional interaction. Chronic inflammation can lead to “anemia of inflammation” through multiple mechanisms, including the direct inhibition of hematopoietic progenitor cell (especially erythroid progenitor) proliferation and differentiation by pro-inflammatory cytokines, the upregulation of hepcidin that results in iron metabolism dysregulation (functional iron deficiency), and the decreased responsiveness to erythropoietin [9]. In patients with MM, the inflammatory marker levels are positively correlated with the severity of anemia [10]. It is worth noting that there is a profound bidirectional interaction between inflammation and anemia. On one hand, chronic inflammation can lead to “anemia of inflammation” through multiple mechanisms. On the other hand, tissue hypoxia associated with anemia can exacerbate systemic inflammatory responses, forming a “inflammation-anemia” vicious cycle [11]. This association has received preliminary supportive evidence in the general population and patients with chronic diseases [12, 13].

However, current studies have predominantly focused on unidirectional investigations, examining the impact of baseline inflammatory markers on MM prognosis or analyzing pre-treatment anemia as an independent prognostic factor [14]. To our knowledge, there is still a lack of systematic studies investigating the bidirectional association between SII and post-transplant anemia specifically in the context of ASCT. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the bidirectional relationship between pre-ASCT SII and post-transplant anemia in patients with MM through a retrospective cohort study. The findings of this study are expected to provide new clinical clues for identifying high-risk patients and optimizing peri-transplant supportive care.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center, retrospective cohort study recruited patients diagnosed with MM who underwent their first ASCT in the Department, the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University between January 2020 and December 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: 2024034). As this study only examined the anonymized clinical data, the ethics committee granted a waiver for obtaining individual patient informed consent.

Patient selection

Potential cases were initially screened by querying the hospital medical record management system using “multiple myeloma” and “autologous stem cell transplantation” as the primary diagnosis and procedure keywords, respectively. The data of the final study cohort were retrieved through sequential filtering.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged ≥ 18 years; patients in whom diagnosis was confirmed according to International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria via bone marrow aspiration cytology, serum/urine immunofixation electrophoresis, and imaging studies; patients who received high-dose melphalan-based conditioning chemotherapy and completed the first ASCT; patients who had a complete blood count result 1 week before ASCT (with neutrophil counts > 1.0 × 109/L; non-neutropenic phase) for calculating the SII; patients who had complete and traceable clinical follow-up and laboratory examination records for at least 6 months post-transplant (with a focus on hemoglobin monitoring data).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with concurrent active malignancies, uncontrolled severe infections (such as sepsis and pneumonia, requiring intravenous antibiotic therapy 1 week before transplantation), or known autoimmune diseases; patients with other well-defined causes of anemia, including iron deficiency anemia (ferritin < 30 ng/mL and transferrin saturation < 20%), vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, active bleeding, or severe renal insufficiency (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or dialysis-dependent); patients who received immunomodulatory therapies other than CD38 monoclonal antibodies (such as daratumumab) before transplantation that can significantly affect lymphocyte or neutrophil counts (including immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy); patients for whom the rate of missing data for variables (including blood count parameters required for SII calculation and baseline Hb value) was > 5%. Data collection and management

Data were extracted independently by two researchers from the electronic medical record system and the hematopoietic stem cell transplantation database. A dual-entry method was employed for data input. Cross-checking and logical validation were performed after establishing an independent database. The multiple imputation by chained equations method was used for imputation for key continuous variables with a missing rate of < 5%, generating 5 complete datasets for subsequent analysis. For variables with a missing rate of > 5% or where non-random missingness was possible, the missing pattern was reported in the Results section, and its potential impact on conclusions was discussed.

During the study period, our hospital implemented a unified red blood cell transfusion criterion (Hb < 80 g/L or presence of obvious hypoxia symptoms), and the transfusion protocol was relatively standardized.

Definitions of study variables

SII

Measurement and Calculation: SII was calculated based on the most recent complete blood count results before ASCT using the following formula: SII = platelet count (PLT, ×10⁹/L) × neutrophil count (NEUT, ×10⁹/L) / lymphocyte count (LYMPH, ×10⁹/L). This integrated index reflects systemic inflammatory activity, immune status and platelet-mediated stress response simultaneously.

Analytical forms: SII was used in two forms in the analysis: (1) as a continuous variable, log10-transformed (log₁₀SII) to improve its distribution; (2) as a binary variable. The optimal predictive cutoff value was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using “occurrence of anemia within 30 days post-transplant” as the endpoint. The cutoff value was 245.5 × 10⁹/L, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.65–0.79), a maximum Youden index of 0.38, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity of 68.0% and 70.0%, respectively. Based on this cutoff value, patients were divided into high SII group (SII ≥ 245.5 × 10⁹/L) and low SII group (SII < 245.5 × 10⁹/L).

Anemia definition and assessment

Diagnostic Criteria: Anemia was defined according to the World Health Organization criteria (adult males: hemoglobin (Hb) < 130 g/L; adult non-pregnant females: Hb < 120 g/L). Anemia severity was graded as follows based on Hb levels: Mild: 90–129 g/L in males and 90–119 g/L in females; moderate (60–89 g/L); severe (< 60 g/L).

Baseline Anemia: When the Hb level measured at the same time point used for SII calculation met the anemia criteria, it was considered baseline anemia.

Post-transplant anemia outcomes

Incidence: The primary outcome was the occurrence of anemia within 30 days post-ASCT. Post-transplant day 30 was selected as the observation time point because this early phase of hematopoietic reconstitution after high-dose chemotherapy has the highest incidence of anemia and the strongest inflammatory stress, directly affecting early recovery and in-hospital management, making it the most clinically relevant short-term outcome.

Severity: This secondary outcome was defined as the worst anemia grade during the observation period.

Duration: This secondary outcome was defined as the number of days from the first diagnosis of anemia post-transplant to the date when Hb levels returned to non-anemic levels on two consecutive measurements (≥7 days apart). Data were censored if anemia had not recovered by the study cutoff date or if the patient started new treatment due to disease progression or died.

Transfusion indication: During the study period, our hospital uniformly implemented the following red blood cell transfusion criteria: (1) hemoglobin (Hb) < 80 g/L; or (2) Hb ≥ 80 g/L with presence of obvious hypoxia symptoms (e.g., chest tightness, shortness of breath, fatigue, decreased exercise tolerance). The transfusion regimen was determined by the attending physician based on the patient’s clinical status, with a routine single transfusion of 2 units of packed red blood cells.

Covariates (Potential Confounders)

The following covariates were examined: demographic data (age and sex); disease characteristics (International Staging System (ISS)/R-ISS stage, cytogenetic risk, and presence of extramedullary disease at diagnosis); treatment information (induction regimen pre-transplant and line of therapy and infused CD34⁺ cell count); transplant-related events (time to neutrophil/platelet engraftment and incidence of infection within 100 days post-transplant); laboratory indicators (baseline C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and renal function); and best treatment response assessed at 100 days (± 28 days) post-ASCT (according to IMWG criteria).

Sample size estimation

This was an exploratory retrospective analysis. Based on previous data from our center, the incidence rate of anemia within 30 days post-ASCT in patients with MM was approximately 50%. To examine the association between high SII levels and anemia, the PASS 2021 software estimated a minimum required sample size of 162 under the following conditions: α = 0.05 (two-sided); power 1-β = 0.80; expected odds ratio (OR) = 2.0; estimated proportion in the high-SII group = 40%. Based on the potential data missingness and screening exclusions, consecutive cases were planned for inclusion with a final target sample size of approximately 200.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.1 software. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and group comparisons were made using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequency (percentage), and group comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Using “occurrence of anemia within 30 days post-transplant” as the outcome variable, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted to determine the optimal predictive cutoff value of SII by maximizing the Youden index. To assess potential optimistic bias in the cutoff determination process, 1000 bootstrap resampling iterations were performed for internal validation of the AUC, and the bias-corrected AUC was calculated.

The primary analysis included bidirectional association tests. For the forward association (SII → anemia), multivariable logistic regression (for the occurrence of anemia) and Cox proportional hazards regression (for the duration of anemia) were used. The models forced the inclusion of a set of pre-defined core confounding variables (age, sex, ISS stage, baseline Hb, whether the induction regimen contained lenalidomide, and post-transplant infection), and additionally included neutrophil engraftment time and platelet engraftment time as covariates. Other candidate variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were selected using a stepwise regression method based on AIC. SII was included both as a categorical variable (high/low) and as a continuous variable (log₁₀SII). For the reverse association (baseline anemia → SII), a multivariable linear regression model was used, with log₁₀SII as the dependent variable, baseline anemia status as the primary independent variable, and adjustment for confounding factors including age, sex, CRP, ISS stage, LDH, albumin, and eGFR.

To assess the robustness of the results, subgroup analyses (stratified by ISS stage, age, baseline anemia, and post-transplant infection) were performed. Interaction tests were conducted using logistic regression models by adding a product interaction term between SII (binary) and the subgroup variable. An interaction term with P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Using SII, NLR, PLR, and CRP as test variables and “occurrence of anemia within 30 days post-transplant” as the state variable, ROC curves were plotted, and the AUC, optimal cutoff value (based on the Youden index), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated for each indicator. The DeLong test was used to compare the differences in AUC between SII and each single indicator, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

A series of sensitivity analyses were also performed, including: changing the SII grouping criterion (using the median), excluding patients with post-transplant infection, using propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1 nearest neighbor matching, caliper value 0.02) to balance intergroup differences, restricting the analysis to patients who achieved complete response or very good partial response before transplantation, and comparing complete case analysis with multiple imputation results.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

This study enrolled 200 patients with MM who underwent ASCT. The patients were divided equally into the high-SII and low-SII groups (n = 100) based on baseline SII levels.

As shown in Table 1, the age (56.3 ± 7.2 years vs. 57.0 ± 7.6 years; P = 0.548) and sex distribution (male proportion: 58.0% vs. 52.0%, P = 0.477) were not significantly different between the high-SII and low-SII groups. Similarly, the ISS stage, cytogenetic risk stratification, and incidence of extramedullary disease at diagnosis were not significantly different between the high-SII and low-SII groups (all P > 0.05), suggesting good comparability of disease stage and biological risk between groups.

However, patients in the high-SII group exhibited a less favorable baseline profile for hematological and inflammation-related indicators. Compared with those in the low-SII group, the incidence of anemia at baseline was significantly higher (57.0% vs. 42.0%; P = 0.034) and the baseline Hb level was significantly lower in the high-SII group [median (IQR): 111.0 (98.0–122.0) g/L vs. 129.5 (118.0–135.0) g/L; P = 0.003].

The high-SII group exhibited significantly higher inflammation-related and tumor burden-related markers, including CRP, LDH, and SII (all P < 0.001). The log₁₀SII levels in the high-SII group were significantly higher than those in the low-SII group (P < 0.001). Indicators related to nutrition and organ function were significantly downregulated in the high-SII group. In particular, the serum albumin levels and eGFR in the high-SII group were significantly lower than those in the low-SII group (both P < 0.001).

The pre-transplant induction regimen composition, line of therapy, infused CD34⁺ cell count, and depth of response pre-transplant were not significantly different between the high-SII and low-SII groups (all P > 0.05), indicating balanced distribution of transplant strategy and treatment-related factors.

Thus, the high-SII group exhibited enhanced inflammatory responses, severe anemia, and impaired organ function at baseline. However, the demographic characteristics, disease stage, and treatment-related factors in the high-SII group were similar to those in the low-SII group.

Forward association between sii and post-ASCT anemia outcomes

Univariate analysis

As shown in Table 2, the incidence of anemia within 30 days post-ASCT in the high-SII group was significantly higher than that in the low-SII group (64.0% vs. 36.0%; χ² = 14.580; P < 0.001). In addition to increased risk of anemia occurrence, high SII values were correlated with severe anemia and delayed recovery.

The distribution of anemia severity significantly varied between the high-SII and low-SII groups (χ² = 6.893; P = 0.0319). Compared with the low-SII group, the high-SII group exhibited a markedly higher proportion of patients with moderate or severe anemia (39.0% vs. 18.0%; P = 0.019).

The duration of anemia in the high-SII group was longer than that in the low-SII group (34.0(18.0–56.0)days vs. 16.4༈7.0–32.0༉days, P<0.001). Furthermore, the proportion of patients whose anemia had not resolved by the end of follow-up in the high-SII group was significantly higher than that in the low-SII group (15.0% vs. 5.0%; P = 0.034).

These results indicate that a high baseline SII level is significantly associated with the occurrence, worsening, and delayed recovery of post-ASCT anemia.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the association between the SII and post-transplant anemia outcomes in patients with MM undergoing ASCT. The bidirectional relationship between baseline anemia and inflammatory burden was examined. The results showed that: (1) a higher pre-transplant SII level was independently associated with the occurrence of early post-ASCT anemia, increased severity of anemia, and delayed recovery; (2) after adjusting for relevant confounding factors, baseline anemia status showed a trend toward association with higher SII, with a dose-response relationship; (3) the adverse effect of SII was further enhanced in patients with ISS stage III disease and those who developed post-transplant infection, demonstrating significant interaction effects.

These findings suggest that the systemic inflammatory state during the peri-transplant period regulates hematopoietic reconstitution after ASCT and reveal the presence of a potential vicious cycle between inflammation and anemia.

This study demonstrated that high SII levels predict adverse post-ASCT anemia outcomes, which is consistent with the classic theory of anemia of inflammation (AI) [6, 15]. Chronic or acute inflammation is reported to suppress erythropoiesis through multiple mechanisms, including cytokine-mediated bone marrow suppression and iron metabolism dysregulation [16]. High levels of SII, a composite index integrating neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, indicate a pro-inflammatory state, impaired immune function, and reactive platelet activation. SII is reported to predict outcomes in various cancers and transplantation settings owing to its good clinical accessibility [17].In this study, the AUC of SII for predicting post-transplant anemia was 0.72, which was significantly superior to that of PLR (0.63) and CRP (0.62) (DeLong test, both P < 0.05), and was numerically higher than that of NLR (0.65). This suggests that SII, which integrates the three dimensions of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, may have advantages over single inflammatory indicators in reflecting systemic inflammatory burden and predicting post-transplant anemia. However, the difference in AUC between SII and NLR did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09), indicating that their predictive performance was comparable, and further validation with larger sample sizes is needed in future studies. The superior predictive performance of SII may be attributed to its ability to reflect the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of anemia in this context. Clinically, inflammatory markers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) are often used to aid in distinguishing functional iron deficiency (FID) from absolute iron deficiency due to dietary lack. In FID, inflammation-driven upregulation of hepcidin restricts iron availability for erythropoiesis, a process frequently accompanied by elevated NLR and PLR [13]. In contrast, absolute iron deficiency without a significant inflammatory background typically does not elevate these ratios. Our finding that SII outperformed PLR and CRP suggests that the integration of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets in the SII formula captures the systemic inflammatory burden mediating anemia more comprehensively than these individual markers. Future studies should directly compare the diagnostic efficacy of SII, NLR, and PLR in differentiating iron deficiency subtypes among MM patients. During ASCT, high-dose conditioning chemotherapy (such as melphalan) can promote extensive tissue damage and disrupt mucosal barriers, inducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β [18]. These inflammatory mediators may directly inhibit the proliferation and differentiation of erythroid progenitor cells (burst-forming unit E and colony-forming unit E). Additionally, inflammatory mediators upregulate the expression of hepatic hepcidin, leading to functional iron deficiency and impaired erythropoiesis [19]. Furthermore, inflammation can diminish bone marrow responsiveness to erythropoietin, exacerbating post-transplant anemia [20]. In this study, patients in the high-SII group exhibited upregulated levels of CRP and LDH and downregulated levels of albumin levels at baseline, indicating a pathophysiological state of “high inflammation, high tumor burden, and low nutrition.” This is consistent with the inflammation-mediated hematopoietic suppression mechanisms. The increased incidence of anemia, high severity of anemia (proportion of moderate or worse anemia: 39.0% vs. 18.0%), and delayed recovery speed (median duration: 34.0 days vs. 16.4 days) in the high-SII group post-transplantation are clinical manifestations of the inhibitory effect that this persistent and intense inflammatory microenvironment exerts on hematopoietic reconstitution. It is worth noting that there was no significant difference in neutrophil or platelet engraftment time between the high and low SII groups, suggesting that the kinetics of hematopoietic stem cell reconstitution were comparable between the two groups. Therefore, the delayed anemia recovery observed in the high SII group is more likely attributable to the selective inhibition of erythropoiesis by systemic inflammation represented by SII, rather than non-specific overall hematopoietic delay.

This study also demonstrated the independent reverse association between baseline anemia status and elevated SII levels. Thus, anemia may not be a passive consequence of chronic inflammation but can actively exacerbate the systemic inflammation in patients with MM. Several mechanisms may potentially underlie this process. The first mechanism involves hypoxia-induced inflammatory activation. Tissue hypoxia resulting from anemia can stabilize hypoxia-inducible factors, upregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factors (such as VEGF, IL-6, and TNF-α) and promoting aberrant myeloid cell proliferation [21]. Meanwhile, the second mechanism involves enhanced oxidative stress. Hypoxia/reperfusion injury associated with anemia can increase the production of reactive oxygen species, activating core inflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB [22]. Finally, the third mechanism involves compensatory neuroendocrine regulation. To cope with reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, stress-like responses are activated through the activation of the sympathetic nervous system, indirectly affecting the inflammatory state. In this study, baseline Hb was significantly and negatively correlated with log₁₀SII (r = − 0.22; P = 0.008). For every 10 g/L decrease in Hb, log₁₀SII increased by an average of 0.08 (P = 0.025). This suggests a continuous gradient relationship between anemia and systemic inflammation through a dose-response pattern. Based on this finding, we propose a hypothesis of an “inflammation-anemia vicious cycle” in patients with MM undergoing ASCT. Baseline inflammation (high SII levels) increases the risk and severity of post-transplant anemia, while pre-existing anemia before transplantation may “pre-activate” or exacerbate systemic inflammation levels. Thus, the inflammatory state is unfavorable even before the transplantation, adversely affecting transplant-related stress tolerance and recovery capacity. Similar inflammation-anemia positive feedback patterns have been proposed in chronic kidney disease, malignancies, and inflammatory diseases [10]. This study offers preliminary clinical evidence that may be consistent with this model, which requires prospective validation.

Subgroup and interaction analyses further revealed the heterogeneous clinical predictive value of SII. The predictive value of high SII levels in anemia was high in patients with stage III (ISS) tumors (interaction P = 0.042). This may be because patients with advanced-stage tumors exhibit enhanced secretion of inflammatory factors from tumor cells, increased tumor burden, and an inflammatory bone marrow microenvironment [23]. The predictive value of SII was also significantly enhanced in patients with post-transplant infections (interaction P = 0.028). This indicates that acute infection (a potent “second inflammatory hit”) and pre-existing inflammatory status (high SII levels) exert synergistic inhibitory effects on the fragile post-transplant hematopoietic function [24]. In patients with anemia at baseline, a high SII level was strongly associated with post-transplant anemia (aOR = 5.04). However, this association was not significant in patients without anemia at baseline. Thus, assessing the inflammatory status (SII) in patients with MM presenting with anemia at baseline is critical for predicting post-transplant hematological recovery. This cohort may benefit the most from targeted anti-inflammatory or hematopoietic support interventions.

The robustness of the findings of this study was confirmed through multiple sensitivity analyses. However, this study has several limitations. First, the retrospective study design cannot completely avoid residual confounding and cannot establish a strict causal relationship. Furthermore, the study enrollment period spanned from 2020 to 2025 (including the COVID-19 pandemic period), during which clinical supportive care, infection control strategies, and transfusion indications may have changed, constituting a potential source of temporal confounding bias. Second, SII is a composite index, and the specific contributions and mechanisms of neutrophilia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytosis cannot be resolved. Third, data on iron metabolism indicators (such as ferritin, transferrin saturation) and cytokine profiles (such as IL-6, hepcidin) are lacking. Although absolute iron deficiency was controlled for in the exclusion criteria, we cannot directly verify the core mechanistic hypothesis that “high SII leads to functional iron deficiency through upregulation of hepcidin.” Fourth, detailed data on peri-transplant red blood cell transfusion and erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) use were not collected. Because patients in the high SII group had more severe inflammation and poorer hematopoietic recovery, they were clinically more likely to receive transfusions or ESA therapy (treatment allocation imbalance). This may lead to an underestimation of the effect estimates for adverse anemia outcomes in the high SII group (e.g., aOR = 2.86), meaning that the actual interventions partially offset the harm caused by high inflammation, and the observed differences represent the “net effect.” Therefore, the conclusions of this study should be interpreted as associations observed without adjusting for these two key clinical interventions. Fifth, the negative correlation between baseline Hb and log₁₀SII may be mediated by MM tumor burden and shared systemic inflammatory pathways. Moreover, due to the lack of longitudinal post-transplant SII data (days 7, 14, and 30), this study cannot rigorously establish the temporal causality of the “anemia → inflammation” direction, and the bidirectional association should be interpreted as a cross-sectional exploratory finding. Future prospective studies should dynamically monitor iron metabolism parameters, cytokine profiles, and longitudinal SII changes to elucidate the specific mechanisms of the “inflammation-anemia” axis and validate the causal relationship.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a significant bidirectional association between pre-transplant SII levels and post-ASCT anemia outcomes in patients with MM undergoing ASCT. High SII levels are independent predictors for the occurrence, severity, and delayed recovery of post-ASCT anemia. Meanwhile, baseline anemia status showed a marginal trend toward elevated inflammatory burden. The findings of this study improve the clinical understanding of the critical role of inflammation in post-transplant hematopoietic recovery and support the use of SII as a simple, accessible tool for risk stratification to optimize peri-transplant anemia and inflammation management strategies.
