Food vendors in the school foodscape: roles, challenges and opportunities for improving child nutrition
Authors: Jessica Ayensu, Kasim Abdulai, Nana Ama Frimpomaa Agyapong, Ivan Addae-Mensah, Isaac Anane, Christiana Nsiah-Asamoah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe school food environment critically shapes child nutrition, yet the role of informal food vendors, individuals selling food on school premises without formal government-issued business licenses, remains understudied in Ghana.ObjectiveThis study explored the dual role of informal food vendors as potential gatekeepers to healthy eating and examined the barriers they face in offering nutritious options.MethodsSix focus group discussions were conducted with 38 purposively selected informal food vendors (37 female, 1 male) across six public basic schools in the Cape Coast Metropolitan Area (three farming and three fishing communities). Data were analyzed thematically using NVivo 12.ResultsVendors demonstrated good knowledge of nutrition and hygiene practices (81.6% had food safety training) but operated under severe constraints: intense price sensitivity among students (meals often purchased for 1-2 Ghana cedis), competition from unauthorized external vendors, lack of infrastructure (shelter, storage, water), and weak policy enforcement. These factors forced vendors to prioritize cheap, calorie-dense foods over nutritious alternatives.ConclusionsVendors are not inherent barriers to healthy eating but constrained entrepreneurs. Their strong accountability (e.g., “We eat the same food we sell”) and willingness to collaborate with schools represent untapped opportunities for intervention.RecommendationsMulti-level strategies including enforced school food policies, vendor subsidies or micro-loans, infrastructure investment, and teacher-vendor partnerships are urgently needed.Keywords: school food environment, informal food vendors, child nutrition, Ghana, qualitative research

Background

The global surge in childhood overweight, obesity, and micronutrient deficiencies has positioned the school food environment as a critical frontier for public health intervention.1,2 Schools are not merely centres for academic instruction but are dynamic ecosystems where children spend a significant portion of their day and consume a substantial proportion of their daily caloric intake. Consequently, the nutritional quality of foods accessible within this environment exerts a profound influence on children’s immediate health, cognitive development, and the establishment of lifelong dietary habits. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Ghana, where school meal programs may be limited or non-uniform, the role of informal food vendors becomes disproportionately significant. Globally, informal food vendors are recognized as critical actors in urban food systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where they provide affordable, accessible, and culturally acceptable food to millions of children.3,4 These vendors often operate as the de facto cafeteria system, making their offerings a primary determinant of students’ dietary patterns and nutritional status, thus rendering them a crucial focal point for any strategy aimed at improving child nutrition.

This duality is well-recognized in the literature.3,5 However, existing studies have predominantly focused on either vendor hygiene practices or the nutritional content of foods sold, with limited attention to the structural and economic constraints that shape vendor decision-making from their own perspective. Furthermore, no study has specifically examined the school food vending environment in Ghana’s Central Region, where fishing and farming communities present distinct socio-economic contexts. The research gap this study fills is, therefore, an empirical and contextual one: it provides vendor-centric evidence on the barriers and opportunities for improving child nutrition within the specific school foodscape of the Cape Coast Metropolitan Area.

Central to this inquiry is an exploration of the motivations and operational logics that drive these informal entrepreneurs. Vendor behaviour is not arbitrary; it is a rational response to a complex set of economic constraints, consumer demand, and supply chain realities. In Ghana, the proliferation of informal food vendors near schools has been documented to coincide with increased availability of energy-dense, nutrient-poor snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages. 6 However, there is limited longitudinal evidence on dietary shifts. Cross-sectional studies suggest that children’s food purchases from vendors are driven primarily by taste, affordability, and peer influence, with fruits and vegetables being less frequently selected. 7 The added value of this study is its focus on the vendors’ own rationales and constraints, offering a bottom-up perspective to complement existing demand-side studies. Their choices regarding what food to sell are influenced by factors such as profitability, shelf-life, ease of preparation, student preferences, and the limited capital for investment in healthier, often more perishable, ingredients.3,7 Unpacking these motivations is a prerequisite for designing effective interventions, as policies that fail to align with the vendors’ socio-economic realities are destined for poor compliance and ultimate failure.

Furthermore, the challenges vendors face extend beyond mere menu selection to the fundamental issue of food safety and hygiene. Barriers to safe food handling practices are often systemic and include a lack of access to potable water at vending points, inadequate storage facilities, limited knowledge of hygienic practices, and the absence of effective regulatory oversight. 3 These challenges pose a direct threat to child health, risking foodborne illnesses that exacerbate malnutrition and contribute to school absenteeism. Investigating these barriers is not just about preventing disease but is a foundational step towards ensuring that the food provided is not only nutritious but also safe for consumption, a non-negotiable aspect of any school food policy.

The practical implications of this research are directed towards devising evidence-based, contextually appropriate, and sustainable interventions. There is a pressing need to move from mere identification of problems to the co-creation of solutions with the vendors themselves. This involves exploring the potential for targeted capacity-building programs that combine nutrition education with business skills training, enabling vendors to see healthy food not as a cost but as a viable value proposition. 2 Simultaneously, findings from this research will inform practical regulatory frameworks that are supportive rather than purely punitive, designed to incentivize best practices in food safety and nutritional quality while acknowledging the informal nature of this sector. 4

Ultimately, this study aims to bridge a critical evidence gap by placing the perspectives and experiences of informal food vendors at the center of the discourse on improving Ghana’s school food environment. By rigorously investigating their roles, motivations, and challenges, this research will generate actionable insights for policymakers, public health officials, and school administrators. The recommendations will be geared towards forging effective collaborations between schools and vendors, designing realistic training modules, and shaping food policies that are pragmatic and impactful. The goal is to transform these influential actors from inadvertent barriers into empowered allies in the collective mission to safeguard the health and future of Ghanaian schoolchildren.3,7

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Cape Coast Metropolitan Area, Central Region, Ghana, a region characterized by both fishing and farming livelihoods. Cape Coast has a population of approximately 170,000 and hosts 87 public basic schools. Many of these schools accommodate informal food vendors due to the absence of centralized school feeding programmes outside the Ghana School Feeding Programme, which covers only selected schools. The metropolitan area was purposively chosen for its diverse community typologies, coastal fishing communities and inland farming communities, enabling comparison of vendor practices across different economic and cultural contexts. Data collection was conducted between January, 2025 and May, 2025.

Study design and sampling

This study employed a qualitative research design, utilizing Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) to collect in-depth data on the experiences and perspectives of informal food vendors. Six FGDs were conducted, one in each of six purposively selected public basic schools within the Cape Coast Metropolitan Area. To capture a spectrum of socio-economic and cultural contexts shaping the school food environment, the schools were selected to represent three communities reliant on farming (Kwaprow M/A Basic School, Jacob Wilson Sey M/A Basic School, and Philip Quaque Presbyterian Basic School) and three reliant on fishing (Efutu M/A Basic School, Ekon M/A Basic School, and Pedu M/A Basic School). This stratification allowed for exploration of potential differences in vendor practices across socio-economic contexts. Within each school, all active informal food vendors, defined as individuals selling food on school premises at least three days per week, were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were: (a) selling food within or immediately outside the school compound, (b) operating without a formal government-issued business license, and (c) having sold to students for at least three months. No vendors were excluded based on gender, age, or type of food sold. Vendors were approached individually at their vending stations by the research assistant one week before the scheduled FGD. All invited vendors agreed to participate, yielding a total sample of 38 participants, with group sizes ranging from six to seven individuals per school.

This criterion ensured all participants possessed direct, relevant experience central to the research objectives. 8 The recruitment target was six to ten participants per focus group to foster dynamic discussion. An over-recruitment strategy was employed to mitigate the impact of potential participant drop-outs. The cohort was predominantly female (37/38, 97.4%), with ages ranging from 18 to above 60 years (mean age category: 30–40 years). The full socio-demographic profile is presented in Table 1. To minimize potential discomfort arising from vendor competition, discussions were conducted separately for each school, and vendors were assured that individual pricing strategies would not be shared with school authorities. The moderator emphasized that the goal was to understand shared challenges, not to critique individual businesses.

In qualitative research, sample size is determined by data saturation rather than statistical power. Based on guidance from Guest et al. (2006)9,10 that saturation often occurs within 6-12 focus groups, we planned six FGDs (one per school). Saturation was assessed prospectively: after the fourth FGD, no new themes emerged; the fifth and sixth FGDs confirmed that thematic saturation had been achieved. No new codes or sub-themes were identified in the final two discussions. No vendor declined participation; however, two vendors were absent on the day of their school’s FGD due to illness.

Procedure

Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were selected as the primary data collection method to capture shared experiences and generate interactive dialogue among food vendors.1,2 Each FGD was facilitated by the lead author (J.A., a Senior Lecturer with five years of qualitative research experience) and co-facilitated by a research assistant (BSc in Nutrition and Dietetics and an MPhil Candidate in Population and Health, trained in FGD moderation), with a note-taker documenting non-verbal cues and group dynamics; none of the facilitators had prior relationships with the vendors. Field notes included observations on group interaction, dominant speakers, environmental distractions, and non-verbal responses to sensitive topics. The lead author is female; the co-facilitator was a male and note-taker was a female. Discussions were held in neutral, private locations within each school (an unused classroom or a shaded area away from the vending zone) to ensure confidentiality and minimise distractions. No non-participants (e.g., school staff, other vendors) were present during the discussions. Recognising that vendors are competitors, three strategies were used to manage sensitivity around pricing and competition: (a) vendors were assured that individual pricing data would not be shared with school authorities or other vendors (b) the discussion focused on shared challenges (e.g., rising ingredient costs, student budget constraints); rather than individual profit margins; and (c) the icebreaker activity emphasised common experiences. Post-discussion debriefing confirmed that participants felt comfortable sharing their views. A semi-structured moderator guide, developed from literature,7,11 ensured consistency while allowing emergent themes to be explored. Discussions were conducted primarily in Fante (the local language), with English used only for technical terms (e.g., “hygiene”, “protein”); the moderator is a native Fante speaker, and all participants were fluent in Fante. No confusion was reported, and participants expressed appreciation for being able to express themselves fully in their mother tongue. All interviews were conducted in the local language. A bilingual research assistant transcribed the audio recordings verbatim and simultaneously translated them into English during the transcription process, before any analysis was undertaken. Prior to each FGD, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, emphasising voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, and confidentiality. Vendors were informed that the facilitator was a university researcher studying school food environments, and that the goal was to understand shared challenges, not to evaluate individual businesses. Ethical approval was granted by the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital Ethics Review Committee (CCTHERC/EC/2025/118). Each FGD was audio-recorded with permission for verbatim transcription and analysis 3 and lasted approximately 60–90 minutes. The session concluded with a summary of key points, a thank you to participants, and information on next steps.

Question guide

The semi-structured moderator guide was organized around seven core thematic areas to comprehensively explore the vendors’ experiences and perceptions.12,13 The discussion commenced with an icebreaker activity prompting vendors to reflect on a popular food item they sell. This was followed by a series of open-ended questions investigating: current food offerings and the decision-making processes behind them, including the perceived healthfulness of best-selling items; vendors’ characterization of the school food environment and the challenges and policies affecting their trade; their conceptualization of healthy food options, the barriers to providing them, and the economic considerations of pricing and affordability; their practical food safety and hygiene protocols and any prior training received 8 ; the primary challenges in sourcing nutritious ingredients; and finally, their vision for future collaboration with the school and the types of support or resources they believed would be essential to successfully integrate healthier options into their businesses. 13 This line of questioning was designed to progress from descriptive and practical matters to more analytical and prospective themes, encouraging deep reflection. The guide was not formally validated using psychometric methods, which is typical for semi-structured interview guides in qualitative research. However, it was pilot-tested with five vendors, and minor wording adjustments were made. Data from the pilot interviews were not included in the final analysis. This is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data analysis

The analysis was guided by the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM), which conceptualizes health behaviors as influenced by multiple levels: intrapersonal (vendor knowledge, attitudes), interpersonal (student preferences, peer vendor norms), organizational (school policies, teacher oversight), community (competition from external vendors, local economy), and policy (municipal regulations, enforcement). This framework was used to organize the thematic findings into barriers and opportunities at each level. Following Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase thematic analysis, all audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Fante, then translated into English. The lead author (J.A.) performed initial line-by-line inductive coding using NVivo 12.

A codebook was developed iteratively, starting with 78 initial codes, which were then grouped into 24 sub-themes and finally into 7 major themes. A thematic map illustrating the coding tree (78 initial codes → 24 sub-themes → 7 major themes) is provided as Supplementary File 2. A second coder (research assistant with qualitative training) independently coded 30% of the transcripts (two randomly selected FGDs). Inter-coder agreement was 86%; disagreements were resolved through discussion, and the codebook was refined accordingly. The remaining transcripts were coded by the lead author using the finalized codebook. Three peer debriefing sessions were held with two senior researchers not involved in data collection. They reviewed the thematic structure and challenged interpretations, leading to the merger of two initial themes (“pricing strategies” and “student economics” into “Pricing & Affordability”) and the splitting of “vendor barriers” into separate financial and infrastructure sub-themes. The lead author, a nutrition researcher with a professional interest in child health, acknowledged a potential bias toward prioritising healthy eating over vendors’ economic realities. A reflective journal was maintained throughout coding and analysis to document and mitigate this bias. Credibility was further enhanced through triangulation (multiple schools, community types) and member checking (summary of findings shared with one vendor per school for confirmation). Transcripts were not returned to participants for line-by-line verification due to time constraints and low literacy levels among some vendors; however, a one-page summary of the main themes was shared orally with one vendor per school, who confirmed that the findings accurately represented their experiences.

Reporting guidelines

The reporting of this study conforms to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines. 14 The completed COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 3.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the socio-demographic characteristics of the 38 participants. The vendor population was predominantly female (97.4%), married (55.3%), and had attained primary or junior high school education (71.1%). Most had more than seven years of vending experience (71.0%), sold cooked meals (60.5%), and sourced ingredients from local markets (78.9%). While 81.6% had received food safety training, only 13.2% belonged to a vendor association.

It should be noted that while 73.7% of vendors were “registered with their school” (i.e., given permission by the head teacher to operate on campus), none held a formal business license from the Cape Coast Metropolitan Assembly, consistent with our operational definition of “informal vendor.” School registration is an informal administrative permission, not a government-issued license.

The findings are organised according to the socio-ecological model (SEM), which conceptualises multiple levels of influence on food vendor practices: individual (vendor knowledge, attitudes), interpersonal (relationships with students and school authorities), community (physical infrastructure, market dynamics), and policy (regulatory environment). The themes and sub-themes are presented below under each SEM level.

Current food offerings

Types of food sold

Vendors reported selling a wide variety of foods, which were categorized into three groups. The first was fruits and beverages, such as “Watermelon and fruits stuffs” (VKWP1, Kwaprow) and natural orange juice with “no sugar” (VEFP1, Efutu). The second category was traditional meals, including “Banku and Okro with cow hyde and chicken” (VJWP1, Jacob Wilson Sey), “Ga Kenkey and fish and fried yam” (VKWP4, Kwaprow), and “Gari and beans” (VEKP5, Ekon). The third category was convenience meals and fast foods, such as “Jollof, Spaghetti, chicken” (VKWP5, Kwaprow), “fried potatoes, fried cassava (apalankye), fried chicken and sausage” (VEFP2, Efutu), and “Ice cream” (VKWP5, Kwaprow).

Decision factors

Vendors’ decisions on what to sell were influenced by two primary factors. The first was school guidance, with some vendors noting that “sometimes the school requests that they go out to buy this particular food… so we decide the kind of foods we should sell” (VKWP3, Kwaprow). The second, and more frequently cited factor, was student preferences. Vendors closely followed demand, as one explained: “When you bring it and they eat then get tired of it, when you bring it again, you’ll realize they are not really buying then you’ll know that they are tired of it then you change it” (VJWP2, Jacob Wilson Sey). Another vendor noted that students dictate recipes: “They prefer for me to let the food have thick base (kanzo). When there’s no kanzo they won’t buy” (VPQP6, Philip Quaque).

School food environment

Student purchasing behavior

Vendors reported that students preferred external vendors. One vendor observed, “The students go out to buy the foods that are not here” (VKWP3, Kwaprow), while another stated, “They take ten cedis outside to buy spaghetti when we sell same for seven cedis” (VJWP5, Jacob Wilson Sey). Vendors also noted low demand for certain items perceived as healthy, particularly fruits: “School children here do not like fruits that much” (VEKP4, Ekon).

Vendor adaptation

Vendors engaged in menu experimentation. One vendor described their strategy: “I prepare different rice dishes daily: plain rice morning, jollof afternoon” (VPQP7, Philip Quaque). Another adapted a recipe: “I tried using spaghetti as spring roll filling but reverted to beans/vegetables for health” (VPQP4, Philip Quaque).

Regulatory compliance

Regulatory compliance (or lack thereof): although most vendors themselves operated without formal permits, they expressed concern about unauthorised sellers who competed unfairly and might sell unsafe food, suggesting an expectation that rules should apply to everyone: “People from outside come to sell without procedure, we complain to no avail” (VJWP1, Jacob Wilson Sey). This was compounded by inconsistent enforcement: “The head is aware of teachers selling sobolo in class but says nothing” (VPEP5, Pedu).

Competition

Market saturation shaped the food environment. Vendors described a crowded marketplace: “I was selling fried yam, someone started selling same which made selling difficult” (VKWP2, Kwaprow). This led to self-regulation: “If I want to sell bread and know someone already sells it, I change to something else” (VJWP2, Jacob Wilson Sey). Vendors also cited competition from unauthorized external sellers: “Hawkers come early during first break, most students buy from them” (VEFP7, Efutu).

Healthy food options

Perceived healthiness

Vendors judged the healthiness of their food based on two criteria. Many pointed to the use of natural ingredients, stating that “Kontomire and garden egg stew helps, rice and waakye too helps and all provide health” (VKWP5, Kwaprow) and that “Beans contains protein so it is fine” (VPEP4, Pedu). Others claimed that their food was healthy due to their hygiene practices, asserting that “Where we prepare the food too is hygienic” (VKWP2, Kwaprow) and “We don’t reheat leftovers. We prepare fresh foods daily” (VEFP7, Efutu).

Perceptions

Vendors held diverse views on healthy food. Some identified local nutritious foods, stating that “Kontomire and garden egg stew helps” (VKWP5, Kwaprow) and “Banku and okro gives strength, protein in both” (VPQP2, Philip Quaque). A common view was a universal health claim: “All foods here are healthy” (All Participants, Kwaprow), echoed by “Every food is good, depends on how well you cook it” (VEKP5, Ekon). Most vendors demonstrated foundational knowledge of nutrition, correctly identifying that fruits, vegetables, and minimally processed foods are healthier than fried or sugary snacks.

Barriers

Vendors identified financial constraints as the main barrier to offering healthier options. They stated, “We have issue with money—financially not okay” (VKWP4, Kwaprow) and “Financial help is most important—everything needs money” (VPQP3, Philip Quaque). One vendor explained the trade-off between ingredient quality and affordability: “Costly ingredients prevent using meat in spring rolls while pricing at 1 cedi” (VPQP4, Philip Quaque). Vendors also noted the rising cost of staples, reporting that a “Sack of maize is 1700 cedis” yet they feel compelled to “sell cheap because they’re children” (VEFP2, Efutu). Vendors reported that high input costs, limited operating capital, and the need to keep prices low led them to offer calorie-dense, less expensive foods.

Student choice

Vendors described student autonomy as a constraint. They stated, “We don’t force students—they buy whatever they feel like buying” (VKWP1, Kwaprow) and “They buy based on preference: some like rice, some kenkey” (VEFP6, Efutu). One vendor noted, “Children change preferences—majority buy one food today, another tomorrow” (VPQP7, Philip Quaque). Vendors also expressed frustration about being blamed for health issues originating elsewhere: “They spend money on sweets before school then blame stomach upset on our food” (VPQP2, Philip Quaque). Vendors perceived their influence as limited to the point of sale.

Food preparation & hygiene

Safety practices

Vendors described daily routines for contamination prevention and sanitation. One vendor washed produce: “I wash all fruits with salt and water before cutting” (VPQP5, Philip Quaque). Another stored “corn dough in an ice chest overnight” (VEFP4, Efutu). Vendors reported covering food “to prevent flies” (VKWP4, Kwaprow) and using protective gear: “We wear gloves, hairnets, aprons; change uniforms daily” (VPQP4, Philip Quaque). A stated policy was “We don’t reheat leftovers. We prepare fresh foods daily” (VEFP7, Efutu).

Compliance mechanisms

Adherence to safety protocols was reinforced through formal and informal mechanisms. Formal pre-screening included statements such as “Before selling, we did labs to know who is fit” (VKWP2, Kwaprow) and “Blood screening reports must be submitted to the school head” (VEFP9, Efutu). Informal monitoring involved teachers who “inspect food before allowing sale” (VJWP2, Jacob Wilson Sey) and provided corrective feedback. Vendors also described a peer network: “Colleague vendors taste each other’s food and share feedback” (VPQP4, Philip Quaque).

Pricing & affordability

Vendors set prices primarily based on students’ ability to pay, often prioritising low-cost, high-quantity items over healthier but more expensive options.Vendors’ pricing was characterized by financial empathy. They collectively stated, “All students are our children-we consider them in money issues” (All VKWP, Kwaprow). Vendors reduced prices compared to the open market: “I sell Kenkey in town for 3 cedis-here I reduce for students” (VKWP2, Kwaprow). Vendors responded to student budget constraints. They observed, “Some come with 2 cedis and want everything” (VKWP3, Kwaprow) and “Parents give five children 1 cedi each-child strives to get satisfied” (VEKP5, Ekon). Vendors reported that these constraints compelled them to offer cheap, calorie-dense foods.

Challenges & support

Infrastructure needs

Vendors identified infrastructure as a challenge. One suggested combining canteens: “combine [canteens] so students buy what they want” (VKWP1, Kwaprow). Vendors requested basic shelter: “When it rains we don’t get place to sit, very necessary” (VKWP4, Kwaprow), a “well-structured canteen with comfortable seats” (VKWP2, Kwaprow), and “covered space with tables/chairs for students” (VEKP6, Ekon). They believed a renovated space would keep students on campus: “Renovate canteen with benches—students won’t go out if comfortable” (VJWP5, Jacob Wilson Sey).

Resource constraints

Vendors cited lack of capital as a critical need. They stated, “We need money for our business” (VKWP4, Kwaprow) and “Financial help is most important—everything needs money” (VPQP3, Philip Quaque). One vendor noted that “Costly ingredients prevent using meat in spring rolls while pricing at 1 cedi” (VPQP4, Philip Quaque). Another requested “storage facilities to store bananas instead of our single room” (VEKP4, Ekon) and “subsidies would help buy fresh ingredients in bulk” (VPQP6, Philip Quaque).

Future plans & ideas

Infrastructure development

Vendors desired permanent shelter: “When rains we pack things to Form Three class—need permanent shelter” (VKWP3, Kwaprow). They proposed a “well-structured canteen with tables/chairs for comfort” (VKWP3, Kwaprow), believing that “students won’t go out if comfortable” (VJWP5, Jacob Wilson Sey).

Food safety collaboration

Vendors requested regulation of external vendors, noting that “Those selling outside haven’t done labs—can’t trace health issues” (VKWP3, Kwaprow). They asked for a teacher-vendor partnership: “We want teachers to help talk to students not to buy outside” (VKWP4, Kwaprow).

Vendor accountability

Vendors expressed personal responsibility for child welfare: “Ours is to make food healthy so children are safe. If I don’t do well, I’m destroying my business” (VKWP3, Kwaprow). They noted that they consume their own products: “We eat same food we sell—can’t buy rotten ingredients” (VJWP1, Jacob Wilson Sey). One vendor stated, “If a child gets sick from my food, teachers will question me—so I maintain standards” (VEFP2, Efutu).

Cross-community comparisons: Farming vs. fishing schools

While the seven major themes were consistent across all six schools, three differences emerged between farming-community schools (Kwaprow, Jacob Wilson Sey, Philip Quaque Presbyterian) and fishing-community schools (Efutu, Ekon, Pedu).

Availability of fish-based meals: Vendors in fishing-community schools reported more frequent inclusion of fresh fish in meals (e.g., “banku with fresh mackerel”), whereas vendors in farming-community schools relied more on dried fish, cow hide, or sausage as protein sources.

Fruit sales: Vendors in farming-community schools (particularly Kwaprow) reported slightly higher sales of locally grown fruits (oranges, bananas, pineapple) compared to fishing-community schools.

Student spending power: Vendors in fishing communities perceived students as having slightly more daily spending money; however, these students also purchased more sugary snacks and ice cream. Vendors in farming communities reported more extreme budget constraints (students with as little as 50 pesewas).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that informal school food vendors in the Cape Coast Metropolitan Area occupy a dual role: they are potential gatekeepers to healthy eating, yet their efforts are constrained by systemic economic and structural barriers. Organised according to the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM), this discussion examines these barriers at the individual, interpersonal, organisational, community, and policy levels.

At the individual level, vendors demonstrated foundational knowledge of nutrition and hygiene, yet financial constraints prevented them from translating this knowledge into practice. Vendors reported that high ingredient costs and limited capital forced them to prioritise affordability over nutritional quality, leading to the sale of calorie-dense, low-nutrient foods. A systematic review in Ghana similarly found that while food handlers possess basic food safety knowledge, the pooled proportion of those demonstrating good practices was only 55.8%, with economic constraints being a primary driver of the knowledge-practice gap. 15

At the interpersonal level, student choice dynamics significantly shaped vendor behaviour. Vendors perceived themselves as passive respondents to student demand, stating that they could not force students to buy healthier items. This consumer-driven market created a feedback loop where vendors avoided experimenting with nutritious foods that students might reject. A Photovoice study in Cape Coast confirmed that adolescents prioritise taste, affordability, and peer influence over nutritional value when making food purchases. 7

At the organisational level, a marked policy-practice gap was evident. While vendors underwent mandatory health screenings and obtained school permission to operate, enforcement against unauthorised external vendors was virtually absent. This inconsistency penalised compliant vendors who incurred costs to meet regulatory standards, while unregulated sellers operated with impunity, posing food safety risks. Research in Ghana has documented weak institutional capacities and overlapping responsibilities as fundamental barriers to effective enforcement of food safety regulations. 16

At the community level, inadequate infrastructure emerged as a critical barrier. 17 Vendors lacked weather-protected vending spaces, secure storage for perishable ingredients, and designated dining areas for students. 18 These deficits forced vendors to abandon their posts during rain, limited their ability to stock fresh produce, and drove students to leave school premises to purchase food. A mixed-methods systematic review of publicly procured school meals programmes in sub-Saharan Africa identified infrastructure support (including funding, monitoring, and coordination) as one of the most critical implementation challenges, noting that without adequate infrastructure, the nutritional impact of such programmes remains severely constrained. 19

At the policy level, a noteworthy and counter-intuitive finding was vendors’ active receptivity to regulation. Rather than resisting oversight, vendors pleaded for school authorities to fence the school, restrict unauthorised external sellers, and establish teacher-vendor partnerships. This openness contrasts with narratives that position informal vendors as adversaries to public health goals. A community readiness appraisal in Ghana found that while leadership support for school food policies was high, actual implementation was weak; engaging vendors as partners could bridge this gap. 5

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The findings reflect the specific context of the participating schools and may not be directly transferable to other settings without considering local differences. Self-reported data from focus group discussions may be subject to social desirability bias, i.e., participants may have under-reported undesirable practices (e.g., selling unhealthy food) or over-reported positive attitudes toward nutrition. To minimise this bias, we assured confidentiality, used non-judgmental probing, and built rapport before questioning. If present, bias may have led to an overestimation of vendors’ health-promoting behaviours and an underestimation of challenges. However, the consistency of responses across different groups suggests that bias, if any, was limited. The study focused exclusively on vendor perspectives; triangulation with views of students, parents, and school administrators would have provided a more comprehensive understanding. Additionally, the semi-structured question guide was not formally validated using psychometric methods, although it was pilot-tested.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that informal school food vendors in Ghana’s Central Region are not inherently barriers to healthy eating but are constrained entrepreneurs operating within a system that prioritizes affordability and economic survival over nutrition. Their strong hygiene practices, nutritional knowledge, and sense of accountability (e.g., We eat what we sell) make them potential gatekeepers. However, without addressing the structural barriers, student poverty, competition from unregulated vendors, lack of infrastructure, and weak policy enforcement, their potential will remain unrealized. Critically, vendors are not resistant to regulation; they actively seek collaboration with schools and teachers. This finding reframes the policy problem from one of vendor non-compliance to one of systemic neglect. Transforming the school food environment will require multi-level interventions that support vendors rather than merely regulating them.

Recommendation

Based on these findings, we recommend three interconnected actions. First, for policy makers (Ghana Education Service, Ghana Health Service, Metropolitan Assemblies): Develop and enforce a School Food Policy that includes (a) a ban on unauthorized external vendors within 200 meters of schools, (b) mandatory health screenings for all on-site vendors, and (c) subsidies or microloans to help vendors purchase nutritious ingredients. Second, for school administrators: Invest in basic infrastructure (shaded dining areas, potable water, storage facilities) and establish teacher-vendor partnership committees for ongoing dialogue and mutual accountability. Third, for future research: Conduct longitudinal intervention studies to test the effectiveness of vendor training, subsidy models, and certification schemes on children’s dietary outcomes. Additionally, comparative studies across Ghana’s regions are needed to assess contextual variability.
