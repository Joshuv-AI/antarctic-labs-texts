Left atrial myxoma as an unusual cause of mechanical mitral prosthesis obstruction: a case report and literature review
Authors: Rozita Jalalian, Fatemeh Mousavi, S. M. Sadegh Mousavi-kiasary, Mahdis Bayat, Soheil Azizi, Farima Sadat Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case study reports on a 43-year-old man with a history of mitral and aortic valve replacements, who presented with worsening dyspnea, palpitations, chest pain, and vertigo. Despite stable vital signs and fluoroscopy, imaging revealed large, mobile masses near the mitral valve, likely indicating left atrial myxomas causing mechanical obstruction. Urgent CT scans ruled out embolism, and surgical intervention was performed, revealing two sizable tumoral masses attached to the left atrium. Preoperative coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries. This case underscores the importance of comprehensive diagnostic evaluation and a multidisciplinary approach in managing complex cardiac conditions, particularly in patients with previous valve replacements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04579-8.

Introduction

Cardiac myxomas (CM) represent a type of benign primary cardiac tumor, out of which myxoma is the most common [1, 2]. Cardiac myxomas are commonly diagnosed in adults, with an annual incidence of 0.5–1 case per million individuals. Sporadic forms constitute 90% of cases, while 5% to 10% are familial and inherited. Myxomas usually manifest as a solitary mass; the occurrence of multiple myxomas is rare [3]. Females are three times more likely than males to be diagnosed with CM, and the majority of cases occur in individuals aged 40–60 years [4–8]. Moreover, Cardiac myxomas induce diverse symptoms, contingent on their size and location [9].

Systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, malaise, anorexia, arthralgia, and fatigue are commonly associated with myxomas, while some individuals may remain asymptomatic [10, 11]. Visceral organs, including the spleen, adrenals, kidneys, and abdominal aorta, can also be affected, leading to symptoms like Raynaud’s phenomenon, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and other peripheral signs of embolization [10]. Patients may also develop pulmonary hypertension [12]. A majority of individuals diagnosed with cardiac myxomas have reported experiencing dyspnea [13]. Valvular obstruction can lead to syncopal episodes, along with palpitations, conduction abnormalities, arrhythmias, and, in cases where right atrial myxomas obstruct the tricuspid valve, heart failure and collapse [14–16]. Prosthetic valve stenosis can occur due to several pathological mechanisms, including thrombus formation, pannus ingrowth, leaflet calcification, particularly noted in bioprosthetic valves, and the presence of vegetations. Conversely, prosthetic valve regurgitation may stem from thrombus formation in mechanical valves, leaflet tears associated with bioprosthetic implants, the development of vegetations, or paravalvular leak (PVL). Understanding these complications is essential for the effective management and long-term outcomes of patients with prosthetic heart valves [17]. Left-sided myxomas primarily manifest symptoms related to mitral valve obstruction or regurgitation, left-sided heart failure, and secondary pulmonary hypertension. Key indicators include dyspnea triggered by exertion, orthopnea, and pulmonary edema [18].

Currently, surgical intervention as the preferred treatment for CM demonstrates low risk and high efficacy for most patients and ensures an excellent postoperative prognosis [19–21]. The primary goal of surgery is to remove the tumor completely to prevent complications such as embolism, mechanical obstruction, or heart failure [22]. The surgical approach typically depends on the location of the myxoma within the heart, with left atrial tumors being the most common [23]. These tumors are often removed through a median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass, allowing for direct access to the heart [24]. In some cases, particularly with larger or more complex tumors, additional procedures, such as valve repair or replacement, may be necessary if the myxoma has caused significant damage to heart structures(20). Despite the low rate of myxoma’s recurrence, postoperative monitoring and long-term follow-up are recommended [25].

This case study details a patient with a history of heart valve replacements who was admitted for worsening symptoms, resulting in the discovery and surgical removal of a large left atrial mass. Most malfunctions associated with prosthetic valves commonly occur due to issues such as pannus formation and thrombosis. Although cardiac myxomas have been associated with a variety of obstructive and embolic complications, reports describing a left atrial myxoma causing functional obstruction of a normally functioning mechanical prosthetic mitral valve remain exceedingly rare in the literature. This case highlights an unusual mechanism of prosthetic valve obstruction and underscores the importance of comprehensive multimodality imaging when clinical and hemodynamic findings are discordant. Further investigation may be warranted to explore this relationship more comprehensively.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old man has been experiencing shortness of breath for a year, with an intensification of dyspnea in the three weeks preceding admission in September 2024 at a tertiary heart center. Adequate information about the treatment procedure was provided for the patient through a written consent form designed for this study, and the patient’s information confidentiality was upheld.

Concurrently, he reported a dry cough, dyspnea, vertigo, palpitations during activities, and a recent onset of dyspnea upon standing. Acutely, he presented with severe chest and back pain, along with vertigo during syncope-inducing activity. Having undergone mitral and aortic valve replacements in 2016, he had a family history of heart attacks.

The physical examination indicated stable vital signs, and the ECG showed a normal sinus rhythm with dyspnea-induced tachycardia. Emergency echocardiography revealed a bi-leaflet mechanical mitral valve with acceptable motion and increased gradient (Vmax = 2.6 m/s, mean gradient = 17 mmHg), and a bi-leaflet mechanical aortic valve with acceptable motion and gradient (Vmax = 1.96 m/s, mean gradient = 7.15 mmHg). Urgent fluoroscopy demonstrated normal mechanical mitral prosthetic leaflet motion. However, given the patient’s persistent symptoms and Doppler echocardiographic findings suggestive of severe prosthetic mitral valve stenosis, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed. Initial TEE demonstrated elevated transprosthetic gradients, with a mean transmitral gradient of 17 mmHg and a pressure half-time of 309 ms, raising suspicion for prosthetic valve obstruction. Fluoroscopic examination showed preserved leaflet mobility without evidence of significant restriction. Given the discrepancy between Doppler findings and fluoroscopy, a comprehensive echocardiographic reassessment was performed.

At a heart rate of approximately 80 beats/min, TEE demonstrated severe functional obstruction with a peak transmitral velocity of 2.42 m/s, mean gradient of 18 mmHg, pressure half-time of 445 ms, calculated mitral valve area of approximately 0.49 cm², mild mitral regurgitation, and estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 60 mmHg. TEE revealed two distinct left atrial masses. The larger mass was highly mobile, multilobulated, pedunculated, and measured approximately 3 × 4 cm. It originated from the atrial septum and intermittently protruded through the mechanical mitral prosthesis into the left ventricle, resulting in severe functional obstruction. A second separate mass measuring 1 × 1.5 cm was identified on the medial aspect of the left atrium posterior to the aortic root (Fig. 1). These findings explained the discrepancy between the normal fluoroscopic assessment of prosthetic leaflet motion and the severe hemodynamic obstruction detected by Doppler echocardiography.

Regarding the differential diagnosis, thrombotic lesions were considered less likely because the patient had regular warfarin use with a therapeutic INR of 3.34 at presentation, and fluoroscopy demonstrated normal mechanical leaflet motion. Infective vegetation was also considered unlikely in the absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of infection. The large size, multilobulated morphology, high mobility, and left atrial attachment of the masses on TEE favored a diagnosis of myxoma. Urgent cerebral and lung CT scans ruled out cerebral and pulmonary embolic events. After discussion of the risk of perioperative thromboembolic complications, surgical excision was scheduled. Preoperative coronary angiography demonstrated normal coronary arteries with slow coronary flow (Video-1), without evidence of hypertrophied branches arising from the left circumflex artery, including the left atrial branch.

Intraoperative examination confirmed the presence of two distinct masses attached to the left atrium, both of which were completely excised. Following surgical removal, the mean transmitral gradient decreased to 3 mmHg, and the estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure improved to approximately 30 mmHg. Histopathological analysis established the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma (Fig. 2). These findings were confirmed by cardiac fluoroscopy, which concluded LAM in the patient (Video-2).

Discussion

The patient presented in this study manifested by multiple myxoma masses in 2024, who had MVR and AVR in 2016. The patient’s symptoms, including dyspnea, chest pain, and syncope, raise concerns about the impact of these myxomas on the function of the replaced valves and overall cardiac performance. The unique challenge in this scenario lies in the potential interaction between the myxomas and the prosthetic valves, which necessitates a comprehensive evaluation.

The relationship between myxomas and previous valve replacements is intricate. Myxomas, as intracardiac tumors, can interfere with the normal functioning of the heart. In this case, the myxomas, identified through echocardiography, were situated near the prosthetic mitral valve, causing severe mechanical obstruction. The presence of myxomas in close proximity to the replaced valves could affect the hemodynamics and functionality of these prosthetic devices. This prompts critical consideration of how myxomas, despite being benign, can have significant implications for patients with a history of valve replacements, affecting both symptomatology and the overall success of the prior surgical interventions. Majumdar et al. describe a case of a right atrial myxoma in a patient who had undergone mitral valve repair 20 years prior. The patient presented with congestive heart failure. The tumor originated from the ostium of the coronary sinus and prolapsed into the right ventricle, resulting in significant obstruction of both right ventricular inflow and outflow [26]. Oliveira et al. reported two cases, 25 and 72 years old. The younger patient was very symptomatic with a large mass, 4 cm long, which involved both leaflets, causing significant obstruction to the left ventricular inflow. The second patient had a smaller mass situated on the atrial side of the posterior leaflet, which caused only minor flow disturbance [27]. Nuns et al. report a case of a man who presented with a left atrial myxoma located near the mitral valve, necessitating mitral valve replacement with a mechanical prosthesis during tumor excision. One year later, he was diagnosed with a recurrent pedunculated left atrial myxoma, which caused mechanical obstruction of the prosthetic mitral valve [28].

The diagnostic approach employed in this case reflects the complexity of evaluating a patient with prior cardiac valve surgery and suspected prosthetic valve dysfunction. Echocardiography, including both transthoracic and transesophageal modalities, played a central role in assessing prosthetic valve function and identifying the underlying pathology, remaining the preferred diagnostic tool for the evaluation of valvular heart disease because of its accessibility and diagnostic accuracy [27]. Urgent CT imaging was additionally performed to exclude embolic complications and further assess the extent of the cardiac masses. The combination of these imaging modalities provided a comprehensive anatomical and hemodynamic assessment that guided subsequent surgical management.

A particularly important aspect of this case was the discrepancy between fluoroscopic and echocardiographic findings. Fluoroscopy demonstrated normal mobility of the mechanical mitral valve leaflets, suggesting preserved prosthetic valve function and making intrinsic causes of prosthetic valve obstruction, such as thrombosis or pannus formation, less likely. In contrast, Doppler echocardiography consistently demonstrated markedly elevated transmitral gradients and severe functional obstruction. This finding highlights an important clinical point that normal leaflet motion on fluoroscopy does not necessarily exclude significant obstruction when the pathology originates outside the prosthetic valve. In the present case, the obstruction was caused by a large, highly mobile left atrial myxoma that intermittently prolapsed through the prosthetic mitral valve into the left ventricle, resulting in severe impairment of transmitral flow despite preserved leaflet excursion. TEE was therefore essential for establishing the correct diagnosis, as it directly visualized the mass and clarified the mechanism of obstruction. Taken together, this case emphasizes the complementary value of fluoroscopy and echocardiography in the evaluation of suspected prosthetic valve dysfunction and supports the use of TEE whenever abnormal Doppler hemodynamics persist despite apparently normal fluoroscopic findings.

However, this report has limitations. As a single case, the findings are not generalizable and do not establish a causal relationship between prior valve replacement and myxoma development. Additional imaging, such as cardiac MRI, was not performed and may have improved tissue characterization. Long-term follow-up is limited, restricting assessment of recurrence and outcomes. This case also highlights the challenge of balancing diagnostic certainty with timely intervention, underscoring the value of a multidisciplinary approach in managing such complex presentations.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the occasional but serious presentation of multiple atrial myxomas in a patient with a history of mitral and aortic valve replacement. The interaction between the myxomas and prosthetic valves creates a significant atypical clinical situation, since the tumors can mainly alter the function of the prosthetic valves, leading to mechanical obstruction and exacerbation of symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, and syncope. Echocardiography was utilized for a comprehensive diagnostic approach to study both the anatomical and functional implications of the myxomas. Surgery was indicated to alleviate the mechanical obstruction and reduce the attendant risks. The emphasis is on careful follow-up and early intervention in patients. The need for a multidisciplinary team in managing such complex cases is highlighted here, as patients with previous valve replacements present to the clinic with new cardiac symptoms. Long-term outcomes and optimal management of patients with atrial myxomas and prosthetic valve replacements need further review.
