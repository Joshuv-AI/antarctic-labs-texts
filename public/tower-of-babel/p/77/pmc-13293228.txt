Shifting perceptions of e-cigarette risk: A secondary analysis from a nationwide, randomized controlled clinical trial of e-cigarettes among smokers
Authors: Emily M Barros, Anna C Ferreira, Brian Neelon, Iyonica E Ravenel, Matthew J Carpenter, Tracy T Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) acknowledges there is a continuum of risk for tobacco products, and that e-cigarettes are generally less harmful than combustible cigarettes. However, most US adults perceive e-cigarettes as more harmful than cigarettes, and negative perceptions have increased over time. Media coverage surrounding e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury (EVALI) may have played a role in the changing relative harm perceptions about e-cigarettes and other tobacco products. The goal of the present study was to compare relative risk perceptions for cigarettes and e-cigarettes before, during, and after EVALI.Methods:The present paper is a secondary data analysis from a US RCT conducted from May 2018 to March of 2022. Participants self-reported demographic information and perceptions of risk for both cigarettes and e-cigarettes at baseline. Changes in relative risk before, during, and after the EVALI outbreak, were compared using a piecewise constant model.Results:Relative risk perceptions of e-cigarettes were significantly greater during (p < 0.001) and after EVALI (p < 0.001) compared to before the onset of EVALI. The differences in risk perceptions between cohorts of participants differed by race (p = 0.014), in that there was a significant increase in perceived risk of harm of e-cigarettes (relative risk perceptions) during and after EVALI compared to before EVALI for non-white participants (p < 0.001, p < 0.001).Conclusions:Onset of EVALI may have impacted relative risk perceptions of e-cigarettes compared to cigarettes. Non-white smokers were more likely to experience increased perceived risk of harm of e-cigarettes (relative risk perceptions). There is a need to continue monitoring risk perceptions among smokers, as negative relative risk perceptions may hinder switching from cigarettes to e-cigarettes.Keywords: Smoking, E-cigarettes, Risk perceptions

Introduction

In 2007, e-cigarettes entered the US market and have been used by both people who do and do not smoke cigarettes. Currently, e-cigarettes are the most used non-cigarette tobacco product among adults in the US (Cornelius et al., 2023). E-cigarette use among adults is most common among current (13.8%) and former (13.3%) smokers (Boakye et al., 2022). The United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) acknowledges that there is a continuum of risk for tobacco and nicotine products, and that e-cigarettes are generally less harmful than combustible cigarettes, as they expose users to fewer carcinogens and toxicants (Tattan-Birch et al., 2020). However, misperceptions about the harm of e-cigarettes are common (Majeed et al., 2017). In 2017, 55.6% of US adults perceived e-cigarettes to be equally as harmful as combustible cigarettes and 9.9% perceived e-cigarettes to be more harmful than combustible cigarettes (Huang et al., 2019). Women, Hispanic and non-Hispanic black smokers, and smokers with lower educational attainment and/or lower income are more likely to perceive e-cigarettes as equally or more harmful than cigarettes (Majeed et al., 2017; Harlow et al., 2023). There has been a shift in relative risk perceptions of e-cigarettes since then such that the percentage of US adults who believe e-cigarettes are just as harmful or more harmful than combustible cigarettes is increasing (Majeed et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2019; Bandi et al., 2022). From 2018 to 2020 the percentage of US adults who believed e-cigarettes were more harmful than cigarettes more than doubled (Bandi et al., 2022).

One potential cause of changing risk perceptions of e-cigarettes is e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury (EVALI). EVALI was a lung illness with pulmonary and gastrointestinal symptoms (CDC., 2022; CDC., 2021) that began in the middle of 2019 and peaked in September of that same year. EVALI included multiple cases of morbidity and even mortality. A majority of EVALI cases were associated with tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) vaping devices and vitamin E acetate, a common ingredient in THC vaping devices (Wackowski et al., 2023). While the cause was initially unclear, the CDC eventually found EVALI to be strongly linked with vitamin E acetate (CDC., 2022; CDC., 2021). However, most of the media coverage failed to distinguish between THC vaping devices and nicotine e-cigarettes when reporting on EVALI and instead focused on e-cigarette safety and absolute risks (Jeong et al., 2022), and many people may have never learned that THC vaping devices were largely responsible for EVALI. Communication surrounding EVALI in the media may have played a role in the changing relative harm perceptions about tobacco products (Bandi et al., 2022). One study of current smokers found that EVALI awareness was significantly associated with e-cigarette risk perceptions such that individuals who had heard of it were more likely to perceive e-cigarettes to be just as or more harmful than smoking cigarettes (Wackowski et al., 2023). However, there has yet to be a granular assessment of risk- if and how perceived risk changed over the EVALI time period. Furthermore, the change in risk perceptions across time during the EVALI outbreak across different demographic groups is unknown.

The purpose of this secondary analysis was to assess whether e-cigarette risk perceptions changed over the time period that EVALI was prominent (September 2019 to February 2020) compared to the period right before and right after, and whether changes differed based on demographic characteristics.

Methods

Participants

The parent trial was a randomized, controlled clinical trial for e-cigarettes, based in the United States, with aims to examine the naturalistic course of uptake and use of e-cigarettes. Detailed methods can be found in the primary paper outcomes (Carpenter et al., 2023). The study consisted of adult smokers with minimal history of e-cigarette use (not having purchased e-cigarettes in the prior six months, nor having ever used a tank system weekly, nor having used any e-cigarette of any kind in the preceding six months) (N=638), enrolled between May 31, 2018 to March 21, 2022, all recruited from 11 different cities across the US. Inclusion criteria included: 1) age 21+, 2) currently smoking ≥5 cigarettes per day for ≥1 year, 3) no recent known history of cardiovascular distress, 4) not breastfeeding or pregnant, and 5) not currently using smoking cessation medication, and 5) minimal history of e-cigarette use. Participants were recruited via online methods (e.g. Craigslist) which then directed them to a secure online platform for initial screening. Eligible participants provided informed consent and were scheduled for a baseline intake call. The consent form was updated to include a brief mention of EVALI after the onset of the cases, noting that most cases were related to vaping THC and advising participants not to add anything to their e-liquid.

Surveys

For the purpose of this secondary analysis, we utilized only the baseline survey, completed prior to randomization and prior to any use of e-cigarettes within the study. Participants completed a demographic survey at baseline capturing sex, race, ethnicity, income, and education, as well smoking history information, which among other assessments included ever-use of an e-cigarette, current number of cigarettes smoked per day (CPD), and motivation to quit smoking (0–10 scale, where 10 is ‘very definitely yes’). Finally, participants completed a Risk Perception Survey, in which they were asked to rate on a scale of 0 to 10 how harmful to them personally they felt smoking cigarettes and using e-cigarettes were (separate questions, 0 ‘not at all’; 10 ‘very much’). Thus, perceived risk was assessed as absolute risk, both for combustible and e-cigarettes (separately).

Analyses

From the above, a relative risk perception score was calculated for each participant by subtracting cigarette absolute risk score from e-cigarette absolute risk score, with a positive score indicating e-cigarettes were perceived to be more harmful than cigarettes (score of 0 indicating equal perceptions of harm). The recruitment period was divided into 3 separate periods of Before EVALI (5/31/2018 – 8/30/2019), during EVALI (9/1/2019 – 2/29/2020) and after EVALI (3/1/2020 – 3/21/2022). The time period of ‘during’ EVALI was identified as September 1, 2019, to February 29, 2020 based on when the peak incidence of EVALI cases occurred; previous research identified September 2019 as the month with the greatest EVALI coverage (Jeong et al., 2022). The offset of EVALI was identified as February 29, 2020 as that is the last time the CDC updated their public reporting on EVALI (CDC., 2022).

Piecewise linear regression and piecewise constant models were used to examine whether risk perception scores among participants enrolled during and after EVALI differed significantly from risk perceptions among participants enrolled before onset of EVALI. Akaike information criterion (AIC) values revealed that the piecewise constant model (3153) better fit the data than the piecewise linear regression model (3157), and is thus reported here. P values were derived via linear contrasts based on the piecewise constant model. Effect modification by demographic characteristics were assessed to determine whether they moderated the difference change in risk perceptions across enrollment cohorts, including race (white vs. other), age (21–40 vs. 40+), and education (high school or less, some college or technical school, or college degree +). Effect modification by race using interaction term was assessed as well.

Fig. 1 shows average relative risk perception scores for all participants before, during and after EVALI. Relative risk scores were significantly higher during and after EVALI compared to before the onset of EVALI (difference in mean scores (95% confidence interval (CI)): 1.11 (0.47, 1.75, p=0.001 and 1.05 (0.55, 1.55), p<0.001, respectively), indicating that participants perceived e-cigarettes as being riskier (in comparison to cigarettes) after the onset of EVALI than they did before. On average, the relative risk perception scores remained negative, suggesting that participants always believed e-cigarettes to be less risky than cigarettes. The average relative risk scores during EVALI did not differ significantly from those recorded post-EVALI. A significant interaction between timepoint and race suggest that the differences in relative risk perceptions between enrollment cohorts differed by racial subgroup (p=0.014). Among non-white participants (N=201), average relative risk perception scores during and post-EVALI differed from pre-EVALI (difference in mean scores (95% CI): 2.32 (1.15, 3.49), p<0.001 and 1.89 (1.02, 2.76), p<0.001, respectively), but there was not a significant difference between relative risk perceptions scores across enrollment cohorts for white participants. Discussion

Relative risk perceptions for e-cigarettes differed between enrollment cohorts in this study. Results suggest that the onset of EVALI may have impacted relative risk perceptions for e-cigarettes, such that there was an increase in relative risk perceptions; i.e., e-cigarettes were perceived as increasingly harmful in comparison to combustible cigarettes. However, e-cigarettes were never found to be as or more harmful than cigarettes, at least at the group level, contrary to findings reported in previous studies (Majeed et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2019). Participants in this study enrolled, in part, due to an interest in e-cigarettes and this may be a factor as to why e-cigarettes were never viewed as being as harmful as cigarettes. The overall pattern of differences in relative risk perception between participants across enrollment cohorts differed by racial subgroup to a statistically significant degree. Other demographic differences in risk perceptions noted in previous literature were not found in these results. There is continuing need to monitor risk perceptions of e-cigarettes among smokers, as e-cigarettes may present as a less harmful alternative to smoking combustible cigarettes (Tattan-Birch et al., 2020; Levett et al., 2023). However, another paper from the same clinical trial indicated that a priori reasons for using an e-cigarette, including relative risk perceptions, were largely unrelated to uptake, frequency, or amount of e-cigarette use across the first four weeks of product provision (O’ Neal et al., 2024).

One limitation of the present analysis is that the COVID-19 pandemic partially overlaps with the EVALI time period. A few months after the EVALI peak, the COVID-19 pandemic reached the US in March 2020. Early messaging in the media was conflicting in regard to the risk of COVID-19 associated with smoking, nicotine use, and e-cigarette use. Media coverage of COVID-19 may have also impacted relative risk perceptions. Our enrollment cohort recruited during EVALI is unlikely to have been affected by COVID as the end date used here (February 29, 2020) precedes widespread mitigation or shutdown efforts for COVID-19 in the US. However, increased risk perceptions in the enrollment cohort that following EVALI may be partially due to COVID rather than EVALI (Bandi et al., 2022; Merz et al., 2022; White et al., 2021; Kamiński et al., 2020).

Most media outlets and public health organizations did not widely report that THC vaping devices were largely responsible for EVALI. However, in recent years, the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products (CTP) has taken steps to correct misperceptions about e-cigarettes among adults. CTP acknowledges that misperceptions about tobacco products may prevent smokers from switching to a lower-risk alternative. One paper found that risk perceptions of e-cigarettes were associated with lower odds of switching to both dual use and exclusive e-cigarette usage among smokers (Harlow et al., 2023), although our prior analysis did not find that association for this data set. As of this writing, the FDA has a public-facing website, as well several published papers and actively funded research, all with the goal of developing corrective messaging on misperceptions of combusted and non-combusted tobacco products (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, xxxx). However, additional steps may still be needed to correct misperceptions that could hinder switching among smokers, especially among non-white participants. Possible steps include educating both smokers and clinicians about relative risks of tobacco products via evidence-based messaging, ensuring that corrective messages are disseminated in outlets accessed by people of all racial and ethnic groups. FDA has experience developing culturally relevant media campaigns for specific populations, and might consider doing so here as well. Clinicians should stress that e-cigarettes are not FDA-approved for smoking cessation, and offer FDA-approved therapies to people who smoke. For people who smoke who are interested in switching to an e-cigarettes, messaging should reinforce the importance of fully transitioning off cigarettes, and emphasize that the eventual goal should be to quit use of all tobacco products (Toll et al., 2024; King and Toll, 2023).
