Spontaneous pneumomediastinum, pneumorrhachis, and subcutaneous emphysema secondary to hyperemesis gravidarum: A case report
Authors: Fernanda Avila-Perez, Estefany Lizeth Campos-Zazueta, Rosa Patricia Cruz-Nieves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous pneumomediastinum is a rare complication of hyperemesis gravidarum and may present with alarming symptoms that mimic more serious conditions, particularly esophageal perforation. A 20-year-old primigravida in the first trimester (11 weeks of gestation) presented with severe vomiting, facial swelling, odynophagia, and cervical crepitus. Imaging studies revealed spontaneous pneumomediastinum, pneumorrhachis, and extensive subcutaneous emphysema. Esophageal perforation was excluded by contrast esophagography.The patient developed electrolyte disturbances and acute kidney injury secondary to hyperemesis gravidarum and was successfully managed with intravenous fluid replacement, oxygen therapy, and close maternal-fetal surveillance. Clinical improvement occurred within five days, allowing hospital discharge without complications. The remainder of the pregnancy was uneventful, and a healthy female infant was delivered at term.This case highlights the importance of recognizing spontaneous pneumomediastinum as a potential complication of severe hyperemesis gravidarum. Associated pneumorrhachis is exceptionally uncommon and may lead to diagnostic uncertainty. Early recognition, exclusion of esophageal injury, and conservative multidisciplinary management may result in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.Keywords: Hyperemesis gravidarum, Pneumomediastinum, Pneumorrhachis, Subcutaneous emphysema, Case report

Introduction

Spontaneous pneumomediastinum (SPM), also known as Hamman syndrome, refers to the presence of free air within the mediastinum in the absence of trauma, invasive procedures, or other identifiable causes. This condition is uncommon and is thought to result from alveolar rupture with subsequent dissection of air along bronchovascular sheaths into the mediastinum, a mechanism known as the Macklin effect [1], [2].

Although SPM has been described during pregnancy, it occurs most commonly during labor and only rarely in association with hyperemesis gravidarum (HG). Repeated episodes of forceful vomiting may generate sufficient intrathoracic pressure to precipitate alveolar rupture and the subsequent leakage of air into the mediastinum [3], [4], [5].

The clinical presentation may resemble more serious conditions, particularly esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome), making prompt diagnosis essential. Computed tomography is considered the most sensitive imaging modality for confirming the diagnosis and assessing the extent of air dissection. Although the clinical course is generally benign and conservative management is usually successful, associated pneumorrhachis is exceptionally uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported in the setting of HG, and may increase diagnostic uncertainty. This report describes a case of SPM, pneumorrhachis, and subcutaneous emphysema secondary to HG during the first trimester of pregnancy, with favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes following conservative management [4], [6], [7].

Case Presentation

A 20-year-old primigravida at 11 + 1 weeks of gestation presented to the emergency department of a tertiary care maternal hospital in northern Mexico with persistent vomiting. The patient reported that symptoms had begun approximately 11 days before admission with nausea and vomiting that progressively worsened, reaching more than 25 episodes per day during the 2 days prior to presentation. She also complained of cough, odynophagia, facial swelling, and voice changes. Her medical history was otherwise unremarkable. She denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use and had no history of asthma, chronic pulmonary disease, thoracic trauma, or previous surgical procedures. On admission, vital signs were within normal limits. Blood pressure was 135/81 mmHg, heart rate was 86 beats/min, respiratory rate was 16 breaths/min, and body temperature was 37.0 °C.

On physical examination, facial asymmetry with increased volume in the maxillary region was noted. Oropharyngeal examination revealed tonsillar erythema and hypertrophy. The neck was edematous, and palpable cervical crepitus raised suspicion for subcutaneous emphysema. Obstetric evaluation demonstrated a viable intrauterine pregnancy with a fetal heart rate of 152 beats/min and no cervical changes.

Initial laboratory studies showed hemoconcentration, hepatic dysfunction, hyperuricemia, and acute kidney injury associated with HG. Hemoglobin was 16.6 g/dL, hematocrit 48.7%, leukocyte count 19.83 × 109/L, platelet count 383 × 109/L, creatinine 1.10 mg/dL, uric acid 14.4 mg/dL, total bilirubin 8.8 mg/dL, AST 100 U/L, and ALT 119 U/L. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a 2.4-cm hepatic hemangioma and minimal biliary sludge. These findings were consistent with severe HG complicated by dehydration and metabolic derangements.

A posteroanterior neck radiograph demonstrated extensive radiolucency involving the cervical soft tissues, compatible with subcutaneous emphysema (Fig. 1). A lateral neck radiograph showed air extending into the retropharyngeal space (Fig. 2). Computed tomography of the neck and chest confirmed extensive subcutaneous emphysema extending from the cervical region into the thoracic wall and mediastinum. Pneumorrhachis extending from C2 to T12 was also identified, together with bilateral small pneumothoraces and pneumomediastinum (Fig. 3).

Because the clinical presentation included cervical swelling, odynophagia, and subcutaneous emphysema, exclusion of Boerhaave syndrome represented a critical diagnostic step. Given the extent of the findings and the risk of airway compromise, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. A thoracic surgery consultation was obtained, and a water-soluble contrast esophagogram was performed to exclude esophageal perforation. No evidence of esophageal injury or contrast leakage was identified.

The patient received conservative management consisting of intravenous fluid resuscitation, electrolyte replacement, oxygen therapy, antiemetic treatment, and close maternal-fetal surveillance. During hospitalization, cervical crepitus gradually disappeared and respiratory symptoms improved, allowing gradual reintroduction of oral intake. Serial clinical assessments demonstrated progressive improvement, and no further respiratory compromise was observed.

The remainder of the pregnancy was uneventful. At 40 weeks of gestation, she was readmitted in active labor and delivered a healthy female infant weighing 3010 g and measuring 51 cm in length, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. No maternal or neonatal complications were observed, and both were discharged in good clinical condition.

Discussion

SPM is an uncommon condition characterized by the presence of free air within the mediastinum in the absence of trauma, surgical procedures, or other identifiable causes. Although SPM has been described during pregnancy and labor, its occurrence in association with HG remains exceptionally rare [1], [8]. In the present case, severe HG likely acted as the precipitating factor through repeated episodes of forceful vomiting and Valsalva-like maneuvers, resulting in a sudden increase in intra-alveolar pressure and subsequent alveolar rupture [1], [9].

The most widely accepted mechanism underlying SPM is the Macklin effect, in which air escapes from ruptured alveoli and dissects along the peribronchovascular sheaths toward the mediastinum [1]. From there, air may extend into the cervical fascial planes, producing subcutaneous emphysema, or, less commonly, spread into adjacent anatomical compartments, including the pleural space, pericardium, and epidural space [1], [6]. Although several cases of SPM associated with HG have been reported, the condition remains rare, and associated pneumorrhachis appears to be exceptionally uncommon [3], [4], [5]. The clinical presentation of SPM is often nonspecific and may include chest pain, dyspnea, cervical swelling, dysphonia, odynophagia, subcutaneous emphysema, or palpable crepitus, as observed in the present patient [3], [4]. Consequently, exclusion of esophageal injury is a critical component of the diagnostic evaluation. In this case, contrast esophagography demonstrated no evidence of perforation, supporting the diagnosis of SPM secondary to HG.

Imaging studies play a central role in diagnosis. While chest radiography may suggest the presence of mediastinal air, computed tomography remains the diagnostic modality of choice because it accurately defines the extent of air dissection, identifies associated complications, and helps exclude alternative diagnoses [3], [4]. In the present case, computed tomography confirmed extensive cervical and thoracic subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, small bilateral pneumothoraces, and pneumorrhachis.

Pneumorrhachis, defined as the presence of air within the spinal canal, is an exceptionally uncommon radiologic finding [6]. It is believed to occur when mediastinal air tracks through the neural foramina into the epidural space [6]. In the present patient, epidural air extended from C2 to T12, indicating extensive air dissection from the cervical region into the thoracic spinal canal. Although generally asymptomatic and self-limiting, its presence reflects extensive air extension beyond the mediastinum and may create diagnostic uncertainty because of its rarity. As shown in Table 1, most published cases of HG-associated pneumomediastinum have been managed conservatively with favorable outcomes following exclusion of esophageal perforation [3], [4], [5]. However, pneumorrhachis has been reported only rarely in this clinical setting [3], [4], [5], [6].

Russell et al. described a patient with refractory HG complicated by pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, trace bilateral pneumothoraces, extensive subcutaneous emphysema, and cervical-thoracic epidural air [5]. Similarly, the present patient exhibited extensive air dissemination beyond the mediastinum. However, unlike some previously reported severe cases that required pregnancy termination because of refractory symptoms or clinical deterioration, the patient experienced complete clinical recovery with conservative management and subsequently achieved a favorable obstetric outcome at term [3], [4], [5].

Management of SPM in the absence of esophageal injury is generally conservative and includes oxygen therapy when indicated, antiemetic treatment, intravenous hydration, correction of electrolyte abnormalities, analgesia, and close clinical observation [1], [3], [4]. In this case, correction of fluid and electrolyte abnormalities secondary to HG, together with multidisciplinary management, resulted in rapid clinical improvement and radiologic resolution, consistent with outcomes reported in previous cases [3], [4], [5].

Strengths of this report include detailed radiologic characterization, exclusion of esophageal perforation, and complete maternal and neonatal follow-up through delivery. The main limitation is the inherent inability of a single case report to establish causality or estimate the incidence of this rare complication.

This case highlights the need to consider SPM in pregnant women with HG who develop cervical swelling, chest symptoms, or subcutaneous emphysema. Early recognition, appropriate imaging, and exclusion of esophageal perforation are essential to avoid unnecessary invasive interventions [3], [4]. Given the small number of published reports, particularly those describing associated pneumorrhachis, this case adds further evidence that a favorable outcome can be expected when the condition is recognized and appropriately managed [5].

Conclusion

SPM should be considered in pregnant patients with severe HG who develop cervical swelling, subcutaneous emphysema, chest symptoms, or respiratory complaints. In this case, computed tomography demonstrated extensive air dissection involving the cervical soft tissues, mediastinum, bilateral pneumothoraces, and epidural space from C2 to T12, while esophageal perforation was successfully excluded. Despite the extent of these findings, conservative treatment resulted in complete clinical recovery and a favorable term pregnancy.

This report contributes to the limited literature describing HG-associated pneumomediastinum with pneumorrhachis. Early diagnosis, appropriate imaging, exclusion of life-threatening differential diagnoses, and multidisciplinary conservative management may lead to excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes, even in cases with extensive air dissemination.
