Molecular modulation of cholinergic and neuromuscular signaling following long-term acetaminophen administration
Authors: Jiwon Jeong, Eunji Park, Narae Kim, Taehee Lee, Dongsun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acetaminophen is widely used as an analgesic and antipyretic; however, its long-term neurochemical actions remain incompletely characterized. Its bioactive metabolite, N-arachidonoylphenolamine (AM404), activates transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1) and cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1), both of which are involved in the regulation of cholinergic signaling. This study investigated whether chronic acetaminophen exposure modulates cholinergic and neuromuscular-related molecular signaling associated with the TRPV1–CB1–acetylcholine (ACh) axis. Human neural stem cells and mouse myoblast cells were treated with AM404 (0–1.0 μg/mL), and Sprague–Dawley rats received oral acetaminophen (0, 10, 100, or 1000 mg/kg/day) for 4 weeks. In vitro, AM404 increased the expression of TRPV1, CB1, choline transporter (CHT), choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), and vesicular ACh transporter (VAChT) in F3 cells. Meanwhile, it upregulated CHRNA1, ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1), and sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺-ATPase 1 (SERCA1) in C2C12 cells. In vivo, chronic acetaminophen administration elevated TRPV1, CB1, CHT, ChAT, and VAChT in the brain and sciatic nerve, increased cerebrospinal ACh concentrations, and increased expression of CHRNA1, RyR1, and SERCA1 in skeletal muscle. Hepatotoxicity was observed only at the highest dose (1000 mg/kg), whereas at lower doses molecular changes in cholinergic and neuromuscular-related markers were observed without overt liver injury. These observations provide new insight into the neuromodulatory properties of acetaminophen and warrant further investigation in disease-relevant models.

Introduction

The global increase in life expectancy has led to a rapidly growing older adult population and a corresponding rise in the prevalence of neurodegenerative diseases. Conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis are major public health challenges because of their progressive course and substantial impact on cognitive and motor function [1, 2]. A common pathological feature of these disorders is the dysfunction or degeneration of cholinergic neurons, which are essential for memory formation, synaptic plasticity, and motor coordination [3]. Even during normal aging, cholinergic neurotransmission undergoes gradual decline, contributing to impairments in memory and muscle performance, which may increase vulnerability to neurodegenerative pathology [3, 4]. In addition, neuroinflammatory processes have been recognized as key contributors to age-related neuronal dysfunction and disease progression [5].

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are widely used to alleviate inflammation and pain, including symptoms associated with neurodegenerative conditions [6]. NSAIDs primarily exert their effects through inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and suppressing prostaglandin synthesis [7]. COX-2 and its downstream metabolites have been implicated in central synaptic regulation [8, 9]. Dysregulated COX activity has been associated with altered acetylcholine (ACh) availability, a neurotransmitter critically involved in cognitive and motor processes [10]. Certain COX inhibitors have also been reported to influence acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity [11, 12], suggesting potential interactions between anti-inflammatory signaling and cholinergic pathways [13]. Acetaminophen, although not classified as a traditional NSAID, has demonstrated neuroprotective properties in experimental models, including protection against amyloid-β-induced toxicity [14]. Thus, acetaminophen may modulate cholinergic signaling; however, its long-term molecular effects on cholinergic systems remain incompletely established [15, 16].

Beyond central neurotransmission, ACh plays an indispensable role in neuromuscular transmission. Effective muscle contraction requires coordinated ACh release at neuromuscular junctions (NMJs), activation of nicotinic Ach receptors, and tight regulation of Ca2⁺ handling within skeletal muscle fibers [17]. Age-related alterations in these pathways can contribute to reduced muscle strength and motor performance [18]. Acetaminophen has been reported to influence exercise tolerance, perceived exertion, and muscle activation [19–21]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying these observations, particularly regarding cholinergic and neuromuscular-related signaling pathways, remain unclear.

Acetaminophen has been reported to undergo metabolic conversion to N-arachidonoyl-phenolamine (AM404), a bioactive metabolite potentially formed through a fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH)–related process [22]. AM404 interacts with transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels and cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1), both of which are known to regulate neurotransmitter release and intracellular Ca2+ dynamics [23, 24]. Given the regulatory roles of TRPV1 and CB1 in neuronal and neuromuscular signaling, a TRPV1–CB1–ACh-associated pathway may participate in acetaminophen-induced molecular changes. However, whether long-term acetaminophen exposure modulates cholinergic and neuromuscular-related molecular signaling in association with this pathway has not been systematically examined.

In the present study, we investigated the effects of chronic acetaminophen administration on cholinergic signaling components and muscle contraction-related molecular markers in human neural stem cells, myoblasts, and rats. We further explored whether alterations in TRPV1–CB1–ACh signaling were associated with these molecular changes. By focusing on molecular endpoints rather than direct functional outcomes, this study aimed to clarify the neurochemical adaptations induced by prolonged acetaminophen exposure and to provide a framework for future investigations in disease-relevant models.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

Five-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were purchased from Daehan Biolink (Eumseong, Korea) and housed under controlled conditions at a constant temperature (22 ± 3 °C), relative humidity (50 ± 10%), and 12-h light/dark cycle. They were fed standard rodent chows and had access to purified water ad libitum. They were orally administered acetaminophen dissolved in 1% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC; Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 weeks after acclimatization to the laboratory environment for 1 week. The rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 7/group) based on the administered acetaminophen dose (0 [vehicle], 10, 100, and 1000 mg/kg/day). The Institutional animal care and use committee of the Korea National University of Education approved all the animal experiments (approval number: KNUE-202302-004-02).

AChE activity in the brain

The brains of 9-week-old Sprague–Dawley rats were immediately collected after transcardial perfusion with cold saline under anesthesia. They were homogenized in nine volumes of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The AChE activity was determined using a modified version of the method described by Ellman et al. in 1961 [23], The assay mixture (850 μL PBS) contained 33 μL of 15 mM acetylcholine iodide and 33 μL of 15 mM 5.5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic) acid. Subsequently, 100 μL of acetaminophen (1–1000 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and 50 μL of brain homogenate were added to the assay mixture. The changes in absorbance at 412 nm were monitored for 5 min using a spectrophotometer (SpectraMax M2; molecular devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The relative enzymatic activity was determined based on the change in absorbance, and it was expressed relative to the activity of the assay mixture containing 100 μL of PBS without acetaminophen, which was set at 100%.

Cell culture

F3 human neural stem and C2C12 mouse myoblast cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Biowest, Nuaille, Cholet, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biowest), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). They were maintained in tissue culture flasks at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The medium was changed every 2–3 days. The cells were grown to > 90% confluence and subjected to no more than 20 passages.

Histological findings

The rats were euthanized 24 h after the final treatment under ether anesthesia. Blood, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and tissue samples were collected. The brain, liver, sciatic nerve, soleus muscle, and gastrocnemius muscle were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded liver tissue sections. (4 µm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined under a light microscope.

Serum biochemistry

Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta at the time of euthanization. It was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min after storage for 30 min at 4 °C to facilitate coagulation. The serum concentrations of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were determined using a blood chemistry analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi Korea, Seoul, Korea).

ACh analysis in CSF

The ACh concentration in the CSF was determined using an amplex® red acetylcholine/acetylcholinesterase assay kit (Invitrogen). In this assay, AChE hydrolyzed ACh to release choline, which was subsequently oxidized to betaine and hydrogen peroxide by choline oxidase. Hydrogen peroxide subsequently reacted with amplex red (7-dihydroxyphenoxazine) in the presence of horseradish peroxidase to form the highly fluorescent compound resorufin. The fluorescence was quantified using a fluorescence microplate reader (SpectraMax M2) with excitation at 530–560 nm and emission detection at 590 nm.

Western blot assay

The cells and tissues were homogenized using RIPA cell lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) containing proteinase and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich). The proteins were extracted through centrifuging the homogenates at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). They were then denatured through heating at 95 °C for 5 min in a 0.5 M Tris–HCl buffer (pH 6.8) containing 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). Subsequently, they were separated via electrophoresis on an SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and transferred to a poly (vinylidene fluoride) membrane using 25 mM Tris buffer containing 20% methanol, 1% SDS, and 192 mM glycine. After washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, and developed using an ECL solution (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Statistical analysis

The normality and homogeneity of variances were ascertained using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. The groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance and post-hoc verification with Tukey's multiple comparisons test. All analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 12.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that chronic low-dose acetaminophen is associated with molecular alterations in cholinergic and neuromuscular-related signaling pathways. Rather than directly establishing functional enhancement, the present findings demonstrated coordinated changes in TRPV1-CB1-ACh-related molecular markers following prolonged exposure. These observations expand the understanding of acetaminophen’s neurochemical profile beyond its classical analgesic and antipyretic actions. The bioactive metabolite AM404 was associated with the upregulation of cholinergic and calcium-regulating proteins in neural stem cells, myoblasts, and rat tissues, indicating a potential mechanistic link between acetaminophen metabolism and cholinergic-related molecular modulation.

In vitro, AM404 increased the expression of TRPV1, CB1, CHT, ChAT, and VAChT in F3 neural stem cells, indicating coordinated regulation of cholinergic biosynthetic components. Parallel increases in CHRNA1, RyR1, and SERCA1 in C2C12 cells indicate alterations in molecular markers associated with neuromuscular coupling and calcium handling. In vivo, chronic acetaminophen administration was accompanied by elevated expression of cholinergic markers in the brain and sciatic nerve, increased cerebrospinal ACh concentrations, and increased contraction-related proteins in skeletal muscles at doses that did not produce overt hepatotoxicity.

Although acetaminophen has been widely used for decades, emerging studies have implied potential neuroprotective or cognitive effects in experimental models [15, 16]. In the present study, acetaminophen did not exhibit a clear dose-dependent inhibition of AChE activity in vitro. In contrast, the observed cholinergic-related molecular changes may be associated with metabolic conversion to AM404. Because AM404 upregulated TRPV1 and CB1 expression in vitro, a possible interaction between acetaminophen metabolism and TRPV1-CB1 signaling may contribute to the observed molecular alterations [25]. However, direct quantification of AM404 and functional inhibition studies were not performed; therefore causality cannot be established.

Acetaminophen metabolism primarily involves CYP450-dependent oxidation leading to NAPQI formation and hepatotoxicity at high doses, whereas alternative metabolic routes have been proposed to generate AM404 [22, 26, 27]. In the present study, only the highest dose (1000 mg/kg) produced hepatic injury, consistent with established toxicity mechanisms. At lower doses, molecular changes in cholinergic and neuromuscular-related markers were observed without overt liver damage [22, 28, 29]. Because TRPV1 and CB1 receptors regulate neurotransmitter release and calcium dynamics [30–32], alterations in these signaling components may influence cholinergic tone in both central and peripheral tissues. Nevertheless, the extent to which AM404 contributes to these changes is yet to be clarified [33].

The increases in CSF ACh concentrations and the upregulation of CHT, ChAT, and VAChT indicate coordinated regulation of ACh synthesis and vesicular transport. Similarly, elevated CHRNA1, RyR1, and SERCA1 expression in the skeletal muscles reflects modulation of proteins involved in neuromuscular signaling and calcium cycling. RyR1 mediates calcium release during contraction, whereas SERCA1 facilitates calcium reuptake during relaxation [34–36]. While these molecular changes are consistent with pathways relevant to muscle contractility [37, 38], direct functional assessments were not conducted in this study.

Importantly, this study was performed in healthy rats and was not designed to model chronic neurodegenerative disease progression. The 4-week exposure period likely reflects subchronic molecular adaptation rather than disease-modifying effects. Future investigations incorporating disease models, AM404 quantification, FAAH inhibition strategies, and functional neuromuscular assessments will be required to clarify the physiological significance of these molecular alterations.

In conclusion, chronic acetaminophen administration was associated with coordinated molecular changes in cholinergic and neuromuscular related signaling pathways. These findings broaden the understanding of acetaminophen’s neurochemical effects and provide a foundation for further mechanistic studies.
