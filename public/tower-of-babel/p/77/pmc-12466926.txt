Engineered BSA nanoparticles: Synthesis, drug loading, and advanced characterization
Authors: Hemlata, A Hariharan, Nandan Murali, Srabaita Roy, Soutik Betal, Saran Kumar, Shilpi Minocha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovine serum albumin (BSA) nanoparticles have attracted a lot of interest as biocompatible and biodegradable carriers for a range of pharmacological and biological uses. BSA nanoparticles have several advantages over other types of nanoparticles, including their ability to increase the stability and solubility of encapsulated drugs, their non-toxicity, and their ease of surface modification. Cancer treatment, immunological modulation, enzyme immobilization, controlled release systems, bioimaging, and theranostics are some of its potential applications. This protocol offers a detailed and accessible methodology for the synthesis, drug encapsulation, and characterization of albumin nanoparticles, with particular emphasis on reproducibility and adaptability. The synthesis uses the desolvation process and crosslinking with the compound glutaraldehyde for stability. The crosslinking ratio, pH, and BSA content are important factors that can be adjusted to control size, surface charge, and dispersity. The methods used for characterization are described in detail, including dynamic light scattering for particle size and zeta potential, transmission and scanning electron microscopy for morphology, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and nanoparticle tracking analysis for stability assessment. The stability of the nanoparticles was evaluated under physiologically relevant ionic and pH conditions by dispersing them in phosphate-buffered saline, providing insight into their colloidal behavior in a simulated physiological environment. This technique facilitates the design of functionalized BSA nanoparticles for certain biomedical and therapeutic applications by acting as a fundamental reference for researchers. This work promotes innovation in nanoparticle-based technology and advances the field by standardizing preparation and characterization techniques.Keywords: bovine serum albumin nanoparticles (BSA NPs), desolvation method, nanoparticle characterization, controlled drug release

Introduction

Nanotechnology has revolutionized the fields of drug delivery, diagnostics, and biomaterials by offering innovative solutions for targeted and controlled therapeutic applications [1–3]. Among the various nanoparticle platforms, protein-based nanoparticles have emerged as a highly promising class due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and functional versatility [4]. Bovine serum albumin (BSA), a naturally occurring globular protein, has been extensively utilized as a matrix for the synthesis of nanoparticles owing to its availability, non-toxic nature, and intrinsic properties, which include its natural biocompatibility and non-immunogenicity, its abundance of functional groups (like amino and carboxyl groups) that facilitate effective drug conjugation, its capacity to bind a variety of hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs through reversible interactions, and its advantageous pharmacokinetics, which include a long circulation half-life that support drug loading and delivery [5–8].

BSA, derived from bovine blood plasma, is an inexpensive and stable protein with a well-characterized structure comprising multiple binding sites that facilitate the encapsulation or conjugation of various therapeutic agents, including small molecules, proteins, and genetic materials [9, 10]. Its surface is rich in functional groups, such as amino and carboxyl groups, which allow for chemical modifications, making it an adaptable carrier for diverse biomedical applications. Additionally, BSA exhibits excellent water solubility, high stability under physiological conditions, and low immunogenicity, making it a preferred material for nanoparticle-based systems [10, 11]. This protocol is based on methodologies developed in our primary research manuscript, currently under revision in journal “Materials and Design,” which investigates the synthesis and characterization of doxorubicin-loaded BSA nanoparticles for drug delivery applications.

The synthesis of BSA nanoparticles can be achieved through various methods, such as desolvation, emulsification, coacervation, and thermal gelation [12–14]. Among these, the desolvation method is widely favored for its simplicity, reproducibility, and scalability [14–17]. This technique involves the gradual addition of a desolvating agent, such as ethanol or acetone, to a BSA solution, leading to nanoparticle formation through protein aggregation [18]. Crosslinking agents, such as glutaraldehyde, are subsequently used to stabilize the nanoparticles by creating covalent bonds between protein molecules. Key parameters, including pH, protein concentration, desolvating agent type, and crosslinker ratio, significantly influence the physicochemical properties of the resulting nanoparticles, such as size, surface charge, and morphology [18–20].

A critical aspect of BSA nanoparticle development is their characterization to ensure consistency and suitability for intended applications. Techniques such as dynamic light scattering (DLS) provide insights into particle size and zeta potential, while transmission and scanning electron microscopy (TEM and SEM) reveal morphological details. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) can be employed to verify the chemical structure and confirm crosslinking. Additionally, stability studies were done using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to mimic physiological ionic strength and pH, which are essential to predict nanoparticle behavior in a biological environment.

This article provides a detailed protocol for the preparation and characterization of blank BSA nanoparticles, focusing on the desolvation method and subsequent analytical techniques. By adding the medicine during the desolvation stage, this basic technique may be easily modified for the encapsulation of other medicinal agents, both hydrophilic and hydrophobic. Because of the protocol’s excellent batch-to-batch consistency, it may be used for preclinical drug delivery system development employing BSA as a biodegradable and biocompatible carrier in addition to basic nanoparticle characterization. It also discusses the key advantages and applications of BSA nanoparticles in biomedical research, including their potential for targeted drug delivery, controlled release, bioimaging, and theranostic applications. By offering a standardized methodology and exploring the multifunctional capabilities of BSA nanoparticles, this article aims to serve as a valuable resource for researchers working in nanotechnology, drug delivery, and related fields while contributing to the broader understanding of protein-based nanocarriers.

Results and discussion

Preparation of BSA nanoparticles

BSA nanoparticles were synthesized using the desolvation method, where ethanol was added dropwise to an aqueous BSA solution under controlled stirring conditions. The production of nanoparticles is a slow process that starts with the addition of ethanol and advances with apparent turbidity as protein aggregation takes place, as seen by the solution’s opalescent look. The desolvation process, ethanol addition rate, and pH of the solution were optimized to achieve uniform particle size and minimize aggregation [18, 23, 24]. The nanoparticles were crosslinked using glutaraldehyde to enhance structural integrity.

Characterization of BSA nanoparticles

Particle size, PDI, and zeta potential measurements

The hydrodynamic diameter and PDI of BSA nanoparticles were analyzed using DLS. The hydrodynamic diameter of blank BSA nanoparticles was 154 nm with a PDI of 0.067, while the drug-loaded BSA nanoparticles exhibited a size of 202.3 nm with a PDI of approximately 0.281. These values indicate a narrow size distribution and good uniformity of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1A and C) [25, 26]. The ethanol addition rate, BSA concentration, and stirring speed influenced the particle size and PDI. A slower ethanol addition rate produced smaller, more uniform particles [10].

(B) Zeta potential distribution of blank BSA nanoparticles, measured at −36.9 mV, suggesting good colloidal stability in DI. (C) Size distribution of doxorubicin-loaded BSA nanoparticles, showing an increased Z-average diameter of 202.3 nm and a higher PDI of 0.281, suggesting a broader size distribution upon drug loading. Both formulations exhibit a single peak, confirming uniform nanoparticle dispersion in DI. (D) Zeta potential of DOX-loaded BSA NPs reduced to −26.4 mV, likely due to surface interaction with doxorubicin, while still maintaining sufficient stability.

Zeta potential measurements showed that blank BSA NPs had a surface charge of −36.9 mV (Fig. 1B), suggesting strong electrostatic repulsion among particles, which contributes to high colloidal stability. In contrast, the DOX-loaded BSA NPs showed a reduced zeta potential of −26.4 mV (Fig. 1D). The decrease in surface charge may be attributed to partial neutralization of the surface carboxyl groups by DOX or interaction of DOX with the protein matrix, slightly compromising surface charge density. However, the zeta potential remains sufficiently negative to ensure stability in suspension.

The smooth and spherical morphology is advantageous for applications like drug delivery, as it minimizes non-specific interactions and improves circulation stability.

FTIR measurements

The FTIR spectra confirm the successful encapsulation of DOX within BSA nanoparticles, demonstrating the strong interactions between the drug and the BSA NPs (Fig. 6). The Amide I band, originating from the C = O stretching vibrations of the peptide backbone, appeared at 1666/cm for pure BSA nanoparticles. Upon DOX loading, this peak exhibited a slight shift to 1663/cm in the DOX@BSA nanoparticles, indicating the non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions, between DOX and the BSA matrix. Additionally, the Amide II band, associated with N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations, was observed at 1546/cm for BSA nanoparticles. In the DOX-loaded NPs, a minor shift to 1547/cm was observed, suggesting that minor changes in the protein’s environment, indicating its secondary structure, are largely retained. Furthermore, the appearance of broadband around 3439/cm in the BSA nanoparticle spectrum, corresponding to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, shifted to 3483/cm after DOX loading [27]. This further confirms modifications in the hydrogen bonding environment because of drug encapsulation.

EE of BSA nanoparticles

EE is a critical parameter in nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems as it determines the percentage of drug successfully loaded into the nanoparticles relative to the total drug used during synthesis. The concentration of free drug is ascertained by comparing the absorbance at the drug’s characteristic wavelength with a standard calibration curve, from which the quantity encapsulated is computed. The EE of BSA NPs for the doxorubicin drug was found to be more than 85% (Table 1). Several factors can influence the EE. Low EE values may result from poor drug entrapment, which can be mitigated by optimizing the BSA-to-drug ratio, adjusting pH, or increasing crosslinking. High free drug concentration in the supernatant may indicate weak drug–nanoparticle interactions, which may be improved by modifying solvent systems or synthesis conditions. Inconsistencies in EE measurements can arise due to nanoparticle aggregation or incomplete drug encapsulation, which can be addressed by sonication and optimizing synthesis conditions. This protocol provides a comprehensive and reproducible approach for measuring EE in BSA nanoparticles. High EE (>85%) indicates effective drug loading and stability, making the formulation suitable for drug delivery applications.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully synthesized and optimized BSA nanoparticles using the desolvation method for the encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs. The uniformly sized, spherical DOX@BSA NPs were confirmed through DLS, NTA, SEM, TEM, and AFM and their drug loading efficiency was confirmed using UV–Vis spectrophotometry, which demonstrated high EE (Table 1). The resulting nanoparticles exhibited desirable size distribution, surface charge, and morphology suitable for biomedical applications. These findings establish a robust protocol for the preparation and characterization of drug-loaded BSA nanoparticles, offering a promising platform for the delivery of hydrophobic therapeutics.
