Evaluation of the influence of freeze–thaw cycles on the joint strength of granite in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau, China
Authors: Lirong Qi, Jiading Wang, Dengfei Zhang, Yongshuang Zhang, Jianfei Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe freeze–thaw cycles will lead to rock deterioration and pose a significant threat to engineering stability in cold regions. In this study, granite samples collected from the Luanshibao Landslide in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau were subjected to a maximum of 120 freeze–thaw cycles at two temperature paths (− 10–20 °C and − 20–20 °C). The Brazilian test, uniaxial and triaxial compression tests were carried out to evaluate the strength behavior of samples while the scanning electron microscopy tests to investigate microstructural changes. The correlation between strength be-havior and microstructural evolution of samples after freeze–thaw cycles was discussed. Results indicate that the uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, tensile strength, and peak strength of samples decreased nonlinearly with freeze–thaw cycles. Besides, freeze–thaw cycles exhibit more pronounced effect on the strength of samples, compared to the temperature path. Based on the Mohr–Coulomb criterion, a joint strength expression was proposed for the tensile, compressive, and shear strengths of granite, which characterizes the influence of freeze–thaw cycles and strength paths on strength behavior of granite samples. SEM images revealed the freeze–thaw damage to the microstructure of granite and further the deterioration of the mechanical properties. The results of this study can provide a valuable reference for assessing the freeze–thaw strength of granite in the context of construction in highland areas, guiding the development of more resilient engineering practices and informing future research on the long-term durability of materials in extreme climates.Keywords: Freeze–thaw cycle, Granite, Joint strength, Rock damageSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Engineering construction in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau plays a strategic role in the social and economic development in China. The Tibetan plateau is the most tectonically active, climate sensitive, and geomorphologically diverse region in the world, which is why engineering construction in the region poses many major scientific and engineering problems1–3. The coupling of temperature and stress fields triggered by freeze–thaw cycles leads to the deterioration of physical and mechanical properties of rock masses, which indirectly induce geohazards such as landslides and collapses4–7. For example, Chiarle et al. deduced that rock slope stability at high altitudes has become more sensitive to climate change in the last decade8. Gunzburger et al. observed the effect of prolonged periods of freeze–thaw cycles on the collapse of rock slopes in Valabres, France, a region known for its complex geological structure9. Matsuoka N indicated that seasonal freeze and thaw accelerate the weathering of hard rocks in Alpine regions10. Paranunzio et al. deduced from a statistical analysis of 41 rockfall events in 12 high-altitude regions in the Italian Alps between 1997 and 2013 that abrupt temperature change accounts for 17 forms of slope failure, underscoring the multifaceted impacts of temperature fluctuations on geological stability11.

Rocks in highland regions are susceptible to freeze–thaw damage12, which is essentially a fatigue evolution process induced by repeated compression-extension loads acting on the intrinsic defects in the rocks. Specifically, the inhomogeneity in the composition of the rock minerals causes expansion and contraction of rocks during freeze–thaw cycles, which concentrates internal forces and fractures13–15. Additionally, water in the crack of the rock freezes and expands, resulting in the expansion of internal fractures and the creation of new fractures16. The combined effect of these phenomena leads to the deterioration of the rock structure, thereby providing favorable conditions for the formation and evolution of geohazards.

Extensive studies have been conducted on the macro-mechanical properties, microstructural evolution, and multiscale damage features of rocks after freeze–thaw cycles. In macroscopic scale, attention was paid to how freeze–thaw cycling affect the change of mechanical indexes such as compressive strength, elastic modulus, shear strength, dynamic strength and dynamic modulus across various lithologies, porosities and water contents, resulting in an in-depth understanding of intrinsic damage mechanisms and the development of evaluation models17–23. In microscopic scale, computed tomography (CT) was effectively used to identify and analyze the structural damage of rocks24–27. Besides, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was applied in both qualitative and quantitative studies to reveal the evolution of rock microcracks (micropores) with freeze–thaw cycles28–32. In addition, the applicability of the acoustic emission (AE) and the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) has also been proved. The NMR method, which obtains two-dimensional pore distribution images, provide valuable insights into the freeze–thaw effect on the microstructure and mechanical properties of rocks33–36. AE enables the tracking of the effect of freeze–thaw cycles on the structural integrity of rocks through the monitoring of AE activities and the dynamic response of rocks37–39. SEM is utilized in this work because of its capacity for high-resolution surface imaging. Crack formation and features, mineral dissolution, and other microstructural changes can be detected and further provide insights into the mechanism for the variation in the granite’s macroscopic behavior.

Rocks are normally in a compressed state, and rock damage is commonly understood to be compression-shear failure, in which shear force primarily causes rock grains to slide over the damaged surface40. Nevertheless, damage appears differently in rocks affected by freeze–thaw cycles, especially those that are 50 cm or less below the surface41,42. In some high-locality landslides on the Eastern Tibetan Plateau, cracks extending up to 200 m are often observed along the edges, indicating a combination of shear and tensile failure. Identifying the failure state becomes particularly challenging under the influence of repeated freeze–thaw cycles and complex stress conditions43. In high-locality landslides, the failure mechanism typically involves a gradual progression of compression-shear failure in the lower slope and tension-shear coupling failure in the upper slope. While many current slope stability studies simplify the tension-shear coupling strength of rock masses using linear methods, experimental results reveal significant nonlinear strength characteristics under tension-shear stress44,45. Consequently, linear strength theories fail to fully capture the tension-shear failure process in the upper slopes. To better describe slope instability, it is crucial to adopt a joint strength model that incorporates both tension-shear and compression-shear mechanisms46. Most existing research on freeze–thaw conditions in rocks focuses primarily on compression or shear characteristics, while studies on the coupled tension–compression–shear (T–C–S) state remain relatively limited. This becomes particularly important in complex stress environments, such as those involving repeated freeze–thaw cycles, where the stress state of rock masses requires careful evaluation. Developing a T–C–S joint strength model is crucial for evaluating the stress state of slopes under long-term freeze–thaw conditions, especially in high-locality landslides.

The main objectives of this research are to investigate the effects of freeze–thaw cycles on the mechanical and microstructural properties of granite from the Luanshibao Landslide in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau. This study employs uniaxial compression, Brazilian splitting, and triaxial compression tests, as well as SEM analysis, to evaluate both macroscopic and microscopic changes in the granite subjected to freeze–thaw cycles. Based on these findings, a joint strength model was developed to describe the T–C–S behavior of granite under varying freeze–thaw conditions. This model is crucial for accurately capturing the complex stress interactions in freeze–thaw environments, providing a more reliable foundation for evaluating rock slope stability. The results aim to improve understanding of granite’s freeze–thaw resistance and offer valuable insights for assessing rock slope stability in cold regions.

Materials and methods

Test material

The Luanshibao Landslide is located in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau, with a landslide volume of about 8.5 × 107 m3, covering an area of 4.25 km2 (Fig. 1)40. The rock at the source of the Luanshibao Landslide is primarily granite. Due to the freeze–thaw activity at high altitude, the granite has undergone intense mechanical weathering47. In this study, the fresh intact rock samples were collected at the slip zone site and transported to the State Key Laboratory of Continental Dynamics for testing.

Freeze–thaw pretreatment of test material

The boiling method was first used to remove air from the granite samples, i.e., boiled for 6 h and then cooled to room temperature (20 °C). The vacuum pumping method was the utilized to obtain a fully saturated sample with a vacuum pressure of − 0.1 MPa for 3 h. The saturated sample was then placed in a low-temperature test chamber for freeze–thaw cycling. The freeze–thaw cycles were configured based on local historical average temperature changes, with two temperature paths of − 10–20 °C and − 20–20 °C. Each cycle lasted 8 h, consisting of a cooling phase to the preset temperature of − 10 °C or − 20 °C for 4 h, followed by a thawing phase in a 20 °C insulated water bucket for 4 h. The samples were categorized into seven groups, each undergoing a distinct number of freeze–thaw cycles (N = 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120). The mass of each sample was recorded after each cycle. The mass change of the granite samples after freeze–thaw ranges from 0.09 to 0.17%, which may be tentatively neglected for its effect on the mechanical properties. The tests, as shown in Fig. 3, were carried out on samples exposed to different freeze–thaw cycles, including the Brazilian test, uniaxial compression, and triaxial compression tests, as well as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tests.

Brazilian tests

The Brazilian test method was applied to indirectly obtain the tensile strength of granite samples, which is widely recognized for its effectiveness in assessing tensile properties of rocks49. This method is to apply a pair of linear loads in the direction of the diameter of the cylindrical rock sample, when the horizontal normal stress on the diameter of the rock is in tension. Since the compressive strength of the rock is far greater than the tensile strength, which allows the rock in the testing machine is not compressive failure, but tensile failure. The tensile strength can thus be determined from the axial pressure when rock failure.

The Brazilian tests were conducted by a microcomputer-controlled uniaxial testing machine with a maximum load of 2000 kN and an accuracy of ± 1%. The samples were positioned between the upper and lower bearing plates of the test machine to guarantee uniform radial pressurization. The displacement-based loading approach was employed with a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min. The test data can be automatically recorded by the data acquisition system throughout the test.

Uniaxial compression tests

The uniaxial compression tests were also conducted on the microcomputer-controlled uniaxial testing machine with the stress-control mode. Samples subjected to various freeze–thaw cycles were mounted on the testing machine, where the axial load was applied incrementally at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min. This methodology allowed for a systematic assessment of the samples’ response to the applied forces. The test proceeded until the failure of the sample, as characterized by the sudden release of the axial load. The test data was recorded by the data acquisition system at an interval of 0.5 s. This precise approach provided a comprehensive dataset for detailed analysis.

Triaxial compression tests

The triaxial compression tests were carried out on the TAW-1000 rock triaxial testing machine. This apparatus is equipped with both servo and temperature control systems, capable of handling maximum loads up to 1000 kN and confining pressures up to 100 MPa. Its extended operational capability reaches 1000 h. It also includes a deformation measurement system, recording axial deformations up to 8 mm and radial deformations within a range of 4 mm, suitable for rock samples of dimensions up to 50 mm × 100 mm. For the triaxial tests, a confining pressure of 5 MPa was applied considering the practical stress state of the granite samples in the Luanshibao Landslide. The displacement-based loading mode was utilized at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min. Test data was acquired at an interval of 1 s, which well facilitates the analysis of peak strength and stress–strain curves of the granite after freeze–thaw cycles.

SEM tests

In order to investigate the evolution of the micropore structure in granite with freeze–thaw cycles, SEM tests were conducted by a Quanta 450 FEG scanning electron microscope. It can reach a maximum magnification of one million times and a minimum scanning image resolution of 3.5 nm. The aim of the study was to investigate the microscopic characteristics of the granite samples after freeze–thaw cycles, with an emphasis on how the micropore structure of the samples evolved. Cylindrical samples, 50 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height, were used for SEM tests. The granite sample after each freeze–thaw cycle were scanned at 400× magnification to obtain images.

Results and analysis

Effect of freeze–thaw cycles on the uniaxial compressive strength of granite samples

Figure 4 illustrates the uniaxial compression stress–strain curves and failure images of granite subjected to various freeze–thaw cycles. Interestingly, the axial strain varies gently while the transverse strain rises, which indicates a visible strain-softening trend. As seen from the stress–strain curve patterns in Fig. 4a,b, freeze–thaw cycles exhibit a pronounced effect on the uniaxial compression behavior of granite samples. In detail, the stress–strain curve shows a distinct downward and rightward shift with freeze–thaw cycles, corresponding to a rise in the axial strain at the onset and conclusion of yielding, coupled with a reduction in the compressive strength. It suggests the progressive development of fractures and pores within the granite sample with freeze–thaw cycles, leading to increased porosity and extended compressive and yielding stages. Further analysis of the uniaxial compression failure mode through stress–strain curves reveals distinct responses of granite to increasing freeze–thaw cycles. During the loading phase, the rock undergoes compression and closure of internal fractures, transitioning from elastic to plastic deformation. The observed decrease in uniaxial compressive strength with freeze–thaw cycles underscores the impact of cumulative damage to the granite sample.

Effect of freeze–thaw cycles on the uniaxial tensile strength of granite samples

In general, the compressive strength of rock materials is much greater than the tensile strength, and in most cases, the damage behavior of engineering rock masses indicates their tensile damage characteristics52. The tensile strength is an important index to evaluate the mechanical properties of rock masses, while the tensile damage is the main form of damage to rock masses. In cold regions with frequent freeze–thaw cycles, a critical aspect of rock engineering construction is to understand the tensile strength of the rock materials.

The typical stress–strain curves of the entire process of splitting damage of granite under considered freeze–thaw cycles and temperature paths are shown in Fig. 6. The curve pattern under uniaxial tensile condition was similar to that of uniaxial compression, which shows multi-stage variation. In the initial loading stage, the granite sample exhibits compression in the stress–strain curve; with the increase of stress, only some cells in the middle part of the sample experienced tensile damage, which were distributed around the middle of the sample, due to the non-uniformity of the material. Due to the small number of micro-element ruptures, the energy released by the damage of these units was small; therefore, the stress–strain curve of the whole sample remained linear, and the macroscopic behavior of the rock was mainly reflected as elastic deformation. With the increase of external load, these damaged units developed into tensile cracks and penetrated each other (these cracks were called main cracks), leading to the splitting damage of the whole sample, as indicated by stress drop on the stress–strain curve. During tensile damage, macroscopic discontinuities appeared in the rock structure, a phenomenon that indicates the formation of a new structural surface and a corresponding adjustment of the stress state, which is a typical structural nonlinearity problem. The fine nonuniformity of material properties and the randomness of defect distribution were the root causes of crack generation within the rock and nonlinearity in the macroscopic stress–strain curve.

Uniaxial tensile stress–strain curves of granite samples subjected to freeze–thaw cycles.

The above stress–strain curves also reflect the pronounced effect of freeze–thaw cycles on the splitting damage of granite samples. As the number of freeze–thaw cycles increases, the axial strains at both the initial and final stages of yielding decrease, along with a reduction in compressive strength. This behavior differs from the typical stress–strain curve observed in uniaxial compression.

The damage patterns of the granite sample in the Brazil test are shown in Fig. 7. Tension-type cracks were clearly observed, with most of the damaged surfaces running longitudinally through the centerline of the section and splitting into two half discs along the radial direction. The splitting surfaces were almost in one plane. Some of the samples manifest secondary cracks close to the intersection of the platform and the outer surface arc. After more freeze–thaw cycles, only a few samples show damage zones near the loading point, and the damaged area was small.

Tensile damage patterns of the granite sample subjected to freeze–thaw cycles.

The uniaxial tensile strength of the granite sample decreases nonlinearly with freeze–thaw cycles, like the uniaxial compressive strength while the decrement in uniaxial tensile strength is rather small. Under uniaxial tensile stress, internal pores and fractures within the rock tend to open, in contrast to that in uniaxial compressive stress state under which they are more likely to close. In natural rock formations, the gravitational stress causes pore water to infiltrate downward. The freeze–thaw process exacerbates the longitudinal expansion of rock fractures, thereby intensifying the longitudinal damage due to frost heave stress.

Furthermore, the differences in failure pattern observed in uniaxial compression and uniaxial tensile tests can be attributed to the inherent anisotropy and structural complexities of the rock samples. During compression tests, the rock is subjected to stress along a single axis, leading to a response that reveals the material’s overall compressive strength. Conversely, in Brazilian splitting tests, tensile forces are applied perpendicular to the loading axis, leading to a distinct failure that highlights the material’s tensile strength. This anisotropic response accounts for the difference in the mechanical behavior of the granite after freeze–thaw cycles observed in uniaxial tensile and compressive tests.

The triaxial compressive stress–strain curves of granite samples at two temperature paths are shown in Fig. 8, with the test data at N = 0, 60 and 120 as the example. A trend emerged where, with lower ambient temperature for freezing, the stress–strain curve of the granite sample shifted downward, suggesting a decrease in peak strength and an increase in the corresponding peak strain. This trend suggests that lower ambient temperature for freezing induces larger frost heave stress, thereby exacerbating freeze–thaw damage. The changes in internal fractures and pores of the granite were thus enhanced, leading to a larger decrement in the strength of the granite. However, when comparing the effect of freeze–thaw cycles to that of the temperature path on the stress–strain curves of granite samples (Figs. 8 and 9), the former was found to be more pronounced on rock deterioration.

Stress–strain curves of granite samples at two temperature paths.

In contrast to the damage pattern observed in uniaxial compression, the influence of both the freeze–thaw cycles and temperature paths on triaxial compression were comparatively minimal. During triaxial compression tests, the development and penetration of internal fractures in the rock led to the formation of a well-defined primary shear damage surface. Notably, this primary damage surface was characterized by two intersecting shear planes, forming a conjugate shear structure resembling an “X” type pattern. These observations indicate that the fracture pattern and damage characteristics in triaxial compression are distinct from those in uniaxial compression. The identification of this “X” type conjugate shear structure is pivotal in understanding the mechanical behavior and stability of rocks under complex environmental conditions. Further studies could delve into the mechanisms and properties associated with this unique shear structure, providing in-depth insights into the behavior of rocks under varying stress conditions.

Tensile-compressive-shear strength properties of granite after freeze–thaw cycles

Based on the peak strengths obtained from the Brazilian test, uniaxial compression test, and triaxial compression tests, the tensile–compressive–shear (T-C-S) Mohr–Coulomb (M-C) strength envelope was constructed for granite under various freeze–thaw conditions. Assuming a linear strength envelope, the shear strength parameters (cohesion c and internal friction angle φ) and the theoretical tensile strength (σt) of granite were determined. This was achieved through linear backward extrapolation of the T–C–S M-C strength envelope (represented as a dashed line) to its intersection with the horizontal axis, thereby obtaining the corresponding theoretical tensile strength values (Fig. 10). A notable discrepancy was observed between the empirically measured tensile strength and the theoretical predictions based on the linear extension of the M-C strength envelope. This suggests that using the extended M-C strength envelope to predict the tensile failure of rock may overestimate or underestimate the tensile strength of granite and the strength under tensile-shear coupled stress condition. However, in engineering applications, rocks are often simultaneously subjected to tensile-compressive–shear coupled stress, such as in high-elevation long-runout landslides where the top is affected by coupled tensile and shear stresses, resulting in rocks being thrown far distances under earthquakes or blasting. Therefore, it is unreasonable to completely neglect the tensile failure of rocks. The issue of rock strength under coupled T–C–S conditions should be paid adequate attention.

While the shear strength of granite under combined T–C–S stress aligns with the M-C strength criterion, it is important to recognize that the failure mechanism of rock under tension is not solely characterized by simple tensile failure but involves a combination of tensile and shear stresses. Thus, the rock’s tensile strength should not be considered as merely the intersection of the extended M-C envelope with the σ-axis, and the failure envelope of granite is not a straight line. In stress states involving tensile stress, the M-C strength envelope in the tension zone should be a smooth curve. Discussion

This study investigated the influence of freeze–thaw cycles on the macroscopic mechanical properties and microstructure of granite derived from Luanshibao landslide. A joint strength envelope is proposed to characterize the tensile, compressive, and shear properties of granite after freeze–thaw cycles. The observed reduction in granite strength and durability following freeze–thaw cycles aligns with previous findings in other rock types57,58. However, some limitations are worth further research:

Wide variety of rock types: the study is based on mechanical test results of granite from Luanshibao landslide in the Eastern Tibetan Plateau, yielding general patterns of granite freeze–thaw damage. Nonetheless, there is a wide variety of rock types in the Tibetan Plateau, differing in mineral composition and structural features, leading to variations in their freeze–thaw resistance and mechanical properties. For instance, common rock types in the Tibetan Plateau include granite, sandstone, shale, and limestone, exhibiting significant differences in mineral composition, grain size, and cementation, thereby influencing their freeze–thaw resistance and mechanical properties. Future research could integrate common rock types in the Tibetan Plateau to conduct more comprehensive studies aiming for broader conclusions.

Limitations of macro- and micro-scale studies: This study combines macroscopic and microscopic approaches to investigate the impact of freeze–thaw cycles on the mechanical properties of granite. However, it only focuses on a specific type of granite composition, such as the common granites dominated by quartz, feldspar, and mica. In reality, granite compositions can be highly complex, potentially containing other minerals such as pyroxene, amphibole, and olivine alongside the main minerals mentioned above, which could affect granite’s freeze–thaw resistance and mechanical properties. Additionally, this study primarily focuses on changes in macroscopic mechanical parameters like compressive strength and elastic modulus, with relatively limited exploration of rock mineral damage characteristics during the freeze–thaw process. Subsequent studies could investigate freeze–thaw damage behavior across granite compositions, integrating macroscopic and microscopic approaches to delve into the influence mechanisms of freeze–thaw cycles on granite’s mechanical properties concerning mineral content.

Limitations of test methods: This study employs indoor low-temperature freeze–thaw box simulations to mimic freeze–thaw cycle environments and assess rock’s physical–mechanical properties59. While this method allows for controlled experimental conditions, disparities still exist compared to natural environments. For instance, natural freeze–thaw cycles often involve influences from factors like wind speed, radiation, snow cover, and rainfall, which could impact rock’s freeze–thaw damage behavior. Future research could refine test methods to closely simulate natural freeze–thaw processes to obtain more practical results. For example, establishing outdoor freeze–thaw test sites for long-term rock observations could provide more comprehensive data.

Overall, this study investigates the macroscopic mechanical properties and microstructure of granite under freeze–thaw conditions, proposing a joint strength model to describe granite’s tensile-compressive-shear properties considering freeze–thaw factors. Despite its limitations, this research is vital for infrastructure development in the Tibetan Plateau region, highlighting the necessity for in-depth studies tailored to specific areas60,61. In regions prone to frequent geological disasters in the Tibetan Plateau, understanding the mechanical properties of different rocks under freeze–thaw cycles can aid in better evaluating potential risks of freeze–thaw-induced geological disasters62, providing scientific foundations for disaster prevention and safe railway operations.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of freeze–thaw cycles on the microstructure and mechanical properties of granite from the Eastern Tibetan Plateau through a combination of on-site geological surveys, macroscopic and microscopic indoor tests, and theoretical calculations. The following key conclusions were drawn:

Uniaxial compression tests revealed that freeze–thaw cycles significantly affect the stress–strain behavior of granite, displaying a distinct four-stage pattern. The number of freeze–thaw cycles had a more pronounced impact on compressive strength than freezing temperature. Quantitative expressions for uniaxial compressive strength and elastic modulus were developed, highlighting the critical role of freeze–thaw cycles in altering the rock’s strength and damage pattern.

A quantitative expression for uniaxial tensile strength was proposed, elucidating the mechanism of crack propagation during freeze–thaw damage.

These findings improve the understanding of granite’s behavior under freeze–thaw conditions and provide valuable insights for assessing rock slope stability in cold regions. Future research should focus on examining the long-term effects of freeze–thaw cycles under varying environmental conditions, such as different humidity levels and freeze–thaw frequencies. Additionally, investigating how different rock types respond to freeze–thaw cycles will offer a broader perspective on the impact of these processes on various geological materials. Advanced models incorporating dynamic loading conditions and real-world stress scenarios should also be developed to enhance the prediction and mitigation of freeze–thaw damage in engineering applications.
