A Three‐Step Diagnostic Strategy for Malignant Pleural Effusions
Authors: Elisa Nuez‐Zaragoza, Vicente Aguadero, Indira Bhambi‐Blanco, Mònica Vidal‐Pla, Isabel Aparicio‐Calvente, M Rosa Escoda‐Giralt, Joana Gallardo‐Campos, Joan Carles Ferreres, Luis Frisancho, Laia Mas‐Maresma, Patricia Aguilera‐Fernández, Sonia Marco‐Continente, Marina Sierra‐Boada, Pablo Andreu‐Cobo, Miquel Gallego, Francesca Canalias, Javier Hernando‐Redondo, Jaume Trapé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNew diagnostic tools, such as high fluorescence cells (HFc) and tumor markers (TMs), have emerged in the quest to accurately diagnose malignant pleural effusion (MPE). This study evaluated the diagnostic performance of the HFc and TMs combination. Hospitalized patients with pleural effusion were recruited at Parc Taulí University Hospital in Sabadell (Spain). Pleural fluid (PF) and serum samples were collected and analyzed in the Clinical Laboratory, and PF samples were sent to the Pathology Department for cytological examination. PF cell counts were determined using an XN‐10 analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan); TMs (CEA, CA 19.9, CA 15.3, CA 72.4, and CYFRA 21.1) were assessed using Cobas e801 (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). An MPE diagnosis was made based on positive cytology and/or positive pleural biopsy. A diagnostic algorithm was developed to detect MPE starting with an initial HFc screening, followed by TM determination with specific cutoffs depending on fluid type. The study included 300 pleural effusions from 209 patients. The algorithm had a sensitivity of 68% and a negative predictive value of 93%; its specificity and positive predictive value were both 100%. When combined with cytology, the sensitivity of the algorithm increased to 85%, and its negative predictive value to 96%. Our algorithm exhibited promising diagnostic performance and could serve as a non‐invasive tool for MPE detection. HFc screening helps identify potential MPE cases, and TM assessment, with its high specificity, contributes to MPE confirmation. Furthermore, establishing different TM cutoffs based on fluid type increases sensitivity without reducing specificity by introducing false positives.Keywords: high fluorescence cells, malignant pleural effusion, tumor markers

Introduction

Pleural effusions (PEs) present a diagnostic challenge due to their wide range of possible etiologies [1, 2, 3]. Benign etiologies such as congestive heart failure or pneumonia are the most common, followed by malignancy, which accounts for 15%–30% of cases [2, 3, 4]. Patients with malignant pleural effusions (MPEs) typically survive less than a year, highlighting the importance of a prompt diagnosis and correct management for such individuals [5, 6, 7].

Thoracentesis may serve both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Therefore, pleural fluid (PF) analysis, performed in the Emergency Laboratory, is crucial, as different etiologies of PEs exhibit distinct cellular and biochemical patterns [8, 9, 10, 11].

Cellularity workup is a useful and simple tool for distinguishing empyemas, characterized by a predominance of polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs). Lymphocytic PEs are mainly due to malignancy, cardiac failure, or tuberculosis; the latter is characterized by a high concentration of Adenosine deaminase enzyme (ADA) [8, 9, 10, 11].

New hematological analyzers methodology is based on fluorescence flow cytometry and enables automatization of the cellularity count and differentiation process to yield robust results in a short amount of time. The XN‐10 analyzer (Sysmex, Japan) allows body fluid cell categorization in PMNs, mononuclear cells (MNs), and High Fluorescence cells (HFc). The primary characteristics of HFc include their large size, high nucleic content, and increased complexity; they include mesothelial cells, macrophages, plasma cells, and tumor cells. As tumor cells are part of this cluster, HFc have been noted as a potential screening parameter for MPEs [5, 12].

A diagnosis of MPEs is based on PF cytology. However, despite being the gold standard this technique has limitations. Its sensitivity ranges from 40% to 87% depending on the series consulted; more invasive diagnostic tools such as a pleural biopsy must be used to ensure an accurate MPE diagnosis [6, 10, 13, 14, 15, 16]. However, invasive techniques are vulnerable to sampling errors and carry a risk of operation‐related complications [16, 17]. For those reasons, there has been growing interest in identifying novel, minimally invasive biomarkers for the detection of MPEs. A related goal is to improve diagnostic accuracy and optimize case stratification, such that invasive procedures are only performed on patients with a high probability of MPEs [10].

Tumor markers (TMs) are widely implemented in clinical laboratories; however, there is conflicting evidence regarding their utility in body fluids. That situation makes it challenging to incorporate them into routine diagnostic workflow [3, 10, 18].

Tumor markers are known for their high specificity. However, their sensitivity is limited and depends on the threshold applied, ranging from 15% to 55% [3, 17, 18]. There is typically a correlation between high concentrations of TMs and tumor cell secretions, and some of them show higher positivity rates and concentrations depending on the tumor type. For example, CEA is typically elevated in lung, breast, and gastrointestinal cancers, whereas levels are low in serous ovarian cancer and mesothelioma [19]. CA19‐9 may be elevated in gastrointestinal and lung tumors, but not in mesothelioma, breast, or serous ovarian cancers [15]. CA15‐3 is often detected in breast, serous ovarian, and lung cancers, and less frequently in gastrointestinal tumors and lymphomas [19]. CA72‐4 is elevated mainly in gastrointestinal and serous ovarian cancers, and less commonly in lung and breast tumors [20]. CYFRA 21‐1 shows sensitivities between 24% and 45%, except in non‐epithelial tumors, where its performance is limited [21]. Therefore, combining several tumor markers is necessary to achieve higher overall diagnostic sensitivity and assist in clinical decision‐making [15, 20, 21].

Nonetheless, in certain non‐malignant conditions—particularly those involving intense inflammation or serosal tissue damage—such as empyema, tuberculous pleuritis, or complicated parapneumonic effusions, TM levels may also be elevated [15, 22, 23]. In these situations, the probability of false‐positive results increases when standard diagnostic thresholds are applied. Therefore, interpretation of TM levels must consider the broader clinical and biochemical context [18].

To address this, the determination of ADA levels and the percentage of PMN cells in pleural fluid is essential [4]. These markers, while not specific for malignancy, are fundamental in identifying infectious or inflammatory etiologies, particularly tuberculosis and bacterial infections [8, 10].

Their inclusion allows a more comprehensive assessment of pleural effusions and supports a contextual interpretation of TM results [4], enabling a more personalized diagnostic approach in which clinically adjusted cutoffs can be applied according to the underlying inflammatory or infectious context, thereby reducing the likelihood of false‐positive TM elevations.

Identifying false positives is crucial for accurately interpreting TMs, as noted in the 1994 Barcelona consensus [15]. The literature highlights two main strategies to do so: increasing the cutoff point and calculating the PF/serum ratio [4]. These strategies help prevent misinterpretations related to TMs, but they need to be validated based on different types of PFs [15]. However, despite their use, guidance on the final interpretation based on PE type remains limited and needs to be improved; such guidance is integral to personalized medicine.

The aims of this study were the following:

To assess the diagnostic accuracy of a panel of tumor markers in pleural fluid and serum for MPE, applying an algorithmic approach to improve the diagnostic yield of thoracentesis using non‐invasive biochemical markers.

To assess the diagnostic accuracy of a panel of tumor markers in pleural fluid and serum for MPE, applying an algorithmic approach to improve the diagnostic yield of thoracentesis using non‐invasive biochemical markers.

To identify patients with a high suspicion of MPE who may benefit from pleural biopsy when cytology is negative.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective, experimental, observational, and unicentric study evaluated laboratory diagnostic tests conducted at Parc Taulí University Hospital in Sabadell, Spain. The study was approved by the hospital's Research Ethics Committee (ref.: 2021/5058) and all patients provided written informed consent to participate. The clinical samples used in this study were identical to those used in a previous study by our group [24]. The methodology we employed, along with the additional tests we conducted, is outlined below.

Participants

The study included adult outpatients and hospitalized patients who were seen from May 2021 through November 2022 in various departments of the hospital: the Emergency Department, Internal Medicine, the Intensive Care Unit, the Gastroenterology Department, Pneumology, and Oncology. Each patient had been clinically diagnosed with PE and underwent PF extraction for routine diagnostic purposes. The patients were anonymized in this study, and data from each patient (age, sex, cause of PE) were recorded. The only patient‐related exclusion criterion applied was the patient's refusal to participate in the study.

Pleural Fluid and Serum Workup

Sample Collection

PF samples were obtained by thoracentesis, and serum samples by puncture. Both were immediately analyzed in the Hospital's Clinical Laboratory. All of the samples were obtained for routine diagnostic purposes, and no additional PF or serum samples were obtained for this study.

The PF was collected in sterile containers without additives and divided into two aliquots. One aliquot, with a minimum volume of 1 mL, was sent to the Urgent Care Laboratory for cell count, differentiation, and biochemical analysis; the other, requiring a minimum volume of 10 mL, was sent to the Pathology Department for cytological workup. In instances where the Pathology Department received the sample directly from the extraction, no additional aliquot was sent from the laboratory. In both laboratories, the sample was processed immediately after collection.

Cell Count and Differentiation

Cell counts were determined using a Sysmex XN‐10 in Body Fluid Mode (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) in the Urgent Care Laboratory. The following parameters analyzed by fluorescence staining cytometry were recorded: white blood cells (WBC; leukocytes), PMN% (neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils), MN% (lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells), and HFc (mesothelial cells, macrophages, plasma cells, and malignant cells). The last parameter was recorded in absolute count (HFc/μL) from the total cell count.

The count for red blood cells (RBC) was determined via electrical impedance and was also recorded.

Cytological Workup

All of the cases were blinded to the Pathology Department, which conducted the cytological workup utilizing Papanicolaou and Diff‐Quick staining for an initial morphological examination. Immunocytochemistry techniques were used to confirm MPE if suggestive neoplastic cells were present. The patients were categorized into two groups: MPE and non‐MPE. The MPE group was defined by positive cytology and/or a positive pleural biopsy. Pleural biopsy was not performed in all cases, as it is an invasive procedure and its indication is individualized according to the clinical context and patient characteristics; however, when performed, it was carried out by pleuroscopy or thoracoscopy, and the results were used for diagnostic confirmation. The final diagnosis was confirmed by consulting the patient's medical history 6 months after sample collection. In cases of multiple diagnoses, we always recorded the one primarily suspected by clinicians, making sure to accurately identify MPE cases.

Biochemical Analysis

Next, the PF was centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm. The supernatant was then used for biochemical and TM analyses. Meanwhile, the blood samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3500 rpm to obtain serum.

ADA was measured in PF using a Cobas c501 (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) with BioSystems ADA reagent.

Concentrations of TMs—CEA, CA 15.3, CA 19.9, CYFRA 21.1, and CA 72.4—were determined in both PF and serum using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Cobas e801; Roche Diagnostics). All of the samples were stored at −80°C and thawed and analyzed in batches.

The TMs in PF and serum were always tested simultaneously, and the PF/serum ratio was calculated only when the value of TMs in PF was above the upper limit of the reference interval (URL) of the serum: 5 ng/mL for CEA, 30 U/mL for CA 15.3, 37 U/mL for CA 19.9, and 6.9 U/mL for CA 72.4.

Given the prospective and blinded nature of the study, the tumor marker panel was applied uniformly to all cases, regardless of prior cancer history, to reflect the real‐world diagnostic setting where malignant pleural effusion may be the first manifestation of malignancy.

Algorithm Design

The core algorithm consisted of three sequential steps. The first step included initial screening using HFc. This screening was followed by a second step classifying the PF type and identifying potential false‐positive TM results. Effusions suspected to be TM false positives—such as those due to empyema, parapneumonic effusion, or tuberculosis—were identified using the following criteria: PMN cell percentage > 70% and/or ADA > 45 U/L.

The third step involved interpreting TMs based on the fluid classification established in the second step. When the second step suggested a false‐positive profile, a high‐cutoff strategy was applied. Conversely, when the second step was negative, the interpretation relied on calculating the PF/serum TM ratio. In both cases, the TM panel was considered positive if at least one TM exceeded its corresponding cutoff value.

Statistical Analysis

Using an asymptotic normal confidence interval and assuming a sensitivity of 75%, we found that 62 MPEs had to be included to achieve a precision of 10% when estimating the sensitivity for a combination of TMs (CEA, CYFRA 21.1, CA 19.9, CA 15.3, and CA 72.4). Assuming a 22% rate of malignancy in our reference population, it would therefore be necessary to recruit 282 PE samples. Taking into account a likely withdrawal rate of 10%, we estimated that 313 PE samples would be needed.

We separated patients based on their final diagnosis for variable comparison. Normality for continuous variables was verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Non‐normal variables were displayed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR, P25–P75). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare samples between the MPE and non‐MPE patient groups. We considered a p value less than 0.05 to indicate statistical significance.

The distribution of continuous variables across MPE histology groups was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test when the sample size (N) was greater than 3. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normally distributed variables are reported as median and IQR.

We used ROC (Receiver operating characteristic curve) analysis to establish cutoff points for each TM at a specificity of 100%. ROC curves for the different tumor markers were also assessed after stratifying PF samples according to the itinerary followed within the algorithm. Diagnostic validity was determined by calculating sensitivity (S), specificity (Sp), and positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV) for each TM and the combination of TMs.

All of the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows v.20 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA), Stata v.10 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA), and R software v.3.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Three‐step algorithm designed to detect MPE. Single TM cutoffs are determined in pleural fluid. Three‐step algorithm: The initial step is HFc screening for the possible presence of neoplastic cells. The second step consists of classifying the PF, identifying potential empyema or tuberculous effusions based on predefined criteria (PMN > 70% and/or ADA > 45 U/L). The third step involves applying different TM cutoffs depending on the result of the second step. If the PF is suspected to be empyema or tuberculosis, higher TM cutoffs are applied. Discussion

We assessed the diagnostic performance of HFc screening in conjunction with TM determination—two non‐invasive biomarkers—for the diagnosis of MPEs. The application of an algorithm using TM cutoffs tailored to the patient's pathology in order to maximize specificity resulted in 100% specificity and 68% sensitivity.

Malignant pleural effusions are produced by primary tumors of the pleura and tumor metastasis, making their diagnosis crucial for determining the management and prognosis of the neoplastic condition. In 40%–60% of cases, MPE diagnoses require invasive procedures [4], but are not always immediately feasible in all patients. To reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, there has been increasing interest in novel biomarkers in PF, which is readily available in routine clinical practice as its analysis is typically the first diagnostic step.

One promising route is HFc, which has shown promise as a screening tool for MPEs. Studies have indicated that low cutoff values offer high sensitivity: previous investigations reported discriminative values between 10 and 17 HFc counts, with sensitivities ranging from 70.8% to 98% and specificities ranging from 42% to 66.2% for detecting malignant cells in body fluids [5, 12, 25, 26]. Our study yielded similar results, with a 12 HFc cutoff corresponding to 84% sensitivity and 45% specificity.

We expected that HFc would correlate well with cytology; however, the fact that HFc includes cells involved in benign processes contributes to the test's low specificity. In our results, among patients with fewer than 12 HFc, only 9% were diagnosed with neoplasia. Among patients with more than 12 HFc, 31% had a MPE. Additionally, the proportion of negative cytology results was higher in the HFc < 12 group (41%) compared with the HFc > 12 group (24%). This finding suggests an association between the number of HFc and cytological detection. As previously noted, the presence of HFc increases the likelihood of a cancer diagnosis.

In some MPE cases, a detection of HFc may not be possible due to a low neoplastic cell count or cell morphology. For example, in hematological neoplasms, a diagnosis often requires performing a lymphocyte immunophenotype [27].

The second step of our algorithm was designed to improve diagnostic yield based on the type of PE used to identify clinical conditions that lead to false positives [10]. This step was added after reviewing previous studies, which recommended different interpretations of TMs depending on whether effusions are potential empyemas or tuberculous [4, 18, 22, 23] and identifying them using ADA and PMN. Consequently, the cutoffs for each group can be personalized based on the patient's clinical conditions. This approach allowed us to achieve maximum specificity in our results, consistent with findings reported in previous studies by Trapé [4, 21, 28].

The third step of our algorithm used a broad combination of markers to detect MPEs, independently of the tumor that produced them. All of the literature that we examined agrees that the use of a combination of TMs enhances the diagnostic yield for detecting MPEs compared with relying on a single TM determination [3, 4, 15, 18, 19, 24, 29, 30, 31, 32].

The combination of CEA and CA 15.3 has been the most extensively studied due to its good diagnostic yield [19]. Porcel et al. reported sensitivities very similar to those of PF cytology and the detection of more than 33% cytology‐negative malignant cases [19]. Other authors have found varying sensitivities with different TM combinations. For instance, Gu et al. combined PF CA 125, CA 19.9, CEA, NSE, and SCC to achieve a sensitivity of 82.11% with a 100% specificity [30]. Similarly, a study by Trapé et al. reported a combination of CEA, CA 15.3, CA 72.4, and CA 19.9, which yielded a sensitivity of 72.7% and a sensitivity of 100% [4]. Our findings with a combination of TMs are consistent with those in the literature (i.e., higher sensitivity compared with that of individual TM markers).

The criteria for interpreting TMs depend on the species and are based on whether the TMs are typically produced by mesothelial cells or not. For TMs not produced by mesothelial cells, such as CEA, CA 15.3, CA 19.9, and CA 72.4 [15], the PF/serum ratio is used to determine whether there is local pleural production [15, 29, 32]. In this series, the ratio threshold was increased up to 1.5 for PF values very close to the cutoff. The PF/serum ratio is recommended unless clinical conditions identified by benign biomarkers in the previous level are suspected, in which case a higher cutoff should be applied without the ratio calculation [4, 10, 18].

Mesothelial secretion of CYFRA 21.1 limits the usefulness of the ratio in this context [15, 29], and two cutoffs were used depending on the positivity of benign biomarkers. This situation is similar to that described in Trapé et al. [21].

Our results revealed that none of the inflammatory non‐MPE cases surpassed the high threshold used, as other authors have described with similar thresholds [4, 10, 18, 19]. Additionally, we evaluated the application of the TM ratio to tuberculous/empyemas, which led to three false positives for empyemas (two CEA‐positive and one CA 15.3‐positive). We also obtained one false positive in non‐tuberculous/empyema cases due to a high concentration of CA 72.4 (in a traumatic PF case). Furthermore, we identified five false negatives, which could have been detected using the respective cutoffs.

In the case of CA19.9, we obtained very high values in two patients, one with pancreatitis and another with cholecystitis. These conditions could have been identified by elevated amylase levels in the first case and elevated bilirubin, gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase, or alkaline phosphatase in the second. In a previous study [15], very high CA 19.9 levels were also found in a patient without positive benign biomarkers due to an effusion caused by fever of biliary origin. Furthermore, the combination of CEA, CA 15.3, CA 72.4, and CYFRA 21.1 did not increase sensitivity when CA 19.9 was added.

We found only three previous studies in which TMs were combined with HFc. Two of those studies concluded that the combination of HFc and CEA improved the detection ability of MPEs compared with single detection methods (Area Under the Curve analysis); however, the authors did not propose any cutoffs to be included in the laboratory workflow [33, 34].

The third study was published by our group. In that investigation, we developed a predictive model where, by combining HFc, CA 15.3 Serum, and Ratio CEA, we obtained a sensitivity of 60% at 100% specificity [24]. In the present study, we increased the diagnostic yield by adding more TMs (CYFRA 21.1 and CA 72.4) and introducing a different approach.

Our algorithm demonstrates a robust diagnostic yield with 100% specificity and 68% sensitivity, enabling the detection of 46 out of 68 MPE cases (67.7%). It had a maximum PPV (100%) and a NPV of 93%. When the evaluation was limited to epithelial and mesothelial neoplasms, with hematological and prostatic cases excluded, sensitivity rose to 76%. Finally, when combined with cytology workup, we achieved the highest diagnostic yield among non‐invasive techniques, with a sensitivity of 87%; we detected 59 of MPE cases (with nine of those cases having negative cytology). Other authors also noted an increase in cytology sensitivity and demonstrated the potential of combining TMs and cytology [35].

Although pleural biopsy remains the gold standard, the clinical utility of our algorithm lies in its role as a non‐invasive, rapid diagnostic tool, with costs comparable to cytology, both in terms of reagents and the time required from specialized personnel. TMs are already routinely measured in serum for the diagnosis, prognosis, and follow‐up of solid tumors, so no additional infrastructure is required for these analyses. Additionally, being fully automated, it eliminates observer‐dependent and interpretive variability errors associated with cytology. Importantly, rather than replacing cytology or pleural biopsy, the algorithm is intended to stratify patients' risk and support early clinical decisions in two distinct scenarios.

Classification according to HFc enabled two groups to be identified with very different cancer prevalences: 9% in the group with low HFc and 31% in the high‐HFc group. This differentiation could be used to guide the selection of laboratory tests to increase the cost‐effectiveness of TM studies in PF. In the low‐prevalence group (HFc < 12), all MPEs detected by TMs were also identified by cytology; in the high‐prevalence group, TMs detected additional MPEs that were not identified by cytology.

In high‐risk patients (e.g., elevated HFc counts or suspicious imaging findings), adding a TM determination adds potential value, because a positive TM result can support expedited referral and workup. The algorithm provides results within hours (versus up to a week for cytology and immunohistochemistry), so it offers early diagnostic orientation with important clinical implications: in clinically stable patients, hospital discharge can be considered while awaiting confirmatory tests, and if histological confirmation is needed, hospitalization time before starting treatment may be shortened.

In contrast, in low‐risk patients, a negative result combined with no clinical suspicion of malignancy may help guide management and avoid unnecessary procedures. Overall, the algorithm facilitates earlier clinical decisions, contributes to reducing unnecessary hospital stays, and helps lower hospitalization costs, which are often driven by prolonged diagnostic confirmation despite patients being clinically stable.

Furthermore, its complementarity with cytology provides a new perspective. When cytology results are negative but the algorithm yields a positive result (in our study, it detected 50% of cytology‐negative MPE cases), it may be reasonable to avoid repeated thoracentesis for additional cytological analyses and proceed directly to invasive diagnostic techniques.

When considering its implications in clinical practice, patients with MPE can generally be classified into two main categories.

First, among patients with a known cancer diagnosis, the development of a pleural effusion often raises suspicion of metastatic involvement. In this context, a positive algorithm result, with 100% specificity, may obviate the need for pleural biopsy particularly when radiological and clinical findings support the diagnosis. This is especially valuable in fragile or advanced‐stage patients, where a minimally invasive diagnostic confirmation is preferred. Additionally, changes in the pattern of TMs expression may suggest the presence of a different primary neoplasm, further guiding the diagnostic approach.

Second, in patients without a prior cancer diagnosis (60% of MPE patients in our study), MPE may represent one of the first manifestations of malignancy. Initial tests are sometimes inconclusive which can lead to significant diagnostic delays and complicate clinical decision‐making (e.g., hospital admission, expedited referral, or discharge planning).

In these cases, a positive algorithm result, available within 24 h, provides immediate and clinically meaningful diagnostic orientation, particularly in patients in whom there was no prior suspicion of cancer and in whom cytological analysis may not have initially been requested. This is particularly relevant in cases where underlying malignant processes initially present as benign conditions; for instance, some patients may present with empyema, while harboring an undiagnosed neoplasm. In our study, among the seven MPE cases with over 75% PMNs, two had negative cytology (yielding a detection rate of 71%), and one of these was detected using our algorithm (resulting in an 86% detection rate). In such cases, the underlying malignancy may remain masked until subsequent medical evaluations (pneumonia resolution and further follow‐up), and the algorithm may therefore facilitate earlier detection.

In such scenarios, regardless of cytology findings, a positive result would prompt the initiation of a comprehensive oncological workup, including pleural biopsy and high‐resolution imaging, with the aim of initiating treatment as early as possible.

The limitations of the present study include its single‐center design, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Further validation in other centers is needed to confirm our findings and ensure broader applicability. Additionally, our study had a small sample size of MPEs (68), which may have impacted the statistical power and robustness of our conclusions. A larger sample size would be beneficial to enhance the reliability of the results.

In conclusion, this TM‐based algorithm offers a rapid turnaround and a robust diagnostic approach with high diagnostic performance. It enhances the diagnostic yield of thoracentesis by complementing cytological evaluation and provides valuable support for early clinical decision‐making.
