Facial Cutaneous Rosai-Dorfman Disease: Dermoscopic Findings with Successful Surgical Treatment
Authors: Keunyoung Hur, Ji Yeon Hong, Kyu Han Kim, Jihui Jeon, Cheol Lee, Yoonjin Kwak, Tae Min Kim, Je-Ho Mun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Rosai-Dorfman disease (RDD) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by an accumulation of activated histiocytes within the affected tissues. It is a heterogeneous disease that includes the classical (nodal) and extra-nodal variants. The cutaneous form of the disease without the characteristic lymphadenopathy is rare and is often misdiagnosed as other dermatologic diseases. Misdiagnosis as lymphoproliferative and infectious diseases such as lymphoma and tuberculosis have been reported in the literature. Herein, we report a case of facial cutaneous RDD with successful surgical treatment. In addition, we provide dermoscopic findings and literature review as dermoscopy can be a useful adjuvant tool in the diagnosis of cutaneous RDD.

INTRODUCTION

Rosai-Dorfman disease (RDD) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by an accumulation of activated histiocytes within the affected tissues1. It is a heterogeneous disease that includes the classical (nodal) and extra-nodal variants, leading to cutaneous, intracranial, spinal, central nervous system, ophthalmic, gastrointestinal, bone, and hematologic involvement. The cutaneous form of the disease without the characteristic lymphadenopathy is rare and is often misdiagnosed as other dermatologic diseases such as sarcoidosis, lymphoma, and nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection. Herein, we report a case of facial cutaneous RDD with successful surgical treatment.

CASE REPORT

A 47-year-old female presented with well demarcated erythematous to yellowish plaques on both cheeks since 18 months ago (Fig. 1). She reported that the lesions occurred two months after injection of filler on her face. She had previously undergone skin biopsy at a local hospital; however, the diagnosis was inconclusive. She was presumptively diagnosed as NTM infection and was treated with clarithromycin and doxycycline for several months. However, there was no improvement. Therefore, she was referred to our department for further examination. Dermoscopic examination revealed yellowish structureless patterns on an erythematous background with linear branched vessels (Fig. 2A). Since empirical antibiotic treatment was ineffective, we performed a skin biopsy. Histopathological analysis revealed a dense lymphocytic and histiocytic infiltration with moderate plasma cell infiltration. Under high magnification, emperipolesis was detected with positive CD68 and S-100 and negative CD1a staining (Fig. 2B~F). Systemic work-up, including the F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography scan, did not reveal any systemic involvement of RDD. The diagnosis of cutaneous RDD was confirmed. Systemic steroid therapy with a tapering regimen for 3 months (initial dosage of 0.5 mg/kg prednisolone) did not render significant improvement. As excision of the nodule on the left lower cheek was satisfactory (Fig. 1E), we performed staged elliptical excision under the patient’s request (left cheek, two times; right cheek, one time), which resulted in a satisfactory cosmetic outcome (Fig. 1C, F). No recurrence was observed for two years.

Written informed consent from the patient for use of images and publication of case details was obtained.

DISCUSSION

Diagnosis of cutaneous RDD is challenging. Misdiagnosis is common due to its rarity and relatively nonspecific clinical findings. Emperipolesis, which indicates the presence of intact inflammatory cells within the cytoplasm of histiocytes, is the hallmark for the diagnosis of RDD. However, it is often subtle and difficult to identify, especially in the absence of a sufficient number of histiocytes showing emperipolesis in the biopsied specimen. The possibility of misdiagnosis is more likely for the cutaneous form of RDD as emperipolesis might be less conspicuous in the cutaneous form of RDD than in the lymph node of nodal RDD2. In addition, due to dense inflammatory infiltration, including lymphocytes and plasma cells, histopathological diagnosis is challenging even to experienced pathologists. Therefore, misdiagnosis as lymphoproliferative and infectious diseases such as lymphoma and tuberculosis have been reported in the literature34.

Dermoscopy can help in the diagnosis of lymphoproliferative and histiocytic diseases. To the best of our knowledge, only four studies have reported the dermoscopic features of cutaneous RDD to date (Table 1)5678. Previous studies reporting dermoscopy of RDD have described similar dermoscopic features, such as yellowish area and surrounding branched vessels. In the view of dermoscopic and histopathologic correlation, a yellowish structureless area on dermoscopy is assumed to correspond to dense histiocytic accumulation. Surrounding blood vessels on dermoscopy may represent increased vasculature, which is required for the proliferation of histiocytes.

Dermoscopic features that require differentiation from cutaneous RDD have been reported. Sarcoidosis was reported to manifest translucent yellow to orange globular-like or structureless areas associated with linear vessels9. Orange-whitish central areas with surrounding looped vessels were reported in one case with NTM infection10. Serpentine vessels with salmon-colored background is suggested for cutaneous B-cell lymphoma. Fine focused telangiectasia on a yellow to orange background is typically described for lupus vulgaris1112. Orange-yellow structureless pattern with erythematous border, named “setting sun sign”, represents the most frequent dermoscopic manifestation of juvenile xanthogranuloma13. However, considering the limited data available in the literature, further studies are necessary to characterize the patterns of various skin diseases to investigate the diagnostic accuracy of dermoscopy.

There are various treatment modalities for cutaneous RDD including excision, oral or intralesional steroids, chemotherapy, radiation, and cryotherapy11415. Due to the absence of randomized controlled trials, treatment guideline was not fully established. Therefore, treatment should be tailored to the individual clinical circumstances1. The treatments have varying efficacy with uncertain treatment duration. Targeted therapy with a mitogen-activated protein kinase inhibitor can be tried in refractory cases with KRAS-mutated RDD16. A recent review article demonstrated that excision was shown to be the most successful option with 59% of the cases achieving complete remission14. However, surgery is not easy for patients with RDD due to the presence of multiple and often large lesions. It is worth noting that observation can be a reasonable approach as studies have shown that 20% to 50% of patients with nodal/cutaneous disease experience spontaneous remission1. In our case, we engaged in a thorough discussion with the patient regarding various treatment modalities and opted for meticulous surgical excision, which yielded a satisfactory outcome considering the disfiguring features on the face.

In conclusion, we report a case of facial cutaneous RDD which was treated with surgery. Dermoscopy can be a useful adjuvant tool in the diagnosis of cutaneous RDD.
