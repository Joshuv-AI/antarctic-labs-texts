Diffusion Tensor Imaging Reveals Altered Centrality of Pain‐Related Regions in SCN9A‐Associated Small Fiber Neuropathy
Authors: Gerhard S. Drenthen, Dennis Kool, Amir Far, Jaymin Upadhyay, David E. J. Linden, Raquel van Gool, Celine P. Goijen, Ingemar S. J. Merkies, Walter H. Backes, Catharina G. Faber, Jacobus F. A. Jansen, Janneke G. J. Hoeijmakers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposeSmall fiber neuropathy (SFN) is a neuropathic disorder that is associated with chronic pain. While most SFN cases are idiopathic, SFN can also have hereditary causes. For example, rare SCN9A gene mutations can impair the NaV1.7 sodium channel, which leads to dorsal root ganglion neuron hyperexcitability, causing SFN. Although chronic pain may induce cerebral changes, the specific structural brain alterations in SCN9A‐associated SFN (SFN‐SCN9A) remain insufficiently characterized. Therefore, potential alterations in the structural brain network of idiopathic SFN and SFN‐SCN9A were explored.MethodsTen SFN‐SCN9A patients, 20 idiopathic SFN patients, and 20 controls were included. All participants underwent 3‐Tesla diffusion MRI (66 gradient directions, b‐value = 1200 s/mm2), and the brain network was quantified using nodal importance, which describes the influence of a group of regions on the whole network.ResultsThe nodal importance of pain‐associated regions (postcentral gyrus, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus) was increased in SFN‐SCN9A patients compared to controls (β = 0.43, p = 0.02) and idiopathic SFN patients (β = 0.43, p = 0.02). Moreover, higher self‐reported pain was associated with higher nodal importance of pain‐associated regions in the SFN‐SCN9A group (r = 0.67, p = 0.03), while this effect was not observed in the idiopathic SFN patients (r = −0.22, p = 0.34). As self‐reported pain did not differ between the SFN groups, it is likely specific to the SCN9A‐mutation and not to differences in pain intensity.ConclusionCombined, these results suggest the potential involvement of a distinct structural pathway related to pain processing in SFN‐SCN9A.

Introduction

Small fiber neuropathy (SFN) is a neuropathic disorder that affects the small nerve fibers in the peripheral nervous system (PNS). It can cause various symptoms, among which chronic pain is the most prevalent [1]. While most SFN cases are idiopathic, they can also have hereditary causes [2]. For instance, approximately 5% of SFN patients harbor a gain‐of‐function variant in SCN9A, the gene encoding the voltage‐gated sodium channel Nav1.7. This channel is essential for generating and conducting action potentials in the physiological pain pathway. The gene mutation causes dorsal root ganglion neuron hyperexcitability, resulting in SFN [3]. Chronic pain has previously been associated with changes in brain morphology and structure, including decreased cortical thickness [4, 5, 6], reduced gray matter volume, and altered cortical brain networks [7]. These structural changes might be a result of long‐term activation of pain‐related neural pathways. The regions involved in neural pain processing, also referred to as the pain matrix, have been identified in previous studies and include the postcentral gyrus, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus [8, 9, 10].

Connections between cerebral regions can be visualized using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Bundles of axons can be reconstructed, thereby characterizing the brain network in terms of white matter tracts that connect to gray matter regions. Graph theoretical analysis can quantify these DTI‐derived brain networks, either on a global scale or in relation to specific regions. While global graph metrics can estimate the efficiency of the whole network, local graph metrics can quantify the role of specific regions in the brain network, for example, using nodal importance. Nodal importance is a metric that estimates the influence a node (i.e., brain region) has on the entire network. While the number of connections (i.e., degree centrality) is a common metric for nodal importance, it does not specifically assess a node's role in overall network connectivity. Instead, the betweenness centrality (BC) [11] and eigenvector centrality (EC) [12] measure how efficiently information can flow through the network.

In addition to morphological changes, prior neuroimaging studies also revealed that chronic pain may induce changes in white matter structure and connectivity [13, 14]. In the case of SFN, white matter connectivity between the thalamus and pain‐related regions was lower compared to healthy controls (HCs) [15]. However, the role of pain‐related regions in the structural brain network as a whole has not yet been studied in SFN. SFN due to SCN9A (SFN‐SCN9A) has previously been associated with widespread morphological changes [5]; however, it remains unclear whether the SCN9A mutations are associated with a distinct reorganization of the pain‐related brain regions. Therefore, we investigated the nodal importance of pain‐related regions in SFN patients versus HCs, and separately compared SCN9A‐SFN with idiopathic SFN (iSFN) and HCs.

Methods

Study Population

Fifty participants were included in this study: 20 iSFN patients (16 females, mean age 52 years), 10 SFN‐SCN9A patients (seven females, mean age 45 years), and 20 HCs (13 females, mean age 50 years). SFN patients were recruited from the SFN database of the neurology department in Maastricht UMC+, the Netherlands, from October 2018 to December 2020. Healthy participants were recruited through advertisements, patients’ families, and colleagues. SFN was diagnosed using the Besta diagnostic criteria for SFN [1]. SFN‐SCN9A was diagnosed based on a genetic variant with possible, probable, or certain pathogenicity in the SCN9A gene, which encodes Nav1.7 (uncertain variants 3, 4, and 5) [16]. Patients in the iSFN group did not carry a variant in the SCN9A, SCN10A, or SCN11A genes. Due to the rarity of SCN9A variants, SFN‐SCN9A patients included in the study were not required to have an abnormal intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD). For iSFN patients, an abnormal IENFD was required to confirm an SFN diagnosis. Furthermore, an additional inclusion criterion for all SFN patients was experiencing pain due to SFN for at least 3 months prior to the MRI scan, with a self‐reported pain score of at least 5/10 on the numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) or a self‐reported maximum pain of 50/100 on the visual analogue scale (VAS), where 0 represents no pain and 100 represents the most intense pain imaginable. Additionally, a VAS score for the self‐reported minimum and current pain (i.e., on the day of the MRI) was also recorded.

Since only idiopathic SFN and SFN‐SCN9A patients were considered, individuals with another known etiology for SFN were excluded. These alternative etiologies included metabolic diseases, autoimmune disorders, infectious diseases, toxic causes, or hereditary causes apart from the SCN9A genetic variant. Additional exclusion criteria included major depression according to DSM‐5 criteria, a history of another major psychiatric disease, alcohol abuse, or a Hospital Anxiety Depression Subscale (HADS) score ≥14 [17, 18].

This study was approved by the local Medical Ethics Committee (identifier: NL53441.068.15/METC:152027) and is registered in the US National Library of Medicine, ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT03889080). All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion in the study. Subject characteristics are presented in Table 1.

MRI Acquisition

All participants underwent 3T‐MRI (Philips Achieva, Best, the Netherlands) using a 32‐element phased‐array coil. For anatomical reference, 3D T1‐weighted images (TR/TE = 9.7/4 ms; flip angle = 12°; matrix = 256 × 256; 192 slices; voxel size = 1 mm3) were acquired. Next, to assess the structural network, a diffusion‐weighted echo‐planar imaging scan (70 transverse 2‐mm‐thick slices; matrix = 112 × 112; pixel size = 2 × 2 mm; TR/TE = 8068/75 ms, 66 gradient directions with b‐value = 1200 s/mm2 and one b‐value = 0 s/mm2 image) was acquired.

Analysis

Preprocessing

The structural images were automatically parcellated into 68 cortical and 16 subcortical regions using Freesurfer based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas, combined with visual inspections and manual corrections [19, 20]. The diffusion‐weighted images were corrected for head motion (including B‐matrix rotation) and eddy currents using ExploreDTI [21]. Due to the unavailability of a reversed‐phase encoding b = 0 s/mm2 image, the Synb0‐DisCo [22] method was used to correct for susceptibility distortions. Subject motion did not significantly differ between the three diagnostic groups (one‐way ANOVA of the framewise displacement, p > 0.10). Subsequently, whole‐brain tractography was performed using constrained spherical deconvolution with a uniform seed point of 2 mm3, a step size of 1 mm, a tract angle threshold of 30°, and a tract length range of 50–500 mm.

Graph Analysis

To construct the individual structural networks, the 84 Freesurfer‐derived regions were used as nodes. Subsequently, we used ExploreDTI to calculate the number of streamlines (i.e., tracts) between each pair of nodes. To minimize the number of unstable and spurious connections, two nodes were considered connected if they were connected by at least two streamlines [23]. Moreover, only nodes and edges present in at least half of the participants’ networks were included in the graph analysis (i.e., group thresholding) [24]. Since the true number of brain network connections is unknown, network sparsity was varied from 72% to 90% in 1% increments to assess the robustness of group differences across a plausible range of network densities of the whole brain network [25, 26]. The sparsity values range from networks that included as many of the connections as possible given the aforementioned criteria for a connection (sparsity value 72%), up to networks that include only the 10% strongest connections (sparsity value 90%). The upper limit of 90% was chosen to prevent networks from containing too many disconnected nodes, which could hamper graph analysis. To investigate the influence of pain‐associated regions in the structural network, we estimated the mean nodal importance of the postcentral gyrus, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus [27] using the BC and EC. This included the bilateral caudal and rostral anterior cingulate, bilateral thalamus, bilateral insular, and bilateral postcentral regions from the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Both the BC and EC are measures of nodal importance, where the BC makes use of the shortest paths between all brain region pairs by counting the number of times a certain node is included in the shortest path, thereby reflecting the influence the node has on the spread of information through the network [11]. The BC of node v is calculated as follows: BCv=∑i,j,i≠v≠jλijvλij, where λij denotes the number of shortest paths between nodes i and j, and λij(v) denotes the number of shortest paths between nodes i and j, passing through node v [28].

Moreover, the EC estimates a relative nodal importance by assigning higher EC values to nodes that connect to other influential nodes (i.e., also have high EC values) [12]. Mathematically, this can be realized by making the EC of a node v proportional to the EC of v’s direct neighbors. This can be expressed using the following formula: ECv=1λ∑i=1nAviECi, where λ is a constant and A is the adjacency matrix representing the structural network. This formula can be rewritten as

λx=A·x,

Which represents the eigenvalue decomposition of the adjacency matrix A. Now, the eigenvector x corresponding to the highest eigenvalue λ represents the EC [29]. This way, the EC depends on both the number and the quality of its connections to neighbors. Therefore, a node with a few high‐quality connections can have a higher EC value compared to a node with many mediocre connections.

To ensure that our results are sensitive to the nodal importance of the pain‐associated regions and are not driven by global differences, we normalized the BC and EC of the pain‐associated regions to the average BC and EC of all regions.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in patient characteristics between the iSFN patients and the SFN‐SCN9A patients were assessed using an independent samples t‐test for continuous variables and the Wilcoxon rank sum test for binary variables (sex, abnormal IENFD, and abnormal quantitative sensory testing).

Potential group differences (iSFN, SFN‐SCN9A, and HCs) in BC and EC of the pain‐associated regions were evaluated using multivariable linear regression analysis, adjusting for age and sex. For this, aggregate metrics of the BC and EC were calculated as the mean over the full sparsity range (72%–90%) [30, 31, 32]. In the first model, differences between the combined SFN‐group and HCs were assessed. Next, using separate linear models, differences between the SFN‐SCN9A group and HCs, as well as between the SFN‐SCN9A group and the iSFN group, were assessed. Subsequently, to investigate whether the nodal importance of the pain‐associated regions relates to self‐reported current (i.e., on the day of the MRI) pain, multivariable linear regression models were used, where the BC and EC of the pain‐associated regions were used as independent variables and the VAS score for current self‐reported pain as the dependent variable, with age and sex as covariates. Moreover, to assess whether the relation differs between the iSFN and SFN‐SCN9A groups, an interaction effect model was used where a group (iSFN or SFN‐SCN9A) and an interaction term (group × VAS score) were added to the model. Besides the aggregate metrics, linear regression models with the graph metrics for each individual sparsity level are also calculated to visualize variations across different sparsity thresholds and therefore to assess the consistency of effects over density (i.e., size) variations of the defined whole‐brain network.

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB (version R2023b). Statistical significance was inferred at p < 0.05.

Results

The iSFN patients and the SFN‐SCN9A patients did not show a significant difference in either age or sex (p > 0.10). Moreover, they also did not show a significant difference in the duration of pain and any of the VAS scores (p > 0.10). The SFN‐SCN9A group has significantly more patients with an abnormal IENFD compared to the iSFN group (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

The combined SFN group (iSFN and SFN‐SCN9A) did not show a significant difference in either the BC of the pain‐associated regions (BCpain) or the EC of the pain‐associated regions (ECpain) compared to HCs (p > 0.10).

BCpain was significantly higher in the SFN‐SCN9A group compared to HCs (β = 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] [0.06, 0.80], p = 0.02) as well as compared to iSFN (β = 0.43, 95% CI [0.07, 0.80], p = 0.02). ECpain was not significantly higher in the SFN‐SCN9A group compared to HCs and iSFN patients (p > 0.05). No significant differences are reported between the iSFN patients and HCs. Discussion

Chronic pain influences both the functional and structural brain network. In particular, the postcentral gyrus, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus play important roles in pain processing [10]. In this study, we investigated the influence of these pain‐associated regions on the structural brain network of patients with iSFN and SFN‐SCN9A. In SFN‐SCN9A patients, measures of nodal importance (BC) were increased compared to iSFN and controls, indicating that pain‐associated regions played a more important role in the structural network of SFN‐SCN9A patients. Furthermore, in SFN‐SCN9A patients, a higher nodal importance of these regions was associated with higher self‐reported current pain, whereas this effect was not observed in iSFN patients.

The nodal importance (or nodal influence) of pain‐associated regions in patients with chronic pain provides valuable insights into their role in pain processing. The increased nodal influence of these regions in SFN‐SCN9A patients suggests a potential neuronal correlate for genetic SFN in pain processing. Interestingly, a prior DTI study also reported a higher nodal importance in pain‐associated regions in individuals with burning mouth syndrome [33]. Burning mouth syndrome can be caused by SFN [34], and the Nav1.7 sodium channels play an important role in SFN‐related conditions such as burning mouth syndrome [35]. Therefore, it is possible that increased functional connectivity between these pain‐related regions contributes to the observed increase in nodal importance [36]. Conversely, a previous DTI study reported a reduction in structural connectivity between the thalamus and pain‐associated regions in SFN [15]. This finding aligns with our study, where the average nodal importance of the pain‐associated regions was lower, though not significantly, in iSFN patients compared to controls.

However, in SFN‐SCN9A patients, the nodal importance of pain‐associated regions was significantly higher than in iSFN and HCs, suggesting a distinct role for pain‐associated regions in SFN‐SCN9A. Additionally, since both measures of nodal importance (BC and EC) were increased in SFN‐SCN9A patients, this indicates that pain‐associated regions not only play an important role in facilitating the flow of information within the structural brain network but are also connected to other highly influential regions (i.e., hubs).

Measures of maximum, minimum, and current pain were recorded. In the SFN‐SCN9A group, a higher self‐reported current pain was related to increased nodal importance of the pain‐associated regions in the SFN‐SCN9A group, while this relation was not observed in the iSFN group. Importantly, self‐reported pain did not differ between the SFN groups, indicating that the observed relation is likely specific to the SCN9A mutation and not to differences in experienced pain intensity. Moreover, the effect of nodal importance on self‐reported pain differed between the two SFN groups for a range of sparsity values. Interestingly, the relationship with the VAS score was robustly found for EC, while for BC, it was only observed at a single sparsity value and might be a spurious finding. This might be explained by the fact that pain processing relies on multiple and redundant pathways [37], whereas BC is restricted to estimating communication along the shortest paths. EC assigns high nodal importance to regions connected to other highly influential nodes, suggesting that the subnetwork of pain‐associated regions plays a pivotal role in the network. In contrast, BC measures how efficiently information can flow through the network, highlighting the complementary nature of these two measures.

The relationship between pain intensity and EC suggests that SFN‐SCN9A patients with the most pronounced network alterations (i.e., higher nodal importance) experience worse pain regulation. Together with the group differences, where higher nodal importance was found in SFN‐SCN9A patients, this indicates that pain may be linked to stronger integration or dominance of pain‐processing hubs within the structural network. Together, these findings suggest that SFN‐SCN9A is characterized by a distinct, possibly maladaptive, pain‐related reorganization of the structural network. The structural network alteration in SFN‐SCN9A could result from direct effects of the SCN9A mutation and changes in Nav1.7 channel activity on the central nervous system (CNS). However, since Nav1.7 is minimally expressed in the CNS [38], it is unlikely that genetically determined structural changes in the brain are directly caused by SCN9A. Instead, the observed difference in the brain may arise as a secondary adjustment due to PNS changes, where Nav1.7 is prominently expressed [38]. Previous studies have shown that chronic pain can lead to structural changes in the brain [39]. For instance, in diabetic neuropathy, structural network differences were observed between individuals with painful diabetic neuropathy and HCs, but not between those with painless diabetic neuropathy and HCs. This suggests that neuroplastic changes are induced by pain signals rather than diabetes or neuropathy itself [39]. Interestingly, in our study, the lack of structural network differences between iSFN and controls and the absence of a relationship between EC and pain in iSFN, despite comparable pain levels, suggest that not all pain signals impact the structural network of the brain and that pain signaling may vary between and within conditions. In SFN‐SCN9A, the Nav1.7 variant may generate a distinct pain signal in small fibers of the PNS, triggering distinct neuroplastic changes in the CNS.

A major strength of this study is that we are the first to characterize structural network changes in a well‐defined SFN‐SCN9A patient group compared to iSFN and HCs. However, a limitation is the limited sample size. Despite this, we identified significant alterations in the structural network architecture specific to the SFN‐SCN9A group. Additionally, the inclusion criteria for SFN‐SCN9A patients were less strict than for iSFN patients. To ensure a definitive SFN diagnosis, iSFN patients were only included if they had an abnormal skin biopsy. However, due to the rarity of the SCN9A variant, requiring an abnormal skin biopsy was not feasible for the SFN‐SCN9A group. Previous research has demonstrated a correlation between IENFD and structural brain network changes in SFN [15]. However, due to the differences in inclusion criteria between the SFN groups, further analysis of the potential effects of IENFD on the structural network was hindered. Furthermore, patient medications may influence dendritic integrity and pain processing, potentially altering structural and functional connectivity [40, 41, 42]. Although no information was available regarding patient treatments at the time of the MRI scan, it is plausible that some patients were taking pain relief medications, which could have an influence on the study outcomes. Even though neuropathic pain treatment is diverse, using different mechanisms to alleviate pain, iSFN and SFN‐SCN9A patients are expected to receive similar pharmacotherapies [43]. Therefore, even if medication does affect the structural network, we expect the overall contrast between both SFN groups to be mostly unrelated to pharmacotherapy. Future studies are needed to confirm the significant role of the pain‐related regions in SFN‐SCN9A and explore the influence of medication on the network.

The nodal importance of the pain‐associated regions in the structural network was higher in SFN‐SCN9A patients compared to iSFN patients and controls. This suggests a specific role of the pain‐associated regions in the structural network in SFN‐SCN9A.
