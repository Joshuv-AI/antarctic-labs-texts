Exploring the prognostic value of T cell exhaustion and mitochondrial dysfunction related genes in breast cancer through bioinformatics analysis and RT-qPCR validation
Authors: Hong Wan, Angqing Li, Han Jiang, Zichen Ling, Rongsheng Su, Xuwen Hao, Jing Pei, Xiaowei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer (BRCA) is a complex cancer with heterogeneous molecular mechanisms. This study aimed to identify prognostic genes related to T cell exhaustion and mitochondrial dysfunction in BRCA, and to construct a prognostic model. First, transcriptomic and clinical data for both tumor and normal samples were retrieved from public databases. Next, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. These DEGs were then intersected with 2030 mitochondrial-related genes and 683 T cell exhaustion-related genes obtained from relevant databases to pinpoint candidate genes. Moreover, regression analyses were carried out to refine the prognostic genes. A risk model was established to assess the risk score of BRCA patients. Cox regression analyses were utilized to determine the independent prognostic factors. Then a prognostic model was constructed. In addition, immune infiltration, drug sensitivity, and single-cell transcriptomics were integrated to dissect mechanisms. The expression of these genes in BRCA was validated by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). From 5041 DEGs, regression identified 7 prognostic genes (BCL2A1, GZMB, IRF7, MTHFD2, TFRC, JUN, and PPP1R15A). The accurate risk model stratified patients: high-risk correlated with suppressed immunity (p < 2.2e−16), elevated TIDE (p = 5.4e−14), and higher CI.1040 IC50 (cor = 0.63, p < 0.0001). Single-cell analysis revealed 6 types and MIF-(CD74 + CD44) crosstalk. RT-qPCR confirmed MTHFD2, TFRC, IRF7, BCL2A1 upregulation in tumor (p < 0.05). Risk score, age, race, N/M-stage were independent factors. Seven prognostic genes effectively predicted BRCA prognosis with independent prognostic factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01888-5.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA), defined as a heterogeneous group of malignancies originating from the mammary epithelium [1], represents the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide with approximately 2.3 million new cases annually, accounting for 24.5% of all female cancers and remaining a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [2]. This disease demonstrates significant molecular diversity, with major subtypes including hormone receptor-positive (estrogen receptor/ progesterone receptor (ER+/PR+), HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), each characterized by distinct pathological features such as genomic instability, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and metabolic reprogramming [3]. Current treatment modalities including surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted agents face substantial limitations such as drug resistance (particularly ESR1 mutations in ER+ cancers) and limited efficacy in TNBC cases [4]. Diagnostic challenges persist due to reliance on invasive procedures like tissue biopsies and costly genetic testing, while conventional imaging methods fail to detect 20–30% of early stage cases [2].

T cell exhaustion is defined as a dysfunctional state of T lymphocytes characterized by progressive loss of effector functions (e.g., reduced cytokine production and cytotoxicity), sustained expression of inhibitory receptors (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3), and epigenetic/metabolic reprogramming [5]. This state plays a pivotal role in tumor immune escape by enabling cancer cells to evade immune surveillance. In BRCA, malignant BRCA cells promote CD8+ T cell exhaustion through tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TEVs) that transfer active TGF-β type II receptor (TβRII) to stimulate TGF-β/SMAD signaling in recipient T cells [6], creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment associated with advanced disease stages and poor prognosis (e.g., shorter progression-free survival and reduced response to immunotherapy). While T cell exhaustion-related genes (TEXRGs) such as TOX, LAYN, and HAVCR2 show promise as diagnostic biomarkers for BRCA (demonstrating area under the curve (AUC) > 0.80 in some studies) [7], the systematic relationship between TEXRGs and disease progression remains poorly understood due to heterogeneity across BRCA subtypes and dynamic changes during treatment, highlighting the need for further research to elucidate their potential as reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is crucial to the pathogenesis of breast cancer. Under normal conditions, mitochondria are responsible for producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP), regulating apoptosis, and maintaining redox homeostasis [8]. However, in breast cancer, abnormalities such as impaired mitochondrial dynamics, increased oxidative stress, and biogenesis defects occur [9]. Factors like mitochondrial DNA mutations alter metabolic pathways, leading to cellular anti-apoptosis and the development of treatment resistance. Mitochondria-related genes (MRGs) may share regulatory pathways with TEXRGs [10], but the mechanisms of interaction between the two in BRCA remain unclear.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a high-throughput sequencing technology that analyzes the transcriptome at single-cell resolution, revealing cellular heterogeneity and overcoming the limitations of traditional bulk RNA sequencing. It can detect cell type-specific transcriptional changes [11]. In BRCA research, it provides new insights into the composition of the tumor microenvironment and can identify drug-resistant subclones and immunosuppressive cell populations. This study applies this technology because traditional bulk transcriptome analysis cannot resolve cell-specific molecular changes in breast cancer, whereas scRNA-seq can accurately depict transcriptional programs, reveal intercellular interactions, and identify potential therapeutic targets.

This study identifies prognosis genes associated with T cell exhaustion and mitochondria in BRCA through single-cell analysis and conventional transcriptome analysis, and constructs a prognostic model. A series of analyses and experimental validations are conducted based on the prognostic genes and model, aiming to provide valuable references for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of BRCA.

Materials and methods

Gene data and clinical information collection

Transcriptomic data, along with the corresponding survival and clinical details of BRCA tissues, were sourced from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-BRCA cohort data available on the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) (accessed on November 18, 2024). This cohort data served as the training set, consisting of 1084 BRCA tumor samples and 112 normal samples. The clinical information included aspects such as age, race, T-stage, N-stage, and M-stage.

The validation set was retrieved from the GSE88770 dataset (GPL570) on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). It was composed of RNA-seq data for 117 tumor samples along with their associated clinical information. Additionally, the single-cell dataset GSE161529 (GPL18573) included 17 mammary tissue samples, with 4 from BRCA tumors and 13 from normal tissues, sourced from both BRCA and normal samples. Another single-cell dataset, GSE243526 (GPL18573), comprised 12 BRCA tumor tissue samples and 4 control tissue samples.

Subsequently, 683 TEXRGs were acquired from the reference [12] (Online Resource S1). In the meantime, 2030 mitochondrial-related genes (MRGs) were obtained from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways) and through Gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) as per the reference [13] (Online Resource S2).

Differential expression and intersection analysis

Subsequently, to detect the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor and normal samples within the training set, differential expression analysis was performed using the R package "DESeq2" (v 1.38.0) [14]. The significance thresholds for DEGs identification were set at adjusted p (p.adj) < 0.05 and |log2 fold-change (log2FC)|> 1. To further visualize the results, the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1) [15] was utilized to generate a volcano plot, and the 10 genes with the most upregulated and 10 genes with the most downregulated genes were also labeled. Additionally, a heatmap was created the 100 genes with the most upregulated and 100 genes with the most downregulated genes utilizing the R package "ComplexHeatmap" (v 2.14.0) [16].

Furthermore, to identify MRGs associated with TEXRGs in BRCA, an intersection analysis was performed among DEGs, MRGs, and TEXRGs. The intersection analysis was visualized using the R package "ggvenn" (v 0.1.8) [17]. The intersected genes were then designated as candidate genes for further study.

Biological function analysis of candidate genes and Protein and protein interaction (PPI) network construction

Furthermore, to investigate the biological functions and pathways associated with the candidate genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were carried out on these genes. The R package "clusterProfiler" (v 4.7.1.3) [18] was employed for this purpose, with a significance cutoff set at p < 0.05. In particular, GO enrichment analysis categorized genes into three aspects: biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs).

Subsequently, in order to evaluate the interactions among proteins which were encoded by candidate genes, the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/) was performed with candidate genes. After obtaining the interaction information, isolated targets were removed, with a confidence score ≥ 0.15. Cytoscape (v 3.7.1) [19] was then used to construct the PPI network.

Screening of prognosis genes via univariate COX and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression

To explore the relationship between candidate genes and the prognosis of BRCA patients, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the candidate genes with the use of the R package "survival" (v 3.5.3) [20]. The candidate genes with p < 0.2 were further subjected to the proportional hazards (PH) assumption test (p > 0.05), and the candidate genes passing the PH assumption test were identified as candidate prognostic genes and were visualized using a forest plot.

To further screen the prognostic genes related to the prognosis of BRCA, LASSO regression analysis was performed with the use of the R package "glmnet" (v 4.1.4) [21] using the ten-fold cross-validation method. The optimal lambda was determined through cross-validation. Among the candidate lambda values, lambda.min, which demonstrated the minimum mean cross-validated error was prioritized over lambda.1se to maximize the model's predictive performance. The prognostic genes were then identified by the optimal lambda.

Using the coefficients derived from LASSO regression, a risk model was developed based on the selected prognostic genes. Using the "survminer" (v 0.4.9) [22]. BRCA samples with survival information in the training set were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the optimal cutoff value of the risk score.

The AUC values greater than 0.6 were indicate favorable accuracy of the model. Risk stratification plots integrating risk score distribution and survival status were visualized using the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1).

Subsequently, the same method was employed to validate the risk model on the validation set.

Independent prognostic analysis

To explore the variations in risk scores across different clinicopathological characteristic subgroups, including age (≤ 65 and > 65), race (Asian, Black, and White), pathologic stage (I, II, III, and IV), T-stage (I, II, III, and IV), N-stage (0, I, II, and III) and M-stage (0 and I), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used in the training set to compare the differences in risk scores among different subgroups (p < 0.05).

In the training set, the R package "survival" (v 3.5.3) was performed to perform univariate Cox regression analyses (p < 0.05) and PH assumption tests (p > 0.05) for seven variables: risk score, age, gender, race, stage, T-stage, and N-stage. Variables that passed the univariate Cox regression analyses and PH assumption tests were further screened using multivariate Cox regression analysis (p < 0.05) to identify independent prognostic factors.

Construction of nomogram model

In the training set, the R package "rms" (v 6.5.0) was utilized to construct a nomogram to visualize the multivariate regression model. Each factor in the tumor samples was assigned a score based on its contribution to the model. Based on the cumulative score, the survival probabilities of BRCA patients were predicted.

To further quantify calibration accuracy of the nomogram model, the R package "rms" (v 6.5.0) [24] was utilized to construct the calibration curves. The closer the slope of the curve was to 1, the more reliable the result.

Furthermore, the decision curve analysis (DCA) was carried out using the R package "ggDCA" (v 1.2) [25] to assess the clinical characteristics of the nomogram model. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the R package "timeROC" (v 0.4) [26]. The area under the curve (AUC) of the model was calculated, and the validity of each curve was evaluated. A threshold of AUC > 0.6 was set for this evaluation.

GSEA of BRCA

Next, differential expression analysis was performed between the high- and low-risk groups with the use of R package "DESeq2" (v 1.38.0) and the log2FC values were then calculated. All genes in the training set were ranked from largest to smallest according to the log2FC values. The "c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt" gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) database is used as the background gene set. Then, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the "clusterProfiler" package (v 4.7.1.3) (|normalized enrichment score (NES)|> 1 and p < 0.05).

Immune infiltration analysis

To investigate immune infiltration in BRCA, the relative proportions of 28 immune cell types [27] were assessed according to all samples of the training set, utilizing the R package "GSVA" (v 1.46.0). Based on all the samples in the training set, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (with a significance level of p < 0.05) was employed to analyze the differential immune cell infiltration between BRCA and normal samples. Moreover, Spearman correlation coefficients were computed to examine the relationships among the differentially infiltrated immune cells, as well as between the prognostic genes, and the differentially infiltrated immune cells. (|correlation coefficient (cor)|> 0.3 and a p < 0.05). To address potential limitations of GSVA analysis and to validate our findings using a deconvolution algorithm, we additionally employed CIBERSORTx [28] to estimate the relative proportions of 22 infiltrating immune cell types. Samples with a CIBERSORTx output p value < 0.05 were considered for subsequent analysis to ensure deconvolution accuracy. The differences in immune cell infiltration between high- and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05).

Tumor microenvironment characterization

The estimation of stromal and immune cells in malignant tumor tissues using expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm was employed to quantify tumor microenvironment components using the R package "ESTIMATE" (v 1.0.13) [29]. Stromal score, immune score, and combined ESTIMATE score were calculated to infer stromal cell abundance, immune cell infiltration levels, and tumor purity, respectively. Higher scores indicated greater stromal/immune cell presence or lower tumor purity.

To compare the score distributions between the risk groups, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were carried out. (p < 0.05). Violin plots were generated using the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1) to visualize these distributions.

Immune therapy analysis

Based on the BRCA patient samples with survival information from the training set, the expression levels of 46 immune checkpoints between the high-risk and low-risk groups were compared for significant differences using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05).

In addition, for the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) analysis, the TIDE scores of high- and low-risk groups were calculated for tumor samples which had survival information in the training set via the TIDE database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/login/). A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to compare the risk score and the TIDE score (p < 0.05).

Drug sensitivity and tumor mutation burden (TMB) analyses

Based on the TCGA-BRCA dataset of tumor samples, the values of the IC50 of 138 common chemotherapy and molecular targeted drugs for all patients were calculated using the R package "pRRophetic" (v 0.5) [30]. Correlation analysis between the calculated IC50 values of 138 common chemotherapy drugs and the risk score was conducted, and the correlation volcano map was drawn utilizing the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1). Drugs with |cor|> 0.3 and a p value < 0.05 were chosen. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was then performed to compare these drugs between the two risk groups. The top three drugs showing the most significant differences according to the p value were selected. The R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1) was used to present these top three drugs.

Simultaneously, the R package "MAfTools" (v 2.14.0) [31] was employed to determine the top 20 mutated genes separately in the samples of the high-risk group and those of the low-risk group. Additionally, the tumor mutation burden (TMB) for each individual sample was computed using the same R package, "MAfTools" (v 2.14.0).

Chromosome localization and gene–gene interaction (GGI) analyses

For chromosome localization analysis, the R package "RCircos" (v 1.36.0) [32] was used to analyze prognostic genes. A circular plot was constructed to visualize the distribution of prognostic genes on chromosomes.

The gene–gene interaction (GGI) network was constructed to identify potential gene interactions and to contribute to the understanding of the functions and processes associated with prognostic genes, using the GeneMANIA database [33].

Construction of regulatory network

Furthermore, to investigate the upstream regulatory factors of the prognostic genes, a transcription factor (TF)-mRNA-microRNA (miRNA) network was established. The TFs and miRNAs associated with the prognostic genes were retrieved from the NetworkAnalyst database (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/home.xhtml). Subsequently, the resulting TF-mRNA-miRNA network was visualized using Cytoscape (v 3.7.1).

Single-cell quality control

The single-cell analysis was performed, using the R package "Seurat" (v 5.0.1) [34]. The eligible cells and genes were selected from the GSE161529 through filtering out the ineligible ones according to the following criteria: 1) the genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed; 2) the cells containing fewer than 200 genes; 3) the cells with the numbers of RNA (nFeature) ≤ 350 and ≥ 4000 were removed; 4) the cells with the total gene expression counts (nCount) ≥ 15,000 were removed; 5) the cells containing more than 10% of mitochondrial genes were also removed. In the GSE243526 dataset, the criteria for cell and gene filtering were as follows: cells with nFeature greater than 5000 were excluded, cells with nCount greater than 20,000 were excluded, and cells in which the expression of mitochondrial genes accounted for more than 20% of the total gene expression were excluded. Then, in the GSE161529 and GSE243526 datasets, the LogNormalize function was used to normalize the eligible data. The FindVariableFeatures function was used to identify highly variable genes (HVGs). The data were normalized again through the ScaleData function. HVGs were then evaluated by principal component analysis (PCA). The optimal 30 principal components (PCs) were selected based on elbow plot inflection and JackStraw significance (p < 0.05). Using the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) method, cells in the GSE161529 and GSE243526 datasets were clustered at a resolution of 0.1 and 0.3, respectively. After that, in the GSE161529 dataset, the cell annotation was accomplished referring to marker expression from the literature [35] by FindAllMarkers function and the R package "SingleR" (v 2.0.0) [36]. In the GSE243526 dataset, marker genes were obtained from the literature to annotate cell clusters (PMID: 34493872, PMID: 38614094).

Cell–cell communication and cell enrichment analyses

In the GSE161529 and GSE243526 datasets, the communication types among annotated cells were detected using the R package "CellChat" (v 1.6.1) [37] according to the "CellChatDB.human" from CelllPhoneDB (https://www.cellphonedb.org/). The pairing status of ligand–receptor pairs among different cells was also visualized in bubble plots by the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1).

Then the R package "ReactomeGSA" (v1.12.0) [38] was performed to detect the functional pathways of the annotated cells between tumor and normal samples. Then the top ten pathways with the greatest changes were shown in a heatmap using the R package "ggplot2" (v 3.5.1).

Cell subclustering and pseudotime analysis

T cells were re-clustered using the R package "Seurat" (v 5.0.1) using UMAP dimensionality reduction. PCs were selected via elbow plot and JackStraw test (p < 0.05). Clustering at resolution of 0.3 then identified subtypes.

In this study, the differentiation trajectories of crucial cells were investigated through pseudotime analysis by the R package "monocle" (v 2.26) [39]. Subsequently, the differentiation trajectory of each crucial cell was identified, and prognostic gene expression trends in the entire differentiation state were also detected.

Expression level of prognostic genes and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) experiment

Then, the expression levels of prognostic genes were compared between tumor and normal samples in the training set (p < 0.05), and box plots were drawn for visualization.

The expression of these prognostic genes was validated via RT-qPCR. A set of 10 samples were obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, including 5 control samples (para-cancerous tissues) and 5 BRCA (tumor) samples. All participants provided informed consent, and the studies involving human participants are reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the SweScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). RT-qPCR was then carried out using the 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences for PCR were shown in Online Resource S3. The specificity of the primers was analyzed via melting curve. If the melting curve showed a single peak, the primers were considered specific. For each sample (5 BRCA samples and 5 control samples), three technical replicates were performed to ensure the reliability of the measurements. GAPDH was used as the reference gene for normalization. The relative quantification of mRNAs was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [40]. The results from the RT-qPCR were exported to Excel, and then imported into Graphpad Prism 10 (https://www.graphpad.com/) for statistical analysis and visualization.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatic analyses were executed using the R programming language (v 4.2.2). To compare the differences between BRCA patients and healthy controls, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. Spearman correlation analysis was carried out to assess the relationships between genes and immune cells.

For the RT-qPCR experiments, a t test was applied to analyze differential gene expression. In all analysis results, a p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. For the RT-qPCR experiments, a t test was used to analyze differential gene expression. In all analysis results, a p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. For RT-qPCR analysis, the t test was employed for statistical comparisons (p < 0.05). Notably, **** represented p < 0.0001, *** represented p < 0.001, ** represented p < 0.01, * represented p < 0.05, and ns represented p > 0.05.

Prognostic model validation

To validate the effectiveness of the risk model, the model was applied to the validation set. First, the risk score for each BRCA patient in the validation set was calculated. Then, the samples were split using a cutoff value of − 0.056. The high-risk group consisted of 117 samples, while the low-risk group had 88 samples. Notably, the risk curve and survival status graphs were constructed, revealing that the proportion of deceased patients was higher in the high-risk group (Fig. 5a).

The K–M curve was plotted according to the high-and low-risk groups. The Log-rank test demonstrated a significant difference in survival between the two groups (p = 0.036), with the high-risk group having a lower survival rate (Fig. 5b).

The AUC values of the ROC curves for 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year survival in the validation set were 0.74, 0.70, and 0.68 respectively. These values demonstrated a notable level of predictive accuracy (Fig. 5c).

Overall, these validation results verified the effectiveness of the prognostic model in the validation set. This provided additional support for the potential clinical application of the prognostic model.

Discussion

BRCA represents one of the most prevalent malignancies among women worldwide, with its development and progression being influenced by multiple factors including genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and immune microenvironment [2]. Conventional diagnostic and prognostic assessment methods exhibit certain limitations, highlighting the urgent need for developing novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

In this study, we analyzed the BRCA transcriptome data from the GEO database and identified seven prognostic genes BCL2A1, GZMB, IRF7, MTHFD2, and TFRC by differential expression analysis, survival analysis, and functional enrichment analysis, JUN and PPP1R15A. The prognostic value of these candidate genes was further validated using PCR and clinical samples, demonstrating their diagnostic and predictive potential. Furthermore, we investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms through which these genes contribute to BRCA pathogenesis, providing novel insights for precision medicine approaches in BRCA management. The integration of bioinformatic predictions with experimental validation offers a comprehensive strategy for identifying reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets in BRCA.

BCL2A1 (Bcl-2-related protein A1) is an anti-apoptotic gene that inhibits programmed cell death, and is a critical mechanism promoting tumor cell survival and proliferation [41]. In diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), BCL2A1 (also known as BFL1) overexpression drives resistance to venetoclax, while in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), particularly monocytic leukemia (FAB M4/M5), CEBPB-mediated upregulation of BCL2A1 confers resistance to multiple agents including MDM2 inhibitors, BH3 mimetics, and venetoclax combinations [42]. These findings underscore the therapeutic implications of BCL2A1 across malignancies and suggest its potential role in BRCA pathogenesis and treatment resistance.

GZMB (Granzyme B) is secreted by activated cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) and NK cells and induces target cell apoptosis through caspase-dependent pathways [43]. Immune infiltration analyses demonstrate that GZMB expression correlates with immune cell infiltration levels in the tumor microenvironment (TME), suggesting its potential role in modulating immunogenicity and influencing BRCA prognosis [44]. Notably, decreased GZMB expression has been associated with dysfunctional tumor-resident NK cells in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), while elevated Granzyme B secretion from CD8+ T cells enhances tumor cell apoptosis in rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) models [45]. Furthermore, recent studies reveal that IFNγ + GZMB + cytotoxic lymphocytes promote CXD101-induced cleavage of Gasdermin E (GSDME), thereby triggering pyroptosis—an inflammatory form of programmed cell death that may enhance antitumor immunity [46]. These findings collectively highlight GZMB's multifaceted roles in tumor immunity, spanning apoptosis induction, immune microenvironment modulation, and pyroptosis regulation across various cancer types.

IRF7 (Interferon regulatory factor 7) serves as a master regulator of type I interferon (IFN-α/β) signaling and plays crucial roles in immune responses and cell death pathways, including inflammatory signaling, interferon response promotion, and ferroptosis mediation under certain conditions [47]. While IRF7 expression is upregulated in tumor samples, its high expression in BRCA correlates with poor prognosis, identifying it as a significant prognostic gene [48]. Interestingly, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), BCL7A-mediated upregulation of IRF7 reduces malignancy and decreases resistance to cytarabine, whereas in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) tissues, IRF7 suppresses RPS18 transcription. Furthermore, IRF7 functions as both a transcription factor and a target of microRNA regulation, demonstrates RPS18 transcriptional suppression in M1 macrophages, exhibits dynamic expression during T cell differentiation, and actively participates in interferon responses [49].

MTHFD2 (Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2), a pivotal enzyme in mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism, has been found to be significantly upregulated in BRCA, with its expression levels strongly correlating with patient survival outcomes, suggesting its potential role in BRCA pathogenesis through the regulation of one-carbon metabolic pathways [50]. Extensive research demonstrates that MTHFD2 is overexpressed across various malignancies, including renal cell carcinoma (RCC), where elevated MTHFD2 expression in sunitinib-resistant RCC cells is associated with poor clinical prognosis [51]. Mechanistic studies reveal that MTHFD2 inhibition disrupts oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in osteoclasts, resulting in diminished ATP production and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential [52]. Given its mitochondrial localization and critical metabolic functions, these findings collectively indicate that MTHFD2 likely contributes to BRCA progression by modulating mitochondrial functionality, potentially through its regulation of energy production and biosynthetic processes essential for tumor growth and survival.

TFRC (Transferrin receptor), identified as a prognostic gene in BRCA in our study, is a cell surface receptor that mediates iron uptake [53]. Published literature confirms TFRC's role as both a prognostic marker in BRCA and a novel predictive biomarker for neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) response [54]. TFRC likely influences BRCA patient outcomes by regulating tumor cell growth, metastasis, and drug resistance through its iron-uptake function, as iron is essential for cellular proliferation and tumor cells require increased iron to sustain rapid growth [55]. Overexpressed in multiple cancers and associated with tumor proliferation and metastasis, TFRC may promote cancer progression by modulating cellular iron acquisition. Additionally, TFRC participates in endocytosis and signal transduction pathways that affect BRCA tumor cell behavior [56], suggesting multiple mechanisms through which it may impact cancer biology.

JUN (Transcription factor JUN), a subunit of activator protein 1 (AP1) required for IL-10 gene expression, was found to exhibit gradually decreasing expression levels in T cells along pseudotime trajectories and downregulated in tumor samples in our study [57]. Previous reports have identified c-JUN (the Jun proto-oncogene, an AP-1 transcription factor subunit) via screening WBP2-overexpressing BRCA cells, with nuclear phosphorylated c-JUN maintained at high levels in treatment-resistant breast tumors and WBP2-overexpressing BC cells [58]. The JUN/AP-1 complex plays crucial roles in transcriptional regulation, tumorigenesis, and immune responses, participating not only in the expression regulation of immune-related genes like IL-10 but also being closely associated with tumor cell drug resistance [59]. These findings collectively suggest that JUN/AP-1 may serve as an important therapeutic target for both cancer treatment and immunotherapy approaches.

PPP1R15A (Protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 15A), an endoplasmic reticulum stress response factor, was found to be significantly upregulated under glucose deprivation conditions along with transcription factor JUN, which binds to and activates the PPP1R15A promoter [60]. This regulatory subunit maintains gastric cancer cell survival during energy stress by modulating autophagy, enabling cellular adaptation to hostile microenvironments [61]. As a myeloid cell-enriched ER stress regulator, PPP1R15A demonstrates elevated expression in gastric tumor tissues and serves as an independent prognostic risk factor in gastric cancer cohorts, where its overexpression promotes proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and induces cell cycle arrest. Notably, rare PPP1R15A variants correlate with reduced BRCA risk, suggesting its inhibition may represent a potential preventive strategy, while its overexpression in human monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) is associated with poor prognosis and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) non-responsiveness in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients [62].

The mitochondrial-immune axis (MRG-TEXRG axis) has been identified as a critical regulator of the immune landscape during BRCA progression [63]. High-risk patients demonstrate suppressed stromal/immune scores, with TIDE analysis revealing significantly reduced immunotherapy response in this subgroup (p = 5.4 × 10⁻14), potentially mediated through IRF7-dependent suppression of type I interferon signaling [64]. Notably, downregulation of granzyme B (GZMB) in high-risk tumors indicates impaired cytotoxic T cell function, mirroring previous observations of PD-1 in hepatocellular carcinoma [65]. These findings collectively establish the MRG-TEXRG axis as a pivotal determinant of both tumor microenvironment composition and therapeutic outcomes in BRCA.

Single-cell analyses have revealed the spatial expression patterns of prognostic genes within the tumor ecosystem: JUN and IRF7 show elevated expression in T cell subsets, potentially influencing exhaustion phenotypes through modulation of IFN-γ signaling, while GZMB deficiency in cytotoxic T cells indicates functional exhaustion [64]. Furthermore, activation of the MIF-CD74 axis in epithelial-macrophage communication may promote immunosuppressive microenvironment formation via the NF-κB pathway, consistent with reduced TIDE scores in high-risk groups, suggesting that targeting mitochondrial-immune interaction nodes could potentially reverse resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [65].

Although this study focuses on BRCA, the tumor microenvironment mechanisms we identified—related to T cell exhaustion and mitochondrial dysfunction—share significant commonalities with various malignancies accompanied by bone metastasis. As a common site of distant metastasis in BRCA, the bone microenvironment also exhibits key processes such as immunosuppression and metabolic reprogramming [66–68]. "Skeletal Metastases of Unknown Primary (SMUP)" poses particular challenges in clinical diagnosis and treatment, often relying on bone metabolic marker-based therapeutic strategies [69]. The genes identified in this study, such as MTHFD2 and TFRC, are not only involved in tumor cell metabolism and the regulation of ferroptosis but may also influence the osteoblast/osteoclast balance and immune cell function in bone metastatic niches, showing remarkable similarities to the biological processes implicated in SMUP [69]. Therefore, the prognostic model we developed and the uncovered microenvironment regulatory mechanisms are not only applicable to BRCA but may also provide new perspectives for understanding the immune–metabolic interplay in bone metastases of other solid tumors, further broadening the translational value and interdisciplinary significance of this research.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the drug sensitivity predictions obtained through bioinformatics methods may not fully capture the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and in vivo pharmacokinetics in patients. Second, the predictive accuracy of the risk model remains moderate and is currently insufficient to directly support clinical decision-making. Third, the validation of the current model is still inadequate, and future studies should expand the sample size for further verification. Additionally, the calibration and discriminative ability of the model across clinically relevant subgroups (e.g., stratified by receptor status, treatment regimen, and disease stage) require further evaluation. Finally, the sample size for RT-qPCR validation is relatively small, and the specific mechanisms of the prognostic genes need to be confirmed through in vivo or in vitro experiments. To address these limitations, we plan to integrate organoid models and pharmacokinetic simulations to construct an in vitro tumor microenvironment system for validating bioinformatically predicted drug sensitivities. We also aim to refine the risk model by incorporating multi-omics data and clinical features to improve predictive accuracy until it meets clinical decision-making standards. Furthermore, we will expand multi-center sample sizes for external validation and evaluate the model’s performance across subgroups stratified by factors such as receptor status and treatment regimens. Additionally, we will increase the sample size for RT-qPCR validation and investigate the mechanisms of prognostic genes through functional cellular experiments and animal models to provide a more robust foundation for clinical translation.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a prognostic model for BRCA by integrating genes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and T cell exhaustion. Although individual genes such as MTHFD2 and TFRC have previously been implicated in cancer, our work highlights the prognostic power of a seven-gene combination comprising BCL2A1, GZMB, IRF7, MTHFD2, TFRC, JUN, and PPP1R15A. This prognostic model provides novel insights into the metabolic-immune crosstalk in BRCA and suggests potential directions for developing mitochondria-targeted immunotherapies.
