Probing the metabolic regulator and candidate tumor suppressor ALDH1L1 as a target for non-small cell lung cancer gene therapy
Authors: Amira A Abdellatef, Blake R Rushing, Susan Sumner, Natalia I Krupenko, Sergey A Krupenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractALDH1L1, a major cytosolic folate enzyme and key regulator of one-carbon metabolism, is strongly and ubiquitously downregulated in lung adenocarcinomas (LUADs) and silenced in LUAD cell lines. Analysis of publicly available databases indicates that ALDH1L1 expression is associated with better prognosis and overall survival in LUAD patients. Here we used lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1 to target metabolism in ALDH1L1-deficient LUAD cell lines A549 and H460. In these cells, ALDH1L1 expression led to strong inhibition of proliferation, colony formation, and migration. Untargeted metabolomic analysis has shown that ALDH1L1 knockin produces a strong effect on the metabotype of A549 cells including alterations in the tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates and acylcarnitines, indicative of the impairment of cellular energetics. We have further demonstrated that in mice ALDH1L1, lentiviral delivery dramatically inhibits growth of subcutaneous xenograft tumors derived from A549 cells and prevents lung colonization in a tail-vein model. These effects prolonged for the duration of the experiments (6 weeks) with no noticeable decrease in ALDH1L1 protein levels at the endpoint of the study. We did not observe side effects of ALDH1L1 delivery on animal health when compared with the control group. Our study suggests that ALDH1L1 delivery could be a promising gene therapy approach for treating LUAD.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, ALDH1L1, lung adenocarcinoma, A549, H460, untargeted metabolomics, mouse xenograft tumors, lentiviral delivery, gene therapy

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths each year.1 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common form of lung cancer, accounting for about 85% of all cases. NSCLC is represented by several histological subtypes, the most common of which are lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).1 LUAD is particularly deadly and characterized by poor long-term survival due to its aggressive behavior, frequent late-stage diagnosis, and high metastatic potential.2 Despite advances in targeted therapies, such as epidermal growth factor receptor and anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitors, as well as immunotherapies, the 5-year survival rate for LUAD remains below 20%, especially for those diagnosed at advanced stages.3 This highlights the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that might improve the outcomes for LUAD patients. Metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer, enabling cells to meet the elevated biosynthetic and energy demands necessary for rapid tumor growth.4 Accordingly, targeting the metabolic vulnerabilities of cancer cells has become an attractive approach in chemotherapy.4

One of the metabolic pathways most intensively investigated as a target for chemotherapy is folate metabolism.5 Although this pathway involves more than 20 enzymes, only a handful of them are targeted in cancer treatment.6 One of the folate enzymes, aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member L1 (ALDH1L1, 10-formyltetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase), catalyzes the irreversible NADP+-dependent oxidation of 10-formyltetrahydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate and CO2.7 This reaction is one of the key regulatory steps in one-carbon metabolism and is essential to support critical cellular functions, including nucleotide synthesis, methylation reactions, and redox homeostasis.7,8 In recent years, several studies have shown that ALDH1L1 is frequently downregulated or silenced in a broad spectrum of human solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma, LUAD, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, neuroblastoma, breast cancer, and oral squamous cell carcinoma (reviewed in Krupenko et al.8). The mechanism underlying ALDH1L1 downregulation in cancers is methylation within a large CpG island covering the proximal promoter, the first exon, and part of the first intron in the ALDH1L1 gene.9 Low expression of ALDH1L1 in malignant tumors has been correlated with increased proliferation, enhanced metastatic potential, and poor survival rate, outcomes indicating that the loss of ALDH1L1 provides a survival advantage for cancer cells. Such an advantage is likely linked to metabolic reprogramming, which re-directs the flux of one-carbon groups toward biosynthetic processes crucial for cancer cell proliferation and tumor progression.8 These findings raised the question of whether ALDH1L1 should be considered as a candidate tumor suppressor.10

In addition to findings in patient cancers, the implication of ALDH1L1 as a candidate tumor suppressor is supported by studies in cell culture and animal models. Thus, in many ALDH1L1-deficient cancer cell lines, the forced expression of the enzyme produces strong antiproliferative effects and induces cell death.11,12 Furthermore, the knockout of Aldh1l1 in mice increases animal susceptibility to liver carcinogenesis in the diethylnitrosamine (DEN) model, suggesting the protective role of ALDH1L1 against liver cancer.13 This proliferation-suppressive effect of ALDH1L1 was also demonstrated in oral squamous cell carcinoma xenograft tumors in nude mice.14 These studies suggest that ALDH1L1 might not be a suitable target for antifolate drugs and, instead, increased ALDH1L1 activity would be desirable in tumor treatments. Gene therapy offers a powerful means to achieve this goal, providing a method to reintroduce or enhance the expression of tumor suppressor genes in cancer cells.15 Therefore, in the present study, we investigated ALDH1L1 as a potential target for cancer gene therapy. For the proof of principle, we focused on xenograft tumors derived from LUAD cells and the lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1.

Results

Downregulation of ALDH1L1 expression is associated with poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma patients

To assess the clinical relevance of ALDH1L1 downregulation in lung cancers, we used the GEPIA2 web server: http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index, which allows the comparison of gene expression between normal and tumor samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) and The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases.16 Our analysis showed a noticeable downregulation of ALDH1L1 mRNA in LUAD and LUSC tissues compared with normal lung tissues (Figures 1A and 1B), with a stronger difference observed for LUAD. Analysis performed using the same platform for LUAD subtypes17 showed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01) in ALDH1L1 mRNA expression between normal tissues and proximal inflammatory tumors (Figure 1B), the subtype associated with p53 mutations.18 Of note, the ALDH1L1 antiproliferative effect in cancer cells was linked to the p53 status.19,20,21 The Kaplan-Meier analysis of the disease-free survival for LUAD patients indicated that low ALDH1L1 mRNA expression is associated with poor long-term prognosis, whereas high expression correlates with improved outcomes (Figure 1C). A similar trend was seen when OncoLnc: http://www.oncolnc.org/, OncoDB: https://oncodb.org/, or UALCAN: https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html web servers were used for survival analysis (data not shown). Although data from Figure 1C did not reach statistical significance, which is a common occurrence for a small group size analysis, they indicate the link between ALDH1L1 expression and outcomes in patients with LUADs. Furthermore, when we applied the Subtype Filter function in GEPIA2, we saw a statistically significant difference for the disease-free survival (Figure 1D). We also assessed ALDH1L1 mRNA levels in a panel of LUAD cell lines using data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA): www.proteinatlas.org. Consistent with our earlier findings,9,11 ALDH1L1 mRNA is very low or undetectable in the majority of lung cancer cell lines (Figure 1E; human bladder transitional cell papilloma RT4 cell line expressing high levels of ALDH1L122 was used as the reference in this analysis). Overall, our analysis underscores a significant underexpression of ALDH1L1 in LUAD tumors and cell lines, as well as the association of low protein expression with poor prognosis.

Lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1 in LUAD cell lines inhibits proliferation and motility

To explore ALDH1L1 as a gene therapy target in LUAD, we investigated the response to ALDH1L1 lentiviral delivery in two LUAD cell lines, A549 and H460, which do not express the enzyme (Figure 1E). Generated recombinant lentivirus was efficient in transducing these cells (Figure 2A, the transduction is evident by green fluorescence indicative of GFP expression). Transduction with viral stocks containing different amounts of viral particles determined that the maximal MOI was reached at 50 particles per cell (Figures S2A and S2B). The kinetics of GFP expression after transduction correlated well with ALDH1L1 protein expression, which was seen as early as 24 h post-infection and reached the peak at 72 h in both cell lines (Figures 2B and 2C). Strong ALDH1L1 expression was further confirmed at the level of ALDH1L1 mRNA, which was measured by RT-PCR in A549 cells (Figure S3). RT4 cells served as a positive control in these experiments (Figure S3). We have previously reported that re-expression of ALDH1L1 in A549 cells evokes several downstream mechanisms, including the activation of JNKs and phosphorylation suppression of actin depolymerization factor cofilin, thus leading to the inhibition of proliferation and motility.11,23 These findings were gained upon transient transfection of ALDH1L1 or in a stable Tet-On clone. Here, we have shown similar cellular responses using the therapy-oriented lentiviral delivery. Transduction of A549 and H460 cells with virus carrying ALDH1L1 caused profound cell death and strongly inhibited proliferation (Figures 2A, and 2C), whereas no effect was seen with lentivirus carrying GFP protein only (Figure S4). The results of the colony formation assays indicate that ALDH1L1 knockin reduced the colony-forming ability of A549 and H460 cells (Figures 2D). ALDH1L1 expression also significantly inhibited the motility of A549 cells, as was demonstrated by the wound healing assay (Figure 2E). Similar responses to ALDH1L1 delivery were observed in H460 cells (Figure 2F). These experiments demonstrated the high efficiency of the lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1 into LUAD cell lines and confirmed the antiproliferative effect of ALDH1L1.

Effect of ALDH1L1 expression on the metabotype of A549 cells

ALDH1L1 effect on cellular proliferation is likely the result of altered metabolism.19,22,24 To investigate the effect of ALDH1L1 expression on the metabotype of A549 cells, we have performed an untargeted metabolomics analysis of cells transduced with either GFP- or ALDH1L1-carrying virus. After preprocessing, a total of 15,486 peaks were assigned to compounds either in the in-house library or in public databases (Table S1). Both unsupervised (principal-component analysis [PCA], Figure 3A) and supervised (Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis [OPLS-DA]) analyses using all peaks showed a good differentiation (OPLS-DA model statistics R2X: 0.28, R2Y: 0.996, Q2: 0.699) between ALDH1L1-null A549 cells and cells transduced for ALDH1L1 expression. Overall, 1,011 metabolites were significantly (fold change>2 and p < 0.05) increased and 774 metabolites were significantly decreased in A549 cells upon ALDH1L1 expression. Pathway analysis indicated that ALDH1L1 expression affects the metabolism of amino acids, nucleotides, and glutathione, as well as tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates and pyruvate (Figures 3B and S5). Of note, a large increase (2- to 20-fold) was also seen for many acylcarnitines in response to ALDH1L1 expression (Figures 3C and S6). PCA based only on acylcarnitines that were assigned in this study (95 total) showed good differentiation between groups with strong OPLS-DA model statistics (R2X: 0.369, R2Y: 0.994, Q2: 0.913) (Figure 3D). Together with decreases in the assigned TCA cycle intermediates (Figures 3B and S5), we interpreted these data as the inability of acylcarnitines to feed acetyl groups to the TCA cycle. ATP levels upon ALDH1L1 knockin were decreased by about 30% (Figure S7), supporting the effect of the enzyme on cellular energetics.

Given the significant impact of ALDH1L1 expression on acylcarnitine levels, we investigated potential differences in carnitine palmitoyltransferase expression, CPT1, and CPT2. CPT1 plays a crucial role in fatty acid oxidation by generating long-chain acylcarnitines from acyl-coenzyme A (CoA), which are then transported across the inner mitochondrial membrane via the carnitine/acylcarnitine translocase. CPT2 subsequently converts long-chain acylcarnitines back to acyl-CoA, facilitating their oxidation to acetyl-CoA25 (schematically illustrated by Figure 4A). We assessed protein and mRNA levels of CPT1A and CPT2 in ALDH1L1-null A549 cells and in cells transduced for ALDH1L1 expression. Our results showed a slight increase in CPT1A protein expression 48 h after ALDH1L1 transfection, whereas no differences were observed at 72 h (Figure 4B). Small differences in the CPT2 protein levels were also seen upon ALDH1L1 expression (Figures 4B and 4C). However, we did not observe differences in mRNA expression levels for either CPT1A or CPT2 (Figures 4D and 4E). Differences in mRNA expression for CPT1B and CROT were small and statistically non-significant (Figures 4D and 4E). Three enzymes of acylcarnitine metabolism, CPT1C, CRAT, and CACT, showed noticeable and statistically significant decrease in mRNA expression in response to ALDH1L1 transduction (Figures 4D and 4E). These findings suggest that the effect of ALDH1L1 on acylcarnitine metabolism might be independent of CPT expression but could involve the function of other carnitine metabolism enzymes.

ALDH1L1 lentiviral delivery inhibits xenograft tumor growth in subcutaneous mouse model

To investigate whether ALDH1L1 antiproliferative effects extend beyond cultured cells, we have established subcutaneous tumors from A549 cells in athymic nude mice. The setup of this study is depicted in Figure 5A. In these experiments, we used A549 cells stably expressing red fluorescent protein (A549-RFP cells), enabling visualization of tumors via fluorescence imaging (Figure S8A). These cells did not show any differences in proliferation compared with parental cells (Figure S8B). When A549-derived tumors reached the size of 50–100 mm3, mice were randomized into two groups. One group was injected intratumorally (i.t.) with 3 × 107 transduction units (TU) of the lentiviral vector carrying GFP, LV-GFP virus (control), whereas the other group received an equal dose of LV-ALDH1L1 lentiviral particles. Tumor growth was monitored for up to 8 weeks. We observed that injection of LV-ALDH1L1 significantly delayed tumor progression compared with the control (Figures 5B–5D). At the endpoint of the experiment (8 weeks), tumors from the ALDH1L1-treated mouse group were smaller and had significantly lower weights than tumors from the control group (Figures 5E and 5F). Of note, a single injection of the recombinant lentivirus carrying ALDH1L1 was sufficient to express a high level of the protein in tumors for 6 weeks. The expression of ALDH1L1 in the LV-ALDH1L1-transduced group was still evident 6 weeks post-injection (Figure 5G). Strong green fluorescence signals observed in flash-frozen tumor samples in both animal groups indicated good transduction efficiency with either control or ALDH1L1-carrying virus (Figure 5H). Of note, we did not observe differences in body weights of mice or organs weight between groups (Figures S9 and S10). The mice remained healthy and showed no signs of distress throughout the duration of the experiment. No signs of organ enlargement, discoloration, or other visible abnormalities were detected during necropsy. These data suggest the lack of noticeable adverse effects of the i.t. lentivirus injection on the overall animal health.

ALDH1L1 lentiviral delivery reduces lung colonization by cancer cells

Next, we investigated the effect of ALDH1L1 delivery on tumor growth using a tail-vein injection model.26 The tail-vein injection is commonly used as a simple metastasis model but is also employed to test gene delivery as a cancer therapy approach.27,28,29 The application of A549-RFP cells in these experiments allowed us to monitor lung colonization and tumor progression in live animals, as well as postmortem tumor burden, which otherwise could be a limitation in this model.30 The setup of this experiment is depicted in Figure 6A, with mice monitored for 42 days. We observed a significant reduction in fluorescence intensities in the lungs of mice treated with ALDH1L1-carrying virus (intravenous injections), compared with the control group treated with only GFP-carrying virus (Figures 6B and 6C), an indication of tumor growth inhibition. Similar differences in tumor burden were observed in isolated lungs at the endpoint of the experiment (Figure 5, Figure 6D and 6E). Furthermore, the weight of the lungs in the treatment group was significantly lower than in the control group (Figures 6F and 6G), consistent with fewer tumor nodules, as lung weight typically correlates with tumor burden.31,32 We have also noted a decrease in body mass at the endpoint of the experiment in the control group compared with LV-ALDH1L1 treatment group (Figure 6H, p = 0.0578 at 6-week time point), which was consistent with distress, immobility, and labored breathing observed in these mice. We attributed these symptoms to the increased tumor burden in the lungs. H&E staining confirmed that fewer metastatic foci were present in the lungs of LV-ALDH1L1-treated mice compared with the control group (Figure 6I), underscoring the tumor-inhibitory effect of ALDH1L1. Histopathological examination of kidney and liver tissues did not show overall tissue abnormalities or noticeable differences between the treatment and control groups (Figures S11A and S11B), indicating the lack of off-target effects or toxicity from lentiviral transfection.

Discussion

ALDH1L1, an enzyme of folate metabolism, is highly expressed in several tissues in humans. At the same time, the prevailing consensus in the literature is that ALDH1L1 expression is significantly reduced in malignant tumors compared with normal tissues (reviewed in Krupenko et al.8). This downregulation, caused by methylation within the CpG island at the 5′-end of the ALDH1L1 gene, has been observed across various cancer types,14,33,34,35,36 suggesting ALDH1L1 as a candidate tumor suppressor. This protein, however, should not be considered as a typical tumor suppressor but rather as a metabolic regulator, which restricts the flux of one-carbon groups toward biosynthetic processes in the cell, thus exerting an antiproliferative effect (schematically depicted in Figure 7). In support of its antiproliferative role, the enzyme is downregulated in the S-phase of the cell cycle in cultured mouse embryonic fibroblasts but expressed in quiescent cells.37 Biochemical pathways under the immediate control of ALDH1L1 include the conversion of serine to glycine, biosynthesis of IMP, and remethylation of homocysteine to methionine.11,22,24,38 Because glycine, purines, and methionine themselves are involved in multiple metabolic and regulatory pathways, ALDH1L1 has a broader effect on cellular metabolism. In support of the role of ALDH1L1 in shaping the metabolic landscape, several studies have shown a widespread effect of ALDH1L1 on cellular metabotypes.22,24,39

Still, the role of ALDH1L1 in cancer is poorly understood. Given the strong correlation between ALDH1L1 downregulation and the development of aggressive tumors, as well as the decreased rate of patient survival, it is likely that ALDH1L1 expression promotes a non-proliferative metabotype, whereas the metabotype associated with the enzyme loss is advantageous for malignant growth (Figure 7). In support of such a role of ALDH1L1 in cancer, our study in Aldh1l1 knockout mice showed that the loss of the enzyme accelerated the growth of larger liver tumors in response to DEN, a chemical carcinogen commonly used to induce hepatocellular carcinoma in rodent models.13 Furthermore, large liver tumors formed in wild-type mice after DEN administration have lost ALDH1L1 expression, the effect commonly seen in human tumors.13 In agreement with these data, Qu et al. showed that the growth of oral squamous cell carcinoma-derived xenograft tumors in mice inversely correlate with ALDH1L1 expression.14 In this study, investigators used lentiviral expression of the protein in cell lines with low ALDH1L1 expression or small hairpin RNA knockdown of the protein in cell lines with elevated levels of ALDH1L1. All these findings imply that the restoration of ALDH1L1 expression in human cancers might inhibit tumor growth and potentially prevent metastasis as well. Therefore, the expression of ALDH1L1 in malignant tumors might represent a novel therapeutic strategy. One of the approaches to achieve such expression is viral delivery of ALDH1L1 to tumors, a strategy commonly used in the past years (reviewed in Roma-Rodrigues et al.,15 Dunbar et al.,40 and Bisserier et al.41).

To explore the therapeutic potential of ALDH1L1 knockin in tumor treatment, we employed the recombinant lentivirus to deliver ALDH1L1 to LUAD xenograft tumors in mice. LUAD tumors commonly have low expression of ALDH1L1, with lung cancer cell lines typically not expressing this protein.9 Our previous studies with one of these cell lines, A549, have shown a strong antiproliferative effect of forced ALDH1L1 expression in these cells.42 In those published studies, we used Tet-On inducible expression of ALDH1L1, which allowed us to investigate the effect of ALDH1L1 expression within physiological levels of the protein observed in human tissues. In the present study, the lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1 in two LUAD cell lines led to protein expression, which was similar to that achieved by inducible expression in the stable Tet-On clone.42 Our comparative untargeted metabolomic analysis of ALDH1L1-deficient and ALDH1L1-expressing A549 cells revealed a dramatic effect of the enzyme on cellular metabotype, supporting metabolic reprogramming as the mechanism for ALDH1L1 antiproliferative effect. The most notable effect of ALDH1L1 was on acylcarnitines, with many of them being highly elevated upon ALDH1L1expression. Of note, an opposite effect on acylcarnitines was observed in cultured human skin fibroblasts for the mitochondrial 10-formyltetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase isozyme, ALDH1L2,43 which was highlighted as a tumor-promoting enzyme.44 In support of the role of acylcarnitine metabolism in ALDH1L1 antiproliferative effect, a recent study in cancer patients has shown decreased levels of short-medium-chain acylcarnitines in NSCLC compared with paired normal tissues.45 Precise mechanisms linking folate metabolism to acylcarnitines are not clear at present. Since the current work characterized the metabolic effects of ALDH1L1 status using untargeted metabolomics methods, future studies with targeted methods and sufficient sample sizes for multiple testing corrections should be used to validate these findings. It is also not clear at present whether ALDH1L1 effects on acylcarnitines occur solely through the input of relevant metabolites or whether additional regulation through acylcarnitine pathway enzymes contributes to these effects.

Gene therapy strategies for the treatment of lung cancer have been explored, and they represent a considerable interest.46,47 The goal of cancer gene therapy is to selectively affect the malignant tissues without producing adverse effects on healthy cells.48 Our data have shown in two mouse models, subcutaneous xenografts and tail-vein injection, that lentiviral delivery of ALDH1L1 resulted in a dramatic reduction in tumor growth, underscoring a potential therapeutic role of ALDH1L1-based gene therapy. Importantly, even single i.t. injection of the recombinant lentivirus carrying ALDH1L1 produced a strong inhibitory effect on tumors in the xenograft model in our study. Injection of lentivirus via the tail vein leads to its systemic circulation and distribution between several organs.49 Although the lungs receive a significant portion of the injected lentiviral particles, to enhance or sustain gene expression in the lungs multiple doses of the lentivirus may be required. In our experiments with the tail-vein/lung cancer model, two injections of the recombinant lentivirus were sufficient to produce a strong tumor suppressor effect. Of note, the tail-vein injection is one of the common models to study metastasis.50 Since ALDH1L1 was shown to inhibit cell motility,23 an important criterion for cancer metastasis,51 the tumor-targeted expression of the protein might also inhibit metastatic tumor spreading.

The role of ALDH1L1 in LUAD is not fully understood, but expression of this protein is likely a contributing factor in LUAD pathogenesis. Based on the findings that the protein is often downregulated in LUAD while also producing an antiproliferative effect upon re-expression in cancer cells, ALDH1L1 might be considered as a target for gene therapy. The present study, which was carried out as a proof-of-principle approach, demonstrated the feasibility of using viral delivery of ALDH1L1 in LUAD for gene therapy. The antiproliferative effect of ALDH1L1 on lung tumors demonstrated a highly promising therapeutic potential for this enzyme in LUAD gene therapy. Although the application of ALDH1L1 as a gene therapy approach faces common challenges such as the efficiency of delivery52 and transgene silencing over time,53 targeting ALDH1L1 expression as cancer treatment approach has several benefits. (1) The enzyme, in general, is not toxic to normal cells, as can be judged from its high expression in several human tissues. (2) Its expression remains stable for an extended period of time, as demonstrated in our study. (3) ALDH1L1 enzyme activity depletes one-carbon groups from the intracellular folate pool, thus producing a metabolic effect reminiscent of that of antifolates. It has yet to be seen whether this approach can be combined with antifolate chemotherapy. Additional investigation of ALDH1L1 tumor suppressor mechanisms and treatment sensitivity analyses are required before considering clinical studies.

Materials and methods

Public database

Publicly available datasets on ALDH1L1 expression in normal human tissues and lung cancer patients were obtained from the TCGA Pan-Cancer project, the HPA: https://www.proteinatlas.org, and Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA): http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses for LUAD patients were conducted using data from the TCGA and OncoLnc databases: http://www.oncolnc.org. Data on ALDH1L1 expression in LUAD cell lines were also retrieved from the HPA.

Cell lines

A549-RFP cells stably expressing red fluorescent protein (RFP) were obtained from Cellomics Technology. According to the company, these cells were authenticated using the Promega GenePrint 10 System kit to analyze 10 human STR loci. A549, H460, and HEK-293a were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection through the Tissue Culture Facility, UNC Lineberger Comprehensive Cancer Center. The facility uses commercial services (Labcorp) for the cell line authentication. A549-RFP, A549, and H460, cells were cultured in F12K Nutrient Mixture Kaighn’s medium (Gibco, #21127-022) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Bio-Techne, #S11150) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, #15-240-062). HEK-293a cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, #11885-084) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Bio-Techne, #S11150) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, #15-240-062). Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All cell lines were mycoplasma free and retained the same morphology and doubling time throughout the study. Experiments were conducted using cells between passages 5 and 10.

Plasmid constructions and amplification

Custom lentiviral vector carrying human ALDH1L1 cDNA was purchased from GeneCopoeia (Rockville, MD, #EX-Q0477-Lv102-B). In this construct, the ALDH1L1 ORF (NM_012190.3/XM_024453325.2) is inserted into pReceiver-Lv201 vector (GeneCopoeia, #EX-Q0477-Lv102-B) downstream of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. The resulting vector carries eGFP under the control of SV40 promoter and ALDH1L1 under the control of CMV promoter. The vector includes puromycin resistance elements for selection. Vector map is depicted in Figure S1.

Transfection and lentivirus packing

Recombinant lentivirus was packaged in HEK-293a cells using the Lenti-Pac HIV Expression Packaging Kit (GeneCopoeia, LT002) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2.5 μg of lentiviral vector was diluted in 200 μL of Opti-MEM I (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA, #A36351-01) and mixed with 5 μL (0.5 μg/μL) of Lenti-Pac HIV in a sterile polypropylene tube. In a separate tube, 15 μL of EndoFectin Lenti was diluted in 200 μL of Opti-MEM I. The diluted EndoFectin Lenti reagent was then added dropwise to the DNA solution while gently vortexing the tube. The mixture was incubated for 10–25 min at room temperature to allow the DNA-EndoFectin complex to form. HEK-293a packaging cells grown in T-75 flask to about 70% confluency were transfected by adding the entire DNA-EndoFectin Lenti complex and incubated overnight (8–14 h). Cells were placed in a fresh medium with 1/500 volume of TiterBoost reagent and incubation for additional 24 h. Pseudovirus-containing culture medium was collected in sterile capped tubes at 36 and 48 h post-transfection. The medium was centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was filtered through 0.45-μm polyethersulfone low-protein-binding filters. The virus particles were concentrated using Lenti-Pac Lentivirus Concentration Solution (6×) (GeneCopoeia, LT007). The titer of the harvested lentiviral stocks was determined by RT-PCR using the Lenti-Pac HIV qRT-PCR Titration Kit (GeneCopoeia, LT005).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was assessed using MTT cell proliferation assay (Promega, G4000). Cells were plated at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well in a 96-well format, MTT was added at specified time points, and plates were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 570 nm was read using a Wallace 1420 Multilabel Counter (PerkinElmer). Changes in cell viability over time were calculated using the following formula: % Relative Cell Viability = (Absorbance at specific time/Absorbance at 0 time) × 100.

Real-time PCR

RT-PCR was performed as described13 using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, #1725271). Total RNA was isolated from cells by RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, #74104). cDNA was synthesized using ABI High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368814). Primers for human ALDH1L1, CPT1A, CPT1B, CPT2, CPT1C, CROT, CRAT, and CACT were designed using the NCBI Primer Design Tool and synthesized by Eurofins.

qPCR reactions were run on Roche LightCycler 480 Real-Time PCR System using primers shown in Table S2. The ΔΔCt value was calculated and used to analyze relative gene transcript levels normalized to the β-actin control. All primers were run at optimal amplification efficiency with all measurements in triplicate run in two independent experiments. Each reaction (10 μL) contained 1 μL of RNA, 0.4 μL of each primer, 5 μL of SYBR RT-PCR Supermix, and 3.2 μL of RNase-free water. The amplification program included 1 cycle at 95°C for 5 min followed by 45 cycles with denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 20 s, and extension at 72°C for 10 s, and the final stage at 95°C for 5 s. The specificity of amplification was confirmed by melt curve analysis.

Colony formation assay

Cells seeded in a six-well plate (500–1000 cells per well) were cultured for 2 weeks with medium change every 3–4 days. Medium was aspirated, wells were rinsed twice with PBS, and colonies were fixed with ice-cold methanol-acetone mixture (1:1) and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma, #548-62-9) for 1 h. Images were taken after wells were rinsed with water and air-dried at room temperature. Colony numbers were counted using ImageJ version 1.54g. Experiments were performed in triplicates.

Wound healing assay

Cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in a 12-well plate and cultured for 24 h to form a monolayer (80%–90% confluency). Scratches were made by a sterile pipette tip followed by washing with PBS to remove detached cells and debris; fresh medium (1 mL) was added to each well. Images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h to assess the extent of wound healing. The cell-free area was quantified using ImageJ version 1.54g. The percentage of wound closure was calculated using the equation: wound closure (%) = (1 – [cell-free area at 24 or 48 h/cell-free area at 0 h]) × 100.

Immunoblot assay

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #89901). Protein content was measured using BCA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23225). A total of 40 μL of each sample (protein concentration: 50 μg/mL) was subjected to SDS-PAGE using an 8%–16% precast Criterion gel (BioRad, #4561104) followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Thermo Scientific, #88518). The membrane was blocked in TBST buffer with 3% dry milk for 2 h at room temperature followed by incubation with in-house ALDH1L1- (1:8,000), CPT1A- (1:1,000), or CPT2- (1:1,000) specific antibody overnight at 4°C and then with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cytiva, #NA934V) (1:2,000) for 1 h at room temperature. CPT1A- (#97361S) and CPT2- (#52552S) specific antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. An actin-specific antibody (1:20,000) has been also used as a loading control (Abcam, #AB49900). After incubating with the chemiluminescent substrate (Millipore), specific bands were visualized using an Odyssey FC Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Protein bands were quantified using the Image Studio Lite Software (LI-COR Biosciences).

Metabolite extraction for untargeted metabolomics

Metabolites were extracted from cells as we previously described.22 Briefly, A549 cells were cultured in 10-cm dishes to 70%–80% confluency. Dishes were placed on ice, and cells were washed twice with 10 mL ice-cold PBS. To quench metabolic activity, 1 mL of ice-cold acetonitrile was added. After the addition of 750 μL ice-cold water, cells were dislodged by scraping and cell suspensions were transferred to 15-mL conical tubes. The procedure was repeated to collect residual cells. The extracts were subjected to freeze-thaw cycles and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were dried using a speed-vac and reconstituted in a 95:5 water:methanol solution proportionally to each sample’s protein concentration. A quality control study pool (QCSP) was created by mixing 10 μL from each sample. LC-MS-grade water was used to prepare method blanks.

Analysis of untargeted metabolomics

Metabolomics data were obtained using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following previously reported methods.22,39 Blanks and QCSPs were interspersed among the randomized study samples at a rate of 10% during data acquisition. The QCSPs and blanks were carried through the same preparation procedures as the study samples. Data were preprocessed in Progenesis IQ and filtered by removing peaks with a higher average abundance in the blanks (compared with the QCSPs). Identification and annotation of peaks to metabolites were performed as we previously described using an evidence-based ontology system that communicates the extent to which peaks match compounds based on retention time, exact mass, and tandem mass spectrometry fragmentation patterns.22,39

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s “Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines at the North Carolina Research Campus, under protocol number 21-010. Male athymic nude mice (Hsd:Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu, 5–6 weeks old) and male SCID/beige mice (C.B-17/IcrHsd-PrkdcscidLystbg-J, 6–7 weeks old) were obtained from Envigo RMS LLC (Indiana, USA) and allowed a 1-week acclimatization period. Mice were maintained on reversed 12-h light/dark cycle in pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water.

Subcutaneous xenograft tumor model

A549-RFP cells were suspended in 200 μL of PBS containing 50% Matrigel (Corning) and injected into each flank of 12 male athymic nude mice. Tumor growth was monitored every other day using calipers; tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume = L × W2 × 0.5236 (L is the mid-axis length and W is the mid-axis width). Once the tumors reached a volume of approximately 50–100 mm3 (around day 17 post-injection), mice were randomly assigned to two groups with six animals per group. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and each received i.t. injections in three sites within each tumor (single i.t. injection of 3 × 107 TU in 200 μL PBS per site) of either the control lentivirus expressing GFP or the lentivirus expressing both GFP and ALDH1L1. Tumor volumes were also monitored by fluorescence imaging twice weekly using a Bruker In-Vivo Xtreme Imaging System (Bruker). The relative intensities of emitted light were displayed using RGB color model, where blue indicates the least intense and red the most intense areas. Bruker Molecular Imaging software was used to overlay tumor’s color images with the X-ray image of the corresponding mouse. No attrition was observed for the duration of the xenograft tumor model experiment. On day 57 post-injection, mice were euthanized and tumors were excised and flash-frozen at −80°C with portions of the tumors preserved in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for further analysis. Tumor sections were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy for GFP expression.

Lung colonization model

Twelve male SCID/beige mice were injected via the lateral tail vein with 1 × 106 A549-RFP cells suspended in 200 μL of PBS. Two mice were lost at an early time point following cell injection and prior to randomization for lentiviral treatment. Mice were then randomly divided into two groups of five. Each group received two intravenous injections (on days 3 and 5) of 3×107 TU of either control or ALDH1L1-carrying lentivirus in 200 μL of PBS. Red fluorescence was monitored twice a week as the readout of tumor burden as described in the previous section. One mouse in the control group was sacrificed before the end of the experiment due to signs of distress, immobility, and labored breathing. At 6 weeks after cell injection, mice were euthanized and the lungs were harvested, weighed, and fixed in 10% formalin. Lung tissues were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 10-μm slices, subjected to H&E staining, and examined for metastatic foci.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using either Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 software. For untargeted metabolomics, data were analyzed using Metaboanalyst 6.054 or SIMCA 18.0 (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics, AB, Umeå, Sweden). PCA and OPLS-DA multivariate statistics were conducted to assess group differences. Pathway analysis was conducted using the “Pathway Analysis” module in MetaboAnalyst 6.0.54 The input for the pathway analysis included compounds that matched to the in-house library by retention time and had a t test p value <0.05 between ALDH1L1-null and ALDH1L1-expressing cells (p values were not corrected for multiple testing to avoid the exclusion of potentially important pathways). Raw and false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p values are shown in Table S3.
