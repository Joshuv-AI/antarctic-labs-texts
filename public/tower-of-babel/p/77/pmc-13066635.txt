Effects of particle size and moisture on respirable coal dust generation in pakistan’s informal bituminous mines
Authors: Sohail Khan, Ziling Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Respirable coal dust remains a major occupational hazard in informal mining operations in Pakistan, where high-ash, low-moisture bituminous coals are handled without engineered dust-control systems. This study evaluates how particle size distribution (PSD), moisture content, and surface wettability influence dust-generation potential in coal from the Darra Adam Khel coalfield. Laboratory analyses were conducted on three particle-size fractions (> 75 μm, 45–75 μm, and < 45 μm) to measure moisture retention, slurry pH, and contact-angle–based wettability under ambient conditions. Results show a size-dependent response, with ultrafine particles (< 45 μm) retaining the highest moisture (up to 6.60%) and exhibiting near-neutral pH (6.7), indicating increased carbonate dissolution. Contact angles ranged from 72° to 109°, confirming moderate–strong hydrophobicity that intensified upon compaction. Because dust-generation chamber tests were not available, respirable dust-suppression efficiency (35–58%) was estimated from established Pakistani benchmarks rather than measured directly. The study also acknowledges that the hypothesized 8% moisture threshold for optimal suppression could not be experimentally validated, as the maximum achievable sample moisture was 6.60%. High-pressure gas-atmosphere wettability trends (He, N₂, CO₂) referenced in the discussion represent conceptual mechanisms from published literature, not in-house experimental data. Overall, the findings indicate that Darra Adam Khel coal exhibits limited natural wettability and low moisture-holding capacity, providing a physicochemical basis for enhanced dust-control strategies such as pH conditioning and nonionic surfactants to address hydrophobic surface behavior in semi-arid mining environments.

Introduction

Background and context

Coal dust generation in informal mining systems is governed by a combination of occupational practices and intrinsic coal properties. In Pakistan, these factors interact under semi-arid climatic conditions and limited engineering controls, shaping both dust exposure and suppression behavior.

Coal remains a central component of energy production in many developing economies, including Pakistan, where semi-mechanized and informal mining practices dominate the sector. These operations frequently rely on manual extraction, lack engineered ventilation systems, and expose workers to high levels of respirable coal dust. Fine particulates, especially PM₁₀ and PM₄.₅, readily penetrate deep into the lungs and are strongly associated with pneumoconiosis, chronic bronchitis, silicosis, and COPD1–4. Recent clinical studies report that nearly one-quarter of occupational respiratory illnesses in Pakistan are linked to coal mining activities, yet systematic dust-control measures remain largely absent in most informal mines5–7.

Pakistan hosts substantial coal reserves estimated at over 186 billion tons distributed across the Darra Adam Khel, Lakhra, Thar, and Salt Range coalfields. Despite this resource base, these coals are typically extracted through outdated practices that generate significant quantities of fine dust8,9. The Darra Adam Khel coalfield is a representative example, characterized by fragile, high-ash bituminous coal, structurally disturbed seams, and unventilated underground workings. Although particle size distribution (PSD) and moisture content are widely recognized as key determinants of dust generation, their interactive effects remain poorly quantified for high-ash South Asian coals. Existing international models, largely developed for Chinese, Australian, and U.S. coals, do not fully account for the brittle mineral matrices, low natural moisture, or semi-arid climatic conditions that influence dust liberation in Pakistan10–13.

Dust generation is strongly influenced by two opposing mechanisms. Finer particles (< 45 μm) remain suspended for longer periods due to low terminal velocities, increasing inhalation risk14,15; however, moisture introduces capillary forces that bind particles together and reduce dust detachment16,17. While these individual processes are well understood, the combined PSD–moisture interaction has not been systematically studied for Pakistani coals, particularly those with high ash and hydrophobic surfaces73–75. This gap limits the application of global dust-control frameworks to regional mining environments and underscores the need for localized assessments18–20,78,79.

Existing studies from South Asia, Latin America, and Africa similarly emphasize the limitations of applying foreign dust-suppression models to informal mining systems28–31,44,45.

Research gap and novelty

Despite extensive literature on dust generation, no prior study has quantitatively examined the coupled influence of PSD, moisture content, and surface wettability for high-ash bituminous coal from Pakistan under laboratory conditions that reflect semi-arid mining climates. Moreover, the lack of dust-dispersion chamber facilities in regional laboratories means that dust-suppression behavior must often be inferred from published benchmarks, a limitation that directly affects the interpretation of dust-control efficiency in Pakistan77.

Hypotheses

This work tests the hypotheses that (i) dust emission increases sharply with decreasing particle size, particularly below 45 μm; (ii) moisture levels above ~ 8% significantly suppress dust through enhanced capillary cohesion; and (iii) a nonlinear PSD × moisture interaction exists, with diminishing suppression at ultrafine scales due to surface-energy barriers21. Although the hypothesized 8% threshold could not be fully validated, because laboratory samples reached a maximum of 6.60% moisture, this study provides the first quantitative framework for understanding PSD–moisture interactions in Pakistani coal22–24.

Overall, this Introduction establishes the scientific and regional context for the study and highlights the need for evidence-based dust-control strategies tailored to the geological and climatic realities of Pakistan’s informal coal-mining sector.

Study area

Geographic setting

The study was conducted in the Akhorwal coalfield of Darra Adam Khel, Pakistan (33°49′–33°59′N; 71°29′–71°38′E), located along the Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) where the Indian and Eurasian plates converge32. This spatial pattern supports the field observations that mining occurs through multiple small adits, a factor that contributes to localized dust accumulation and limited ventilation pathways.

Stratigraphic and geological framework

The coal seams belong to the Paleocene Hangu Formation, consisting of interbedded sandstone, shale, and carbonaceous shale. Seam thickness ranges from 0.5 to 1.5 m, with local variations controlled by minor faulting and drag folding34. Laboratory testing of representative overburden samples from the field yields a uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of < 5 MPa, reflecting a mechanically weak roof and floor system35. Such low UCS materials are prone to slabbing, joint reactivation, and impact-induced fragmentation, increasing the availability of ultrafine coal particles during mining and handling36.

The structural fabric is dominated by close-spaced cleats and persistent bedding-plane shears.

Fig. 4Coal seam from Darra Adam Khel showing a substantial calcite vein infilling the coal matrix.

The prominent calcite-filled vein cutting through the coal matrix, illustrating the presence of secondary carbonate mineralization within the seam, is shown in Fig. 4. Such mineral infill not only influences the mechanical breakdown of the coal but also explains the near-neutral pH behavior observed in slurry tests, as carbonates partially dissolve during water interaction.

These discontinuities reduce block size and facilitate breakage under minimal mechanical force, creating a geological setting where particle-size reduction naturally progresses toward the respirable fraction (< 4 μm and < 10 μm). This structural predisposition explains the high dust burden observed in the region’s underground workings and supports the need for laboratory evaluation of PSD-dependent moisture and wettability behavior.

Coal characterization

Coal from Akhorwal is classified as high-ash bituminous, with properties that enhance its dust-generation potential. Proximate analysis indicates inherent moisture of 3–6%, ash content of 18–24%, volatile matter of ~ 35%, and fixed carbon in the range typical of medium-rank coals. Mineralogical assessment shows quartz contents approaching the 5% threshold associated with elevated pneumoconiosis risk, alongside calcite, clay minerals, and iron-rich phases that influence pH buffering and surface wetting behavior. These key physicochemical parameters and their health/processing implications are summarized in Table 2. These characteristics are comparable to coals from similarly deformed basins in India and Colombia, where dust liberation is strongly controlled by ash-rich, brittle matrices.

Because of the low natural moisture and high mineral impurity, the coal tends to break into finer fractions during extraction, producing dust that remains airborne for extended periods in confined mining environments. These physical and chemical attributes justify the need to experimentally examine how particle size interacts with moisture and surface chemistry to influence dust-generation potential.

Climatic context

The Akhorwal region experiences a semi-arid climate with average summer temperatures of 32–38 °C and relative humidity of 18–35%40. These conditions promote rapid desiccation of freshly mined coal and increase the detachment and suspension of fine dust. Seasonal fluctuations in humidity and temperature also alter the moisture-retention behavior of surface-exposed coal, making climatic influences an important contextual factor for interpreting laboratory results41. Although dust-measurement data and exposure statistics are reported in later sections, they are driven in part by the broader climatic setting described here42,43.

Rationale for site selection

Akhorwal was selected because it typifies the operational and geological conditions of informal coalfields across Pakistan’s northwest region: thin seams, weak overburden, intense structural deformation, high-ash bituminous coal, and manual handling practices. These features combine to produce an environment where dust generation is governed primarily by intrinsic coal properties and extraction methods rather than mechanized equipment. Understanding PSD-moisture interactions in such settings has direct implications for dust-control strategies in comparable mining regions of the Global South, where engineered ventilation and modern suppression systems are largely absent.

Global relevance

While the physical conditions of Akhorwal differ from large, mechanized mines, the coal’s rank, ash content, and brittle mineralogy closely resemble those of many small-scale operations in South Asia, Latin America, and parts of Africa44. This parallel allows findings from this study particularly those concerning moisture retention, surface chemistry, and wettability to inform dust-mitigation approaches beyond Pakistan. The study area therefore provides a realistic and broadly representative setting for examining the physicochemical factors that influence respirable-dust generation in resource-constrained mining environments45.

Sample collection and field handling

Field sample collection

Coal samples were collected from active adits in the Akhorwal block of the Darra Adam Khel coalfield (33°49′–33°59′N; 71°29′–71°38′E). A total of 2 kg of run-of-mine coal was obtained from each selected working face using sterilized stainless-steel scoops. Samples were collected from freshly exposed benches to avoid surface contamination and oxidation effects.

Sample storage and transport

Coal was immediately sealed in airtight polyethylene bags and stored in insulated containers to minimize moisture loss during transport to the laboratory. All sampling procedures followed local mining-permit conditions and standard coal-handling protocols46.

Particle size distribution (PSD) analysis

PSD analysis was performed using a nested stainless-steel sieve series (2.00 mm, 75 μm, and 45 μm mesh sizes) following ISO 3310 − 147. Samples were oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h (ASTM D3302)52, cooled in a desiccator, and sieved for 20 min at 60 Hz using a calibrated mechanical shaker48.

Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and mass-balance recovery exceeded 97% for all runs. PSD curves were generated for quality assurance, but numerical analysis was restricted to the defined fractions49–51.

Moisture content adjustment

Moisture adjustment followed ASTM D330252. Dried coal aliquots were uniformly sprayed with deionized water to target moisture levels of 4%, 6%, and 8% (wet basis). Samples were sealed in airtight containers and equilibrated for 24 h with periodic mixing to ensure uniform distribution.

Because Darra Adam Khel coal has a naturally low hygroscopic capacity, the maximum achievable moisture level during equilibration was 6.60%, even when 8% was targeted22,53. This limitation is acknowledged explicitly in the Results and Discussion.

pH measurement

Slurry pH was determined using a 1:5 solid-to-liquid ratio, following standard soil-slurry protocols. Coal samples (10 g) were mixed with 50 mL of deionized water, agitated for 30 min, and allowed to stabilize for 1 hour63. Measurements were taken using a multi-parameter meter (± 0.01 accuracy), calibrated at pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00. All pH values were recorded in triplicate for each PSD fraction and moisture condition64,65.

Pellet preparation and surface conditioning

Coal beds were prepared by lightly compacting 5 g of material into 25 mm aluminum discs using a standardized 2.5 kg load to ensure comparable surface roughness.

Contact-angle measurement protocol

A 3 µL deionized water droplet was placed on the sample surface using an automated syringe. Images were captured at 5-frame/s for 60 s, and contact angles were computed using baseline and polynomial-fitting algorithms.

Each test was replicated 5–10 times per PSD fraction to account for heterogeneity in surface energy. Wettability tests were performed only under ambient laboratory pressure, consistent with the study’s experimental design.

All wettability measurements followed internationally recognized standards for sessile-drop analysis, including ASTM D7334 (Standard Practice for Surface Wettability by Contact Angle Measurement), GB/T 22,237 − 2008 (Chinese standard for coal contact-angle determination), and ISO 19403-2:2020 (Wetting Behavior of Solid Surfaces)66–68,80. These standards ensure precision in droplet placement, image-capture geometry, and angle-determination algorithms, thereby improving reproducibility across samples with varying particle sizes and surface chemistries73–75,81. The use of 5–10 replicates per sample further minimizes error arising from surface heterogeneity and supports statistically robust comparisons between particle-sizefractions69,70.

Inferred dust generation approach (literature-based)

Direct respirable-dust generation tests (e.g., dust-dispersion chambers or micro-orifice uniform deposit impactor systems) were not available in Pakistan during this study54–58. Therefore, dust-suppression efficiency was inferred, not measured, using experimental findings from this work (PSD, moisture, wettability, pH trends) combined with validated dust-control relationships from peer-reviewed Pakistani and international literature59–62. Accordingly, all dust-suppression values reported in this study represent indicative potential ranges rather than experimentally validated efficiencies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26. One-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate the effect of PSD fraction on moisture content, pH, and contact angle. Tukey’s HSD was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons where significant differences were detected (α = 0.05)30. Effect sizes were calculated using η2 (eta squared) to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to PSD differences.

Because moisture and wettability measurements involve independent replicates rather than repeated observations on the same specimen, interaction terms were not included in the ANOVA model. All data were screened for normality and homoscedasticity before statistical testing69,70.

Contact angle and wettability behavior

Contact-angle measurements showed a clear size-dependent wettability response across the three particle-size fractions. The finest fraction (< 45 μm) exhibited the highest hydrophobicity, with mean contact angles approaching 89°, whereas intermediate particles (45–75 μm) showed moderately lower values (~ 80°). The coarsest fraction (> 75 μm) remained the most wettable, with angles near 74°73–75,90−93.

Correlation analysis revealed only a very weak association between moisture content and contact angle (r = 0.092), indicating that small variations in moisture did not substantially influence surface wetting behavior. In contrast, pH demonstrated a weak negative correlation with contact angle (r = − 0.356), suggesting that slightly more alkaline fine particles tended to exhibit marginally higher hydrophobicity73,74,85.

The markedly higher contact angles observed in the loose state indicate stronger hydrophobicity when particles are unconsolidated, whereas compaction reduces surface irregularities and partially exposes mineral sites, leading to lower angles. This contrast highlights how mechanical disturbance and particle packing significantly influence apparent wetting behavior.

No high-pressure wettability measurements were conducted in this study; therefore, any pressurized gas-contact-angle images referenced elsewhere in the manuscript are included solely for conceptual context and appear in the Discussion Sects90–93.

Contact-angle dataset and enhanced wettability interpretation

To strengthen the wettability assessment, the complete contact-angle dataset for all fifteen samples (A1–A5 across the three particle-size fractions) is presented in Table 6. The results confirm that most coal surfaces exhibit strong hydrophobic behavior (θ > 90°), with only a few cases displaying weak hydrophilicity or transitional wetting73–75,90−94. Hydrophobicity remained most pronounced in the fine fraction (45 μm), while the intermediate fraction (75 μm) displayed the greatest variability ranging from distinctly hydrophilic (A3-75: 71.81°) to strongly hydrophobic (A4-75: 100.02°). Coarser particles (150 μm) generally showed weakly hydrophobic behavior, often close to the 90° threshold, indicating smoother macro-surfaces and reduced exposure of reactive organic sites.

These particle-size–dependent variations align with the mechanistic understanding that smaller particles tend to retain more hydrophobicity due to higher exposure of organic macerals and greater surface-energy heterogeneity73,74,95. Conversely, the hydrophilic behavior observed in selected 75 μm samples (e.g., A3-75) suggests local oxidation, mineral coatings, or clay-rich domains9,88 that increase the density of hydrophilic functional groups. Such localized mineralogical influences help explain the greater variability within the mid-sized fraction, despite uniform sample-preparation conditions.

Overall, the dataset reinforces the conclusion that Darra Adam Khel coal possesses intrinsically limited wettability across all particle-size fractions73–75,90−94. The consistently high contact angles corroborate the coal’s resistance to water spreading, which directly impacts the effectiveness of conventional water-based dust-suppression systems53,77,97. The presence of hydrophobic surfaces particularly within the < 45 μm respirable fraction suggests that water-film formation and liquid-bridge cohesion are inherently constrained, thereby limiting the suppression potential even under controlled laboratory moisture adjustment. These findings further justify the recommendation that surfactant-assisted wetting or pH conditioning be considered to overcome the natural hydrophobic barrier of this coal type.

Inferred dust-suppression efficiency

Dust-suppression efficiency was inferred by integrating the experimentally measured PSD, moisture, and wettability characteristics with established predictive models reported for high-ash coals53,56,57,76. The analysis indicates that increasing moisture from 4% to the maximum experimentally achievable level of 6.60% is associated with an inferred dust-suppression potential of approximately 35–58%, depending on particle-size fraction and handling disturbance59,60. These inferred values fall within the ranges reported for comparable Pakistani and international bituminous coals.

Discussion

Overview of key findings and novel contributions

This study integrates particle size, moisture retention, pH behavior, and surface wettability to explain the dust-generation potential of high-ash Darra Adam Khel coal9,20 under laboratory conditions representative of the region’s semi-arid climate40. Together, these parameters clarify the physicochemical controls on respirable-dust formation in informal mines1,7.

Finer particles (< 45 μm) show slightly higher moisture and marginally elevated pH, reflecting mineralogical variability9–12. Contact angle measurements demonstrate predominantly hydrophobic surfaces across fractions, with compaction increasing hydrophobicity through maceral alignment and reduced exposure of hydrophilic sites90,93. These characteristics limit the effectiveness of water-only suppression, and the inferred suppression range (35–58%) is consistent with Pakistani benchmarks rather than chamber-derived values54,57.

The combined evaluation of PSD-dependent moisture, pH, and wettability provides a more regionally relevant framework than international datasets25,27 for understanding dust-control limitations in Pakistani coalfields. This integrated approach underscores the need for future work on real-time dust monitoring and detailed mineralogical analyses88,89.

Influence of particle size on moisture retention

Moisture variation across fractions is modest; finer particles (< 45 μm) retain slightly more moisture due to greater surface area, pore connectivity, and mineral inclusions52,53. However, mineralogical composition exerts a stronger influence than size alone9,88. Comparisons with Padhrar coal, which contains more hydrophilic carbonates and clays, highlight why Darra Adam Khel coal exhibits weaker moisture uptake9,12.

Overall moisture levels (3.15–6.60%) are far below those of humid-climate coals (8–12%)25,27, reflecting low hygroscopic capacity and rapid evaporation in semi-arid settings40. These constraints limit water-only suppression and support the need for auxiliary treatments such as pH conditioning or surfactant-assisted wetting57,76.

Moisture-pH-mineralogy interaction and its effect on wettability

Role of carbonate buffering

The slight pH increase in finer fractions reflects enhanced exposure of carbonate minerals9,88, whose partial dissolution buffers slurry toward near-neutral values82 and modifies surface functional-group protonation84. Mildly higher pH may promote deprotonation, reducing hydrophilic OH density and contributing to marginally higher contact angles83,86,87.

Implications for surface wettability

The weak negative correlation between pH and contact angle73,74 indicates that pH exerts a secondary influence relative to particle size, maceral composition, and compaction. Padhrar coal, containing more clays and carbonates, exhibits lower contact angles9,90, whereas Darra Adam Khel coal, richer in inertinite and poorer in hydrophilic inclusions, shows stronger hydrophobicity. These contrasts explain the distinct moisture–pH–wettability relationships reported for different Pakistani coalfields9,88. Overall, the mineralogy-induced limits on hydrophilicity, coupled with semi-arid evaporation40, reinforce the need for surfactant-assisted wetting or mild pH adjustment90–94.

Wettability behavior and compaction effects

Contact-angle measurements indicate moderate to strong hydrophobicity across size fractions (72°-109°), with fine particles showing greater variability due to mineral inclusions and micro-topography73–75. Mechanical compaction further increases hydrophobicity through maceral alignment, masking of hydrophilic mineral phases9,88 and smoother surfaces that reduce capillary penetration90–94.

These mechanisms limit water-only suppression during handling and storage88,91. No high-pressure gas wettability tests were conducted; referenced images are conceptual models from prior studies66–68. Overall, the coal’s naturally water-repellent surface, intensified by compaction, supports the need for enhanced wetting strategies96–98, particularly under low-humidity, high-temperature conditions40.

Integration of contact-angle mechanisms into discussion

The hydrophobicity of the < 45 μm fraction results from organic-rich macerals and surface-energy heterogeneity, limiting film formation and droplet spreading73,74,95. Variability in the > 75 μm fraction, including the hydrophilic A3-75 response, reflects localized mineral features such as clay coatings, carbonates, or oxidative alteration9,88. Wettability therefore depends on interactions among macerals, mineral phases, and micro-surface chemistry90–94.

Because overall hydrophobicity remains high despite occasional hydrophilic sites, water-only suppression is insufficient88,91. Surfactant-based or pH-modifying additives can enhance adhesion and spreading in semi-arid environments53,76.

Inferred dust suppression efficiency: local vs. international benchmarks

Suppression efficiencies (35–58%) are inferred from Pakistani and international benchmarks rather than chamber tests due to limited regional facilities56,57. Integrating PSD, moisture, pH, and wettability yields estimates consistent with high-ash bituminous coal behavior88.

International comparisons show that higher-moisture coals (8–12%)25,27 outperform Darra Adam Khel coal, which reached only 6.60% moisture under semi-arid constraints40. Hydrophobicity further reduces water-film stability and cohesion limiting gains even at higher moisture levels90–94.

Regional alignment with other Pakistani coals9 indicates that intrinsic coal properties not methodology constrain suppression effectiveness90. Complementary measures such as surfactants or pH conditioning are therefore needed74.

Practical implications for mine dust control

Limited moisture uptake, persistent hydrophobicity, and compaction effects restrict water-only suppression40,88. Moisture delivery should prioritize the ultrafine < 45 μm fraction, where respirable dust is concentrated88, though low hygroscopic capacity52,53 limits retention.

Reducing unnecessary compaction can improve spreading90,93. Mild pH adjustment may increase hydrophilic functional groups82,84, while nonionic surfactants (e.g., Triton X-100, Tween-80) can reduce surface tension and enhance film uniformity76.

A hybrid strategy combining optimized moisture delivery, reduced compaction, pH conditioning, and surfactant-assisted wetting is best suited to informal mining environments88.

Limitations of the study

Moisture levels did not exceed 6.60%, below the 8% threshold cited for effective.

suppression25,27. Direct respirable-dust measurements were not possible due to the absence of dust-dispersion chambers54–58, therefore, all suppression values discussed herein are benchmark-derived56,57 indicators rather than experimentally measured efficiencies90–94.

Mineralogical interpretations rely on regional datasets rather than sample-specific XRD or SEM-EDS9. Fixed laboratory conditions approximate but cannot fully replicate semi-arid field variability40, and laboratory PSD fractions may not capture run-of-mine diversity88.

Recommendations and future research directions

Field-scale validation using real-time monitoring tools is needed to compare laboratory estimates with operational dust levels56,57. Detailed mineralogical analysis (XRD, SEM-EDS, automated mapping) can better link mineral phases to pH and wettability behavior9,88.

Surfactant-assisted wetting and environmentally benign additives should be evaluated through laboratory optimization and field trials76. Modeling tools response-surface methods, two-way ANOVA, and CFD can refine predictions of PSD-moisture-surface-chemistry interactions88,95.

Microclimatic variability should be integrated into conditioning protocols40 to improve dust-control reliability in semi-arid Pakistani coalfields88.

Conclusion

This study indicates that particle size distribution, moisture retention, pH behavior, and surface wettability collectively influence the dust-generation potential of high-ash Darra Adam Khel coal, providing regionally grounded insights for semi-arid mining environments. Finer particles (< 45 μm) exhibited slightly higher moisture and marginally elevated pH due to greater surface area and carbonate exposure, yet overall moisture levels (3.15–6.60%) remained well below those reported for international bituminous coals. Mild carbonate buffering (pH 5.90–6.70) and persistent hydrophobicity intensified under compaction through maceral alignment and reduced expression of hydrophilic mineral phases limit the coal’s inherent wetting response and constrain the efficiency of water-only suppression systems.

In the absence of dust-dispersion chambers, suppression efficiency (35–58%) was inferred from validated Pakistani benchmarks, reflecting the combined influence of low hygroscopic capacity, semi-arid climatic conditions, and hydrophobic surface behavior; the inability to reach the commonly cited 8% moisture threshold, with a laboratory maximum of 6.60%, further underscores this limitation. These outcomes highlight the need for enhanced wetting strategies such as nonionic surfactants and mild pH conditioning and for minimizing unnecessary compaction to improve liquid adhesion and spreading, especially in informal mining operations with limited ventilation.

Future efforts should incorporate field-based validation, detailed mineralogical characterization (XRD, SEM-EDS), and advanced modeling frameworks to better resolve PSD × moisture × surface-chemistry interactions. Overall, this work provides the first integrated assessment of PSD-moisture-wettability relationships for Darra Adam Khel coal and establishes a scientific foundation for developing more effective, context-specific dust-control practices in Pakistan’s semi-mechanized mining sector.
