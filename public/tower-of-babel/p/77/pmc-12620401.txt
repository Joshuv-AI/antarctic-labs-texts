Tomato Chlorotic Virus Minor Coat Protein Interferes With the Host Proteasome Function by Competitive Disruption of SlPAD1–SlPA4 Interaction to Enhance Viral Infection
Authors: Xipan Wang, Kaijie Shang, Wenju Liu, Chenchen Wang, Ting Zhang, Yongguang Liu, Shumei Zhou, Hongmei Liu, Xiaoping Zhu, Changxiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) is one of the most destructive plant viruses affecting tomato crops, leading to significant agricultural losses. As an obligate parasite, ToCV depends on the macromolecular machinery of host cells for replication. The ubiquitin 26S proteasome system maintains the intracellular protein homeostasis, which is essential for plant growth and development. Our study found that the CPm protein of ToCV interacted with SlPAD1, a component of the 26S proteasome, to enhance viral infection. This interaction disrupts the binding between SlPAD1 and SlPA4, thereby impairing the 26S proteasome function. In addition, SlPAD1 and SlPA4 positively regulate plant resistance to ToCV. Our findings reveal a mechanism by which ToCV proteins facilitate infection by interfering with 26S proteasome function.Keywords: 26S proteasome, minor coat protein, protein–protein interaction, tomato chlorosis virus, viral infection

Introduction

During growth, plants are vulnerable to infection by various pathogenic microorganisms, which can significantly impair plant development and, in severe cases, lead to plant mortality. The gene expression and replication cycles of plant viruses are fundamentally dependent on the utilisation of the macromolecular machinery of the host cell. Virus–host protein interactions play an essential role in multiple stages of viral infection, including viral entry, genome replication and systemic spread within the host organism (Pallas and García 2011; Rothenburg and Brennan 2020). Research on the interactions between viral and host proteins has emerged as a focal point in the field of plant virology.

Tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV), first identified in Florida (United States), is transmitted by whiteflies and causes substantial damage to global tomato production (Wisler et al. 1998). ToCV is a member of the genus Crinivirus; species contain two single‐stranded RNAs, RNA1 and RNA2, that encode 13 proteins (Lozano et al. 2006). Recent studies have significantly enhanced our understanding of the functional mechanisms underlying ToCV infections. The p22 protein, encoded by RNA1, functions as an RNA silencing suppressor. It binds to the host proteins S‐phase kinase‐associated protein (SKP1.1) and B‐cell lymphoma 2‐associated athanogene 5 (NbBAG5), interferes with the auxin signalling pathway, and inhibits autophagy, thereby promoting viral infection (Cañizares et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2021; Shang et al. 2023). The HSP70h and a 59.4 kDa protein, encoded by RNA2, are involved in viral movement and virion assembly, while the coat protein (CP) interacts with the host S‐adenosyl‐L‐homocysteine hydrolase (SAHH) to regulate local gene silencing (Cañizares et al. 2013; Alzhanova et al. 2001; Peremyslov et al. 1999). The P27 protein, also encoded by RNA2 promotes ToCV infection by interacting with and inhibiting the antiviral activities of tomato catalases SlCAT1 and SlCAT2 (Sun et al. 2023). Thus, the pathogenesis of ToCV involves diverse interactions between viral proteins and specific host factors, which are crucial for successful infection.

The minor coat protein (CPm), encoded by RNA2, is a symptom determinant. It not only facilitates virus transmission by vectors but also suppresses the plant's immune defence system through post‐transcriptional gene silencing (Kiss et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2023). The ectopic expression of ToCV CPm using a potato virus X (PVX) vector resulted in severe mosaic symptoms followed by a hypersensitive response in Nicotiana benthamiana (Yang et al. 2023). CPm effectively suppressed local RNA silencing induced by single‐stranded RNA, but not by double‐stranded RNA (Yang et al. 2023). However, whether CPm directly interacts with host proteins and the precise mechanisms remain unclear.

In plants, ubiquitination plays an important role in a variety of signal transduction pathways, such as hormones, growth and development and pathogen defence signalling. The ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS) is the most important protein degradation pathway (Luo et al. 2010). The 26S proteasome is a 2.5 MDa complex containing a 20S core particle and a 19S regulatory particle (Coux et al. 1996; Murata et al. 2009). The core particle, shaped like a barrel, is the site of protein degradation, whereas the regulatory particle recognises ubiquitinated substrates and translocates them to the core particle (Voges et al. 1999). The core particle is composed of α‐subunits and β‐subunits and arranged in four stacked heteroheptameric rings with a stoichiometry of ɑ1‐7β1‐7β1‐7ɑ1‐7 (Borissenko and Groll 2007). Regulatory particles are ATP‐dependent and consist of 17 subunits (Voges et al. 1999).

Multiple subunits of the 26S proteasome are involved in plant immune responses through various mechanisms, such as interacting with pathogen effector proteins, or influencing viral transport. The expression of N. benthamiana 20S proteasome subunits β1, α3 and α6 is associated with systemic acquired resistance (Suty et al. 2003). The Arabidopsis thaliana proteasomal α5 subunit PAE1 interacts with multifunctional helper component proteinase (HcPro) of lettuce mosaic virus (LMV), and Pae1 knockout plants are more susceptible to LMV (Dielen et al. 2011). Furthermore, the effector proteins Xanthomonas outer protein J (XopJ) and HopZ4 from pathogenic bacteria target the regulatory particle AAA‐ATPase (RPT6), inhibiting proteasome activity and subsequent turnover of immune regulators such as NPR1 to promote virulence (Üstün et al. 2014; Üstün and Börnke 2015). Additionally, the 19S regulatory subunit RPN9 facilitates the systemic movement of multiple viruses, and its suppression restricts viral infection (Jin et al. 2006). These studies suggest that different 26S proteasome subunits participate in plant immunity via different pathways.

In the present study, we observed that CPm overexpression in plants promotes ToCV infection. The interaction of CPm and SlPAD1 interferes with the interaction of SlPAD1 and SlPA4, thus affecting the function of the 26S proteasome, which in turn influences the degradation of ubiquitinated‐modified proteins to promote viral infection. In particular, ToCV CPm directly interferes with proteasome function rather than regulating substrate degradation. In addition, SlPAD1 and SlPA4 positively regulate plant resistance to ToCV.

Results

ToCV CPm Promotes Virus Infection

ToCV infects a wide range of plant species and causes substantial economic losses in agricultural production. ToCV CPm plays an important role in viral infections of plants. To investigate the molecular mechanisms of the CPm protein in viral infection, we generated CPm‐overexpressing (CPm‐OE) lines by constitutively expressing a CPm‐GFP fusion protein under the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter in N. benthamiana (Figure S1a,b). The PCR and reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) results showed CPm expression in N. benthamiana, and we selected two lines, CPm‐OE L1 and L2, for subsequent experiments (Figure S1c,d). Notably, CPm‐OE plants showed vein pigmentation and leaf chlorosis, copying the symptoms observed in ToCV‐infected wild‐type (WT) plants (Figure S2). This phenotypic resemblance suggests that CPm may serve as a determinant of viral pathogenicity responsible for symptom development (Yang et al. 2023). At 28 days post‐inoculation (dpi), ToCV‐infected plants developed characteristic chlorosis and yellow spotting on leaves, whereas CPm‐OE lines exhibited more pronounced symptom severity (Figure 1a). Total RNA was extracted from systemic leaves to quantify viral accumulation. Consistent with their phenotypic severity, CPm‐OE plants had significantly higher ToCV CP transcript levels than the WT plants (Figure 1b). These results demonstrated that CPm facilitates ToCV infection.

CPm Affects the UPS by Interacting With SlPAD1

To investigate the mechanism by which CPm promotes ToCV infection, we constructed a pGBKT7‐CPm recombinant plasmid to conduct yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening of a tomato cDNA library and identified SlPAD1 as a potential interactor. SlPAD1 is a component of the 26 proteasome 20S core subunit. To further validate the interaction between CPm and SlPAD1, we co‐transformed the pGADT7‐SlPAD1 recombinant plasmid with the pGBKT7‐CPm recombinant plasmid into the Y2H system, and the results confirmed that CPm interacted with SlPAD1 (Figure 2a). Glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assays provided further support for the interaction between CPm and SlPAD1 (Figure 2b). This interaction was corroborated in plants by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, which demonstrated that CPm interacted with SlPAD1 in the cytoplasm (Figure 2c).

Typically, the subcellular localisation of a protein determines its function. To investigate CPm localisation, we transiently expressed 35S:CPm‐GFP in N. benthamiana and observed its distribution after 48 h. The results demonstrated that CPm‐GFP was localised in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm (Figure S3). Similarly, we transiently expressed 35S:SlPAD1‐GFP in N. benthamiana, observed its subcellular localisation, and found that SlPAD1‐GFP was localised exclusively in the cytoplasm (Figure S3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CPm and SlPAD1 co‐localise and physically interact in the cytoplasm, suggesting their potential functional cooperation in modulating host–pathogen interactions.

To assess the effect of ToCV infection on the UPS, we examined the accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins. Both ToCV infection and CPm expression induced the accumulation of high‐molecular‐weight ubiquitin‐conjugated proteins. This pattern mirrored the effects observed following treatment with MG132, a 26S proteasome inhibitor (Figure 3 and Table S3), suggesting impairment of UPS function and indicating that ToCV CPm promotes ToCV infection via the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway.

SlPAD1 Positively Regulates the Plant's Resistance to ToCV

To investigate the function of SlPAD1 during ToCV infection, we overexpressed SlPAD1 in N. benthamiana. Compared with plants overexpressing pROKII (the empty vector control), plants overexpressing SlPAD1‐pROKII exhibited milder chlorosis and fewer ToCV CP transcripts (Figure 4a,b). Next, we silenced SlPAD1 expression by virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) and inoculated a ToCV infectious clone. RT‐qPCR showed that the relative expression level of SlPAD1 in SlPAD1‐TRV2‐inoculated plants was significantly lower than that in the control (TRV2) (Figure 4d). At 5 weeks post‐inoculation, the lower leaves of SlPAD1‐silenced tomato plants exhibited pronounced chlorosis in the lower leaves and accumulated significantly more ToCV (Figure 4c,e). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that SlPAD1 enhances plant resistance to ToCV.

CPm Interferes With the Binding of SlPAD1 and SlPA4

The 26S proteasome is assembled via the coordinated association of multiple distinct subunits. Based on our findings, we hypothesised that the CPm–SlPAD1 interaction might interfere with the interaction between SlPA4 and other proteasomal subunits, potentially compromising proteasome function during viral infection. Through bioinformatics prediction followed by Y2H screening, we identified SlPAD1 as specifically interacting with the 20S proteasome subunit SlPA4 while showing no detectable interaction with other predicted proteins (Figures 5a and S4). This interaction was further validated by BiFC and GST pull‐down assays (Figure 5b,c). Based on the predicted domain architecture of SlPAD1, we constructed pGADT7‐SlPAD1 C (1–210 amino acids) and pGADT7‐SlPAD1 N (211–249 amino acids), two deletion SlPAD1 mutant recombinant plasmids (Figure S5). Y2H analysis revealed that both CPm and SlPA4 interacted with SlPAD1C but not with SlPAD1N (Figure 6a,d). To further confirm these interactions between SlPAD1C and CPm or SlPA4, we performed BiFC assays with SlPAD1C‐YFPC, CPm‐YFPN and SlPA4‐YFPN recombinant plasmids and GST pull‐down experiments with SlPAD1C‐His, CPm‐GST and SlPA4‐GST fusion proteins, both of which confirmed the interaction (Figure 6b–f).

Because both CPm and SlPA4 bind to the C‐terminal region of SlPAD1, we hypothesised that CPm competitively binds to SlPAD1, thereby inhibiting the interaction between SlPAD1 and SlPA4. To test this hypothesis, we examined the interaction between CPm and SlPA4; Y2H and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays showed that CPm did not interact with SlPA4 (Figure S6). The LCI assay showed that, when myc (control) was present, there was a strong interaction between SlPAD1 and SlPA4, whereas when CPm‐myc was present, the interaction between SlPAD1 and SlPA4 weakened (Figure 7a). In addition, we performed a competitive pull‐down assay using the CPm‐maltose‐binding protein (MBP), SlPAD1‐His and SlPA4‐GST fusion proteins. The results showed that increasing concentrations of CPm‐MBP reduced the amount of SlPAD1‐His bound to SlPA4‐GST (Figure 7b). We used microscale thermophoresis (MST) to quantitatively assess the binding affinity between SlPAD1 and SlPA4. The results showed that SlPAD1 bound strongly to SlPA4 (Kd = 0.0026 nM). This interaction was not affected by GST (Kd = 0.0027 nM) but was significantly weakened by CPm‐GST (Kd = 0.0016 μM) (Figure 7c), indicating that CPm competitively inhibits the SlPAD1–SlPA4 interaction in a dose‐dependent manner.

SlPA4 Regulates ToCV Accumulation Similar to SlPAD1

To investigate the role of SlPA4 in plant immunity, we overexpressed SlPA4 in N. benthamiana, using the pROKII empty vector as a control, and inoculated ToCV infectious clones. Similar to the effects observed with SlPAD1, SlPA4 also significantly inhibited ToCV infection (Figure 8a,b). To further validate these findings, we employed VIGS to downregulate SlPA4 expression (Figure 8d) and simultaneously inoculated the ToCV infectious clone. SlPA4‐silenced plants displayed significantly enhanced susceptibility to ToCV infection, as evidenced by increased chlorosis and ToCV accumulation (Figure 8c,e). These results indicated that SlPA4 enhanced plant resistance to ToCV.

To determine whether overexpression of SlPAD1 or SlPA4 could rescue the impairment of the UPS caused by CPm, we co‐expressed either SlPAD1‐GFP or SlPA4‐GFP with CPm‐GFP in tomato plants and monitored the accumulation of high‐molecular‐weight ubiquitin‐conjugated proteins. Overexpression of CPm resulted in the accumulation of high‐molecular‐weight ubiquitin‐conjugated proteins, a phenotype similar to that induced by MG132 treatment (Figure S7 and Table S4). However, this accumulation was significantly reduced in plants co‐expressing either SlPAD1 or SlPA4 (Figure S7 and Table S4), indicating that SlPAD1 and SlPA4 can partially rescue the UPS impairment caused by CPm.

Discussion

The ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway plays an important role in plant responses to environmental changes. Many viruses promote infection by attacking the UPS. However, whether ToCV uses the same strategy is unknown. Our study established that ToCV CPm functions as a determinant of viral pathogenicity. Mechanistically, CPm competitively binds SlPAD1 during infection, disrupting its interactions with SlPA4, thereby impairing the proper function of the 26S proteasome, creating a proteolytic environment conducive to viral proliferation (Figure 9). However, overexpression of either SlPAD1 or SlPA4 significantly alleviated the impairment of proteasome system functionality induced by ToCV CPm (Figure S7).

CPm interacts with tomato SlPAD1 to block 26S proteasome function. Under physiological conditions, SlPAD1 interacts with SlPA4 to form the proteasome, degrade ubiquitin‐modified proteins and maintain intracellular protein homeostasis, ensuring normal developmental processes. When tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) infection occurs, the viral CPm protein competitively binds to the host protein SlPAD1, leading to the disruption of the SlPAD1–SlPA4 complex and thereby impairing the functionality of the 26S proteasome, inhibiting the degradation of ubiquitinated modified proteins, and thus facilitating viral pathogenesis.

CPm is a key component of ToCV and is involved in virion assembly and stability. In addition, CPm possesses dual activities of pathogenicity and RNA silencing, which might inhibit host post‐transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS)‐mediated resistance and is pivotal in the primary process of ToCV infecting hosts (Yang et al. 2023). This functional versatility makes CPm indispensable for ToCV host colonisation, transmission and ecological success. Consistent with previous studies, our results suggest that CPm promotes ToCV infection, manifesting as more severe chlorosis and yellow spotting (Figure 1).

Viral proteins exploit host factors through various mechanisms to promote infection and pathogenesis (Rothenburg and Brennan 2020). The potato virus S (PVS) triple gene block1 protein (TGB1) interacts with StLTP6 to suppress jasmonic acid synthesis and attenuate RNA silencing‐mediated antiviral resistance (Shang et al. 2025). Similarly, potato virus A (PVA) potyviral helper component proteinase (HCPro) stabilises viral particles via AGO1 (Pollari et al. 2020), and Chinese wheat mosaic virus (CWMV) 19 kDa coat protein (CP19K) recruits cytosolic glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenases to inhibit autophagy (Niu et al. 2022). In this study, we screened ToCV CPm‐interacting proteins in tomato and found that it interacts with SlPAD1 (Figure 2). Subcellular localisation analysis indicated that both proteins were localised in the cytoplasm. Their co‐localisation supports the hypothesis that they may physically interact and functionally influence one another (Figure S3).

The 26S proteasome system is an integral component of the plant immune system, regulating key defence processes. RXLR effector Pi06432 of Phytophthora infestans interacts with potato ubiquitin domain‐containing protein (UDP) to degrade StPR3T, which compromises 26S proteasome stability and consequently suppresses plant immune pathways (Wang, Li, et al. 2023). Downregulation of RPN9 expression inhibited the systemic spread of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and turnip mosaci virus (TuMV). This may be because the 26S proteasome regulates auxin transport and brassinosteroid (BR) signalling by targeting the degradation of a group of regulatory proteins (Jin et al. 2006). Our data revealed that ToCV infection and CPm expression led to the accumulation of high‐molecular‐weight ubiquitin‐conjugated proteins (Figure 3), indicating that CPm promotes ToCV infection by influencing the 26S proteasome. Overexpression of SlPAD1 in N. benthamiana reduced ToCV accumulation, while silencing SlPAD1 expression enhanced ToCV infection (Figure 4). The CPm protein competitively binds to SlPAD1, disrupting the SlPAD1–SlPA4 complex (Figures 6 and 7). SlPAD1 or SlPA4 overexpression was sufficient to rescue UPS functionality (Figure S7). This results in the creation of a proteolytic niche that facilitates viral replication. However, the specific immune responses in plants affected by this interaction remain unclear and require further investigation.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials and Virus Inoculation

Nicotiana benthamiana was used as the wild‐type (WT) plant. Transgenic lines ectopically expressing CPm (gene ID: 3608000) (CPm‐OE) were generated using the pROKII‐CPm recombinant plasmid (specific primers are listed in Table S1). N. benthamiana seeds were germinated on Murashige and Skoog solid medium and transferred to vermiculite after 1 week. Plants were cultured under a 16‐h light (25°C) and 8‐h dark (20°C) photoperiod.

The ToCV infectious clone pCass4‐Rz‐ToCV was preserved in our laboratory (Shang et al. 2023). The method for Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated plant infection was performed according to the protocol described by Shang et al. (2023).

TRV‐based recombinant plasmids were used to silence SlPAD1 (Gene ID: 544128) and SlPA4 (Gene ID: 101251952) expression. For the infection assays, the ToCV infectious clone (pCass4‐Rz‐ToCV) and A. tumefaciens harbouring the TRV recombinant plasmids were co‐infiltrated into 1‐week‐old tomato seedlings.

Subcellular Localisation of CPm and SlPAD1

The pROKII‐CPm and pPROKII‐SlPAD1 recombinant plasmids were independently transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Subsequently, the transformed Agrobacterium cultures were infiltrated into the epidermal cells of N. benthamiana leaves. After 3 days, fluorescence was observed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss; LSM880 Meta). GFP fluorescence was excited at 480 nm and captured within the emission range of 510–550 nm. The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S1.

RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from plant leaves using TriQuick Reagent (Solarbio). Briefly, 0.1 g of plant leaves was homogenised in 1 mL of TriQuick Reagent. RNA was extracted with chloroform, followed by precipitation with isopropanol. The RNA pellet was then dissolved in RNase‐free water. First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using the All‐in‐One First‐Strand Synthesis MasterMix (iScience). qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche) using the SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Biosharp). The relative expression levels of each gene were analysed using the formula 2−ΔΔCt method (Wang, Song, et al. 2023). The primers used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S2.

Yeast Library Screening and Y2H Assay

The full‐length coding sequence (CDS) of CPm was cloned into the pGBKT7 plasmid to construct a bait vector for screening the tomato yeast library, following the protocol described by Shang et al. (2023).

SlPAD C , SlPAD1 N and the full‐length CDS of SlPAD1 were cloned into the pGADT7 plasmid. These constructs were co‐transformed into the Y2H Gold yeast strain using pGBKT7‐CPm. Similarly, the full‐length CDS of SlPA4 was cloned into the pGBKT7 plasmid and co‐transformed into the Y2H Gold yeast strain, along with pGADT7‐SlPAD1, pGADT7‐SlPAD1 C and pGADT7‐SlPAD1 N . The pGBKT7‐53 and pGADT7‐T plasmid pairs were used as positive controls, whereas pGBKT7‐Lam and pGADT7‐T served as negative controls. The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S1.

BiFC Assays

For BiFC assays, the CDS of CPm and SlPAD1 were cloned into the pSITE‐nYFP and pSITE‐cEYFP plasmids, respectively. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, according to the protocol described by Shang et al. (2023). Additionally, to verify the interaction between SlPAD1 (or its C‐terminal fragment SlPAD1C) and SlPA4, pSITE‐SlPA4‐nEYFP (SlPA4‐YFPN) was co‐expressed with pSITE‐SlPAD1‐cEYFP or pSITE‐SlPAD1C‐cEYFP (SlPAD1‐YFPC). Fluorescence was observed 3 days post‐infiltration using a confocal microscope (Zeiss; LSM880 Meta). YFP fluorescence was excited at 514 nm and captured within the emission range of 565–585 nm. The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S1.

GST Pull‐Down Assay

The CDS of CPm or SlPA4 was cloned into the vector pGEX‐4 T‐1, and the CDS of SlPAD1 or SlPAD1 C was cloned into the pET30a vector. The constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells to express CPm‐GST, SlPA4‐GST, SlPAD1‐His and SlPAD1C‐His fused proteins. To verify protein interactions, combinations of GST with SlPAD1‐His, CPm‐GST with SlPAD1‐His, GST with SlPAD1C‐His, CPm‐GST, SlPAD1C‐His, SlPA4‐GST with SlPAD1‐His, and SlPA4‐GST with SlPAD1C‐His were incubated with BeyoMag GSH Magnetic Agarose Beads (Beyotime). For the competitive pull‐down assay, different volumes of CPm‐MBP were incubated with immobilised SlPAD1‐His and SlPA4‐GST at 4°C. Interactions were detected by immunoblotting with anti‐GST (Beyotime) and anti‐His (Beyotime) antibodies. The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S1.

LCI Assay

To perform LCI assays, specific genes were cloned into pCAMBIA1300‐nLUC and pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC and then transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The Agrobacterium strain carrying nLuc or cLuc was adjusted to a final concentration of OD600 = 1.0 and infiltrated into four different regions of N. benthamiana leaves. Three days later, 0.3 mM luciferin (Solaribio) was used to infiltrate the leaves, and luciferase activity was measured using an in vivo imaging system. The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S1.

Ubiquitination and Western Blot Analysis

Tomato plants were employed for ubiquitination modification assays. Total protein was extracted from plant leaves using Buffer F, following a previously described protocol (Han et al. 2018). The extracted proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE. Ubiquitinated proteins were detected using a monoclonal mouse anti‐ubiquitin antibody (P4D1, #3936, Cell Signalling Technology). To normalise for protein loading, the membrane was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) to visualise the RuBisCO large subunit (RubL). Band intensities corresponding to ubiquitin and RubL were quantified using ImageJ. Ubiquitin levels were expressed relative to the RubL signal in each sample. Protein Expression = Anti‐Ub/CBB Relative Expression = Protein Expression (treatment)/Protein Expression (Mock).

MST Assay

The MST assay was performed as previously described by Jin et al. (2016). Using SlPAD1‐GFP‐GST proteins as the target. In brief, a gradient dilution of SlPA4 was prepared and mixed with a fixed concentration of the target protein SlPAD1‐GFP‐GST, followed by the addition of a fixed concentration of either GST or CPm‐GST. After a short incubation, the samples were loaded into MST standard treated glass capillaries. Measurements were performed at 25°C in phosphate buffer, using 20% LED power and 20% MST power.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was based on the Student's t test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. All data are presented as at least three replicates.
