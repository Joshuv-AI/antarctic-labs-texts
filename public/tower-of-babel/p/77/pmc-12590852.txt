Endocan levels in patients with hyperprolactinemia
Authors: Aykut Hacıömeroğlu, Aşkın Güngüneş, Selim Yalçın, Ercan Tekin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveElevated prolactin levels are considered a potential parameter associated with increased cardiovascular risk. The aim of our study is to investigate the possible role of Endocan levels in the cardiovascular risk associated with hyperprolactinemia by comparing patients with hyperprolactinemia to a healthy control group.MethodsThe study included 39 patients with hyperprolactinemia of various etiologies and 39 healthy controls. In both groups, cardiovascular risk-related parameters—including height, body weight, BMI, waist circumference, CRP, and lipid levels—were evaluated and recorded. Endocan levels were measured using the ELISA method.ResultsThe groups were comparable in age, sex, height, body weight, BMI, and waist circumference. No significant differences were found between the patient and control groups in terms of height, body weight, BMI, waist circumference, WBC count, fasting glucose, insulin levels, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, lipid profile, CRP, and 25(OH) vitamin D levels. However, serum Endocan levels were significantly higher in the hyperprolactinemic group (p = 0.028). In this group, Endocan levels showed a significant positive correlation with waist circumference, body weight, and BMI, and a negative correlation with WBC count.ConclusionOne of the key findings of our study is the elevated Endocan levels in patients with mildly increased prolactin levels (45.90 ± 2.72 ng/mL), indicating a higher cardiovascular risk. This may reflect the association between elevated prolactin and endothelial dysfunction. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to better evaluate Endocan levels in patients with hyperprolactinemia.Clinical trial numberNo applicable.

Introduction and objective

Hyperprolactinemia may result from physiological, pharmacological, or pathological causes and can lead to clinical manifestations such as hypogonadism, oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea, galactorrhea, and infertility [1].

Beyond these clinical effects, hyperprolactinemia is also considered a risk factor for cardiovascular events. Elevated prolactin levels are associated with reduced nitric oxide production and direct vasoconstriction, contributing to increased blood pressure and endothelial proliferation [2, 3]. It is well established that endothelial cell proliferation triggers angiogenesis [4]. Increased carotid intima-media thickness has also been reported in patients with prolactinoma [5–7]. Due to the effects of prolactin on adipose tissue, elevated levels of total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and apolipoprotein B may be observed [6, 8]. These mechanisms may collectively contribute to the heightened cardiovascular risk associated with hyperprolactinemia [9].

The endocan molecule is a free, soluble dermatan sulfate proteoglycan secreted by vascular endothelium, which can also be observed in the bloodstream of the healthy population [10]. Due to these properties, it has been shown to increase in vascular pathologies and inflammatory diseases. It has been shown that Endocan levels increase due to endothelial damage in cardiovascular diseases such as coronary artery disease, acute myocardial infarction, and hypertension, and it is considered to potentially be accepted as a biomarker for cardiovascular events [11, 12].

Although there are many studies correlating high prolactin with cardiovascular risk, to our knowledge, there is no study directly examining prolactin levels and Endocan levels. The aim of our study is to compare Endocan levels in patients with hyperprolactinemia to the control group and to reveal the potential role of Endocan levels in the cardiovascular risk associated with elevated prolactin levels.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and inclusion/exclusion criteria

This study was conducted as a prospective cross-sectional study at the Department of Internal Medicine and the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolic Diseases of Kırıkkale University Faculty of Medicine, between December 2023 and June 2024. The minimum sample size was determined to be 70 individuals using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 software, with parameters set at α = 0.15, 1-β = 0.85, and effect size d = 0.5. The power of the study was calculated as 0.8524293.

Patients who were 18 years of age or older, non-smokers, had elevated prolactin levels for the first time due to any etiology, and had not received any treatment prior to this, were included in the study.

The exclusion criteria for the patients were: being under 18 years of age, having a chronic disease (such as chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease), smoking, being pregnant or in the lactation period, having previously received any treatment after being diagnosed with elevated prolactin levels, having a chronic disease associated with elevated prolactin (such as chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, primary hypothyroidism), and refusing to participate in the study.

The control group inclusion criteria were defined as healthy individuals aged 18 years and over, without any known diseases, not using any regular medication or smoking, and not pregnant or in the lactation period. A total of 39 patients and 39 controls meeting these criteria were evaluated within the scope of the study.

Study design

Venous blood samples were collected from the individuals included in the study (both patient and control groups) at the time of admission, following an 8-hour fasting period.

Blood samples collected for serum-based tests were drawn into serum separation tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h, and subsequently centrifuged at 1000×g for 20 min. All serum-based parameters, except for Endocan, were analyzed immediately after serum separation using an automated analyzer. For Endocan analysis, the serum samples were aliquoted into labeled tubes and stored at − 80 °C until testing. After the completion of sample collection, all Endocan measurements were performed simultaneously using a manual ELISA kit in the biochemistry laboratory of our institution. For complete blood analyses, samples were collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes.

In our study, serum levels of prolactin, 25-OH vitamin D, beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG), insulin, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and free T4 were measured using the Electrochemiluminescence Immunoassay (ECLIA) method on the Cobas 8000 e801 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Japan, 2019). C-reactive protein (CRP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), triglycerides, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels were measured photometrically using the Cobas 8000 c702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Japan, 2018). The complete blood count (CBC) was performed using the BC-6800 Auto Hematology Analyzer (Mindray, Hong Kong, 2018) based on the SF Cube method for white blood cells and subtypes, DC impedance for platelet count, and colorimetric method for hemoglobin. HbA1c was measured using the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method on the Premier Hb9210 device (Trinity Biotech, USA, 2020).

Serum Endocan levels were measured using the Human ESM1 (Endothelial Cell-Specific Molecule 1) ELISA Kit (Cat. No: E-EL-H1557, Elabscience), based on the Sandwich-ELISA principle. During manual ELISA procedures, a BioTek ELX50 microplate strip washer (USA, 2008) was used for plate washing, and the ChemWell microplate reader (Awareness Technology, USA, 2015) was used for absorbance reading. According to the kit’s performance specifications, the sensitivity was 9.38 pg/mL, the detection range was 15.63–1000 pg/mL, and the intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was reported to be less than 10%.

The HOMA-IR index (homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance) for each participant was calculated by multiplying the fasting plasma glucose level by the fasting insulin level and dividing the result by 405 [13]. The results were recorded on laboratory forms.

In the study, the patients’ age (years), height (cm), and body weight (kg) were measured. Based on these data, the BMI (kg/m²) was calculated. BMI (Body Mass Index) was calculated by dividing the participants’ body weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters. Additionally, the participants’ waist circumference (cm) was recorded. Waist circumference measurement was taken while standing upright on a flat surface, by measuring the circumference at the level of the midline, just above both anterior superior iliac spines and aligned with the umbilicus. The data were recorded on patient forms.

Before starting the study, all volunteers were provided with information and signed the “Informed Consent Form”. Permission for the study was obtained from the Kırıkkale University Clinical Research Ethics Committee with decision number 22/03 on 16th November 2023.

All data collected in the study were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. For numerical variables, the mean and standard deviation were calculated. Normality analysis was performed to determine the appropriate statistical method. When both groups met the assumption of normality, the independent samples t-test was used. When the assumption of normality was not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. Additionally, Pearson and Spearman correlation tests were used in correlation analyses. Multivariate regression analysis was also performed to identify independent predictors of the outcome variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while a p-value of > 0.05 was considered not significant.

Results

A total of 39 patients with hyperprolactinemia due to various etiologies were included in the study, along with a control group of 39 healthy individuals matched for age, gender, BMI, smoking status, and clinical history.

The patient and control groups consisted of individuals without chronic comorbidities and not on any long-term medication for any reason. In addition, all participants in both the patient and control groups were non-smokers with no history of smoking. Nearly all patients in the study group exhibited the classical clinical symptoms of hyperprolactinemia.

The patient and control groups were compared in terms of age, sex, waist circumference, height, body weight, and BMI. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to these parameters. The prolactin level used to differentiate between the patient and control groups was statistically compared. The mean prolactin level was 45.90 ± 2.72 µg/L in the patient group and 12.07 ± 0.46 µg/L in the control group. As a result of the comparison between the two groups, serum prolactin levels were found to be significantly higher in the patient group than in the control group (p < 0.001).

The concept of mild prolactin elevation has been defined in the literature. Although different threshold values have been proposed for this elevation, it is most commonly defined as 50 ng/mL or below [14, 15]. In our study, 26 patients (66%) in the patient group had mild prolactin elevation, while 13 patients (33%) had high prolactin levels.

The patient and control groups were compared in terms of fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin levels, HOMA-IR score, hemoglobin levels, white blood cell count, platelet count, neutrophil count, ALT, AST, urea, creatinine, eGFR, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, CRP, albumin, thyroid function tests, vitamin levels, and lipid profiles. Correlation between endocan levels and hemogram and biochemical parameters

In this study, the effects of various clinical and biochemical parameters, which are associated with cardiovascular risk, on Endocan levels were evaluated using multivariate regression analysis. Endocan was defined as the dependent variable, while age, prolactin, white blood cell count, triglycerides, LDL, CRP, HOMA-IR, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, 25-OH vitamin D, waist circumference, and BMI were included as independent variables. The resulting model was statistically significant (R²=0.923, p < 0.001), demonstrating a strong relationship between Endocan levels and metabolic as well as inflammatory cardiovascular risk factors. Notably, positive correlations between Endocan and obesity indicators such as waist circumference and BMI, as well as glycemic control markers including HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, suggest that Endocan may serve as a significant biomarker in the cardiovascular risk profile.

Examination of the regression coefficients revealed that obesity measures, including waist circumference (β = 0.653, p < 0.001) and BMI (β = 0.536, p < 0.001), significantly increased Endocan levels. Additionally, prolactin, HbA1c, and fasting plasma glucose showed positive effects, whereas white blood cell count had a significant negative effect on Endocan levels. Other cardiovascular risk factors did not exhibit statistically significant influences. The high explanatory power of the model (Adjusted R²=0.881) and the significance of these parameters support the potential use of Endocan as a biomarker for assessing cardiovascular disease risk. These findings indicate that Endocan may play a role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular risk through mechanisms involving metabolic dysfunction and inflammation. Discussion

In our study, the Endocan level, a newly identified marker of endothelial dysfunction, was found to be significantly elevated in the patient group with hyperprolactinemia. Although numerous studies have established a correlation between elevated prolactin levels and cardiovascular risk, to the best of our knowledge, no study has directly examined the relationship between moderate hyperprolactinemia and Endocan levels.

In the study conducted by Yavuz et al., the mean prolactin level in patients with prolactinoma was 156.5 ± 77.2 ng/mL, and it was also found to be associated with endothelial dysfunction [16]. Similarly, Serri et al. also showed that cardiovascular risk was increased in patients with prolactinoma. In this study, the mean prolactin levels of the patients were determined to be 88.3 ng/mL [17]. In the study conducted by Yazıcı et al., a significant increase in epicardial fat tissue and carotid intima-media thickness was found in patients with prolactinoma. This result was associated with an increased cardiovascular risk. The mean prolactin levels in the prolactinoma group of the study were 35.5 ± 29.6 ng/mL [7]. Similar to hyperprolactinemia, there are also studies indicating that elevated endocan levels are significantly associated with increased cardiovascular risk. The prognostic role of Endocan levels in coronary artery disease was explored in the study conducted by Lin et al. The results revealed that Endocan levels in patients with stable coronary artery disease were significantly associated with cardiovascular outcomes, such as myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular events [18]. In the study conducted by Efe et al., Endocan levels were found to be higher in patients with coronary artery disease compared to those without coronary artery disease [19]. Many similar studies in the literature have reported comparable results [20–23]. Supporting the current findings, a meta-analysis conducted by Zhao et al. showed that Endocan levels were significantly elevated in cardiovascular diseases and that high Endocan levels serve as a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [24]. It is believed that Endocan levels are important indicators of endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular risk, and can be used as a marker for cardiovascular risk. In line with this, one of the most important findings of our study is the elevated Endocan levels in patients with hyperprolactinemia. This may indicate a potential relationship between hyperprolactinemia and cardiovascular risk.

There are many studies in the literature demonstrating the relationship between high prolactin levels and cardiovascular risk. However, there are only a limited number of studies investigating the cardiovascular risk associated with mild hyperprolactinemia. In the study conducted by Koca et al., the relationship between mild hyperprolactinemia and arterial vascular stiffness was examined. The mean prolactin level in the patient group was found to be 45.15 ng/mL, and no relationship was observed between mild hyperprolactinemia and arterial vascular stiffness. The authors suggested that this result may be due to differences in study group selection, as well as factors such as age, gender, and the follow-up period of the patients [25]. One of the most important findings of our study is the increase in Endocan levels, an important indicator of cardiovascular risk, in the patient group with mild prolactin elevation (45.90 ± 2.72 ng/mL). We believe this may be the result of the relationship between elevated prolactin levels and endothelial dysfunction.

In the study by Delibaş et al., Endocan levels were examined in patients with PCOS and found to be significantly higher in the PCOS group compared to the control group [26]. Although hyperprolactinemia is a common condition in patients diagnosed with PCOS, the patient group in this study was selected from individuals with normal prolactin levels. However, it is known that PCOS is a chronic inflammatory process that triggers inflammation through an increase in mediators such as IL-1, IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α. Additionally, insulin resistance, elevated insulin levels, increased atherosclerosis, greater calcium accumulation in coronary arteries, and elevated blood pressure have been found to occur secondary to PCOS [27, 28].

In our study, Endocan levels were found to be positively correlated with BMI, body weight, and waist circumference measurements. In the study conducted by Delibaş et al., a negative correlation was found between Endocan levels, BMI, and CRP in the PCOS group. The authors highlighted that this correlation was an unexpected finding [26]. Similar to our study, atherosclerosis and Endocan levels were evaluated in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus in a study conducted by İçli et al., and a positive correlation was found with body mass index [29]. Similar to the results of our study, in a study conducted by Boyuk et al., Endocan levels were examined in patients with metabolic syndrome, and a significant correlation was found between waist circumference and Endocan levels [30]. All of these studies confirm that the increase in BMI, body weight, and waist circumference triggers the inflammatory process.

In our study, no correlation was found between Endocan levels and CRP, while a negative correlation was observed with WBC levels. A review of the literature reveals publications suggesting that Endocan levels are correlated with infectious events. In the study conducted by Scherpereel et al., a significant increase in Endocan levels was detected in patients with sepsis [31]. In the study conducted by Seo et al., a significant correlation was found between the degree of bacteremia and serum Endocan levels [32]. Additionally, in the study conducted by Kao et al., it was found that Endocan levels are more useful than CRP and WBC count in determining the severity of community-acquired pneumonia [33]. In the study conducted by Efe et al. on coronary artery disease and coronary slow flow, no correlation was found between Endocan levels and CRP [19]. The existence of inconsistent correlations between our study and the literature may be attributed to the fact that the patients were not in an infectious state. In fact, similar to our results, Efe et al. found no correlation in patients without infections. Additionally, there are studies suggesting that CRP and Endocan increase as a result of different inflammatory pathways, and therefore, it is normal for there to be no correlation between them [19].

In our study, no statistically significant difference was found between the patient and control groups in terms of waist circumference. However, when the correlation analysis was examined, a significant positive correlation was found between Endocan and waist circumference. To our knowledge, there is no specific study on this subject in the literature. In the study conducted by Atmaca et al., two groups with and without hyperprolactinemia were compared, and no statistically significant difference was found between the groups regarding waist circumference [34]. In another clinical study supporting this result, Ponce and colleagues grouped patients according to waist circumference and examined prolactin levels. Similarly, no significant difference was found between waist circumference and prolactin levels [35].

Our study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to focus on mild hyperprolactinemia in this field. Additionally, the relationship between hyperprolactinemia and Endocan has been evaluated for the first time. One of the strengths of our study is that the patient and control groups were similar in many parameters, except for prolactin levels. The similarity between the two groups in this regard was crucial in revealing the relationship between Endocan and hyperprolactinemia.

However, there are some limitations to our study. Instead of including only patients diagnosed with prolactinoma, we included patients with elevated prolactin levels due to various etiologies. Additionally, the duration of hyperprolactinemia in the patients is unknown. The study was conducted with a limited number of patients, and as a cross-sectional study, the follow-up period for the patients was also limited. Furthermore, when examining similar studies in the literature that assess cardiovascular risk, one limitation of our study is that we did not evaluate parameters such as nitric oxide levels and carotid intima-media thickness, which indicate endothelial dysfunction, apart from Endocan.

Conclusions

It has been known for many years that hyperprolactinemia is a risk factor for cardiovascular events due to its role in causing endothelial dysfunction. Endocan is a molecule associated with endothelial dysfunction. One of the most important findings of our study is that Endocan levels, an important indicator of cardiovascular risk, were elevated in the patient group with mild prolactin elevation. We believe that this may result from the relationship between elevated prolactin and endothelial dysfunction. More studies are needed to explore Endocan levels in patients with hyperprolactinemia, including a larger sample size, longer prospective follow-up periods, and the examination of other parameters indicating endothelial dysfunction, such as nitric oxide levels and carotid intima-media thickness.
