“Ferroptosis-immune” cycle: Research progress of tumor microenvironment responsive nanomaterials in the treatment of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Ying Liu, Tianhao Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPancreatic cancer (PC) therapy is hindered by a dense stroma and immunosuppressive microenvironment. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death, has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. This review introduces the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle concept. Ferroptosis in tumor cells not only directly kills them but also releases damage-associated molecular patterns that promote antitumor immunity. Conversely, activated immune cells enhance ferroptosis sensitivity in tumor cells. Precisely initiating this cycle remains a key challenge. We focus on tumor microenvironment-responsive nanomaterials as innovative tools that enable controlled co-delivery of ferroptosis inducers and immune modulators, thereby amplifying the synergistic ferroptosis-immune cycle. Current challenges and future directions are discussed, providing a theoretical foundation for developing novel nanotherapies for PC based on ferroptosis-immunity synergy.Subject areas: Microenvironment, Immunology, Materials in biotechnology, Cell biology, Cancer

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most malignant digestive tract tumors.1 The core reason for its high mortality rate lies in two treatment bottlenecks. On the one hand, the physical barrier composed of cancer-related fibroblasts and dense extracellular matrix has seriously hindered the infiltration and delivery of drugs.2 On the other hand, the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment renders various immune therapies generally ineffective. Traditional radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy have limited efficacy and poor prognosis in this context.3,4 Therefore, exploring new therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously overcome the matrix barrier and reshape the immune microenvironment has become an urgent key scientific issue in the research field of PC.

In recent years, ferroptosis, a new type of programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and dependent on iron, has brought new hope for solving the above dilemma.5,6 Due to unique metabolic reprogramming, such as high ROS substrate level and iron demand, PC cells show significant sensitivity to ferroptosis.7 More importantly, there is a profound two-way dialogue between ferroptosis and anti-tumor immunity, which this article systematically elaborates as the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle.8 To begin with, the damage-related molecular patterns released by ferroptotic cells can effectively activate dendritic cells and promote the recruitment and activation of cytotoxic T cells, thereby transforming cold tumors into hot tumors.9 Besides, activated T cells can downregulate the expression of key defense proteins such as SLC7A11 in tumor cells by secreting cytokines such as interferon - γ, thereby feedback enhancing their sensitivity to ferroptosis.10 Initiating and amplifying this virtuous cycle is expected to achieve a synergistic effect of direct tumor killing and immune system activation.11

However, how to accurately and efficiently start this cycle in the local area of PC and overcome its complex internal barrier is the core technical challenge currently facing.12 The rise of tumor microenvironment-responsive nanomaterials provides a revolutionary tool for solving this problem.13 This kind of smart nano carrier can use the conditions of PC’s unique weak acidity, high reducibility, or specific enzyme expression to achieve targeted enrichment and controlled release of drugs, so as to efficiently co-deliver ferroptosis inducers and immune regulators, and jointly start and strengthen the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle.14,15 This review will systematically sort out the latest research progress in this field, deeply discuss the design strategy, mechanism of action and treatment prospect of smart nanomaterials, and look forward to the challenges they face and the future development direction, aiming to provide theoretical basis and cutting-edge perspective for the development of the next generation of comprehensive treatment mode for PC (Figure 1).

Molecular mechanism and regulatory network of the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle

Biochemical basis and key regulatory pathways of ferroptosis

As is well known, the essence of ferroptosis is the breakdown of intracellular redox homeostasis, with the core biochemical characteristics of glutathione-specific depletion and loss of lipid peroxide clearance ability, leading to the uncontrolled accumulation of cytotoxic lipid peroxides, especially phospholipid hydroperoxides, on the cell membrane, ultimately causing membrane rupture.16 This process is precisely regulated by several highly conserved molecular pathways.17

The core triggering mechanism of ferroptosis is the failure of the glutathione peroxidase 4-axis.18 This system is the primary line of defense for cells against phospholipid hydroperoxide.19 Its operation relies on a sophisticated supply chain: the System Xc transporter located on the cell membrane, a heterodimer composed of light chain SLC7A11 and heavy chain SLC3A2.20 Transports extracellular cysteine into the cell, while exchanging intracellular glutamate to the extracellular space.21 Cysteine entering the cell is rapidly reduced to cysteine, which is a key rate-limiting precursor for the synthesis of glutathione, the main antioxidant in the cell.22 Ultimately, GPX4 utilizes reduced GSH as a cofactor to specifically catalyze the reduction of lipid hydroperoxides in phospholipid bilayers to the corresponding harmless lipid alcohols, thereby directly interrupting the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation.23 Therefore, any factors that directly or indirectly lead to the inhibition of System Xc - function, depletion of GSH, or inactivation of GPX4 will undermine this core defense and inevitably induce ferroptosis.

Given the central position of the GSH-GPX4 axis, cells have evolved multiple “backup” defense systems independent of this axis, collectively forming a powerful compensation network, which is also an important mechanism for tumor cells to develop drug resistance.24 First, the FSP1-CoQ10 pathway: located on the plasma membrane, the ferroptosis inhibitory protein 1 uses NAD (P) H as an electron donor to reduce ubiquinone to panthenol.25 The latter, as a potent lipophilic free radical scavenging antioxidant, can directly quench lipid peroxidation free radicals in the cell membrane.26 Second, the DHODH-CoQ10 pathway: dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, located in the inner membrane of mitochondria, not only plays a role in pyrimidine synthesis, but also utilizes its electron transport chain to reduce CoQ10 in mitochondria, thereby specifically protecting mitochondria from lipid peroxidation damage.27 Third, the GCH1-BH4 pathway: GTP cyclase 1 is the rate-limiting enzyme for the synthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin, while BH4 itself is an effective free radical scavenger, and its increase can selectively promote the production of CoQ10 with antioxidant capacity.28 These pathways can be significantly activated after GPX4 inactivation, becoming key barriers for cells to resist ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis is iron-dependent, and the unstable iron pool with catalytic activity inside the cell is the “engine” driving the lipid peroxidation chain reaction.29 Through the Fenton reaction, Fe2+ can convert hydrogen peroxide or lipid peroxides into highly active hydroxyl radicals or lipid radicals, greatly accelerating the process of lipid peroxidation.30 Therefore, the homeostasis of cellular iron metabolism is another key factor determining ferroptosis sensitivity. Cells store iron through ferritin, and the iron oxidase activity of their heavy chain subunits can convert catalyzed Fe2+ into inert Fe3+ for storage, effectively reducing unstable iron pool levels.31 On the contrary, the negative regulatory mechanism for maintaining iron homeostasis is that cells eliminate excess iron from the cell through the only iron export protein - membrane iron transporter.32 Any factor that leads to an increase in iron intake, storage of iron release, or obstruction of iron efflux can cause intracellular iron overload, greatly enhancing the sensitivity of cells to ferroptosis.33

Overall, through the systematic regulation of the above levels, cells establish a sophisticated defense system to determine their fate against ferroptosis. Understanding these fundamental biochemical mechanisms is the theoretical foundation for targeted ferroptosis in cancer treatment.

The immune activation function of ferroptosis: From cell death to immune response

As a new type of programmed cell death, one of the most important features of ferroptosis is its ability to effectively activate the body’s anti-tumor immune response, which is mainly achieved by releasing a series of immune-stimulating signaling molecules.34 When cells undergo ferroptosis, the accumulation of lipid peroxides inside the cell leads to the destruction of cell membrane integrity, promoting the release of cellular contents into the tumor microenvironment.35,36,37 These released substances contain a class of immune-active molecules called damage-related molecular patterns, which form a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity through ferroptosis.

DAMPs released into the microenvironment interact with immune cells through pattern recognition receptors, initiating preliminary immune activation.38 The high-mobility group protein B1 triggers the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway by binding to Toll-like receptor 4 and advanced glycation end product receptor on the surface of dendritic cells.39 Extracellular ATP acts as a “find me signal” and binds to purinergic receptor P2RX7, promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly.40,41 The simultaneously released nucleic acid components can be recognized by TLR3/7/9, and so forth.42 The synergistic effect of these signals induces dendritic cells to undergo mature transformation, manifested by the upregulation of major histocompatibility complexes and co-stimulatory molecules, as well as the increased secretion of inflammatory cytokines.43

Mature dendritic cells carry tumor antigens and migrate to draining lymph nodes, initiating adaptive immune responses.44 In the lymph node, dendritic cells present engulfed tumor antigen peptides to CD8+ T cells via the MHC-I cross-presentation pathway, and to CD4+ T cells via MHC-II molecules, thereby priming them for differentiation into IFN-γ producing Th1 cells.45 Under the synergistic effect of co-stimulatory signals and cytokines, initial T cells are activated and proliferate extensively, differentiating into effector T cells.46 Among them, cytotoxic CD8+T cells acquire cytotoxic activity and can recognize and eliminate tumor cells expressing corresponding antigens.47 Th1 cells provide necessary auxiliary signals by secreting cytokines such as interferon-γ, further amplifying the intensity of the anti-tumor immune response.48

In addition to classical DAMPs, specific oxidized phospholipids produced during ferroptosis have unique immune regulatory functions.49 Hydroxyphosphatidylethanolamine peroxide, as a specific lipid signal for ferroptosis, can directly act as an immunomodulatory agent.50 Research has shown that this type of oxidized phospholipid can promote the polarization of M1 macrophages, enhance their antigen presentation ability, and secrete pro-inflammatory factors.51 At the same time, it can directly activate innate lymphocytes such as gamma delta T cells, induce their production of interferon - γ and granzyme B, and exert rapid anti-tumor effects.52 This immune activation, directly mediated by lipid peroxidation products, constitutes the unique immune stimulation pathway of ferroptosis.53

Ferroptosis effectively transforms simple tumor cell death into a comprehensive anti-tumor immune response through the multi-level and multi-pathway immune activation mechanism mentioned above, providing a key theoretical basis for understanding its synergistic effect in tumor immunotherapy54 (Figure 2). This unique immunogenic feature makes it an ideal strategy for combination immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Feedback regulation of the immune system on ferroptosis: Establishing a bidirectional interactive cycle

There is a precise bidirectional regulatory relationship between the immune system and ferroptosis, which is not only reflected in the immune system’s response to ferroptosis but also in the immune system’s ability to actively shape the sensitivity of tumor cells to ferroptosis.55 The core of this feedback regulation mechanism is that activated immune cells directly intervene in the antioxidant defense system of tumor cells by secreting specific cytokines, thereby establishing a self-amplifying positive circulation loop.56

Activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) initiate the regulation of ferroptosis sensitivity in tumor cells by releasing interferon-γ.57 IFN-γ secreted by activated Th1 cells binds to receptors on the surface of tumor cells, thereby activating the JAK-STAT1 signaling pathway, promoting STAT1 phosphorylation, and leading to dimer formation and nuclear translocation.58 In the nucleus, activated STAT1 directly binds to the gamma activation sequence in the promoter region of the SLC7A11 gene, recruiting transcriptional corepressor complexes and effectively inhibiting the transcriptional expression of SLC7A11.59 This regulatory process has high specificity, reflecting the precise intervention ability of the immune system on the metabolic pathways of tumor cells.60

The downregulation of SLC7A11 expression directly leads to impaired cysteine uptake ability in tumor cells, which in turn triggers a series of metabolic chain reactions.61 Due to cysteine being a key precursor for the synthesis of glutathione, its reduced uptake directly leads to restricted intracellular GSH synthesis.62 The decrease in GSH levels prevents glutathione peroxidase 4 from effectively catalyzing the reduction reaction of lipid peroxides, while also disrupting the cellular redox homeostasis.63 This metabolic reprogramming makes tumor cells more vulnerable to ferroptosis-induced stimuli, significantly reducing their threshold for ferroptosis.

This regulatory mechanism constitutes a complete “ferroptosis-immune” positive feedback loop. The initial ferroptosis cells activate dendritic cells by releasing damage-related molecular patterns, thereby initiating T cell immune responses.64 Activated CD8+T cells downregulate the expression of SLC7A11 in tumor cells through the IFN-γ-JAK-STAT1 signaling axis, enhancing their sensitivity to ferroptosis.65 The increase in sensitivity leads to more tumor cells undergoing ferroptosis, releasing more immune stimulation signals, thereby further expanding the scale of the immune response.66 This self-amplifying cyclic effect can continue to operate in the tumor microenvironment, forming an increasingly enhanced anti-tumor immune response.

This sophisticated bidirectional interaction mechanism not only deepens our understanding of the relationship between ferroptosis and the immune system but also provides an important theoretical basis for developing novel combination therapy strategies.67 By making reasonable use of this naturally occurring biomagnification system, it is expected to achieve more efficient and long-lasting anti-tumor treatment effects.68

The unique influence of PC microenvironment on the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle

The unique tumor microenvironment of PC constitutes a complex regulatory network, which has a profound impact on the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle.69 This microenvironment is characterized by a dense fibrotic matrix, unique metabolic features, and a highly immunosuppressive state, which not only creates potential opportunities for ferroptosis therapy, but also poses significant implementation challenges.70

PC cells are activated by the persistent mutation of the KRAS gene, and show unique characteristics of metabolic reprogramming.71 This mutation drives an increased dependence of cells on glutamine and promotes glucose metabolism reorganization by activating the MAPK and PI3K signaling pathways, resulting in a sustained increase in intracellular reactive oxygen species levels.72 This inherent oxidative stress state should theoretically make PC cells sensitive to ferroptosis, providing an ideal target for treatment.73 However, this sensitivity is often counteracted by complex adaptive mechanisms in the real tumor microenvironment. PC cells enhance their resistance to oxidative stress by upregulating the antioxidant pathway of nuclear factor E2-related factor 2.74 Meanwhile, cancer cells reduce the risk of lipid peroxidation at the substrate level by altering the composition of membrane lipids and decreasing the proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids.75

The most characteristic dense fibrotic matrix of PC constitutes multiple barriers, which seriously hinder the effective establishment of the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle.76 The large amount of collagen and fibronectin secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) forms a physical barrier, which not only limits the delivery of therapeutic drugs, but also hinders the infiltration of immune cells into the core area of the tumor.77 More complexly, tumor associated macrophages and myeloid derived suppressor cells abundant in the tumor microenvironment effectively inhibit T cell activation and function by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as transforming growth factor β and interleukin-10, as well as expressing metabolic enzymes such as arginase-1.78,79 This deep state of immune suppression directly weakens the immune system’s feedback regulation ability on ferroptosis, making it difficult for key immune feedback mechanisms such as the IFN-γ-mediated downregulation of SLC7A11 to effectively function.80

The common gene mutations in PC further complicate the regulatory network of the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle. The mutated p53 protein not only loses its classic tumor suppressive function, including the ability to promote ferroptosis by the transcriptional inhibition of SLC7A11, but some mutant p53 may even gain new functions, actively enhancing the cell’s antioxidant defense ability.81 In addition, the nutrient deficiency state in the tumor microenvironment, especially the limited supply of cysteine, may selectively promote the survival of tumor cell subpopulations with stronger antioxidant capacity, thereby forming an intrinsic resistance population before treatment.82

These factors together determine the final efficacy of the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle in PC. Deeply understanding these complex interactions is of great guiding significance for the development of combined treatment strategies for PC83 (Figure 3). It is expected to break through the bottleneck of current treatment of PC through multi-target intervention aimed at ferroptosis sensitivity and immunosuppressive microenvironment at the same time.

Tumor microenvironment responsive nanomaterials: An engineered strategy to initiate and amplify the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle

Design of intelligent nanomaterials: The construction principle of the Trojan horse

In order to achieve effective treatment of PC, smart nanomaterials need to have the core capabilities of precise targeting, deep penetration and long-term circulation. Its design imitates the strategic idea of the Trojan horse, using surface engineering, physical parameter optimization, and stealth treatment to enable the therapeutic carrier to break through multiple biological barriers and ultimately achieve specific enrichment at the tumor site.84

The targeting function of nanomaterials is mainly achieved through the surface modification of specific ligands.85 The ligands that target overexpressed receptors on the surface of tumor cells, such as folate, transferrin, and hyaluronic acid, which target CD44. Identifying targets of tumor matrix components, such as RGD peptides targeting fibronectin and collagen-binding peptides targeting collagen. Emerging targeting molecules, such as monoclonal antibodies and their fragments that recognize specific antigens of PC. These targeted molecules not only enhance the accumulation efficiency of nanomaterials at the tumor site by binding to specific biomarkers in the tumor microenvironment, but also promote cellular uptake of nanomaterials through receptor-mediated endocytosis.86

The physical parameters of nanomaterials have a decisive impact on their permeability in tumor tissues.87 Research has shown that particle sizes between 20 and 100 nm are most advantageous for achieving high permeability and retention effects in tumor tissues, while maintaining sufficient drug loading capacity.88 In terms of morphology design, anisotropic structures such as rod-shaped and disc-shaped particles exhibit enhanced tissue penetration ability compared to traditional spherical particles, due to their differential interactions with extracellular matrix components.89 In addition, the development of variable-size “intelligent” nanosystems - which maintain a large size during the cycling phase to ensure stability, and upon reaching the tumor microenvironment, respond to specific stimuli, such as matrix metalloproteinases to disassemble into smaller units—has become a cutting-edge strategy for enhancing deep tumor penetration.90

The circulation time of nanomaterials in the body is a key factor determining their therapeutic effect. Untreated nanoparticles enter the bloodstream and are quickly recognized and cleared by the mononuclear phagocytic cell system.91 Surface PEGylation can form a hydration layer on the surface of nanoparticles, effectively reducing protein adsorption and immune cell recognition, significantly prolonging their blood circulation half-life.92 In addition to traditional PEG, emerging stealth materials include zwitterionic polymers, cell membrane-coated biomimetic materials, and so forth.93 These materials maintain long cycling characteristics while avoiding the immunogenicity issues that PEG may cause.94 The optimized stealth design provides more opportunities for nanomaterials to infiltrate into tissue gaps through the abnormal structure of tumor blood vessels, laying a solid foundation for effective tumor targeting.

This multi-level design concept together constitutes the “Trojan horse” characteristic of smart nanomaterials, enabling them to overcome the complex biological barrier of PC, and creating the necessary conditions for the subsequent microenvironment response to drug release95 (Table 1).

Microenvironmental responsive drug-controlled release: Precise detonation within tumors

Because of the dense matrix barrier and complex microenvironment of PC, it is difficult for traditional chemotherapy drugs to effectively penetrate and act on cancer cells.98 Microenvironmental responsive drug-controlled release technology cleverly designs intelligent drug carriers to “sense” and respond to unique physiological signals in the tumor area, thereby achieving targeted and timed drug release, like installing a “smart bomb” inside the tumor waiting for specific instructions to detonate.99

One of the most significant characteristics of the microenvironment of PC is its weak acidity, which is due to the abnormal glycolysis of tumor cells (Warburg effect), which produces a large amount of lactic acid.100 The pH-responsive strategy utilizes this characteristic to design chemical bonds or materials that are unstable under acidic conditions. For example, using semicarbazide bonds, ketal bonds, or pH-sensitive polymers as the “switch” or “shell” of drug carriers. At the normal pH of blood circulation, these structures remain stable. Once the carrier permeates and accumulates in acidic tumor tissue with the blood, the acidic environment will trigger these chemical bond breaks or cause conformational changes in the polymer structure, thereby releasing the encapsulated drug and achieving preliminary spatial targeting of the tumor area.

At the same time, there is a key reducing signal within tumor cells—high concentrations of glutathione (GSH).101 The intracellular GSH concentration (2–10 mM) is much higher than the extracellular concentration. The restoration responsive system takes advantage of this huge concentration difference. The core is to introduce disulfide bonds as a “safety plug” in the carrier.96 When the carrier enters tumor cells through endocytosis, high concentrations of GSH inside the cells will cut off disulfide bonds, reducing them to thiol groups, causing the carrier to completely disintegrate and rapidly release drugs. This mechanism acts as an “intracellular insurance” for drugs, ensuring that they are primarily activated in reducing environments such as cytoplasm or organelles, greatly reducing leakage and damage to normal tissues during transportation.

In PC, in order to invade and metastasize, cancer cells will secrete a large number of matrix metalloproteinases, while the activity of cathepsin in lysosomes is also significantly increased. For example, drugs can be linked to nanoparticles through a short peptide sequence that can be specifically recognized and cleaved by MMP2.102 When the carrier reaches the tumor tissue, these overexpressed proteases precisely “cleave” the peptide chain, releasing active drug molecules. This strategy not only achieves high tumor specificity but also utilizes the high efficiency of enzymatic reactions to achieve rapid and amplified drug release and even helps to degrade dense tumor stroma and enhance drug permeability.

A single response mechanism may lead to insufficient control accuracy in complex in vivo environments due to signal overlap or individual differences. For example, the site of inflammation may also be acidic, and pH response alone may lead to false release. Therefore, cutting-edge research focuses on multi-responsive systems, which integrate two or more response mechanisms to construct intelligent carriers with “AND gate” logic. This means that multiple conditions must be met simultaneously to trigger drug release, thereby achieving higher levels of control accuracy and specificity. Through the above layered and progressive response strategy, microenvironment-responsive controlled drug release technology is pushing the treatment of PC from “indiscriminate bombing” to a new era of “precision surgery,” bringing new hope to overcome this persistent disease.

Co-delivery of nanocarriers synergistically initiates the “ferroptosis-immune” positive cycle

When fighting complex diseases such as cancer, single therapy often falls short. The collaborative therapy strategy aims to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects by combining drugs with different mechanisms.103 Among them, co-delivery nanocarrier technology is like a precise “combination punch,” which can simultaneously deliver drugs that induce ferroptosis and immune modulators to the tumor site, not only directly killing cancer cells, but also initiating and strengthening the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle to systematically strike the tumor.

Iron-dependent programmed cell death is caused by the accumulation of lipid peroxides. However, efficiently and specifically inducing ferroptosis at the tumor site faces challenges.104 Nanocarriers have become a “Trojan horse” for loading various ferroptosis inducers through co-delivery strategies. Firstly, for small molecule inhibitors such as system Xc - inhibitors or GPX4 inhibitors, nanocarriers can encapsulate them in hydrophobic cores or connect them through chemical bonds, effectively solving problems such as poor water solubility, poor in vivo stability, and high systemic toxicity.97 Secondly, nanotechnology can directly deliver the core element of ferroptosis. By designing iron-based nanoparticles or iron ion chelates, the carrier can specifically release iron in the tumor microenvironment, directly catalyze the Fenton reaction, and generate a large amount of reactive oxygen species, adding fuel to the explosion of lipid peroxides.105 This precise delivery ensures that the ferroptosis effect is efficiently and controllably activated in the tumor area.

Ferroptosis, while killing tumor cells, releases a large number of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor-associated antigens, which is equivalent to issuing an “enemy alert” to the body’s immune system.106 However, if the tumor microenvironment is in an immunosuppressive state, immune cells may not be effectively activated. Therefore, co-delivery of immune modulators is crucial. Nanocarriers can simultaneously load. Immune adjuvants, such as CpG oligonucleotides, directly stimulate antigen-presenting cell maturation.107 Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as nucleic acid drugs or small molecules targeting PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, are used to relieve the “brakes” on T cells. STING agonists can strongly activate the cGAS STING pathway, promote the production of type I interferon, and transform “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors.108 By delivering these immune modulators together with ferroptosis inducers to the same tumor area, nanocarriers ensure that during the “golden window period” of tumor cell death and antigen exposure, local immune suppression is synchronously released, thereby efficiently awakening and arming the immune army in the body.

Therefore, co-delivery of nanocarriers is like a precise “initiator”, which not only simultaneously drops the “bomb” (ferroptosis inducer) that kills tumors and the “signal bomb” (immune modulator) that activates allies, but more importantly, it promotes a self-reinforcing cycle.109 Ferroptosis enhances anti-tumor immunity, and the enhanced immune response in turn promotes broader ferroptosis. This closed-loop linkage greatly enhances the depth and persistence of treatment, providing the possibility of completely clearing tumors, forming immune memory, and preventing recurrence.

The double-edged sword: Ferroptosis in immune cells and the quest for selective nanomaterial design

While the pro-ferroptotic targeting of tumor cells represents a central tenet of ferroptosis-immunotherapy, an emerging body of evidence reveals that ferroptosis exerts a dualistic role within the tumor immune microenvironment, a complexity that must be acknowledged to rationally advance this therapeutic paradigm.

On one hand, effector immune cells are vulnerable to ferroptosis. Activated CD8+ CTLs and Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells, due to their high metabolic activity and membrane lipid unsaturation, exhibit heightened sensitivity to lipid peroxidation and glutathione depletion. Recent studies have demonstrated that tumor-derived IFN-γ-driven systemic redox stress can trigger ferroptosis in tumor-infiltrating T cells, thereby curtailing their proliferative capacity and cytotoxic function.110 Similarly, dendritic cells undergoing ferroptosis display impaired antigen cross-presentation and reduced migratory capacity, further compromising the priming phase of anti-tumor immunity. On the other hand, ferroptosis induction in immunosuppressive cellular compartments may be therapeutically advantageous. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which abundantly infiltrate the PC stroma, are known to accumulate iron and exhibit distinct lipid metabolic profiles.111 Preclinical interventions have shown that pharmacologically triggering ferroptosis in these populations can selectively deplete them, alleviate local immunosuppression, and restore T cell infiltration and activity.

In summary, the ferroptosis-immune axis is not unidirectional but bidirectional and context-dependent. Acknowledging this complexity not only enriches the conceptual framework of this review but also delineates a roadmap for the rational design of next-generation ferroptosis-immunotherapies. The field must now pivot from proof-of-concept efficacy studies toward mechanistic dissection of cell-type-specific ferroptosis sensitivity, and from monolithic nanocarriers toward intelligent, selective, and immune-aware delivery systems. Only through such refined engineering can the promise of ferroptosis-immunotherapy be fully translated into durable clinical responses for patients with PC.

Challenges, opportunities, and future prospects

The tumor microenvironment of PC, especially pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, is marked by severe immunosuppression and dense fibrosis, which is significantly different from solid tumors. The physical barrier formed by CAFs and excess extracellular matrix (ECM) not only severely hinders drug penetration, but also creates a low-oxygen and nutrient-deficient metabolic niche, driving metabolic symbiosis and treatment resistance.112 At the same time, the widespread infiltration of regulatory Tregs, M2 TAMs, and MDSCs leads to the dysfunction of CTLs, resulting in limited immune checkpoint blockade in PC. In this context, ferroptosis immunotherapy exhibits unique mechanistic compatibility. Ferroptosis, as an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven non-apoptotic cell death, can effectively kill tumor cells that are tolerant to apoptosis. Its inherent immunogenicity converts immune “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors by releasing DAMPs and tumor antigens, restarting dendritic cell maturation and T cell activation.113 The dual ability of “directly breaking through the matrix barrier” and “synchronously reversing immune suppression” makes ferroptosis immunotherapy a unique strategy for simultaneously overcoming the physical and immune barriers of PC. In addition, the microenvironmental characteristics of fibrosis and oxidative stress in PC itself may enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to ferroptosis inducers, providing a therapeutic window for the rational design of nanomaterials.

Although the strategy of initiating and amplifying the “ferroptosis-immune” cycle based on tumor microenvironment responsive nanomaterials has brought revolutionary new perspectives to PC therapy (Figure 4). Ferroptosis simultaneously activates multiple negative feedback mechanisms, limiting the persistence and strength of anti-tumor immunity. At the molecular level, ferroptosis tumor cells release immunosuppressive byproducts, which inhibit dendritic cell maturation and promote regulatory T cell differentiation. At the cellular level, chronic or excessive ferroptosis stress can induce T cell exhaustion, premature immune cell failure, and actively recruit MDSCs into the microenvironment. At the microenvironmental level, uncontrolled high-load iron-induced death can lead to secondary oxidative damage and abnormal inflammation, while reinforcing the fibrosis and immunosuppressive niche of PC. A comprehensive understanding of this bidirectional association requires future nanomedicine strategies to go beyond the one-dimensional thinking of “maximizing ferroptosis” and shift toward actively intercepting these negative feedback nodes, designing pulsed ferroptosis induction regimens, and combining with immune checkpoint blockade. Only by acknowledging and treating this inherent duality can the potential of the ferroptosis immune axis be fully unleashed.

PC is not a single disease, and there are fundamental differences between its classical and basal subtypes in terms of interstitial abundance, immune cell infiltration spectrum, metabolic reprogramming, and redox homeostasis. These differences directly affect the inherent sensitivity to ferroptosis and subsequent immune amplification efficiency. The basal phenotype is characterized by higher matrix density and immune suppression dominance, requiring the nano platform to prioritize matrix penetration and myeloid cell reprogramming. The classic type of antigen presentation machine, which preserves relatively well, is more suitable for focusing on maximizing immunogenic ferroptosis and CD8 + T cell activation. However, the current mainstream paradigm for nanomaterial design is still a one-size-fits-all approach, with very few efficacy evaluations or customized delivery systems based on tumor subtype stratification. Future research urgently needs to integrate subtype perception design principles, such as TME biomarker response modules or targeted strategies accompanied by diagnostic guidance, to achieve precise adaptation to the unique ecological niches of different PC subtypes and fundamentally solve the heterogeneity problem. Related to this is the challenge of the complexity of nanocarriers themselves, how to accurately regulate the drug loading ratio and release kinetics of various components, such as ferroptosis inducers and immune modulators, ensuring their optimal spatiotemporal synergistic effects, is a major test for drug design.

Although multi-responsive nanoplatforms are widely advocated, research directly comparing the superiority of multi-responsive and single-responsive systems head-to-head under the same PC model, drug loading, and matching administration regimen is still extremely scarce. Current work mostly focuses on concept validation and lacks a systematic deconstruction of the contributions of various response modules. However, from the perspective of delivery biology, multiple response design has solid theoretical support. PC presents a sequential and heterogeneous physiological barrier, and a single trigger can only break through a single bottleneck. The integration of multiple systems with “AND” logic gates or temporal activation has theoretical gains in tumor specificity, penetration depth, and avoiding early leakage. The direction of progress in the field should be to conduct rigorous controlled studies, quantify therapeutic efficacy while paying more attention to treatment indices and intratumoral distribution, and elevate the “multiple response” label from design fashion to evidence-based engineering principles. Another core bottleneck that collaborates with it is the physical barrier of the PC dense matrix. The primary obstacle limiting the accumulation and distribution of nanomaterials is the abundance of CAFs, excessive ECM deposition, and elevated interstitial hydraulic pressure.114 The current material design has shifted from passive diffusion to active matrix penetration and remodeling, with the emergence of diverse strategies such as size and morphology engineering, surface charge flipping, ECM degradation enzyme functionalization, and CAF targeted reprogramming. The recently emerging multi-stage sequential delivery system has shown synergistic advantages in terms of accumulation and uniform distribution. In the future, we should focus on clinically convertible penetration evaluation indicators and deeply integrate penetration ability with on-demand triggering of ferroptosis function, so as to transform the “matrix barrier” from an insurmountable obstacle into a designable treatment interface.

The biosafety of iron-based nanomaterials is a hard constraint for clinical translation. Preclinical studies have reported that the dosage of iron is 200 μg and 600 μg per mouse models. However, these values are rarely validated in the context of PC tumor disease.115 Iron-based nanomaterials tend to accumulate in the liver, spleen, and kidneys. In addition, the nonlinear pharmacokinetic characteristics of iron and the dose-dependent transition from benign storage to toxic loading still lack systematic characterization in large animal models of transformation. Therefore, in the future, it is necessary to prioritize systematic dose escalation toxicology research, integrate organ chips and other platforms, and establish a therapeutic index benchmark that balances the efficacy of ferroptosis.

At the same time, clinically accessible ferroptosis immunotherapy must also address the potential risks of excessive immune activation. Although this review has elucidated the immune stimulatory ability of ferroptosis, it has not yet delved into how to actively constrain the amplitude, duration, and spatial range of subsequent immune responses through rational material design. Emerging strategies include co-delivery of immunomodulators to set the “brakes” on immune activation. TME restricted immune activation engineered to release immune stimulation loads only within the tumor bed, avoiding systemic immune compartment disturbances. Temporal gating strategy, which delays the release of anti-inflammatory drugs after ferroptosis induction and initiates the inflammation resolution program after tumor clearance. The deep integration of such immune homeostasis-oriented design principles into the nano platform is the core guarantee for ferroptosis immunotherapy to move steadily from a preclinical efficacy model to a safe treatment plan for patients with PC.

In conclusion, ferroptosis immunotherapy provides a therapeutic paradigm with both mechanism innovation and transformation potential for PC, the “king of intractability.” These challenges do not exist in isolation, but are intertwined and progressively layered. For example, subtype-specific matrix features directly affect the selection of penetration strategies, and the strength of negative feedback is closely related to the spatiotemporal patterns induced by ferroptosis. Therefore, future research needs to break the “single-dimensional optimization” thinking, construct an integrated nano platform for multi-objective collaborative optimization, and systematically validate it in disease models that are closer to clinical practice, such as patient-derived organoids, genetically engineered mice, and large animal models. Only in this way can ferroptosis immune nanotherapy complete the breathtaking leap from “beauty of concept” to “clinical reality,” and bring real and accessible survival benefits to patients with PC.
