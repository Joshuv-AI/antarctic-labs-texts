Spatio-temporal constraints on thrusting across fold-and-thrust belts worldwide
Authors: Manuel Curzi, Andrea Billi, Eugenio Carminati, Stefano Tavani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fold-and-thrust belts (FTBs) are fundamental geological structures whose spatio-temporal evolution can be reconstructed using s–t graphs that integrate thrust positions and timing to trace sequences of activation and reactivation. However, accurately constraining the timing of thrusting events remains difficult due to methodological uncertainties and inconsistencies among different dating techniques. To address these challenges and offer a refined view of FTB evolution, we compile and integrate time constraints from stratigraphic, thermochronological, and radiometric data across ten FTBs that span diverse geodynamic contexts, including the Rocky Mountains, Idaho–Montana Belt, Sevier Belt, Mexican Fold Belt, and Central Andes in the Cordilleran orogen, and Apennines, Southern Pyrenees, Jura Mountains, Southern Alps, and Himalayas in the Alpine-Himalayan orogen. We assess the strengths and limitations of each method: stratigraphy provides broad but often imprecise age constraints; radiometric dating yields precise yet spatially and temporally sparse data; thermochronology offers insights into vertical displacements linked to thrusting. By constructing and comparing s–t graphs, we identify consistent thrusting patterns across regions. FTBs display a piggy-back propagation style characterized by long-lasting, partially overlapping activity of multiple thrusts. These observations support a revised piggy-back model that incorporates prolonged, synchronous thrust activity within a generally forward-propagating system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19660-w.

Introduction

Fold-and-thrust belts (FTBs) rank among the most prominent and significant structures on Earth, forming the backbone of many convergent orogens and playing a foundational role in the development of modern geology1,2. In particular, conceptual models of convergent plate margins rely heavily on a robust understanding of the kinematic and temporal evolution of FTBs3.

One effective tool for reconstructing this evolution is the spatio-temporal (s–t) graph, which synthesizes spatial (i.e., structural position within the belt) and temporal (i.e., age of thrusting) data in cross-sectional view4–7. These graphs consolidate geometric and chronological information for individual thrusts, enabling the analysis of deformation sequences—whether piggyback, out-of-sequence, or synchronous8,9. Despite their utility, s–t graphs are often limited by significant uncertainties stemming from measurement errors, aleatory variability, and model-dependent assumptions10. While recent studies have begun to address geometric uncertainties in structural models10,11, temporal constraints—critical for kinematic interpretation—remain underdeveloped, due in part to the complexity of applying precise dating techniques to brittle upper-crustal deformation12.

Historically, stratigraphic approaches have dominated, but these are prone to two major sources of error: (1) strong interpretative subjectivity—particularly in distinguishing syn-from pre-kinematic deposits—and (2) the propagation of unverified age estimates from older studies, which are frequently cited as constraints despite a lack of direct evidence13.

Four primary methods are commonly used to constrain the timing of thrusting in the upper crust, each with unique strengths and limitations: (1) growth strata analysis14 allows for the identification of the full duration of thrust activity, from initiation to cessation; (2) relative dating from footwall and hanging wall stratigraphy15 provides minimum ages for thrust cessation; (3) low-temperature thermochronology16 tracks vertical exhumation patterns but lacks direct temporal linkage to fault motion; and (4) radiometric dating of syn-tectonic mineralizations17 yields precise ages but only for isolated thrust-related events. Integrating these heterogeneous datasets into coherent spatio-temporal models remains challenging due to their often unevenly distribution, different uncertainties, and partial coverage of the deformation history.

In this study, we critically evaluate the potential and limitations of combining these diverse datasets through ten case studies selected to represent a broad range of tectonic settings and data quality. These include FTBs from both the Cordilleran orogenic system of the Americas (Rocky Mountains, Idaho–Montana Belt, Sevier Belt, Mexican Fold-Thrust Belt, and Central Andes) and the Alpine-Himalayan system of Eurasia (Apennines, Southern Pyrenees, Jura Mountains, Southern Alps, and Himalayas) (Fig. 1). Our results demonstrate that, despite limitations in data resolution and completeness, it is possible to derive s–t graphs that capture first-order thrusting behavior across diverse orogens. While dating uncertainties persist, we show that their assessment allows the identification of consistent temporal patterns. By synthesizing these patterns, we propose a conceptual framework for interpreting FTB dynamics, with direct implications for plate tectonic reconstructions in convergent settings. Our findings consistently reveal the prevalence of piggy-back-style thrust propagation, often involving coeval motion on multiple structures—challenging the traditional view of strictly sequential deformation. This behavior aligns with critical Coulomb wedge theory, which predicts forward propagation via frontal accretion until a critical taper is achieved.

The refined s–t graphs we present offer valuable first-order constraints for both numerical18 and analogue19,20 models of FTB evolution, as well as geodynamic models of subduction, continental collision, and lithospheric delamination21–23. Moreover, a precise understanding of thrust timing is essential for assessing seismic hazard in active orogens24, where reactivation of inherited structures often governs present-day deformation.

Background on dating approaches

Dating thrusting and folding events in FTBs within the upper crust has traditionally relied on four broad methodological categories: biostratigraphy, magnetostratigraphy, (radiometric) geochronology, and thermochronology, implemented into the following four approaches.

Ages of rocks in the hanging wall and footwall

This is the earliest historically and most widely used approach for constraining the timing of thrusting, largely due to its simplicity. However, it is often misapplied to estimate the onset of thrusting events15,25. In practice, the age of the youngest sediment in the footwall—derived from paleontological, magnetostratigraphic, or geochronological data26—constrains the termination of thrusting as being “younger than” the dated material (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Representative cartoon of the most adopted dating approach and methods for thrusting.

The reliability of this approach hinges on accurate identification of growth strata, the completeness of the stratigraphic record27, and the resolution of the dating techniques employed28. Importantly, erosion or a hiatus in sedimentation can severely impact the preservation of the youngest footwall rocks. In many cases, footwall exposures may consist of rocks significantly older than the most recent slip events. For instance, a Pliocene thrust may juxtapose Precambrian rocks in the footwall—not because the thrusting is that old, but because older units have been displaced. This underscores the method’s limitations in directly dating thrusting, particularly in structurally or stratigraphically complex settings.

Growth strata

Originally described by Riba14, growth strata form contemporaneously with deformation, as folding or faulting modifies accommodation space for sediment deposition. These strata typically exhibit diagnostic features such as wedging geometries and progressive unconformities29–31 (Fig. 2b), making them valuable records of active deformation.

When a continuous sedimentary record is available—such as in offshore settings with high-quality seismic reflection data corroborated by drill cores—the interpretation of pre-, syn-, and post-kinematic units is relatively straightforward and less susceptible to user bias. However, in onshore poorly outcropping areas, distinguishing these units becomes increasingly interpretative. Additionally, differential compaction or sediment infill of topographic depressions may cause post-kinematic deposits to mimic syn-kinematic geometries, further complicating analysis. Challenges also arise from potential gaps in the sedimentary record and uncertainties inherent in the dating methods used (e.g., radiometric, biostratigraphic, or magnetostratigraphic techniques, or combinations thereof).

Low-temperature thermochronology

Thermochronologic techniques—including fission-track dating, (U-Th)/He analyses on bedrock or detrital minerals (e.g., zircon, apatite)32,33, and dolomite thermoluminescence34—can provide time constraints on thrust-related exhumation (Fig. 2c). These methods track the cooling of rocks as they are exhumed along thrust ramps, recording the moment when minerals transit through closure temperatures and isotopic systems become fixed.

By employing multiple thermochronometers with varying closure temperatures (e.g., apatite, zircon, mica), this approach can reconstruct multi-stage exhumation histories, provided suitable minerals are present. Recent methodological advances—such as inverse thermal history modeling35–37, integration with thermokinematic simulations38,39, and provenance studies40–42—have enhanced the interpretive power of thermochronological data.

Nevertheless, these methods primarily capture crustal cooling related to uplift and erosion, and thus mainly reflect the vertical component of shortening43. Consequently, while informative, thermochronological constraints are indirect proxies for thrusting and do not date thrust motion directly.

Radiometric dating

This approach yields absolute ages directly tied to thrust activity, minimizing interpretative ambiguity. The most commonly applied methods are Ar–Ar and K–Ar dating of synkinematic clay minerals in fault gouges, and U–Pb dating of carbonate minerals precipitated during thrust-related fluid flow12,44 (Fig. 2d). These methods assume the isotopic systems remained closed during and after mineral growth.

Despite analytical challenges12, these techniques provide discrete time markers for fault activity. However, they typically represent single deformation events within potentially long-lived thrust systems. The approach is limited to settings where thrusts are either exposed or accessed via drilling, and where mineralizations are well-preserved and isotopically datable.

Results from fold-and-thrust belts worldwide

Rationale

The integration of chronological data in FTB studies is complicated by the varying accuracy, resolution, and temporal sensitivity of the available dating methods. To illustrate how these challenges can be effectively addressed, we present ten case studies (Fig. 1) from which we derive s–t graphs. For each FTB, we selected published regional-scale geological cross-sections (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7) aligned parallel to the dominant tectonic transport direction. The FTBs and their corresponding chronological data are described below, grouped by orogenic system and presented in alphabetical order within each system.

Although multiple cross-sections often exist for each region—reflecting different interpretations of surface and/or subsurface data—we prioritized those supported by the most comprehensive chronological datasets. This strategy emphasizes the reconstruction of broad, first-order tectonic patterns across different FTBs globally, rather than delving into case-specific structural models or local geological complexities. This strategy inherently limits the representativeness of our results to the vicinity of each transect, and not to the orogen as a whole.

Chronological data (Table S1) were projected onto the selected cross-sections using a consistent graphical approach. Radiometric ages directly linked to thrust activity—such as U–Pb dates on syntectonic carbonates and K–Ar or Ar–Ar dates on thrust-related clay minerals—are projected as point data with associated uncertainty bars. Stratigraphic constraints, including ages derived from growth strata and from the youngest syn- and post-kinematic rocks in thrust hanging walls and footwalls, are projected as time intervals (rectangles), where the vertical height reflects the duration between the onset and cessation of thrusting.

We combined the stratigraphic data described under approaches (i) and (ii) in Section “Background on dating approaches”—i.e., the ages of rocks in the thrust hanging wall and footwall, and growth strata—into a single category. This is justified by their shared sources of uncertainty, including analytical errors, interpretive variability, and limited traceability in published datasets. Thermochronological data (e.g., fission-track and (U–Th)/He dating on detrital minerals and thermal modeling on K-feldspar) are projected as points with error bars, representing the timing of exhumation associated with thrusting (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7).

To minimize spatial uncertainties, we systematically excluded chronological data located more than 50 km from the selected cross-sections. The sole exception is the Rocky Mountains case study, where data from up to ~ 300 km away were included, following the methodology and interpretations of Haines and Van der Pluijm45.

FTBs of the cordilleran orogenic system

Central Andes

The Central Andes represent an active thick- and thin-skinned retro-wedge formed by ongoing subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate, driven by the eastward convergence of Pacific Ocean plates46. Thrusts are predominantly N–S striking and east-verging, involving the Proterozoic–Paleozoic basement and the overlying Mesozoic–Cenozoic cover, which includes volcanic rocks, siliciclastic sequences, carbonates, and evaporites42. Shortening initiated in the Late Cretaceous and propagated eastward toward the foreland, continuing to the present day42,47,48.

We selected the E-W striking cross-section from Barnes et al.48, located in Bolivia at 19.5°S and 67–63°W, spanning 335 km (Fig. 3a). Stratigraphic data projected from a ~ 50 km-wide transect indicate that thrusting began in the late Eocene49, progressively migrating eastward during the Oligocene and Early Miocene, and continuing through the Miocene to Holocene42,48,50 (Fig. 3a). Thermochronological data (fission tracks and U–Th/He dating on apatite) constrain thrust-related cooling at 30.3 ± 5.6 Ma, 27.6 ± 5.4 Ma, 21.8 ± 9.6 Ma, 19 ± 4.8 Ma, 26.9 ± 4.2 Ma, 19.4 ± 2.8 Ma, and 17.8 ± 3 Ma48. Total shortening along this section is estimated at ~ 285 km50.

Idaho–Montana belt

The Idaho–Montana Belt is a thin- and thick-skinned retro-wedge within the Cordilleran orogen, formed in response to subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the North American Plate51,52. Thrusts strike NW–SE and verge NE, involving the Precambrian basement, intrusive igneous bodies, and Phanerozoic marine and continental sedimentary rocks53,54.

We selected the 350 km-long cross-section at 42°N, 114°W from Parker and Pearson53, onto which Kaempfer et al.54 projected thermochronological data (U–Th/He and fission-track dating on zircons and apatites) from a ~ 50 km-wide transect. These data (Fig. 3b) indicate that thrust-related exhumation began in the Early Cretaceous and progressively propagated northeastward through to the Eocene. Total shortening along this cross-section is ~ 245 km53.

Mexican belt

The Mexican Belt is a thick- and thin-skinned retro-wedge forming the southern segment of the Cordilleran orogen. It developed through subduction of the Pacific and Cocos plates beneath the North American Plate55,56. NE-verging thrusts deform the Mesozoic–Cenozoic sedimentary cover and associated volcanic units56,57.

The selected cross-section by Fitz-Díaz et al.55 is 120 km long and located at 20°N, 100°W, incorporating stratigraphic and radiometric constraints from a ~ 30 km-wide transect. Stratigraphic data indicate that thrusting initiated in the Late Cretaceous and propagated toward the foreland (NE) until the Eocene58,59 (Fig. 5b). Radiometric ages from syn-kinematic clay gouges (Ar–Ar dating) confirm Eocene thrusting, with key ages at 83.5 ± 1.5 Ma, 82.7 ± 0.5 Ma, 76.5 ± 1.0 Ma, 64 ± 2.0 Ma, and 43.5 ± 0.5 Ma55 (Fig. 4a). Total estimated shortening is ~ 140 km57.

Rocky mountains

The Rocky Mountains are a thin- and thick-skinned retro-wedge forming the northern Cordilleran orogen, developed in response to subduction of the Farallon and Kula plates beneath the North American Plate60. Thrusts verge NE, stacking Paleozoic and Mesozoic sedimentary rocks onto the underlying metamorphic basement60,61.

We selected a 40 km-long cross-section of the Canadian Rockies (53°N, 119°W) from Pană and van der Pluijm17. The cross-section includes radiometric age constraints projected from a ~ 300 km-wide transect. Syn-kinematic Ar–Ar ages from clay gouges indicate thrusting from the Late Jurassic to Eocene, migrating progressively northeastward, with recorded events at 163 ± 7.6 Ma, 99.2 ± 10.2 Ma, 74.8 ± 2.1 Ma, 74.2 ± 6.7 Ma, and 52.4 ± 12.3 Ma (Fig. 4b)17. Thermochronological data suggest cooling in the external belt between 64 ± 8 and 63 ± 11 Ma, and between 67 ± 8 and 59 ± 7 Ma62. Estimated total shortening is ~ 200 km17.

Sevier belt

The Sevier Belt is a retro-wedge located in the central Cordilleran orogen, commonly interpreted as the thin-skinned component of the Rocky Mountain system63,64. It formed through subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the North American Plate51,63,65,66. Thrusts verge E–NE and involve the Precambrian basement and the overlying Proterozoic to Eocene sedimentary sequences53,63,65.

We selected a 40 km-long cross-section in central Utah (39°N, 119°W) from DeCelles and Coogan65, incorporating stratigraphic and thermochronological constraints from a ~ 50 km-wide transect. Stratigraphic data indicate that thrusting initiated in the Early Cretaceous and propagated toward the foreland (E–NE) through the mid–Upper Cretaceous and into the Paleocene–Eocene67 (Fig. 5). Thermochronological data on detrital zircons constrain cooling in the internal belt at 96.3 ± 1.8 Ma68. The total shortening along this section is estimated at ~ 220 km65.

FTBs of the Alpine-Himalayan orogenic system

Central Apennines

The Central Apennines represent an active FTB characterized by both thick- and thin-skinned deformation. This pro-wedge developed in response to the eastward rollback of the west-dipping subduction of the Tethys Ocean beneath the European continental margin69. Shortening affected Mesozoic–Cenozoic evaporites and carbonates, as well as the underlying Paleozoic metamorphic basement70,71. NE-verging thrusting progressively migrated toward the foreland, where it remains active today69–71.

We adopted the structural cross-section by Cosentino et al.71, modified by Curzi et al.7, spanning 190 km between 41–42°N and 12–14°E. Stratigraphic and radiometric time constraints were projected onto this section by Cavinato and DeCelles6 and Curzi et al.7, based on data from a ~ 25 km wide transect (Fig. 6a). Stratigraphic constraints13,71 indicate thrusting in the axial and external (NE) sectors during the late Miocene, late Miocene–Pliocene, and Pliocene–Holocene (Fig. 6a). In the internal portion, radiometric data reveal Miocene-age thrusting, with K–Ar dating of syn-kinematic clay minerals in fault gouge yielding 22.1 ± 0.6 Ma7 (Fig. 6a). Toward the thrust front, U–Pb ages on tectonic carbonates show a younging trend: 15.6 ± 1.9 Ma, 14.3 ± 1.3 Ma, 12.7 ± 2.0 Ma, 7.0 ± 1.6 Ma, and 5.1 ± 3.7 Ma72,73 (Fig. 6a). The total estimated shortening across the Central Apennines is ~ 170 km74.

Himalayas

The Himalayas constitute an active, thick-skinned pro-wedge formed by the ongoing collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates following the closure of the Neo-Tethys Ocean75. This S-verging orogen involves Precambrian crystalline basement rocks and an overlying succession of Proterozoic to Cenozoic sedimentary and metamorphic units76. Our analysis is based on the 125 km long cross-section from DeCelles et al.5, spanning the region between Tibet and Nepal (81–81.3°E, 28–30°N). Stratigraphic time constraints projected from a ~ 50 km wide transect indicate the onset of thrusting in the early Miocene (~ 22–15 Ma) within the internal part of the belt, followed by progressive southward propagation through the Miocene (~ 15–12 Ma), middle Miocene–early Pliocene (~ 12–5 Ma), and into the Quaternary4,5,77–79 (Fig. 6b). Total shortening along this section is estimated at 480–740 km78, with a mean of ~ 610 km5.

Jura belt

The Jura Belt forms the thin-skinned pro-wedge of the western and central Alpine orogen, associated with the continental collision of the European and African plates80. Shortening deformed Mesozoic–Cenozoic evaporitic, carbonate, and siliciclastic sedimentary cover rocks, detached from a Paleozoic metamorphic basement along Triassic evaporites81. The NW-verging thrusts initiated during the Miocene and propagated forelandward into the Quaternary, accompanied by out-of-sequence thrusting82–84. This was coupled with the uplift and deformation of the Miocene foreland molasse basin, now preserved as a piggyback basin atop advancing thrust sheets83.

We utilized a 50 km long cross-section from eastern France (6–7°E, 47–46°N)84, which incorporates radiometric data from a ~ 15 km wide transect. U–Pb dating of tectonic carbonates constrains thrusting at 11.4 ± 1.1 Ma in the innermost structures, with subsequent ages of 10.6 ± 0.5 Ma, 9.6 ± 0.3 Ma, and 7.5 ± 1.1 Ma marking progressive foreland propagation84 (Fig. 6c). These ages align with stratigraphic data indicating Miocene thrusting82,85,86. The estimated shortening along the section is ~ 12 km85.

Southern Alps

The Eastern Southern Alps comprise a thick-skinned retro-wedge of the Alpine orogen, developed during the convergence of the European and Adriatic plates following closure of the Alpine Tethys87. SE–SSE-verging thrusts deform the Paleozoic basement and overlying Mesozoic–Cenozoic sedimentary and volcanic units87.

We analyzed a 40 km long cross-section at 46°N, 12°E, based on Curzi et al.88, Schönborn89, and Doglioni and Carminati87, incorporating data from a ~ 15 km wide transect (Fig. 7a). The preserved stratigraphic record suggests inner belt thrusting during the Eocene–Miocene90. Apatite fission-track and U–Th/He analyses indicate cooling ages of 77.4 ± 13.8 Ma and 11.5 ± 4.2 to 9.1 ± 2.4 Ma91,92. Radiometric ages from fault gouge and tectonic carbonates reveal a prolonged history of thrusting: 77.9 ± 15.1 Ma and 76.2 ± 1.4 Ma (K–Ar), followed by 46.0 ± 15.0 Ma, 23.2 ± 2.1 Ma, 15.0 ± 11.0 Ma, 9.5 ± 0.8 Ma, and 6.0 ± 1.7 Ma (U–Pb)88 (Fig. 7a). Stratigraphic and radiometric data indicate southward thrust propagation from the Eocene to the Pliocene87,88,90,93, with events dated at 29.9 ± 11.2 Ma and 23.0 ± 14.0 Ma88 (Fig. 7a). Thrusting continued through the Miocene–Holocene87,93 and remains active at depth in the external thrusts87. Total shortening along this section is estimated at ~ 50 km89.

Southern pyrenees

The Southern Pyrenees comprise a thin- and thick-skinned pro-wedge formed by the collision of the Iberian and Eurasian plates during Africa–Europe convergence94. Thrusts strike WNW–ESE, verge SSW, and deform the Paleozoic basement along with Mesozoic–Cenozoic sedimentary sequences composed of siliciclastic rocks, carbonates, and evaporites94–97.

We adopted the 45 km long cross-section at 42°N, 2°E from Travé et al.98 (Fig. 7b), where Haines and van der Pluijm45 projected constraints from a ~ 30 km wide transect. Stratigraphic data94,96 indicate that thrusting began in the Upper Cretaceous in the internal sector, propagating southward during the Eocene–Oligocene (Fig. 7b). Thermochronology of K-feldspar suggests cooling began at ~ 50 Ma in the belt’s axial sector99. Ar–Ar dating of syn-kinematic clay minerals documents progressive SSW migration of the FTB with ages of 49.5 ± 4.4 Ma, 40.9 ± 7.2 Ma, 38.6 ± 5.8 Ma, 36.1 ± 4.8 Ma, and 31.9 ± 3.9 Ma45 (Fig. 7b). The total shortening along this cross-section is ~ 125 km100.

Discussion

Potential and limitations of the methods

We compiled the spatio-temporal data of each FTB into a cumulative s–t graph (Fig. 8), where the distance along each cross-section was normalized by rescaling to a common dimensionless scale from foreland to hinterland. This approach enables direct comparison of deformation timing across FTBs, irrespective of their absolute length.

Viewed from a broader perspective, the most striking feature of the s–t graph is the low temporal resolution of stratigraphic data, which are often constrained only to the scale of geological periods. Due to imprecise age brackets, many stratigraphic records cluster around period boundaries, effectively reducing the temporal continuum to discrete, step-like intervals. As a result, some FTBs appear to initiate or terminate deformation synchronously across widths spanning tens to hundreds of kilometers—an implausible tectonic scenario. This uncertainty limits our ability to robustly model the processes governing the onset and termination of thrusting.

Such limitations are particularly evident when estimating the duration of thrust activity. For example, in the Pyrenees, where the most complete record of growth strata exists14,101, thrusting durations of ~ 10 Myr are inferred for individual faults (Fig. 8). In contrast, in the Apennines—where growth strata are preserved only in the outermost belt—durations of ~ 2 Myr are estimated for thrusts accommodating over 30 km of offset71,74. This mismatch implies unrealistically high slip rates exceeding 10 mm/yr, significantly higher than the well-constrained rates of 0.5–4 mm/yr for the outermost Apennine thrusts102–104.

Radiometric dating of thrust-related minerals offers more precise but highly localized temporal markers, which often fail to constrain the onset or cessation of thrusting accurately. Large error margins further diminish their resolution, and individual data points may not reflect the relative importance of thrusting events. For instance, minor reactivations and major tectonic pulses can yield indistinguishable signatures. Tavani et al.73 demonstrated this in the Apennines, where U–Pb dating of tectonic carbonates recorded both compressional and extensional phases, whose error bars overlap73, illustrating the inherent ambiguity in radiometric interpretations.

Thermochronology shares many of these issues, combining the limited resolution of stratigraphic methods with the localized nature of radiometric data. Although capable of reconstructing complex thermal histories100, thermochronology often fails to unambiguously resolve the onset, termination, or variability of slip rates. Consequently, as illustrated in Fig. 8, reconciling broad stratigraphic records with sparse radiometric and thermochronological data remains challenging.

Interpreting the s–t graphs

The analyzed FTBs span a wide range of geological settings, including: (1) active (e.g., Central Andes)42 and fossil belts (e.g., Sevier Belt)65; (2) retro-wedges (e.g., Southern Alps)88 and pro-wedges (e.g., Apennines)69; (3) systems related to continental collision (e.g., Himalayas)76 and oceanic subduction (e.g., Andes)46; (4) domains of fast (e.g., Himalayas)75 or slow to null convergence (e.g., Apennines)69; and (5) belts undergoing high (~ 600 km in the Himalayas)5 or low shortening (~ 12 km in the Jura Belt)85. Despite variations in tectonic setting and erosion rates, piggy-back-style propagation remains the dominant mode of deformation. This long-term trend aligns with predictions from critical Coulomb wedge theory, which describes wedge growth through frontal accretion up to a critical taper19,105. Temporal averaging in s–t graphs effectively filters out short-term noise, emphasizing the robustness of wedge mechanics in capturing first-order kinematic patterns. In mature collisional systems, slowing convergence should lead to deceleration of the thrust front. Yet, this signal is absent from our s–t graphs. Fossil belts such as the Pyrenees, Rocky Mountains, Mexico, Jura, Sevier, and Idaho-Montana show no systematic deceleration compared to active systems like the Andes, Southern Alps, Himalayas, and Apennines. This absence may reflect intrinsic FTB dynamics or insufficient data resolution, particularly in the youngest, most external portions of the belts, which may have been eroded or remain unsampled. If real, the lack of a deceleration phase would suggest that FTB cessation is abrupt rather than gradual, consistent with predictions from critical taper theory19,105, in which wedge stabilization halts frontal propagation, potentially triggering reactivation of internal thrusts.

Conclusions

This study provides a global synthesis of the spatio-temporal evolution of FTBs, integrating data from ten representative case studies across diverse geodynamic settings. By compiling and normalizing disparate datasets, we offer a comparative framework that illuminates first-order patterns in FTB development. Although some variability may stem from uneven data availability and resolution, the recurrence of key features—such as prolonged, partially overlapping thrust activity and consistent forward propagation—emerges as a robust signal across all examined systems. These patterns should be regarded as a first-order insight from selected transects, capturing key aspects of thrust system dynamics while not fully reflecting the complexity of entire orogens, and thus representing a preliminary step toward more comprehensive, orogen-scale reconstructions.

To our knowledge, this is the first synthesis to explicitly evaluate the limitations and implications of current temporal datasets when applied to crustal-scale reconstructions of FTBs and their geodynamic context. Despite recent methodological advances in thrust dating, a fundamental challenge remains: reconciling high-resolution but spatially limited radiometric and thermochronological data with broader, often coarser stratigraphic records. The mismatch in temporal resolution across these datasets hampers our ability to define the initiation, duration, and cessation of thrusting events with confidence.

We aim to stimulate debate on the spatiotemporal evolution of orogens and the use of s–t diagrams, with the expectation that future studies will expand this approach using alternative and more data-rich transects. Future progress in this field depends on densifying the spatial and temporal coverage of age constraints across FTBs. Systematic acquisition of absolute dates for individual thrusts—rather than localized or opportunistic sampling—will be critical to capturing the full history of belt propagation. Equally important is improving the accuracy and internal consistency of chronological datasets, alongside resolving intrinsic uncertainties in the geometric and kinematic reconstructions of FTB architectures.

Our results underscore a broader methodological point: geodynamic models that rely on spatio-temporal data from FTBs are only as reliable as the chronological constraints on which they are built. Without a denser, more accurate, and spatially coherent temporal framework, efforts to model plate convergence and orogenic evolution remain vulnerable to misinterpretation. Addressing this gap is essential for advancing both the conceptual understanding and the predictive power of orogenic models.
