Assessment of genetic diversity and phenotypic trait profiles among sweetpotato using single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers and agronomic variables
Authors: Josefina Ndamononghenda Abed, Dorcas Olubunmi Ibitoye, Solomon Olufemi Afuape, Bunmi Olasanmi, Hapson Mushoriwa, Paterne A Agre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSweetpotato is an important global crop of food and nutritional significance and rural income generation, especially in Africa. Although, the crop is well adapted to diverse agroecological conditions, its genetic and phenotypic diversity is yet to be well explored. In this study, the diversity of 72 sweetpotato genotypes were assessed using phenotypic traits and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers. Genotypes differed significantly for phenotypic traits (P ≤ 0.001) such as number of leaf lobes and vine internode length. Storage root weight had strong positive correlation to commercial root yield (r > 0.80). Principal component analysis showed that the first seven components with eigenvalues > 1 explained 75.68% of the total variation among the sweetpotato genotypes. Cluster analysis grouped the genotypes into four major clusters with cluster I exhibiting the highest genetic diversity. Fifteen genotypes were identified as top-ranking using the Multi Trait Genotype Ideotype Distance Index (MGIDI). Analysis of SNP markers revealed observed heterozygosity to be between 0.00 and 0.85 and expected heterozygosity between 0.09 and 0.50. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) identified four genetic clusters with cluster 1 having the largest number of genotypes. Results from both phenotypic and genotypic evaluations were complementary and facilitated the identification of superior sweetpotato genotypes with potential for breeding improvement.Keywords: Genetic diversity, Phenotypic traits, SNP markers, SweetpotatoSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sweetpotato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam), a dicotyledonous plant, is a perennial plant with alternating leaves and medium-sized violet flowers, belonging to the Convolvulaceae family1–4. It is an annual crop with ability to grow in many agroecological regions of the world with little input. Botanically, it has an elongated, slender, starchy storage roots with an outer skin and inner flesh colours which can range from cream yellow, orange, red, and purple to brown5. The crop has 2n = 6x = 90 chromosomes and adapts well to wide range of agroecological zones6,7. Vine length, yield of storage roots, and leaf structure are consistently marked as significant determinants of phenotypic variability and they are commonly used to cluster and differentiate landrace. Additional traits including root color, shape, and defects, aboveground biomass, and dry matter content have high variability and discriminating power amongst accessions8–10. Most studies attest that most genetic and phenotypic variation occurs within households or local groups instead of regions or settlements, in line with the outcrossing nature of sweet potato and frequent sharing among farmers. Moreover, clustering analysis has shown that landraces do not necessarily sort by geographic source, but by phenotype, confirming the contributions of farmer selection and trade in generating that diversity9,11,12.

At the global level, sweetpotato is one of the most significant and most versatile food crops, with a yearly production of approximately 133 million tons13. According to Udemezue14 China produces 67.1% of the world’s sweetpotato, while Nigeria with about four million tons, is the 3rd world largest producer with 3.7% of global production. This is due to many factors including low genetic potential of indigenous cultivars for yield, poor quality planting materials, biotic and abiotic stress factors, especially the sweetpotato virus disease and drought stress15,16. Mitigating these challenges requires an effective and efficient crop improvement plan. Both SSR and SNP markers have been extensively used as a tool to analyse genetic diversity with studies demonstrating high to moderate levels of polymorphic information content (PIC), which suggests that these are highly useful as a genetic diversity tool. These molecular and phenotypic classifications are not always congruent and it is therefore necessary to consider both methods so as to have a complete description of the germplasm characterization8,10.

Mourtala et al.17 highlighted agro-morphological diversity of a group of sweet potato clones in Niger and Nigeria and showed that there can be significant phenotypic variation, that can be used in selection and breeding objectives. Zhao et al.18 evaluated the genetic diversity of sweet potato germplasm in China with insertion-deletion (InDel) markers. In their study, they found that there were high variations in yield and morphological traits among multiple accessions, which provides further value to phenotypic traits in bringing genetic diversification that is needed in breeding improvement. In addition, Slonecki et al.19 indicated an adequate level of genetic diversity in the USDA sweet potato collection by indicating that specific subsets of breeder germplasm are essential to future improvement efforts due to the use of population structure. The high degree of genetic diversity in sweet potato exhibits by this diverse germplasm has great implications to the improvement of food security through strategic breeding. According to Naidoo et al.20 SSR markers were highly informative in evaluating genetic diversity and useful in the selection of parents in breeding programs of sweet potato genotypes. Similarly, Bararyenya et al.21 developed a GWAS that used SNP markers to identify candidate genes which demonstrated the success of SNP markers in identifying genetic variation concerning storage root phenotypes and bulking in sweet potato.

An effective sweetpotato breeding begins with the availability of genetic diversity for important traits in the germplasm population. As such, morphological and molecular characterization using morphological descriptors and molecular markers, respectively, is the first step towards the utilization of any crop germplasm22,23. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers are increasingly being used because they are abundant and can be easily genotyped at high throughput24. Mourtala et al.17 and Jiao et al.25 have successfully used SNPs as the tools for assessing sweetpotato genetic diversity and population structure. Advances in next generation sequencing (NGS) have greatly improved the detection and genotyping SNPs efficiently in breeding populations17. Compared with previous techniques, SNPs are abundant in the plant genome, making fast bulk genotyping possible because of their two-allelic structure. The objective of this research was to assess genetic and phenotypic variation among sweetpotato genotypes using both morphological and molecular approaches, with the aim of identifying superior genotypes for breeding programs and exploring how genetic diversity can be leveraged in selection strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and experimental design

This study involved 72 sweetpotato genotypes obtained from the National Root Crops Research Institute, Umudike, Nigeria (Table 1). The experiment was carried out from July to December 2025 in the research field of the National Institute for Horticultural Research (NIHORT) (3° 56’ N, 7° 33’ E; 168 m above sea level), Ibadan, Nigeria. Planting distances were maintained at 0.3 m between plants within a row, 0.5 m between plots, 1.0 m between blocks, and 1.5 m between replications. Each plot consisted of a single row, 1.5 m in length, containing five plants. The experimental design was a 6 × 19 alpha lattice with two replications. Weeds were manually controlled with the first weeding performed three weeks after planting (WAP) and as necessary afterwards. Inorganic fertilizer (N-P-K 15-15-15 + 10.2S) was applied at 6 WAP at a rate of 5 g/plant (167 kg/ha). To protect the leaves from defoliating pests, plants were sprayed with Promethrin® (containing profenofos 40% and cypermethrin 4% EC) at planting and at two weeks intervals at the rate of 30 ml active ingredients per 30 ml/15 L of water per hectare using knapsack sprayer. As one genotype with poor vigour could not survive to maturity, 71 genotypes are therefore reported in this study.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected at 90 and 138 days after planting. All morphological descriptor trait data collected were as described by the International Plant Genetic Resources Institute (IPGRI, 1991) Descriptors for sweetpotato.

DNA extraction

Leaf samples were collected from the field 8 WAP. Tools such as DNA extraction plates, gloves, 70% ethanol, puncher, cutting mat, sample list, and paraffin to prevent cross-contamination during sampling, and a cooler box containing ice were utilized during leaf sampling. After each collection, samples were placed in the collection plates and placed in the ice to prevent denaturation of DNA. Approximately six discs were collected from healthy leaves of each genotype. Leaf samples were quickly transported to the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) and stored in the Bioscience Centre using a LABCONCO FreeZone 18 Liter − 50 °C freezer-dryer. The leaves were sent to the Diversity Array Technology (DArT) in Canberra, Australia, for downstream analysis.

Data analyses.

Phenotypic data analysis

The data were performed via least squares means (LSMeans) using R software (version 4.4.0). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on LSMeans (mean + /- SE) from 71 sweetpotato genotypes. The FactorMineR package is based on Lê et al.26 methods for this analysis, and the missMDA package is used for handling missing data27. The number of discriminant factors used was based on the principle of Peres-Neto et al.28, who retained only those factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. Variables with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.5 for each discriminant factor were discriminated, and a correlation plot in R was generated for them. Thus, a Gower dissimilarity was used to quantify the dissimilarity among the genotypes via the cluster package in R. Variables of different types, ranging from leaf traits, flower features, and root dimensions to yield traits, were defined in this way. Before analysis, the quantitative data were log-transformed via the ratio function to address skewness and compatibility across variables. The genotypes were clustered via the Ward method. D2 and the vegan library via hierarchical clustering according to phenotypic similarity, and the diversity of clusters was assessed29. Multiple diversity indices were applied to make the conclusions concerning phenotypic variability more robust.

Analysis of genotypic data

In this study, 71 sweetpotato genotypes were genotyped via the diversity array technology (DArT) platform, and 11,722 SNP markers were filtered for call rate (CR > 89%), minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.05), and missing data rate (MDR > 20%). Data cleaning resulted in the retention of 9467 SNP markers, which was 19.2% less than the original data. For summary statistics, VFCtools and PLING 1.9 were utilized to estimate the expected and observed heterozygosity, minor allele frequency (MAF), and polymorphic information content (PIC). Population structure analysis was conducted following Jombart30 methods, which involved testing cluster numbers ranging from 2–10 to identify the optimal numbers and employing k-means analysis based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to determine the most suitable clustering solution. Genotypes were assigned to their cluster of origin using a cut-off ancestry probability (at 59%), and genotypes with lower probabilities were classified as admixed. Discriminant analysis of principal component (DAPC) was performed via the adegenet package in R to establish a robust framework for visualizing genetic relationships between the sweetpotato genotypes, where variance within genotypes is minimized and variance between genotypes is maximized31. The pairwise genetic dissimilarity distances between genotypes given SNP profiles were calculated via the Jaccard method and element of the Philentropy R package, which quantifies genetic differences between genotypes. A visual representation of the genetic relationships among the sweetpotato genotypes was created via Ward’s minimum variance hierarchical cluster dendrogram created via Analysis of Phylogenetic and Evolution (APE) package in R31. The factorMineR and FactoExtra R packages were used to analyse the results and visualize the PCA results on the basis of the genetic relationship between sweetpotato genotypes.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

According to the results presented in Table 3, the observed traits have been reduced to seven main PCAs, which had eigenvalues equal to or greater than one, and explained 75.68% variability within tested genotypes. The PC1 with eigenvalue of 5.338, contributed 22.24% to the total diversity, variation that was largely influenced by PD, VD, VID, CRW and RW. The second component (PC2) had with eigenvalue of 3.721, had 15.51% contribution to the total variability which was influenced by four traits (NONCR, NCRW, NVRY and TY). The third component (PC3) had an eigenvalue of 2.874 that contributed 11.98% to the total diversity. Principal components four to seven (PC4 to PC7) had more than one eigenvalue. The results showed vine diameter (0.426), petiole diameter (0.421) and root weight (0.405) positively contributed to PC1. In PC2, the traits that contributed positively and most were total root yield in tons per hectare (0.459) and non-commercial root yield (0.424) . Furthermore, PC3 diversity was highly and positively influenced by vine internode length (0.482), vine length (0.451), and petiole length 0.325). In PC4, the majority of observed genetic diversity was accounted for by traits such as number of leaf lobes (0.432) and the number of non-commercial roots (0.372) which contributed positively to this component, while the number of storage root yield (− 0.308) and commercial root yield (− 0.533) contributed strongly and negatively. Moreover, character that strongly and positively contributed to PC5 was days to 50% flowering (0.309), while mature leaf width (− 0.585) contributed negatively to the PC5. Additionally, in PC6, the number of days to 50% flowering (0.455) contributed positively to the total variability while flower width (− 0.462) and number of flowers (− 0.552) contributed negatively. Finally, storage root length (0.666) positively contributed to the PC7, whereas, mature leaf length (− 0.475) negatively contributed to the total variation observed in this component.

Correlation between phenotypic traits

There were significant positive relationships between key phenotypic traits of the sweetpotato genotypes (Fig. 1). The storage root weight and commercial root weight had significant correlation with petiole diameter (r > 0.9), vine diameter (r > 0.9) and vine internode diameter (r > 0.9). On the other hand, commercial root weight also had significant positive correlation with number of commercial roots (0.68), but number of commercial roots had negative correlation with dry matter content (–0.30). Total root yield had strong (0.88) correlation with non-commercial root yield, whereas traits such as number flowers had weak (0.31) to strong (0.85) correlations with other floral and yield traits.

Clustering patterns based on phenotypic traits

Key discriminating trait used in grouping clusters included vine length, number of commercial storage roots, storage root weight, root diameter, and leaf lobe number. Based on these traits, the 71 sweetpotato genotypes were grouped into four distinct clusters. The largest and most diverse group was Cluster I with 55 genotypes including 6 exotic, 8 checks and some UMUSPO4/OP series clustered into five sub-groups. Cluster II included 2 genotypes from 86/OP/175 and UMUSPO4/OP series. The second largest cluster (Cluster III) contained 13 genotypes and had moderate diversity. The one highly distinct genotype in the UMUSPO4/OP series was found in cluster IV (Fig. 3).

Genotypic characterization

Based on the results shown in Fig. 4, the expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.09 to 0.50 with average of 0.30. However, the observed heterozygosity (Ho) ranged from 0.00 to 0.85 with average of 0.35. In addition, the minor allele frequency (MAF) ranged from 0.049 to 0.50 with average of 0.25 while the polymorphic information content (PIC) ranged from 0.089 to 0.38 with average of 0.28.

Summary statistical based on 9467 SNP markers of 71 genotypes: (A) Distribution of expected heterozygosity (He), (B) distribution of observed heterozygosity (Ho), (C) Distribution of minor allele frequency, and (D) Distribution of polymorphic information content.

The results for the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) are presented in Fig. 5. The BIC decreases from 0 to approximately 5 clusters, which implies that the partitioning of data improves the fit of the model. The BIC stabilized when it reached approximately 4 clusters, and the changes were only observed to slightly increase as the number of clusters increased. Therefore, the optimum number of clusters captured by the analysis is equal to 4, as this number of clusters provided the best explanation of genetic diversity.

The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and numbers of clusters (K = 4) for sweetpotato genetic diversity based on 9467 high-quality SNPs. The choice of K = 4 was justified base on the elbow method, where the sharp drop in BIC slows down around K = 4.

To identify genetic diversity among the 71 sweetpotato genotypes based on 9467 polymorphic SNP markers, the Unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) was generated from a genetic identity matrix that classified the genotypes into four clusters (Fig. 6). However, these results aligned with the BIC result, which captured 4 optimal clusters (k = 4). The largest cluster 1 contained 29 genotypes, of which 4 were exotic, 3 were checks, 4 were 87/OP/175 series and 18 were from the UMUSPO4 series. The fourth cluster (C4) was the second largest, containing 27 genotypes. Among these genotypes, 4 were 87/OP/175 series, 5 were checks, 4 were exotic, and 18 were UMUSPO4 series, while 1 of the genotypes within this cluster was admixture. Cluster 2 was the third largest and was composed of 9 genotypes of which 1 was check and 8 were UMUSPO4 series. The last cluster, Cluster 3, comprised of 5 genotypes: 1 check, and 4 UMUSPO4 series.

Unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) displaying the genetic diversity of 71 sweetpotato genotypes based on 9467 DArTSeq SNP markers.

Genetic diversity among 71 sweetpotato genotypes based on 9467 SNP markers via PCA is presented in Fig. 7. The first two PCs explained 37.2% of the total variation, of which the first principal component (PC) explained 19.7%, while the second PC explained 17.5% of the total variation observed among the genotypes. These results aligned with the Unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) results in which four distinct clusters were obtained. The PC1 (19.7%) effectively separates clusters, with Cluster 3 being on the negative side, and Clusters 1 and 4 being on the positive side. The genotypes in Cluster 3 were genetically different from the genotypes in Clusters 1 and 4. In addition, PC2 (17.5%) separates Clusters 1 and 4 on the positive side from Cluster 3 which is on the negative side; however, some of the genotypes from Cluster 4 which contained 12 genotypes were mixed with Cluster 1 which comprised of 19 genotypes, which implies a genetic relationship between these two clusters. Cluster 2 comprised of 26 genotypes, which represents the largest and most diverse sets of genotypes, which are widely dispersed along Dimensions1 and 2 (Dims 1 and 2, respectively). This cluster generally contains genotypes from different populations, such as exotic, UMUSPO4 series, 87/OP/175 series and Check. Also, Cluster 3 contained 14 genotypes that were tightly grouped and distinct from the others, suggesting that these genotypes were more uniform genetically and may likely be from the same ancestry. Moreover, the genotypes in Cluster 2 were tightly grouped and distinctly separated from those in Dim1, suggesting strong genetic differentiation from the other clusters. However, the results showed overlapping between genotypes in Clusters 1 and 2, indicating a strong genetic relationship between genotypes within these clusters.

Discussion

Significant differences (P ≤ 0.01) in the number of non-commercial roots and number of flowers per plant among genotypes indicate significant genetic variability in these traits indicating possibilities of selection of genotypes with desirable characteristics. Veasey et al.11 also reported significant variation in the number of leaf lobes as well as in the flowering traits of sweetpotato. The findings of this study generated through PCA provided deeper insight into the distribution of genetic variability in sweetpotato genotypes and the level of contribution of the observed traits to the variability among the genotypes. This indicates that there is a strong structure of different observed variables in complicated traits in the breeding program, which can be explained well by reducing the variables using appropriate tools like the PCA. According to Lebot et al.32, genetic variability and effective selection of key sweetpotato traits for crop improvement can be achieved using PCA. The competitive growth traits, such as vine diameter and root weight that contribute to PC1 generally play a significant role in determining plat vigour and yield potential. Various characters contributed to different PCs and this suggests that a multi-trait model of key traits with significant influence on variability, and which is key to effective selection, is key for sweetpotato crop improvement17.

The correlation analysis of the phenotypic traits elucidated the complex interrelations among the various attributes that are critical for breeding and selection strategies. The weak positive correlations of number of leaf lobes with mature leaf width and mature leaf length indicates that these traits have limited influence on each other, providing insight into the morphological development of sweetpotato leaves32. The strong correlations between petiole diameter and storage root weight, vine diameter, and commercial root weight strengthen the understanding that improving vegetative structure can significantly enhance storage root traits, which is particularly beneficial for crop management strategies focused on yield maximization33,34. The selection of the top 15 genotypes using the multitrait genotype ideotype distance index (MGIDI) highlights the importance of integrating multiple yield-related traits for optimal selection under genetic improvement programs. The clustering of the 71 sweetpotato genotypes revealed insightful genetic diversity based on morphological traits as have been reported also by Dewi et al.35 and Mourtala et al.17 based on quantitative and qualitative traits among the genotypes. The clustering analysis has helped in grouping genotypes with close kinship while showing those with distant similarities. This could lead to the exploitation of heterosis in sweetpotato breeding as being tested at the International Potato Centre presently.

A heterozygosity value is a measure of the proportion of a given locus (or loci in a multi-locus system) that is expected to be heterozygote in an individual. Using 9467 informative SNP markers in this study, the expected heterozygosity (He) that ranged from 0.10 to 0.50, and the observed heterozygosity (Ho) that ranged significantly from 0.01 to 0.85 depict 85% chance that a genotype will be heterozygote for many of the important traits. As a vegetatively propagated crop that thrives on dominant gene effect, high level of heterozygosity is key to high productivity and vigour. In fact, homozygosity in sweetpotato occasioned by selfing often leads to severe inbreeding depression with severe loss of vigour. A study conducted by Rodriguez-Bonilla et al.12 showed a relatively high level of observed heterozygosity of 0.72, with values ranging from 0.15 to 0.95 across different loci on the basis of their study of genetic diversity in sweetpotato. In the present study, the obtained PIC values ranging from 0.089 to 0.38 indicates the degree of polymorphism of the SNPs genetic markers that were used in this research. Recently, similar result was reported among West African sweetpotato collections where the PIC value ranged from 0.25 to 0.50, using 7,591 polymorphic SNPs17. A PIC mean value of > 0.50 is highly informative, 0.25–0.50 is moderately informative, and < 0.25 is slightly informative36. However, the average PIC value in the current study was 0.28, which is lower than the 0.50 value indicative of a highly informative value. This indicates that the markers employed were slightly informative but they nevertheless managed to identify genetic variation among sweetpotato genotypes. Based on population structure analysis of the 71 genotypes which were assigned to four sub-populations (K = 4), and with sub-populations having a mixture of genotypes of other sub-populations, the study suggests a complex genetic structure among the sweetpotato genotypes analysed with shared ancestry among them. The pattern suggests that sweetpotato breeding populations in the study region are interconnected reflecting historical exchange of genes and planting material among farmers. The complexity, rather than limit, opens significant breeding opportunities. The extensive allelic foundation enhances the chances of finding genotypes integrating several merits of adaptation and guarantees a more robust and resilient yield under practicing different conditions. Sweetpotato populations with such complex genetic diversity in traits variation had also been reported by Xiao et al.37 while multiple sub-populations and admixtures among genotypes were also reported through polyploid analysis of sweetpotato genotypes for anthocyanin content by Haque et al.38.

Conclusion

A high level of phenotypic and genetic diversity was observed among the sweetpotato genotypes characterized, while key yield and yield-related traits were also identified. These are indications that an effective and efficient selection can be undertaken using these collections. The identification of 15 top performing genotypes with desirable agronomic characteristics can successfully lead to the release of a new variety with key multi-trait performance that satisfy varied end-uses. The identified clusters that open the door for heterotic breeding, the high observed heterozygosity that depicts that most of the genotypes are heterozygote at many loci for important traits measured, and the understanding of the structure of the sweetpotato population, will provide valuable basis for an effective and efficient sweetpotato breeding program. The findings can be used to develop high yielding, nutritious, and resilient sweetpotato varieties suitable to varied agroecological conditions and end-uses.
