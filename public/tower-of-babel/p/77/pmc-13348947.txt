Shared clinical decision-making for vaccination: A view through the lens of mid-adult HPV vaccination
Authors: Gregory D Zimet, Monica L Kasting, Erika L Thompson, Katharine J Head
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the summer of 2019, the U.S. Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) updated its recommendations for HPV vaccination. The new recommendation regarding adults ages 27–45y (i.e., mid-adults) indicated that a “shared clinical decision-making” (SCDM) approach, rather than a routine recommendation, should be used. The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR) publication that described this SCDM recommendation included specific conditional guidance and considerations for SCDM. However, some aspects of the guidance are vague, and others are inconsistent with SCDM, which could lead to confusion for adult health care providers and ultimately lead to under-vaccination of eligible individuals who would benefit from HPV vaccination. More recently, in the U.S. there have been policy proposals to shift additional vaccine recommendations from routine to SCDM. In this commentary, we briefly outline the ACIP recommendations, provide a clear overview of SCDM, and then offer potential implementation challenges for this guideline. For both unvaccinated adult patients and the providers who serve them, clear guidance on effective SCDM practices for HPV vaccination is an essential component for addressing the prevention of HPV-related disease. Using the context of mid-adult HPV vaccination, we also discuss the implications of SCDM recommendations for other vaccines.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccination, vaccination, shared clinical decision-making, adult, prevention

Introduction

In October 2018, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration expanded approval of the 9-valent human papillomavirus vaccine (9vHPV) to adults ages 27 through 45 y.1 Subsequently, at their June 2019 meeting, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) voted to approve a shared clinical decision-making policy recommendation (SCDM), rather than a routine recommendation, for HPV vaccination in the 27–45 age group. SCDM in this context implies that a patient and provider engage in a discussion about whether HPV vaccine is right for the patient. This updated policy was published in the August 16, 2019 issue of Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Reports (MMWR) and in JAMA in 2020.2,3 The Healthy People 2030 goal is for 80% of 13–15 y old males and females to complete the HPV vaccine series.4 Given that HPV vaccination has persistently lagged behind the Healthy People 2030 goal, many young people have aged out of the age range for routine recommendation (11–12 y old) or routine catch-up vaccination (13–26 y old) without having been vaccinated, indicating the potential health protective value of this expanded adult age range for further opportunities for HPV vaccination. As of 2019, only 15.6% of mid-adults ages 27–45 y had ever received the HPV vaccine. Although some of the unvaccinated 84.4% might not benefit from vaccination because of prior infection with all vaccine-covered HPV types, many may could still benefit from vaccination under the SCDM guideline.5

HPV vaccination of adolescents and preadolescents should continue to be the national priority for the prevention of HPV-related cancers; however, this does not exclude a continued critical examination of the SCDM guidelines for adults ages 27–45. In this paper, we will review and summarize the tenets of SCDM, consider how the guidelines have been implemented to date, and provide recommendations for future implementation of this guideline. In light of the issues with mid-adult HPV vaccination, we also discuss the potential implications of the SCDM guidelines for other vaccines. In addition to mid-adult HPV vaccination, SCDM has been recommended by the ACIP for Meningococcal B (MenB) vaccination for adolescents/young adults, hepatitis B vaccination for adults aged 60 y and older with diabetes mellitus, pneumococcal conjugate vaccination for some adults aged 65 y and older, and additional doses of COVID-19 vaccination for those who are immunocompromised.6 Furthermore, in January of 2026, the CDC, without review from the ACIP, indicated that SCDM should be used for the following additional pediatric vaccines: rotavirus, COVID-19, influenza, hepatitis A and B, and meningococcal ACWY.7 As of the writing of this paper, a federal judge has temporarily blocked these changes from being implemented.8 Through our review of SCDM we hope to provide useful clinical direction around SCDM as it applies to HPV vaccination for adults ages 27 through 45 y and to stimulate research and discussion about this important issue as it relates to other vaccines.

Discussion

Review of SCDM

Much has been written about SCDM, sometimes referred to simply as shared decision-making, and there are many working definitions. Typically, SCDM has been recommended when a patient faces two or more treatment options that have different cost-benefit profiles. For example, a person at average risk for colon cancer may be offered two initial screening options, a colonoscopy or a stool-based DNA colorectal screening test. A SCDM approach would involve a discussion around the advantages and disadvantages of each screening approach, with the patient deciding which one is best for them. A focused and systematic review of the SCDM literature identified deliberation and patient participation as two essential elements of SCDM.9 A well-cited clinical practice model concentrates on clinicians facilitating three forms of talking as key steps to ensure SCDM is occurring in clinical practice: choice talk (ensuring patients know that reasonable options are available), option talk (providing detailed information about options), and decision talk (supporting the work of considering preferences and deciding what is best).10 In a JAMA commentary outlining some of the difficulties in implementing SCDM with patients, it was noted that “physicians should never make a choice for the patient” and that SCDM “does not pertain to sharing choices, but rather involves sharing information”11 (p. 2516). Rooted in both ethics and law, this process aims to facilitate patient autonomy, with the term “shared” as an acknowledgment “that clinicians often play a critical role in educating patients about their treatment alternatives, and in helping patients align their choices with their values.”12 (p. W11). In sum, the concept and the practice of SCDM rely heavily on the idea that clinicians ensure their patients know that there are choices and that they talk with their patients about these choices. With vaccination, the choice is not among multiple equal intervention options but between vaccination and non-vaccination, with the vaccination option nearly always being the safer and more effective of the two choices.

SCDM for HPV vaccination

So, how do the ACIP guidelines align with SCDM? In the MMWR paper,2 readers are directed to view the Box on page 700 for guidance on the SCDM recommendation, entitled, “Considerations for shared clinical decision-making regarding human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination of adults ages 27 through 45.” The call-out box includes an introductory paragraph and a bulleted list of informational points and is aimed at health care providers who see adults in their clinical practices. In the introductory paragraph, the authors state, “HPV vaccination does not need to be discussed with most adults aged > 26 y” (p. 700). This sentence directly contradicts the central principle of SCDM, which would require a discussion with a patient about their clinical options and the associated benefits and risks. Although it is likely true that many adults > 26 will not derive significant individual benefit from HPV vaccination, this assertion should not preclude a discussion about HPV vaccination during patient encounters, particularly as it is not possible for patients and providers to determine the potential value of HPV vaccination for the patient without discussion. Given the limitations on time in a clinical encounter, HPV vaccination discussions can be situated within similar clinical discussions, such as other vaccinations or behavioral counseling for those at increased risk of sexually transmitted infections.13 According to national data, two-thirds of U.S. adults are aware of HPV vaccination generally, and awareness significantly varies by educational attainment and sex.14 Thus, unintentional disparities may occur if healthcare providers do not initiate conversations on HPV vaccination and, instead, the onus for being aware of eligibility under these guidelines is placed on patients.

The guidelines also indicate that “clinicians can consider discussing HPV vaccination with persons who are most likely to benefit.”2 There is a risk associated with health care providers using their own discretion to appropriately identify patients who may benefit from HPV vaccination. An online survey of physicians in 2021 revealed that while the physicians were largely aware of the SCDM guidelines (80%), objective knowledge (i.e., responding to factual questions) related to the guideline was much lower.15 Moreover, these physicians were uncertain about which discussion points to prioritize during conversations with mid-adult patients. A more recent survey in 2024 of physicians found that less than half were implementing the SCDM HPV vaccination guidelines into practice (47%).16 Barriers to implementation included a perceived lack of time and perceived inadequate insurance of patients, while one-quarter of participants were unsure which patients would benefit. Finally, a 2021 survey in Texas found that awareness of the SCDM recommendation for patients ages 27–45 y varied by provider type, and for those that were aware of it, only 45% anticipated no barriers in engaging in SCDM with their eligible patients.17 Thus, despite the approval of this ACIP guideline over six years ago, implementation of the policy is uneven and unclear. Similar issues have been identified for implementation of SCDM for MenB vaccination of adolescents.18

A recent analysis aimed to examine the cost-effectiveness of vaccinating adults ages 27–45 based on different behavioral or demographic characteristics that may align with the risk of acquiring new infections.19 Along with an earlier report,2 this paper found that mid-adult vaccination was substantially less cost-effective than vaccination at younger ages, which is largely why the SCDM recommendation was made, rather than a routine recommendation. The authors concluded that population-level cost-effectiveness for HPV vaccination of 27–45 y olds was higher for those adults with higher sexual activity (i.e., 10 or more lifetime sexual partners), adults who have recently separated from a partnership, and women who have been under-screened for cervical cancer.19 Similarly, another recent paper proposes a risk-based approach to HPV vaccination for mid-adult men for prevention of oropharyngeal cancer.20 However, the implication of a risk-tailored, or precision HPV vaccination approach for adults ages 27–45 opens a range of concerns for implementation. In particular, health care providers inconsistently ask about sexual history at routine health visits, and patients may face stigma and fear during sexual health discussions and avoid disclosure of risk-based information.21,22 Thus, a risk-tailored approach to this guideline may inadvertently miss people who may otherwise benefit from the vaccine and/or result in additional time for HPV vaccine decision-making. We propose a risk-agnostic approach that respects the patient’s need for informed decision-making with input from their health care provider through SCDM.

Implementing SCDM around HPV vaccination with mid-adults may be challenging, but clear guidance can help ensure that the necessary discussion meets those challenges in a time-efficient manner. Importantly, even when clear guidelines are issued, it can be difficult for HCPs to incorporate them into their practices. For example, a recently published study found that most SCDM discussions about lung cancer screening were poor, despite clear guidelines from multiple organizations, including the United States Preventive Services Task Force.23 Because the ACIP guidance for HPV vaccination of persons ages 27–45 y is vague and even somewhat contradictory at times, the resulting confusion is likely to inhibit, rather than promote, implementation of SCDM with this adult patient population. Rather than suggesting that HPV vaccination need not be discussed, clinicians should be empowered to engage in SCDM with their patients about this potentially life-saving vaccination. Patient decision tools and other resources can be useful in the implementation of this guideline to empower patients with information while also minimizing time spent in the clinical setting.24 One such tool has been developed to be patient-facing, web-based, and time-efficient (less than 5 minutes to complete). In a randomized trial, the patient decision tool was found to be efficacious at improving decision-making outcomes (i.e., reducing decisional conflict, improving decisional self-efficacy) and acceptable to adult patients and clinicians.24,25 Beyond patient-facing strategies, other clinical strategies to improve implementation of this guideline may include clinical decision support tools for health care providers and health care provider training on SCDM.

SCDM for vaccines in general

Despite some states and professional organizations taking matters into their own hands in terms of vaccine guidance, the ACIP still sets the national agenda for vaccine recommendations, which has potential implications for what payers will cover in the United States.26 The ACIP currently has five SCDM policy recommendations on their list,6 but, as noted previously, the CDC, without ACIP discussion, recently recommended SCDM for additional vaccines that have traditionally been routinely recommended.7 There is an argument to be made that SCDM is not the appropriate approach for most vaccinations, as it rarely constitutes a choice between two comparable options (i.e., vaccination is nearly always both more effective at infection prevention and much safer than non-vaccination). Nonetheless, the reality is that SCDM recommendations for certain vaccines in the U.S. will continue to be public health policy for the foreseeable future. In considering SCDM, it is important to acknowledge that implementation of this approach is more time-consuming and more confusing to both providers and patients and can lead to under immunization. Providers searching for guidance on how to implement SCDM with their patients around these vaccines will find on the ACIP website that “it’s up to the provider” about whether to discuss SCDM vaccine recommendations with patients (similar to the MMWR report on HPV vaccination).6 Shared clinical decision making, however, can never be simply “up to the provider” and must always involve genuine discussion and information sharing between the provider and patient. Despite this issue, the ACIP web page provides some accurate, helpful information on SCDM, for instance, stating, “ … shared clinical decision-making vaccinations are not recommended for everyone in a particular age group or everyone in an identifiable risk group. Rather, shared clinical decision-making recommendations are individually based and informed by a decision process between the health care provider and the patient or parent/guardian.”

Conclusion

This commentary and the suggestions that we have offered here, in response to the ACIP SCDM HPV vaccination guideline in the expanded age range, is a starting point for both practice and research into SCDM. Looking ahead, while the primary focus for HPV vaccination should still be young adolescents, we must also recognize the potentially important health benefit of vaccinating mid-adults and the minimal risk associated with vaccination. The reality is that the extended age range has been approved by the FDA and has received a SCDM recommendation from the ACIP. It is important, therefore, that HPV vaccination for the 27–45 age range be clinically implemented in ways that are consistent with the FDA approval and the ACIP recommendation so that further progress can be made in reducing HPV-related diseases in our society.

More broadly, this focus on engaging in true SCDM is especially important at a time when trust in vaccinations is waning and rates are dropping; we must work to engage all eligible patients so that they can make the best choice for protecting themselves from vaccine-preventable diseases.27 As noted previously, decision support tools have been developed to make the SCDM process easier to implement in busy primary care practices for both mid-adult HPV vaccination23,24 and MenB vaccination for adolescents.28 Similar tools can be developed for other SCDM vaccines as well.

Biography

Gregory D. Zimet is a Clinical Psychologist and Professor Emeritus of Pediatrics and Psychiatry at Indiana University School of Medicine. Since he arrived at IU School of Medicine in 1993, he has guided an extensive and multi-faceted behavioral and social science research program focused on attitudes about and determinants of vaccination, with a particular focus on human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination. Dr Zimet’s research also has involved randomized clinical trials designed to evaluate the effects of behavioral interventions on hepatitis B virus (HBV) and HPV vaccine uptake and on acceptance of HIV testing. On retiring from Indiana University, Dr Zimet has continued collaborations on research as an independent consultant through his LLC, Zimet Research Consulting.
