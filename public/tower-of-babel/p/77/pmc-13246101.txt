Construction and validation of a machine learning model to predict the sarcopenic obesity population
Authors: Mingrong Zhang, Dandan Dong, Chenming Liu, Yuxing Liu, Zhenfu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenic obesity (SO) imposes a heavy medical burden on both individuals and society, highlighting the importance of early identification and timely intervention. This study aims to construct a clinical prediction model based on machine learning (ML) methods to assist in the early screening of SO. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Survey from 2011 to 2018 were used for model development. The included data were randomly divided into a training set and an internal test set with a ratio of 7:3. Eight ML methods – logistic regression, decision tree, neural network, K-nearest neighbor, Naive Bayes, support vector machine, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and light gradient boosting machine – were used to develop the model. The area under the receiver operating characteristic, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis were employed to evaluate the predictive performance of the constructed model. The SHapley Additive exPlanations method was used to illustrate the importance of variables in the model. After feature screening, 7 indicators were used as ML features to construct the model, including hepatic steatosis index, bone mineral content, skeletal muscle mass to visceral area ratio, history of heart failure, atherogenic index of plasma, neutrophil-to-lymphocytes ratio, and serum phosphorus level. The area under the receiver operating characteristic values of logistic regression, decision tree, neural network, K-nearest neighbor, Naive Bayes, support vector machine, XGBoost, and light gradient boosting machine were 0.83 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.81–0.85), 0.82 (95% CI = 0.80–0.85), 0.86 (95% CI = 0.84–0.88), 0.86 (95% CI = 0.85–0.88), 0.83 (95% CI = 0.81–0.85), 0.85 (95% CI = 0.83–0.87), 0.88 (95% CI = 0.86–0.90), and 0.87 (95% CI = 0.85–0.89), respectively, in the training set, and 0.86 (95% CI = 0.83–0.89), 0.79 (95% CI = 0.76–0.83), 0.83 (95% CI = 0.80–0.87), 0.82 (95% CI = 0.79–0.86), 0.83 (95% CI = 0.80–0.86), 0.85 (95% CI = 0.82–0.88), 0.84 (95% CI = 0.81–0.88), and 0.85 (95% CI = 0.82–0.88), respectively, in the internal test set. Among these models, XGBoost showed the best prediction performance in the training set. The calibration and decision curve analysis curves showed good predictive performance. The XGBoost-based model in this study performed well and combined 7 clinical predictors to help diagnose SO in adults.

1. Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive, age-associated disorder marked by the loss of skeletal muscle function and/or mass. It predisposes individuals to a spectrum of unfavorable clinical outcomes, including an increasing rate of fractures, disabilities, and even deaths.[1,2] The age-related decrease in total energy expenditure, combined with a sedentary lifestyle, promotes both muscle exhaustion and the accumulation of adipose tissue. In addition, obesity itself can accelerate the deterioration of muscle, and when obesity and sarcopenia occur concurrently, the health consequences are often more severe than those of either condition alone, partly attributable to the heightened release of inflammatory mediators, the occurrence of insulin resistance (IR), and metabolic disorders.[3] Sarcopenic obesity (SO), although there is no standardized definition, generally manifests as the combination of declined skeletal muscle mass, impaired function, and a high level of adiposity.[4,5] Studies have confirmed that individuals with SO often exhibit a higher incidence and mortality of respiratory, cardiovascular, and digestive system diseases.[6–8] Therefore, the urgent need to develop a novel and easily accessible prediction model for the early screening of SO is of great importance for the whole society. However, due to the relatively low incidence of SO in the general population, no widely applicable predictive model has yet been established for early screening.

Machine learning (ML), a key outcome of advances in artificial intelligence, uses computer algorithms to mine and analyze data.[9] It is widely applied in sectors like healthcare, manufacturing, and education to support data-driven decision-making. In clinical practice, ML’s strong predictive capabilities further aid in making evidence-based decisions.[10] This study aims to develop and compare multiple ML algorithms for predicting the risk of SO in a large-scale United States population and provide certain value for the early screening of SO at the same time.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted using data from 4 consecutive survey cycles (2011–2018) of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES is a nationally representative health and nutrition survey administered by the National Center for Health Statistics, designed to assess the health status of the noninstitutionalized US population. All NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent before inclusion.[11] All relevant data analyses were available on the official website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

The diagnosis of SO was based on the concurrent presence of sarcopenia and obesity. Sarcopenia was evaluated according to the diagnostic criteria proposed by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health[12]: appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. ASM was defined as the sum of the lean mass of all limbs (arms and legs). The appendicular skeletal muscle index (ASMI) was calculated by dividing ASM by the body mass index (BMI), yielding ASMI in kg/m2. Sarcopenia was defined as an ASMI < 0.789 for men and an ASMI < 0.512 for women. According to the consensus reached by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism and the European Association for the Study of Obesity on the assessment of SO, the definition of obesity in SO was primarily based on BMI and waist circumference (WC).[13] Specifically, obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 or a WC > 102 cm in men and >88 cm in women. Participants who met the criteria for both sarcopenia and obesity were considered to have SO.

Due to the low prevalence of SO in the overall survey population, resulting in a significant disparity in the number of SO and non-SO individuals, and to enable our developed model to reflect the ability to predict the SO population and exclude the non-SO population objectively, we applied a 1:1 optimal propensity score matching (PSM) method. Matching was performed based on age, sex, race, poverty level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, aiming to make our model independent of demographic characteristics and reduce the influence of potential confounding factors. After matching, a total of 1822 participants aged 20 years or older with complete body measurement data – including dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, BMI, and WC – were included in the final analysis. They were equally divided into SO and non-SO groups. A detailed flowchart of selection is shown in Figure 1.

2.2. Data collection

We collected comprehensive data on patients’ demographic characteristics, medical history, body composition measurements, laboratory examinations, and derived indicators to fully reflect potential metabolic and functional changes in the body during the progression of SO. Demographic variables included age, sex, race, poverty level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Medical history covered hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (including heart failure and coronary heart disease), cerebrovascular disease (stroke), and cancer. Body composition measurements comprised BMI, WC, ASM, visceral fat area, and body mineral content (BMC). Laboratory examinations included albumin, globulin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, γ-glutamyltranspeptidase,serum triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, and iron, phosphorus, calcium, and uric acid levels. Derived indicators included skeletal muscle mass to visceral fat area ratio (SVR), visceral area index, hepatic steatosis index (HSI), fibrosis-4 score (FIB-4), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), triglyceride-glucose index, IR, and atherogenic index of plasma (AIP). Missing data were imputed using the missRanger package from the random forest algorithm.[14]

2.3. Statistical analysis

Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the 2-independent samples t test; otherwise, they were described as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (4.4.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and a two-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

2.4. Model development, evaluation, and validation

The included data were randomly divided into a training set and an internal test set with a 7:3 ratio. This study applied univariate logistic regression to screen for variables with statistically significant differences in the training set, which were then incorporated into a multivariate regression model. Those variables with statistically significant differences screened from the multivariate regression were included in the construction of ML models. Simultaneously, the training set underwent 10-fold cross-validation to thoroughly evaluate model performance. Parameters were optimized and adjusted based on regularization, tree depth, learning rate, etc, to prevent the risks of underfitting and overfitting. Eight ML models were constructed to predict the risk of SO occurrence using logistic regression, K-nearest neighbor, Naive Bayes, decision tree, support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and neural network.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was applied to evaluate the predictive performance of these models, with a higher AUC indicating better predictive ability. Additional metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, were also reported. Furthermore, calibration curves and decision curve analysis were utilized to assess the consistency of predictive capability and clinical net benefit, respectively. All models were subsequently evaluated on the internal test set to examine their generalizability and robustness.

2.5. Model interpretation based on SHAP

Finally, to enhance the interpretability of the optimal ML model, we utilized SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). This method quantified the contribution of each feature to individual predictions, thereby improving the transparency of the model’s decision-making process.

3.2. Variable screening and model construction

We subsequently performed univariate and multivariate analyses of all included clinical characteristics, with detailed results provided in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content. Multivariate analysis showed that the history of heart failure, BMC, SVR, NLR, HSI, AIP, and serum phosphorus level were independent predictors of SO. Therefore, we incorporated these 7 factors into the constructed ML model subsequently.

3.3. Model performance evaluation and internal validation

The performance of each model was evaluated through resampling and 10-fold cross-validation. In the training set, the ROC curves (Fig. 2A) demonstrated that all models exhibited relatively strong predictive ability, with XGBoost and LightGBM showing the highest AUC values of 0.879 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.861–0.897) and 0.872 (95% CI = 0.853–0.891), respectively. Calibration curves in the training set showed good goodness of fit (Fig. S1A, Supplemental Digital Content). The decision curve analysis revealed that each model in the training set was able to achieve higher net returns than the “all-intervention” or “no-intervention” strategies across a wide range of prediction thresholds (Fig. 3A).

For the internal test set, the performance of all the constructed models remained consistent with that of the training set, indicating no risk of overfitting. The top 2 models for prediction performance were logistic regression (AUC = 0.862, 95% CI = 0.832–0.892) and SVM (AUC = 0.853, 95% CI = 0.822–0.884; Fig. 2B). Moreover, both calibration and decision curves indicated satisfactory goodness of fit and clinical utility in the test set (Figs. 3B and S1B, Supplemental Digital Content).

As summarized in Table 2, the models achieved high sensitivity (0.87) and specificity (0.74) in the training set, demonstrating a robust ability to accurately identify SO-positive individuals while effectively excluding those without the condition.

3.4. SHAP interpretation of the model

Figure 4A showed the extent to which the variables in the SHAP plot in the XGBoost model contributed to the predicted situation, in which the top 3 predictors with the highest contribution were HSI, BMC, and SVR. The beeswarm plot illustrated how SHAP values of features were related to the risk of SO occurrence (Fig. 4B), where each person was represented by a single point of each feature located according to its SHAP value. The color of the points reflected the magnitude of the eigenvalues, with yellow indicating higher values and purple indicating lower values. For example, for HSI, high values were mainly concentrated in the region of SHAP > 0, indicating that higher HSI values were associated with a higher risk of SO. 4. Discussion

As the adverse consequences and outcomes brought by SO are a major clinical problem, there is an urgent need to develop a simple, noninvasive, and easily promotable prediction model to accurately forecast the occurrence of SO.[15] Based on 7 indicators, including HSI, BMC, SVR, and others, we successfully constructed the first ML model to predict the incidence risk of SO independently of demographic information such as age, gender, and race. Our models showed excellent predictive performance (AUC > 0.8 for all models), goodness of fit, and clinical decision-making ability in both the training and test sets. This study may offer valuable insights for the accurate prediction of SO and the optimization of prevention strategies.

Our study confirmed that HSI, as an effective indicator for assessing the development of liver steatosis, can serve as a strong predictor of SO. Our findings were consistent with recent discoveries in this field. A multicenter study[16] showed the predictive role of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in the development of sarcopenia. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, they found the risk of low skeletal muscle mass and function increased with increasing HSI (Q4 vs Q1, OR [95% CI] = 3.46 [2.23–5.35]; Q4 vs Q1, OR [95% CI] = 5.81 [3.67–9.21]). Similarly, a 5-year follow-up study involving individuals aged 20 and above showed that those with NAFLD experienced a more significant reduction in appendicular skeletal muscle over 5 years compared to those without NAFLD (δ = −39.9 g, 95% CI = −53.1 to −26.8, P < .001).[17] However, the mechanisms underlying the association between hepatic steatosis and SO are not yet fully understood. Among the principal mechanisms proposed are IR, inflammatory cascades, and vitamin D deficiency, with IR considered a central driver.[18,19] Notably, placental protein A – an endogenous inhibitor of the insulin receptor tyrosine kinase in skeletal muscle – has been shown to be markedly overexpressed in NAFLD.[20] This finding implies that liver steatosis can aggravate the development of IR, consequently contributing to the deterioration of muscle. In parallel, excess adipose tissue drives ectopic lipid infiltration and immune cell recruitment, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor) and adipokines (e.g., leptin), as well as the generation of oxidative stress. These interrelated events act in concert to further amplify IR and systemic inflammation. The resulting IR and systemic inflammation further promote fat accumulation, creating a vicious cycle.[21] Given this detrimental cascade, early monitoring of HSI in the population may play a key role in preventing the onset of SO.

BMC refers to the total amount of minerals in a specific bone site, which is helpful to assess the body’s nutritional status and monitor the occurrence of certain diseases.[22,23] Our study confirmed that BMC could predict the occurrence of SO to some extent. Furthermore, subsequent SHAP analysis revealed that BMC held a relatively significant weight in predicting SO. Currently, there may be a bidirectional association between BMC and the occurrence of SO. Low BMC may lead to bone pain or limited mobility, thereby reducing physical activity.[24] Long-term lack of exercise may indirectly affect muscle mass and fat metabolism, increasing the risk of SO.[25,26] Conversely, obesity, especially SO, may lead to the infiltration of adipose tissue, further amplifying inflammation and oxidative stress. At the same time, excess fat can absorb more free vitamin D into adipose tissue, reducing the absorption of calcium from the intestine, increasing bone resorption, and subsequently lowering bone density.[27]

In addition, we found that the predictive role of certain cardiovascular diseases (such as heart failure/coronary heart disease) in SO could not be overlooked. This may be related to vascular lesions that affect muscle blood supply and cause muscle atrophy.[28] A 6-year follow-up study of 2405 elderly Americans aged 70 to 79 showed that baseline pulse wave velocity (reflecting arterial stiffness) was associated with skeletal muscle decline in both white men and women.[29] Meanwhile, reduced muscle mass leads to a decrease in basal metabolic rate, facilitating fat accumulation, triggering IR and inflammatory responses, and promoting the development of cardiovascular diseases.[30,31] Thus, the interaction between cardiovascular diseases and SO forms a “metabolic-vascular” vicious cycle that requires our close attention.

Interestingly, our multivariate regression analysis confirmed that higher blood phosphorus levels acted as a protective factor against SO (OR = 0.44, 95% CI = 0.20–0.99), although its overall contribution to the prediction model was relatively small. This finding was not commonly reported in previous studies. Blood phosphorus is a key element in human energy metabolism, participating in physiological processes such as ATP generation and cellular signal transduction.[32,33] Low serum phosphorus levels may lead to disorders of energy metabolism, impair the function of normal muscle cells, increase the risk of fatigue and reduced mobility, and potentially promote muscle atrophy and fat accumulation.[34] On the other hand, decreased hepatic ATP levels due to low phosphorus status send low-energy signals to the central nervous system, which may stimulate increased appetite, leading to excessive food intake and subsequently raising the risk of SO. Future prospective studies should further explore the role of serum phosphorus levels in SO.

Given the low prevalence of SO in the general population (only 7.4% in our cohort), we used PSM to balance the difference between baseline characteristics, thereby fully restoring the true predictive performance of our established models. This approach not only demonstrated a strong ability to diagnose SO-positive individuals (with a sensitivity as high as 0.87 for the SVM model) but also effectively excluded SO-negative individuals (with a specificity of 0.74 for the LightGBM model). Moreover, since these predictive factors are easily accessible in real-world settings, they can be widely implemented in healthcare institutions at all levels, including community health service centers. Future prospective studies should focus on exploring the applicability of these indicators in other ethnic populations.

However, we have to admit that our research has the following limitations: first, although we used PSM to minimize confounding factors affecting SO, selection bias remained somewhat unavoidable, and the predictive power of our model might be artificially improved. Second, due to limited data availability, we did not evaluate indicators reflecting skeletal muscle function, such as handgrip strength or gait tests, which are important for comprehensively assessing the status of muscle. Third, the predictive models we developed were based on US ethnic populations, and their predictive ability for other ethnic groups worldwide remains unclear. In the future, it is necessary to develop a model that fully reflects the mass and function of skeletal muscle and is globally scalable to accurately predict the occurrence of SO.

5. Conclusions

We developed a ML model to predict the risk of SO based on 7 predictors (including HSI, SVR, BMC, NLR, history of heart failure, AIP, and serum phosphorus level) for early screening of the susceptible population for SO in the United States.
