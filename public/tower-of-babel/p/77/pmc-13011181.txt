Effects of exercise on behavior and hippocampal neuroinflammation in rat model of autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Wanting Lan, Jiugen Zhong, Yinhua Li, Yingying Shen, Jiaheng Gong, Zhi Zou, Xiaohui Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRunning exercise has been shown to relieve symptoms of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), but the mechanisms underlying the positive effects of exercise are unclear. Neuroinflammation, involving dysregulated microglial activation and cytokine release, is implicated in ASD pathophysiology. The hippocampus is a key region for social and cognitive functions impaired in ASD and is susceptible to neuroinflammatory changes. However, the effects of running exercise on hippocampal neuroinflammation in ASD have not been studied.MethodsAn ASD rat model was induced by prenatal valproic acid (VPA) exposure, followed by a 6-week voluntary running wheel intervention starting from postnatal day 28. The behavioral performance before and after the intervention was evaluated using the three-chamber social interaction test, the open field test, and the Morris water maze test to assess social, exploratory, and cognitive functions. After the intervention, hippocampal tissues were collected, and Iba1+ microglial activation subregions were analyzed by immunofluorescence, and cytokine levels were quantified based on luminex.ResultsThe results showed that 6-week of voluntary running exercise could improve the social ability and social novelty preference of ASD rats, increase their exploration behavior and spontaneous activities, and improve the ability of learning and memory. In addition, 6-week of voluntary running exercise attenuated the VPA-induced microglial activation in the CA1 and CA3 region of the hippocampus. Furthermore, exercise could reduce the level of IL- 2, IL-4 and increase the level of IL-7 and IL-10.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the antidepressant effects of exercise may be mediated by reducing the number of microglia and inhibiting microglial activation and neuroinflammation in the hippocampus.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a pervasive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in social communication and interaction, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (World Health Organization; Bangerter et al., 2017). In recent years, the prevalence of ASD has been steadily increasing. According to the latest report from the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network (ADDM Network), the prevalence of ASD in Western countries is as high as 1 in 44 individuals (Christensen et al., 2018). In 2019, the “Report on the Development of Autism Education and Rehabilitation Industry in China III” (Report on the Development Status) indicated that there are over 10 million individuals with ASD in China, including approximately 2 million children, with a prevalence rate of about 1%. Moreover, it is growing at a rate of nearly 200,000 new cases annually.

The etiology of ASD remains unclear but likely involves genetic, environmental, and immune factors (Toscano et al., 2021). Some studies suggest that neuroinflammation may be a significant contributing factor to ASD. Neuroinflammation is characterized by the sustained activation and proliferation of glial cells (including microglia and astrocytes) in the brain following injuries, infections, or diseases. Changes in glial cell density can be associated with phenotypic and morphological alterations, potentially leading to peripheral and central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, affecting various bodily functions (Bennabi et al., 2019; Liu Cui et al.; Lee et al., 2017). Glial cells can release multiple cytokines and chemokines, participating in immune responses and regulating neuronal function. Therefore, abnormal activation of glial cells may lead to immune dysfunction, subsequently affecting neural signaling and cognitive function. Postmortem examinations have revealed abnormal glial cell activation, elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in the brains of individuals with ASD, and imbalances in cytokines in cerebrospinal fluid (Saetre et al., 2007; Vargas et al., 2005). These pathological changes likely play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of ASD. The hippocampus plays a crucial role in mediating social communication deficits, one of the core symptoms of ASD (Banker et al., 2021). In individuals with ASD, hippocampal abnormalities—including altered volume, structure, and function—have been widely reported and are implicated in social interaction, memory, and spatial reasoning impairments (Hitti et al., 2014; Okuyama et al., 2016; Barnea-Goraly et al., 2014). Given the importance of the hippocampus in ASD, investigating alterations in hippocampal function may provide valuable insights into the etiology of ASD.

In the absence of a clear understanding of the etiology of ASD and a lack of definitive clinical treatment methods, existing research primarily explores intervention strategies that alleviate symptoms and improve the quality of life for affected individuals. These interventions include behavioral therapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, comprehensive behavioral programs for children, and social skills training (Findings and Conclusions, 2015). With the growing recognition of the idea that “exercise is medicine”, an increasing body of research suggests that exercise therapy is an effective approach to improving ASD symptoms (Tan et al., 2016; Bremer et al., 2016). Regular physical activity has been shown to enhance cognition, social interactions, behavior, and motor skills in individuals with ASD (Pan, 2010, 2011; Lang et al., 2010; Sowa et al., 2012; Ferreira et al., 2019). While exercise demonstrates clinical benefits, its underlying biological mechanisms in ASD remain unclear. In other neurological disorders, exercise ameliorates symptoms by mitigating neuroinflammation. For instance, reducing hippocampal glial activation and neuroinflammatory markers in depression models (Xiao et al., 2021). We hypothesize that exercise may similarly improve ASD symptoms through anti-neuroinflammatory effects, particularly via modulation of glial cells and cytokines (Toscano et al., 2021).

To test this hypothesis, we employed a well-established valproic acid (VPA)-induced ASD rat model. Subjects underwent a 6-week period of voluntary exercise protocol, with behavioral assessments evaluating social interaction, exploratory behavior, and cognitive function. We used immunofluorescence techniques to assess the expression levels of activated glial cells in various regions of the rat hippocampus. Luminex liquid suspension chip technology was employed to measure cytokine levels in the hippocampus of the rats. This study investigated whether voluntary wheel running exercise ameliorates ASD-like behaviors in VPA-induced rats by modulating hippocampal neuroinflammation, with specific focus on glial cell activation and cytokine alterations. Our findings provide novel insights into the neurobiological mechanisms through which exercise may improve ASD symptoms.

Materials and methods

Animals

Pregnant Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University, China. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Research Committee of Guangzhou Sport University and conducted inaccordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23).

Timed-pregnant Sprague-Dawley rats (with the presence of a vaginal plug designated as gestational day 0.5) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University, China. The supplier confirmed that dams were generated by pairing nulliparous females with experienced males overnight. Male autism model rats were generated by intraperitoneal injection of valproic acid (VPA, 400 mg/kg) to pregnant dams on gestational day 12.5. Control offspring were obtained from saline-injected dams (n = 14), while ASD model rats (n = 24) were selected based on behavioral assessments (three-chamber social, open field, and water maze tests) at postnatal day 23. From these, subsets were randomly allocated for hippocampal analysis (ASD, n = 8; CON, n = 7) using Luminex cytokine profiling and Iba1 immunofluorescence. The remaining ASD rats were divided into exercise (E + ASD, n = 8) and non-exercise (NE + ASD, n = 8) groups, with the E + ASD group undergoing 6-week voluntary wheel running (minimum 2 km/day) beginning at postnatal day 28, followed by post-intervention behavioral and hippocampal analyses compared to baseline and control groups (n = 7).

Voluntary wheel running protocol

Voluntary wheel running exercise: The intervention began on the day of weaning and lasted for 6 weeks. During this time, the rats were single-housed in transparent cages equipped with a running wheel and were free to engage in voluntary exercise. Rats were single-housed during the voluntary running wheel intervention to accurately monitor individual running distances and avoid social dominance effects that could influence exercise behavior. Although social isolation may induce stress in rodents, the voluntary nature of the exercise, the short duration of isolation (6 weeks), and the transparent cage design (allowing visual contact between adjacent animals) were designed to minimize confounding effects on anxiety-like and social behaviors. Furthermore, all groups (including CON and NE + ASD) were housed under identical conditions (transparent cages with consistent environmental enrichment), ensuring that any isolation-related effects were consistent across experimental groups. Running distance was calculated by multiplying the total number of wheel revolutions by the circumference of the 35-cm wheel. Daily running distance was then derived over each 24-h cycle, providing a high-precision, high-resolution measure of voluntary activity while minimizing disturbance to the animals. The resulting running distance data were further used as a quantitative indicator of exercise intensity and were incorporated as a covariate in the behavioral statistical analyses. From the initial cohort of 10 rats assigned to the exercise group, 2 rats were excluded from the final analysis due to an average daily running distance of less than 2 km, which was predetermined as the threshold for an effective exercise intervention. Thus, 8 rats were included in the E + ASD group for analysis.

Behavior tests

The behavioral tests employed in this study were all classic ASD model assessment methods (Wang et al., 2021; Schneider et al., 2005). All behavioral experiments were conducted in a quiet, sound-isolated, adequately lit, and temperature- and humidity-controlled behavioral laboratory to ensure consistent conditions.

Morris water maze (MWM) test: The MWM test was performed to assess spatial learning and memory ability using a cylindrical water pool (a diameter of 120 cm and height of 50 cm) filled with water (a water depth of 30 cm and water temperature about 24 °C) with a video capture system (XinRuan Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The pool was divided into four quadrants, with a hidden platform (1 cm below water surface) placed in one quadrant. During training trails, each rat was first placed on the platform for 30s before being released into a random quadrant to located the hidden platform within 120s. Unsuccessful rats were guided to the platform and allowed to remain for 30s. On test day (day 5), the platform was removed, and all rats were released from the same quadrant. Their performance was assessed over 120 s by measuring: (1) path length in the target quadrant, (2) time spent in the target quadrant, (3) platform crossings, and (4) latency to reach the target location.

Open field test: Each rat was individually put at the center of a box (50 cm × 50cm × 50 cm). The rat was allowed to explore the box for 5 min, and the distance traveled, distance traveled in the center, static time in the corner, and the number of visits to the center were recorded. A solution of 75% alcohol and water was used to clean the box after each test.

Three-chamber social test: The test was performed in a white three-chamber box (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm). All the test rats were placed in the test room for a day to adapt to the surrounding environment before the experiment began. The chamber box was wiped with alcohol to disinfect and eliminate odors before each trial. First, each test rat was placed in the middle chamber of the three-chamber box for 5 min, with one empty cup located in each of the two side chambers. The rats could access both side chambers freely to explore. Then, a stranger rat (S1) was placed in the cup of the left chamber, and a 10-min video recording was taken to track the rat's activities and trace its trajectory. Finally, another stranger rat (S2) was placed in the cup of the right chamber, and the video recording was continued for another 10 min to track the rat's activities and trace its trajectory. The durations the rat spent in the S1 chamber, object chamber (Ob, the chamber with the empty cup), and the S2 chamber were recorded.

Tissue processing for cytokine analysis

After the completion of behavioral tests, the rats were subjected to a 12-h fast. Anesthetization was achieved by intraperitoneal injection of a 1% pentobarbital sodium solution at a dosage of 1 ml/100 g of body weight. Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, and hippocampal tissue was rapidly dissected. Tissue homogenization was performed at 60 Hz for 120 s, followed by ultrasonic disruption in cycles of 2 s sonication and 5 s on ice for a total of 1 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay to standardize sample loading. Cytokine levels were quantified using the Bio-Plex Pro Rat Cytokine Group 1 Panel 23-Plex kit (#12005641) on a Bio-Plex 200 system according to the manufacturer's protocol. The assay included 23 cytokines: G-CSF, GM-CSF, GRO/KC, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IL-17, IL-18, M-CSF, MPC1, MIP-1a, MIP-3a, RANTES, VEGF, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. Standard samples were serially diluted to create an 8-point calibration curve. Sequential incubations included 1-h sample incubation, 30-min detection antibody incubation, and 10-min streptavidin-PE incubation. Data were acquired and processed using Bio-Plex Manager software.

Immunofluorescence

Four rats per group were selected for immunofluorescence. Animals were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (4 ml/kg) and transcardially perfused with heparinized saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions, and sectioned at 40 μm using a cryostat. Every sixth hippocampal section was systematically sampled. Sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% goat serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Iba1 primary antibody (1:2000). After washing, sections were incubated with DyLight™ 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000) for 2 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica Thunder Imager 3D microscope, and Iba1 fluorescence intensity in hippocampal subregions was quantified with ImageJ software.

Statistics

Data entry, processing, analysis, and graphing were performed using Excel, IBM SPSS Statistics 24, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and Cytoscape software.

For data that conformed to a normal distribution and had homogeneity of variance, independent samples t-tests were used to compare the data between the pre-intervention ASD group and the CON group. For the post-intervention data from the E + ASD group, NE + ASD group, and CON group, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by post-hoc LSD multiple testing. If the data did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, data transformation was carried out to make it conform to a normal distribution before conducting t-tests. If data transformation did not yield normal distribution or equal variance, non-parametric tests were used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between post-intervention behavioral and neuroinflammatory markers, and the results of the correlation analysis were further subjected to network analysis.

Running exercise reversed the increases in the protein levels of cytokines in the hippocampus of the ASD rats

The results of Luminex liquid-phase suspension chip detection (Fig. 4, Fig. 5) show that, before intervention, the protein levels of cytokines IL-6 (p = 0.032) and IFN-γ (p = 0.028) in the hippocampal tissue of the ASD group rats were significantly higher than those in the CON group; the protein levels of IL-4 (p = 0.037), IL-7 (p = 0.033), and IL-10 (p = 0.038) in the ASD group were significantly lower than those in the CON group. After 6 weeks of voluntary wheel running exercise intervention, the results of Luminex liquid-phase suspension chip detection show that the protein levels of cytokines IL-2 (p = 0.044) and IL-4 (p = 0.034) in the hippocampal tissue of the E + ASD group rats were significantly lower than those in the NE + ASD group, and the protein level of IL-10 (p = 0.040) was significantly higher than in the NE + ASD group.

Discussion

This study provides experimental evidence that voluntary wheel running ameliorates core behavioral deficits in a VPA-induced ASD rat model. We demonstrate that a 6-week exercise intervention not only improves social interaction, novelty preference, and cognitive function but also attenuates hippocampal neuroinflammation—as evidenced by reduced microglial activation and modulated cytokine profiles. While the therapeutic potential of physical activity in ASD has been recognized clinically, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms, particularly those involving hippocampal immunomodulation, remain less understood. Our findings suggest that the behavioral benefits of exercise may be mediated, at least in part, through its anti-inflammatory actions within the hippocampus, a brain region critically implicated in social and cognitive processing. These results align with emerging literature positioning neuroinflammation as a modifiable target in neurodevelopmental disorders and extend the mechanistic understanding of exercise as a non-pharmacological intervention in ASD.

In addition to the core symptoms, individuals with ASD may also exhibit various comorbid psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety and depression (Gifford et al., 2021; Lai et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2020). Furthermore, ASD patients often experience other functional impairments, including deficits in executive function, spatial reasoning, working memory, and episodic memory (Bangerter et al., 2017). Our research indicates that exercise can improve both the core symptoms of ASD and associated features. In fact, many studies in human populations have already demonstrated the positive effects of exercise on various aspects of ASD core symptoms (Pan, 2010, 2011; Lang et al., 2010; Sowa et al., 2012). In animal experiments, our results align with the findings of Andoh and colleagues. Andoh's research (Andoh et al., 2019) revealed that exercise reversed the abnormal behaviors and synaptic surplus in adult MIA offspring.

Neuroinflammation is a complex immune response of the central nervous system (CNS) triggered by various factors that disrupt homeostasis, such as injury, infection, and toxins. It involves multiple immune cells and resident cells (Griffin, 2013), manifesting as sustained activation and proliferation of glial cells and overexpression of immune cytokines (De la Rosa et al., 2020). Microglia play a crucial role in brain development, function, homeostasis maintenance, and the mediation of neuroinflammation (Mossad et al., 2020). When exposed to external stimuli, microglia alter their morphology to rapidly adapt to brain changes and initiate immune responses through a series of signaling molecules, such as cytokines and ATP (Kronfol et al., 2000). Current evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of ASD is associated with neuroinflammation. Postmortem studies have revealed abnormal microglial activation and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in the brains of ASD patients, alongside cytokine imbalances in the cerebrospinal fluid (Saetre et al., 2007; Vargas et al., 2005). Therefore, changes in microglial quantity, activation, phenotype, and cytokine levels may influence the development and progression of ASD.

Research indicates that the complex interactions between cytokines, immune cells, and messengers of the neuroendocrine system (primarily neurotransmitters and hormones) are key factors in the inflammation associated with neuropsychiatric disorders (Li et al., 2009). Numerous studies have identified alterations in cytokine levels in the brains, cerebrospinal fluid, and serum of individuals with ASD (Businaro et al., 2016; Goines et al., 2013; Liu Xie et al., 2021). In this study, measurements of cytokine levels in the hippocampus of ASD rats revealed that, prior to intervention, the ASD group exhibited significantly lower levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4, IL-7, and IL-10 compared to the control group (CON group), while pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IFN-γ were significantly elevated. These findings align with previous research. For instance, Liu et al. (Eissa et al., 2019) observed reduced levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the hippocampus of ASD rats in a study investigating the effects of arachidonoylethanolamide on neuroinflammatory responses. Similarly, Eissa et al. (Erickson et al., 2017) reported increased levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 in the hippocampal tissue of ASD mice. In human studies, Li et al. (Businaro et al., 2016) detected significantly elevated levels of IL-6 and IFN-γ in the brains of ASD patients, with high IFN-γ expression closely linked to stereotyped behaviors and communication deficits (Solleiro-Villavicencio et al., 2018).

While the precise role of IL-4 in ASD pathogenesis requires further elucidation, our findings reveal its potential involvement in neuroinflammatory regulation. The observed hippocampal IL-4 reduction in ASD rats suggests its participation, alongside IL-7 and IL-10, in anti-inflammatory processes. Notably, our 6-week exercise intervention modulated cytokine profiles, downregulating IL-2/IL-4 while upregulating IL-7/IL-10 - consistent with established exercise-induced anti-inflammatory effects (Calegari et al., 2018). The inconsistency in IL-4 levels before and after intervention in this study may be related to the timing of cytokine testing. Pre-intervention testing was conducted post-weaning (at d23, equivalent to human adolescence), while post-intervention testing occurred after 6 weeks of exercise (at d76, equivalent to human young adulthood, based on Schneider et al.‘s age classification (Schneider et al., 2006): d30-50 as childhood/adolescence, d90-120 as adulthood). Studies suggest that in the adult brain, IL-4 primarily exerts neuroprotective effects and is associated with higher-order cognitive processes. IL-4 levels are upregulated during CNS inflammation, inducing glial cell activation and protecting cells from apoptosis (Garg et al., 2009; Sholl-Franco et al., 2009). Regarding the elevated IL-4 levels in the NE + ASD group compared to other groups after intervention, this may represent a compensatory response to sustained neuroinflammation in ASD rats without exercise intervention. In the absence of exercise-mediated anti-inflammatory effects, the persistent activation of microglia and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IFN-γ) in NE + ASD rats may trigger a feedback mechanism to upregulate IL-4 production, attempting to counterbalance the pro-inflammatory environment. However, this compensatory upregulation of IL-4 appears insufficient to alleviate neuroinflammation and associated behavioral deficits, as evidenced by the sustained microglial activation and impaired behaviors in NE + ASD rats. In contrast, exercise intervention not only reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines but also modulates IL-4 to a physiological level, which may be more effective in restoring cytokine balance and mitigating neuroinflammation. This interpretation is supported by previous studies showing that compensatory cytokine responses in neurodevelopmental disorders often fail to reverse pathological changes without external interventions (Goines et al., 2013). These results collectively demonstrate that voluntary exercise can restore cytokine equilibrium in ASD, counteracting the characteristic imbalance of elevated IL-6/IFN-γ and reduced IL-4/IL-7/IL-10.

Microglia are dynamic immune cells that respond to environmental changes, whether external or internal. Upon stimulation or infection, they rapidly activate, proliferate, or alter their morphology to protect the brain (Salter et al., 2017), initiating immune responses through signaling molecules such as cytokines and ATP (Gautier et al., 2012a). Their activity and polarity are regulated by both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines (Beutner et al., 2013). Studies have found that microglial activation in the brains of individuals with ASD is abnormal, with changes in their quantity, morphology, and interactions with neurons (DiSabato et al., 2016). This study observed significantly higher expression of the microglial marker Iba1 in the CA1 and CA3 subregions of the hippocampus in ASD rats compared to the control group (CON group). This phenomenon may be related to the dynamic changes in microglial activation during development: in rodents, microglial activation is high during the second postnatal week (d10-d15), followed by a gradual decline in activation (Colonna et al., 2017; Gautier et al., 2012b; Kim et al., 2015). Thus, at the time of pre-intervention testing (rat age d23), microglial activation may have been in a declining phase, resulting in no significant difference between the two groups. Six weeks of voluntary wheel running significantly reduced Iba1 expression in hippocampal CA1/CA3 subregions of ASD rats, demonstrating exercise's ability to normalize microglial activation. These findings parallel observations in depression models, where similar exercise regimens alleviated both behavioral symptoms and hippocampal neuroinflammation, suggesting a shared anti-inflammatory mechanism. (Xiao et al., 2021). The coordinated reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-2, IL-4) and microglial activation, coupled with increased anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-7, IL-10), suggests exercise exerts multimodal anti-inflammatory effects. These findings align with emerging understanding of neuroimmune axis dysregulation in ASD, where microglial-cytokine crosstalk may drive both core symptoms and neuropathological progression.

This study conducted a correlation analysis between behavioral outcomes and neuroinflammatory markers following exercise intervention. The results revealed that neuroinflammation is associated with exploratory behavior, spontaneous activity, and social novelty preference in rats. The total distance traveled in the open field test reflects spontaneous activity (Di Stadio et al., 2019), while the number of entries into the central zone indicates exploratory behavior (Littlefield et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2005). Our analysis revealed significant negative correlations between three core behavioral domains and neuroinflammatory markers in exercised ASD rats. Specifically, exploratory behavior (quantified by center entries) inversely correlated with microglial activation (Iba1 expression), spontaneous activity (measured by total distance traveled) showed an inverse relationship with IL-2 levels, and social novelty preference (assessed through head contact frequency) demonstrated a negative association with IL-4 concentrations. These consistent patterns strongly suggest that the behavioral improvements observed following exercise intervention may be mediated through its simultaneous regulation of both microglial activity and cytokine balance. Further analysis revealed that IL-2 levels increased with heightened microglial activation. IL-2, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is closely linked to microglial activation (Yan et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019; Perry et al., 1985; Wang et al., 2018). Studies have shown that LPS-activated microglia in mice induce the expression of IL-2 receptors, which in turn regulate microglial development and mediate their activation (Luo et al.; Yin et al., 2022). Neuroinflammation in ASD is a dynamic process characterized by abnormal microglial activation and persistent changes in cytokine levels (Salter et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2021). Through comprehensive network analysis, we identified IL-2, IL-4, and Iba1 as principal nodes within the neuroinflammatory-behavioral network. The therapeutic efficacy of exercise intervention appears to stem from its capacity to concurrently attenuate hippocampal microglial activation (as evidenced by reduced Iba1 expression) and normalize cytokine profiles (characterized by decreased IL-2/IL-4 and increased IL-7/IL-10 levels), resulting in amelioration of core behavioral deficits in ASD model rats.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the Luminex assay measured the levels of 23 cytokines, which increases the risk of Type I errors due to multiple comparisons. The p-values reported for cytokine levels were not adjusted for these multiple comparisons, and future studies would benefit from applying correction methods (e.g., Bonferroni or False Discovery Rate). Second, only male rats were used in this study to avoid confounding effects of the estrous cycle on behavior and neuroinflammation. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to females, and future research should explicitly investigate the effects of exercise in female ASD model rats.

Conclusion

A 6-week period of voluntary exercise intervention enhanced social ability, social novelty preference, exploratory behavior, spontaneous activity, and learning and memory in ASD rats. Simultaneously, exercise alleviated hippocampal neuroinflammation, restored cytokine balance (reducing IL-2 and IL-4 levels while increasing IL-7 and IL-10 levels), and decreased abnormal microglial activation in the CA1 and CA3 subregions. Thus, the amelioration of abnormal behaviors in ASD rats through exercise may be linked to the mitigation of hippocampal neuroinflammation, with microglial activation and cytokine level changes playing critical roles. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
