Breath-by-breath quantification of inhaled nitric oxide absorption and modeling of methemoglobin kinetics
Authors: Run Dong, Hatus V. Wanderley, Elisa Mereto, Bijan Safaee Fakhr, Roberto Nardini, Binglan Yu, Lorenzo Berra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies uncovered extrapulmonary effects of inhaled nitric oxide (NO) therapy. However, no method exists to quantify NO absorption. This study developed a breath-by-breath system for inhaled NO absorption measurement, which consists of a rapid chemiluminescent NO analyzer and a pneumotachograph. The accuracy of the system was validated in vitro and in an animal model, achieving excellent agreement with the reference gas-collection method (linear regression R2 0.98 and 0.97; mean bias 1.5% ± 15.4% and −3.6% ± 14.4%, respectively). We established a methemoglobin kinetic model that followed single-compartment first-order elimination kinetics, and included NO absorption rate, methemoglobin conversion factor, time constant, treatment duration, hemoglobin, and blood volume. The model was trained and validated in 11 pigs receiving 81 treatments at 20 – 300 parts per million (ppm) and 14 human subjects, including 10 healthy subjects and 4 patients who received 64 treatments at 20 – 300 ppm. The approach yielded stable parameters of elimination constant 0.012 min−1 (coefficient of variation [CV] 4.1%) and conversion factor 0.015 g/μmol (CV 2.3%). When comparing methemoglobin-estimated NO absorption and measured values, linear regression R2 reached 0.89, and the mean bias was 1.0% ± 26% in human subjects. Over 84% of estimates fell within ±30% of measured values, indicating strong estimation capability of the model. This study demonstrated methemoglobin as a bedside marker for NO absorption and provides a framework for optimizing NO dosing strategies and advancing dose-response studies in inhaled NO therapy.

Introduction

As a selective pulmonary vasodilator, inhaled nitric oxide (NO) was approved by the U.S. FDA in 1999 for improving oxygenation and reducing the need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in term and near-term (>34-week gestation) neonates with hypoxic respiratory failure associated with clinical or echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Meanwhile, mounting evidence has revealed extrapulmonary organ protective effects of the gas delivered via inhalation or extracorporeal circuits on heart, kidney, brain, and hemostasis [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Recent preclinical and clinical studies have also demonstrated that high concentrations of inhaled NO (≥160 ppm) act as a broad antimicrobial agent [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. The mechanism, which is still under extensive investigation, may involve both direct effects on microorganisms and modulatory effects on the host immune system [[14], [15], [16]].

Studies have shown that inhaled NO therapy demonstrated dose-dependent deposition of NO and its metabolites in extrapulmonary organs [17]. In a murine model of cardiac and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, a U-shaped dose-responsive profile was observed during the administration of nitrite, which is a biological storage reserve of NO [18]. However, clinical trials evaluating the extrapulmonary effects of NO delivered via inhalation or extracorporeal circuit have shown conflicting results that persisted despite similar gas concentration, timing, and delivery protocols [3,[19], [20], [21]]. This suggests that achieving extrapulmonary organ protection depends not only on the delivered NO concentration but also on NO bioavailability, the variability of which may underlie these discrepancies. These findings highlight the need for pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic research to guide the dosing, monitoring, and targeting of the therapy, especially when extrapulmonary effects are sought.

After diffusion in the blood compartment, more than 80% of inspired NO is rapidly inactivated by reaction with oxyhemoglobin to form methemoglobin (MetHb) and nitrate [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Most of the MetHb is reduced to ferrous hemoglobin by NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase (CYB5R) in erythrocytes. A lesser proportion of the absorbed gas converts to nitrite or combines with plasma proteins, which might preserve NO bioactivity. MetHb is more easily monitored than nitrogen oxides and nitrosothiols and follows kinetics that could be modelled [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. While conventionally monitored for safety concerns, MetHb stands as a potential point-of-care marker of the amount of NO absorption and dosing target [31].

Inhaled NO is currently administered as a volumetric concentration measured in parts per million (ppm), with the FDA approving concentrations of up to 20 ppm for clinical use. However, the absorption of inhaled NO depends not only on the concentration delivered but also on individual factors such as minute ventilation, dead space, lung volume, hemoglobin, and blood volume. In a recently published study, healthy volunteers breathing 300 ppm of NO exhibited a doubling of MetHb levels during exercise compared with rest, suggesting that the change in cardiopulmonary conditions significantly influences NO absorption [32].

To date, no standardized methods currently exist to quantify inhaled NO absorption. In this study, we designed and applied a novel system to quantify NO absorption on a breath-by-breath basis and established a NO absorption estimation model using MetHb kinetics. The aims of the study include: (1) To build and validate a system to quantify breath-by-breath NO absorption; (2) To develop and validate the model of NO absorption and MetHb kinetics allowing bedside estimation of NO absorption; (3) To investigate the contributing factors to the amount of inhaled NO absorption.

Methods

System setup

To build a system to quantify breath-by-breath NO absorption, we constructed a closed breathing circuit that delivers NO through an endotracheal tube or a sealed mask at constant concentrations (Fig. 1A). A rapid and sensitive dual chamber NO/nitrogen dioxide (NO2) analyzer, which uses the chemiluminescence method for NO and laser-induced fluorescence for NO2 (CLD 50, Cambustion, Cambridge, UK) and a pneumotachograph (NM3, Philips-Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA) were connected to the y piece, allowing continuous monitoring of the NO and NO2 concentrations, airway flow, airway pressure and end tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) throughout the respiratory cycle. The NO/NO2 analyzer was connected closer to the patient instead of the pneumotachograph sensor to prevent interference from the NO in the bias flow. The analog output channels of the monitoring devices were connected to an analog-to-digital converter (Powerlab 8/35, ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand) and sampled at 100 Hz. Theory and algorithms

To measure the absorption of inhaled NO in the lung, we calculated the difference between the measured amounts of NO entering and leaving the lung. Since it is infeasible to measure changes in NO content within the lung, we inferred absorption from the inhaled and exhaled amounts of NO measured at the y piece.

Accordingly, NO absorption was calculated as the integral of the product of airway flow measured by the pneumotachograph and NO concentration measured by the NO/NO2 analyzer. The airway flow signal is positive when there is inflow to the subject and negative when there is outflow from the subject. The cumulative amount of NO absorption was calculated as Equation (1).(1)VNO‐tot=∫t1t2V˙(t)cNO(t)dtWhere: t1 and t2 (min) = start and end of measurement; VNO‐tot (ml) = cumulative volume of NO absorption; V˙ (ml/min) = airway gas flow; cNO (ppm) = concentration of NO.

The start of a breath cycle was identified by the airway flow turning from negative to positive and remaining positive for the next 0.3 s. The end of the breath cycle was identified by the start of the next breath cycle. Application of Equation (1) to the time range of the breath cycle generated the breath-by-breath absorption of NO.(2)VNO‐b=∫tb1tb2V˙(t)cNO(t)dtWhere: tb1 and tb2 (min) = the start and end of each breath cycle; VNO‐b (ml) = volume of NO absorption in each breath cycle.

Volume of nitric oxide stored in the lung

We did not correct for changes in intrapulmonary gas storage due to the ambiguous impact on the accuracy of pulmonary gas uptake measurements reported in previous gas-exchange studies [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]].

NO/NO2 analyzer sampling flow

The NO/NO2 analyzer continuously samples gas at a constant flow, which creates a theoretical discrepancy between the measured and the actual amount of NO absorption. However, we did not correct for the analyzer's sampling flow for two reasons. First, we only performed brief measurements to minimize the loss of delivered NO due to analyzer sampling. Second, the accuracy of the pneumotachograph was ±0.5 L/min or ±3%, whichever is greater, while the NO/NO2 analyzer sampling flow ranged from 0.4 to 0.9 L/min. This sampling flow was therefore close to the measurement error of the pneumotachograph, rendering any correction challenging to distinguish from measurement variability.

Calibration

A signaling delay of the NO/NO2 analyzer was caused by the gas transport and mixing within the sampling line of the NO/NO2 analyzer and the response time of the device. Since it is a considerable source of error in pulmonary gas exchange studies, previous studies adopted various methods to calibrate the delay [34,37,39,40]. We conducted the calibration by repeatedly connecting a breathing tube delivering constant concentration of NO at a fixed gas flow to the pneumotachograph and NO/NO2 analyzer (Fig. 1B). The calibration was performed before measuring each subject with at least 30 waveforms recorded for calculation.

The recorded delay between the NO concentration and gas flow during the calibration process is composed of three parts. First, the time for NO-containing gas to transport through the volume of the respiratory tube to the sampling line of the NO/NO2 analyzer (TΔv, second) was calculated using the tubing volume (ΔV, ml) and flow-time integration (ml).(12)ΔV=∫t0t1V˙(t)dt(13)TΔv = t1 – t0

Second, the time for NO containing gas to transport through the sampling line of NO/NO2 analyzer (Tt, second) was calculated by subtracting TΔv from the time difference measured from the start of the flow signal rising to the start of the NO signal rising.

Third, the rising phase of NO concentration represents the response time of the analyzer and the end of gas transport through the sampling line. Since the rising curve of the NO concentration curve in our system is more sigmoidal shaped than exponential, third-order regression showed the best fitness (R2 > 0.88) [41]. The rise time (τ, second) was defined as the time reaching 63.2% of the full rising curve of NO concentration.

The signaling delay caused by the NO/NO2 analyzer's sampling line and response time (Td, second) was calculated as the sum of Tt and τ:(14)Td = Tt + τ

The mean value of the signaling delay measurements in each calibration session was used for shifting the NO concentration signal, and the standard deviation (SD) was used for error sensitivity analysis.

The pneumotachograph was calibrated at room temperature using a constant flow generated by a flow controller with an accuracy of ±0.4% (MCP-100SLPM-D-DB9M/5 M, Alicat Scientific, AZ, USA).

Testing the system

Validation

The breath-by-breath measurement of NO absorption was validated using the gas-collection bag method. A simulation lung (ASL 5000, IngMar Medical, PA, USA) was set in spontaneous mode with modifiable tidal volume (TV), respiratory rate (RR), and inspiratory time. An activated charcoal filter was connected as part of the simulation lung to absorb NO. One Ambu valve and a one-way valve were used to separate inhaled and exhaled gas (Fig. 1C). A 6-liter bag was prefilled with a mixture of air and NO and connected to the inspiratory limb. The other bag was connected to the expiratory limb to collect expired gas. The expired volume of gas was measured by a 1-liter syringe, and the volume of inspired gas was assumed to be the same as the expired. NO concentration was measured in both bags after mixing, and absorbed NO was calculated as the inspired amount subtracted by the expired amount. Breath-by-breath measurement of NO absorption using our system was conducted simultaneously and compared with the gas-collection method. The same validation test was performed in intubated and spontaneously breathing swine.

Error sensitivity analysis

The accuracy and robustness of the breath-by-breath NO absorption measuring system were examined through error sensitivity analysis. Error ranges of basic measurements, including airway flow, NO concentration, and NO/NO2 analyzer signaling delay, were determined using manufacturer's instructions or SD of repeated measurements. For each error source, the induced error of the NO absorption measurement was calculated as (breath-by-breath measurement – bag method measurement)/bag method measurement × 100% in the same treatment. Linear regression was fitted for the induced system error against the error of basic measurements.

In vivo application

Animal study

An animal study was conducted to develop the model linking measured NO absorption rate to MetHb kinetics, and to identify the ventilatory and physiological determinants of NO absorption. The study was approval by the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH, Boston, MA, USA) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Three groups of intubated and mechanically ventilated Yorkshire pigs (CBSET Inc., Lexington, MA, USA) were studied with detailed methods described in the Appendix and Fig. S1: (1) healthy pigs ventilated via single lumen endotracheal tube for 24 h; (2) Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) infected pigs with double lumen endotracheal tube for 72 h; (3) acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) model with single and double lumen endotracheal tube for 12 h. All animals received inhaled NO treatments during which breath-by-breath NO absorption was measured and MetHb was closely monitored.

Animals were intubated, sedated and mechanically ventilated (Maquet Servo-I, Getinge, Merrimack, NH, USA) with a tidal volume (VT) of 6 - 10 ml/kg body weight, a respiratory rate (RR) to maintain an ETCO2 between 35 and 45 mmHg and blood pH of 7.3 - 7.5, a positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 - 15 cmH2O, and an inspired fraction of oxygen (FiO2) to maintain a partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) > 60 mmHg. Volume controlled ventilation with constant inspiratory flow was used except for the experiments identifying the effects of ventilatory settings on NO absorption. The femoral artery and internal or external jugular vein were cannulated, and Swan-Ganz catheter was placed. All animals were euthanized after 12 - 72 h of mechanical ventilation.

The P. aeruginosa infected group received an inoculation of a 75 ml suspension of P. aeruginosa (106 colony forming units/ml [CFU/ml]) to the lung lobes via bronchoscopy after the preparation phase [32]. The inoculation was repeated by nebulizing 30 ml of suspension containing the same pathogen (106 CFU/ml) at 24 and 48 h. The concentration of P. aeruginosa was verified at each inoculation by incubating serially diluted suspension on blood agar plates. Infection was evaluated by postmortem bacterial culture of tissue sampled from 4 lobes. The animals were placed prone and in a reverse Trendelenburg position after the first inoculation through 72 h. The ARDS model was induced by instilling 3.5 ml/kg of hydrochloric acid (HCl, pH 1.0) in to the lobar bronchi via bronchoscopy and confirmed by a PaO2 to FiO2 (P/F) ratio less than 200 mmHg. The animals were placed supine during the experiment.

Inhaled NO therapy was administered at doses of 20 to 300 ppm for 30 to 135 min using a custom tank-based system (INOMAX EVOLVE DS, Mallinckrodt Manufacturing LLC, WI, USA), which delivered NO proportional to the airway flow and resulted in constant NO concentrations in the inspiratory limb. Intervals of 30 min to 4 h between treatments allowed MetHb to recover to below 2.0%. All healthy animals received inhaled NO therapy through single lumen endotracheal tube which delivered NO to both lungs. At the same NO concentration of 80 ppm, we changed ventilatory settings, i.e. tidal volume, inspiratory time, inspiratory pause, flow pattern, and PEEP, and manipulated CO at an interval of at least 15 min. For the P. aeruginosa infected pigs, the single lumen endotracheal tube was exchanged by a double lumen (left bronchial) endotracheal tube with a bronchial ID of 4.3 - 4.5 mm and a tracheal ID of 4.5 - 4.7 mm after bacterial inoculation. The two lumens were connected to separate ventilators, and inhaled NO was delivered to one lung through one of the lumens. In the ARDS model, the single lumen endotracheal tube was exchanged by a double lumen (left) endotracheal tube, and inhaled NO was delivered before (to both lungs) and after (to one lung) tube exchange.

Parameters recorded during the treatment included NO and NO2 concentrations, airway flow, airway pressure, ETCO2, heart rate (HR), arterial blood pressure (ABP), central venous pressure (CVP), pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac output (CO). Brief measurements (less than 1 min) of NO and NO2 concentrations were performed at the beginning, middle and end of each treatment to minimize the loss of delivered NO due to sampling by the NO/NO2 analyzer and to prevent obstruction of the analyzer by respiratory secretions. RR was determined using the previously mentioned method for recognizing breath cycles, while TV was calculated by integrating positive and negative airway flow over time within each breath cycle. NO absorption rate and breath-by-breath absorption was calculated using our newly developed and validated system. Meanwhile, breath-by-breath NO absorption was measured and compared before and after manipulating CO and altering ventilatory settings. A noninvasive pulse co-oximeter (Masimo Rad7, Irvine, CA, US) was attached to the tail and used to continuously monitor MetHb and oxyhemoglobin levels. Arterial blood gas samples were obtained before and after each treatment, with arterial blood MetHb concentration tested using co-oximetry (ABL 800 flex, Radiometer Medical, Copenhagen, Denmark). Partial pressure of arterial oxygen to fraction of inspired oxygen (P/F) ratio and driving pressure (airway plateau pressure - PEEP) were used to reflect lung injury.

Healthy subject study

To develop and validate the MetHb kinetic model for estimating NO absorption in humans, we conducted a healthy subject study in which NO absorption was measured and MetHb kinetics were continuously monitored. Healthy volunteers were enrolled in a phase I trial (NCT05612074) approved by the MGH institutional review board (IRB) on December 5th, 2022 (2022P002884) to evaluate the safety and kinetics of MetHb under intermittent high-dose inhaled NO administration. Informed consent was obtained and all procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was described in detail by a previous study [32]. Briefly, participants were allocated to two groups with FiO2 21% or 80% and received inhaled NO via a sealed mask at 300 ppm for 30 min, 3 times daily for 5 consecutive days [42]. Subjects underwent interventions at rest on days 1 to 4 and during stationary cycling exercise on day 5. Physiological and laboratory parameters were collected before, during, and after each treatment, including hemodynamic parameters (heart rate, blood pressure), lung function test (forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1], forced vital capacity [FVC]), ventilation and gas exchange parameters (pulse oximetry saturation to fractional inspired oxygen ratio [SpO2/FiO2], VT, RR, minute ventilation, ETCO2). MetHb was monitored noninvasively through a pulse co-oximeter (Masimo Rad7, Irvine, CA, US) attached to a finger.

In addition, another group of healthy volunteers were enrolled and received continuous inhaled NO at 80 ppm for 60 – 120 min with the aforementioned data collected.

Measurements were identified as valid based on the following criteria: (1) Simultaneous recording of both airway flow and NO concentration at the y piece was conducted; (2) To exclude measurements with gas passing external to the face mask, the percentage of breaths with over 30% difference between expiratory and inspiratory tidal volume should not exceed 30%; (3) The difference of minute ventilation between measurement and treatment period remained within 30% to ascertain the representativeness of the brief measurement.

Clinical study

To develop and validate the MetHb kinetic model in clinical settings, we conducted a prospective, observational study (NCT05612074) in which both breath-by-breath NO absorption and MetHb kinetics were measured in patients receiving inhaled NO therapy. After the approval by the MGH institutional review board on December 26th, 2024 (2024P001999), adult patients who were planned to receive inhaled NO therapy were enrolled after oral consent due to the observational nature of the study. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. To avoid physiological complexity, we excluded: (1) pregnant or actively lactating patients; (2) patients on renal replacement therapy; (3) patients actively participating in other studies that might interfere with the inhaled NO therapy or data collection; (4) patients who had received inhaled NO therapy within one week.

Before starting the treatment, the NO absorption monitoring apparatus and a non-invasive co-oximetry were connected to the subject. We conducted continuous recording of non-invasive MetHb, NO concentration, NO2 concentration, airway flow, airway pressure, ETCO2, HR, ABP, SpO2, and/or pulmonary artery catheter parameters during the first hour of the treatment. Arterial blood gas was tested per clinical practice. Demographic and clinical characteristics were obtained via medical record review. Lung function test results within 1 year prior to the hospital admission were retrieved.

Methemoglobin kinetic model

The relationship between MetHb kinetics and NO absorption rate, i.e. the amount of NO absorption in unit of time, was investigated in both animal and human studies. Data from inhaled NO treatments were collected and used to develop a MetHb kinetic model assuming a single-compartment, first-order elimination framework. Three assumptions were made: NO is absorbed at a constant rate when delivered at a constant concentration over a short period, MetHb formation remains in the blood compartment, and the elimination of MetHb follows first-order kinetics [[28], [29], [30]].

In the original equation, the rate of MetHb concentration change depends on MetHb formation rate, intrinsic oxidation rate of hemoglobin, and elimination rate of MetHb. The MetHb formation rate is determined by NO absorption rate, conversion factor of NO to form MetHb and the fraction of reduced hemoglobin to react. The original model is as follows(15)d[MetHb]dt=F·αVd·[Hb]‐[MetHb][Hb]+ks·[Hb]‐ke·[MetHb]where: [MetHb] (g/L) = MetHb concentration; [Hb] (g/L) = hemoglobin concentration; F (g/μmol) = conversion factor of NO to form MetHb; α (μmol/min) = absorption rate of NO; ks (min−1) = intrinsic oxidation rate of MetHb; ke (min−1) = elimination constant; Vd (L) = distribution volume, i.e. blood volume which was estimated according to a previous study in swine and Nadler equation for human [43,44].

Theoretically, 1 mol of NO oxidizes 1/4 mol of hemoglobin tetramer, giving F = 0.016 g/μmol based on the molecular weight per hemoglobin tetramer. However, in vivo, not all absorbed NO react with hemoglobin [45]. Therefore, F was estimated from the collected treatment data and represents the effective conversion efficiency.

Before NO administration, the intrinsic oxidation of MetHb equals its rate of reduction:(16)ks·[Hb]=ke·[MetHbbl]where: [MetHbbl] (g/L) = baseline concentration of MetHb.(17)d[MetHb]dt=F·αVd·[Hb]‐[MetHb][Hb]+ke·[MetHbbl]‐ke·[MetHb]

The full derivation of the model is specified in Appendix, and a simplified form was adopted based on two additional assumptions that the baseline MetHb is typically less than 2% and the NO induced MetHb formation rate (F·αVd·[Hb]) is much smaller than the elimination constant (ke) [29,30].

During the accumulation phase, when NO was delivered, MetHb change is related to NO absorption rate, conversion factor of NO to form MetHb, elimination constant, and treatment duration:(18)MetHb(%)=F·αke·Vd·Hb·(1‐e‐ket)+MetHbbl(%)where: MetHb (%) = measured value of MetHb; MetHbbl (%) = baseline value of MetHb; t (min) = treatment duration.

During the plateau state (steady state during continuous delivery):(19)MetHb(%)=F·αke·Vd·Hb+MetHbbl(%)

During the elimination phase (after stopping NO delivery):(20)MetHb(%)=(MetHbpeak−MetHbbl)·e‐kete+MetHbbl(%)where: MetHbpeak (%) = peak value of MetHb; te (min) = time after treatment cessation.

Continuous pulse co-oximetry MetHb was used for modelling in animal and human studies and was adjusted for arterial MetHb by linear regression in the animal study. For animals receiving sequential treatments with less than 2-h interval, the residual MetHb formed during the prior treatment may still be undergoing elimination. To account for this, the pre-experiment resting MetHb measured before the first treatment was used as the true baseline MetHb for each animal with the modified equation specified in the Appendix.

The model of MetHb kinetics during inhaled NO treatments and post-treatment elimination phase was fitted using a nonlinear least squares model optimized by Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno with Bound constraints (L-BFGS-B) algorithm. Model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), where each subject's data was iteratively held out as a test set while training on the remaining subjects. For each fold, F and ke were optimized using the training data, and predictions on the held-out subject's complete time series were evaluated. We used the following parameters to assess model performance: (1) coefficient of variance (CV) of F and k; (2) coefficient of determination (R2) for both training and validation data sets; (3) root mean square error (RMSE). The model was fitted and validated based on both the simplified and original equations. Using the simplified equation, the mean F and ke derived from the LOOCV folds were used to estimate the NO absorption rate for each treatment. The estimated values were then compared to the measured NO absorption rates to assess the predictive accuracy of the model.

Statistical analysis

According to data distribution and sample size, continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range) or median (range). Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare baseline characteristics and NO absorption rates between groups. Breath-by-breath NO absorption before and after changing ventilatory settings or cardiac output was analyzed using linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with the altered setting as fixed effect, individual animals and treatment pairs as random intercepts. Clinical significance was defined according to potential system error in the error sensitivity analysis. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons.

For the comparison of NO absorption before and after ventilatory adjustments, the sample size was determined using LMM power analysis to detect a clinically meaningful difference of 20% in NO absorption between ventilation settings. With over 50 breaths analyzed per setting per subject, 5 paired comparisons would achieve 80% power at α = 0.05 significance level, accounting for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

Sample size determination for the MetHb kinetic model, which is a nonlinear regression model, requires a minimum of 25 animals or subjects considering the 20% variance of time constant in previous literature [28,46]. However, our design of multiple treatments per subject with washout intervals increases the effective sample size beyond the number of enrolled subjects. For the cross-validation, 5- to 10-fold cross-validation should be preferred over LOOCV [47]. Accordingly, we planned to enroll at least 10 subjects receiving 2 to 3 treatments each.

Inhaled NO absorption calculation and statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R CoreTeam, 2024).

Results

Validation

To determine the accuracy of the breath-by-breath NO absorption measurement system, validation tests were performed using spontaneous breaths generated by a simulation lung and by swine (Table S1 and Fig. 2). The mean NO absorption per breath measured by the breath-by-breath system was compared with that obtained from the reference bag-collection method. Linear regression demonstrated excellent agreement over a variety of NO concentrations, TV and RR, with R2 = 0.98 for the simulation lung and 0.97 for swine. Bland-Altman analysis showed mean biases of 1.5% (SD 15.4%; 95% limits of agreement, −28.6% to 31.6%) and −3.6% (14.4%; −31.8% to 27.4%), respectively. Error sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the sensitivity of the measurement system to errors in basic measurements, we performed linear regressions between the error percentage of each basic measurement and the resulting system error (Table S2). The system was sensitive to errors in airway flow measurement (regression slope 4.7, R2 0.24) and the determination of NO/NO2 analyzer sampling delay (regression slope 2.6, R2 0.21). This indicates that the ±3% error in airway flow measurement or ±5% error in sampling delay determination would result in approximately 14.1% and 13% system error, respectively. These results suggest the threshold for distinguishing true differences between measured NO absorption and highlight the importance of precise airway flow and NO/NO2 signaling delay calibration.

Methemoglobin kinetics model

To investigate the relationship between MetHb kinetics and NO absorption, we conducted animal and human studies in which MetHb was continuously monitored before, during and after inhaled NO treatments, and NO absorption was measured using our validated breath-by-breath measurement system.

The animal study included 11 pigs (3 – 9 months old, 32 – 84 kg, both males and females) which received 81 inhaled NO treatments at 20 – 300 ppm for 30 – 135 min. Seven of the animals were healthy, three given intrapulmonary inoculation of P. aeruginosa to build a model of ventilator-associated pneumonia and one instilled hydrochloric acid to build an ARDS model. Pulse co-oximetry monitoring during and after inhaled NO treatments showed that MetHb levels varied based on both NO concentration and whether the gas was delivered to both lungs or single lung with corresponding difference in NO absorption rates (Fig. 3A and B). Further comparison across the three groups at the same inhaled NO dose of 300 ppm and matched ventilation type (double or single lung) reveled that animals with worse oxygenation (P/F ratio) exhibited lower NO absorption (Table S3). To investigate the variance in MetHb reduction, we calculated the elimination constant (ke) for each treatment and individual with methemoglobin (MetHb) tracing during the elimination phase. In the animal study, ke was 0.042 ± 0.016 min−1 (t1/2 = 18.8 ± 7.3 min) across 74 treatment sessions from 11 pigs, with an intra-individual CV of 31.8% and inter-individual CV of 51.6%. Animals with disease models (P. aeruginosa pneumonia or ARDS, n = 4) showed similar clearance compared with the healthy ones (n = 7): ke 0.041 ± 0.011 min−1 vs 0.044 ± 0.027 min−1 (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.53). The human study showed less variability, with a ke of 0.015 ± 0.005 min−1 (t1/2 = 51.2 ± 14.3 min) across 59 treatments of 11 subjects, with an intra-individual CV of 20.4% and inter-individual CV of 27.5%. Healthy subjects (n = 10) had a ke of 0.015 ± 0.002 min−1, while Patient 3 showed faster clearance with ke values of 0.026 min−1 and 0.033 min−1 in two treatment sessions.

Contributing factors to NO absorption

To investigate the contributing factors to NO absorption, animal studies were conducted in 6 pigs receiving 40 inhaled NO treatments at 80 ppm under systematically varied ventilatory settings and/or CO, yielding 9 to 441 breath-by-breath NO absorption measurements per treatment. Linear mixed-effects regression analysis revealed statistically significant factors affecting breath-by-breath NO absorption (Table 3 and Fig. 5). Increased TV, inspiratory pause and inspiratory time showed positive effects (17.1% per ml/kg [95%CI, 16.6 - 17.6], 14.6% per second [13.0 - 16.2] and 14.1% per second [11.5 - 16.8], respectively) with adjusted p values < 0.001. Conversely, decelerating flow reduced absorption by 7.6% (5.9 - 9.3, adjusted p < 0.001). At the weighted mean CO of 4.6 L/min, higher PEEP decreased NO absorption by 9.5% (8.6 - 10.4, adjusted p < 0.001). A 7.8% (7.3 - 8.3, adjusted p < 0.001) decrease in NO absorption was noted on each 1L/min increase of CO at low PEEP (3 - 5 cmH2O). Accounting for a potential system error of 14%, TV, inspiratory pause and inspiratory time demonstrated clinically significant effects on NO absorption. Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a system to quantify NO absorption breath-by-breath during inhaled NO therapy. Using this system, we established a model linking MetHb kinetics to NO absorption rate, providing the first evidence supporting the use of MetHb as a point-of-care marker for estimating NO absorption in clinical settings.

The breath-by-breath system achieved excellent correlation with the reference bag gas-collection method and acceptable bias levels, indicating reliable accuracy across diverse breathing conditions. The mean differences from the bag method (1.5% ± 15.4% for in vitro and −3.6% ± 14.4% for in vivo tests) were comparable to systems measuring the uptake of other gas components using similar principles [37,48]. Error sensitivity analysis revealed that the system accuracy was mostly affected by airway flow measurement and NO/NO2 analyzer sampling delay determination, which induced errors of ±14% and ±13%, respectively. To minimize these errors, we calibrated airway gas flow measurement using a precision flow controller. The NO/NO2 analyzer sampling delay is one of the main challenges in gas exchange measurement systems [34,37,[39], [40], [41]]. As reported by Noguchi et al. and Arieli et al. we decomposed the delay into gas transport lag time and analyzer response time which fitted a third-order correction [39,41]. The predicted error is also comparable to previously reported gas exchange studies [37,48].

A previous study using gas-collection measurement reported NO absorption rate of 0.5 ml/min (20 μmol/min) at 100 ppm among healthy volunteers, which is comparable to our results at 80 ppm [25]. We advanced the work by setting up a system without interfering with the respiratory support, which allowed its clinical application, especially in critically ill patients. In addition, the breath-by-breath resolution of the system enables further development of real-time measurement approaches and investigation into the interaction between gas delivery and absorption.

MetHb has been used as a safety parameter of inhaled NO therapy since it reduces the oxygen carrying capacity of hemoglobin [49]. However, it also represents a quantitative marker of NO absorption due to its unique oxidation and reduction kinetics. The reaction between NO and hemoglobin is extremely fast, such that the formation of MetHb is independent of its own concentration, and MetHb remains confined to the vascular space [50]. Under constant rate of NO absorption, the production of MetHb should be nearly constant. The elimination of MetHb follows the first-order kinetics even at levels approaching 20% [25,29,30].

A previous study modelled MetHb kinetics at different doses of inhaled NO by incorporating treatment duration and elimination constant [25]. However, the model should also depend on the NO absorption rate, blood volume, and hemoglobin concentration. Our data demonstrated the complexity that the same NO concentration could generate different levels of absorption and MetHb (Table 1, Table S3 and Fig. 3), with diseased animal models and patients with pulmonary conditions exhibiting lower rates of NO absorption and smaller MetHb changes than healthy subjects. Thus, we advanced the model by including NO absorption rate, blood volume, hemoglobin, and MetHb conversion factor. The model yielded stable parameters, including an elimination constant of 0.012 min−1 and a MetHb conversion factor of 0.015 g/μmol in human subjects, which are consistent with previous studies [25,[28], [29], [30],51]. Our approach demonstrated strong predictive capability for NO absorption, particularly with human subjects (both healthy volunteers and critically ill patients), achieving R2 0.89 and a mean bias of 1.0% with 84% of estimated within ±30% of measured values. With further clinical validation, this model could guide NO dosing strategies and inform future dose-response studies.

The wider limits of agreement in the validation of our MetHb kinetic model probably reflected individual variability in MetHb formation and reduction. We further investigated this by estimating individual elimination constants from elimination-phase data, revealing considerable intra- and inter-individual variance. This variability likely represents differences in the enzymatic reduction of MetHb, which is predominantly mediated by NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase (CYB5R). The activity of CYB5R is known to vary with genetic polymorphisms, age, oxidative stress, and nutritional factors [30,[52], [53], [54]]. The limited sample size in our study, especially of patients with elimination-phase data, precludes definitive conclusions regarding disease effects on MetHb clearance, but our individual-level analysis provided a framework for characterizing this variability in future studies with larger patient cohorts.

Our study identified significant positive effects of tidal volume, inspiratory pause and inspiratory time on NO absorption, which explained the variation in absorption at identical doses observed in both animals (Fig. 3A and Table S3) and humans (Fig. 3C and Table 1) [55]. These findings highlight that systemic NO absorption is primarily governed by ventilatory mechanics: the inspired volume determines the amount of NO delivered to the lungs, while the duration of inspiration and inspiratory pause regulate the time available for gas transfer across the alveolar–capillary membrane [56]. This provides clinical insights into adapting NO delivery strategies in response to changes in ventilation.

Another important observation was that healthy lungs absorbed more NO than diseased lungs, as demonstrated in P. aeruginosa infected and ARDS animals, as well as patients with pulmonary conditions. The association between oxygenation and lung capacity with NO absorption indicates that lung volume, diffusing capacity, or intrapulmonary shunt may also contribute additional variance, which warrants further investigation [55]. Taken together, these findings support the concept that NO uptake and MetHb kinetics could be quantitatively modelled and may serve as physiological indicators of lung injury, disease severity and progression. Future studies are warranted to explore whether breath-by-breath NO absorption can serve as a noninvasive tool to characterize lung function.

Our study has some limitations. First, some treatments did not reach the plateau of MetHb, potentially compromising the model accuracy. However, extending high-dose NO treatment beyond 30 – 45 min was intentionally avoided, as sustained exposure at such high doses is clinically hazardous; thus, the model reflects safe and realistic treatment conditions. Second, human blood volume was estimated using the Nadler equation, which may not accurately reflect the wide variations seen in critically ill patients. Direct measurement of circulating volume, however, would have required invasive tracer studies that were not feasible in this translational design; the use of a validated equation provided a consistent and noninvasive approximation across subjects. Third, the 95% limit of agreement between estimated and measured NO absorption rates, as well as the variance in MetHb clearance, likely reflects individual variabilities of MetHb formation and reduction. However, the core physiological factors, including the time constant, hemoglobin, blood volume and MetHb conversion factors have been captured in the current model [25,57]. Future population pharmacokinetic studies with larger sample sizes could identify additional patient-specific covariates to further optimize estimation accuracy. Fourth, this study focused on MetHb as a marker of NO absorption without measuring NO metabolites or S-nitrosothiols, which might represent reservoirs of systemic NO bioavailability. We selected MetHb due to its relatively stable kinetics, whereas NO metabolites exhibit short half-lives and multiple routes of metabolism and excretion that would confound real-time absorption quantification [58,59]. Future investigations correlating NO absorption with its bioavailability would provide valuable insights into the therapeutic dose-response relationship.

Conclusion

We developed and validated a novel breath-by-breath system for accurate measurement of NO absorption during inhaled therapy. By establishing the model between MetHb kinetics and NO absorption rate, we demonstrated that bedside MetHb monitoring could serve as a surrogate marker for NO absorption. This innovation represents a step-change for future investigations, providing the quantitative framework needed to individualize NO therapy, integrate pharmacokinetics with physiology, and expand the frontier of gas-based therapeutics.
