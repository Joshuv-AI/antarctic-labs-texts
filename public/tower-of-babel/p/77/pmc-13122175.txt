Automated linear DNA assembly of A. thaliana’s chloroplast and mitochondrial genome
Authors: Jianting Zhou, Xuyan Wang, Longfei Zhou, Haoran Zhang, Sheng Ye, Ying-Jin Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic genomics is advancing from microbial toward multicellular organisms. However, current manual methods for DNA and genome assembly remain inadequate for the efficient, large-scale production of long DNA constructs. Here, we present Programmed DNA Assembly via Cas9 and Conjugative Transfer (PACT), a method that integrates a linear vector system, bacterial conjugation, and programmable Cas9-mediated cleavage to achieve highly efficient, iterative assembly of large DNA fragments. PACT enhances assembly efficiency by ~30-fold compared to conventional circular vector strategies, enabling one-step assembly of DNA up to 80 kb. We engineered four single guide-RNA-Marker donor cassettes to support iterative assembly workflows. PACT can utilize low-recombination Escherichia coli strains as hosts to efficiently assemble Arabidopsis thaliana mitochondrial genome with high repeat units. Integrated with an automated robotic platform, we developed an unattended, high-throughput pipeline (aPACT) toward large-scale parallel DNA assembly. Using aPACT, we successfully assembled three large DNA constructs: a 210 kb digital DNA, the designed chloroplast (120 kb) and mitochondrial (350 kb) genome of A. thaliana. This automated system offers a powerful tool for scalable assembly of large DNA molecules, accelerating synthetic genomics research toward complex multicellular organisms.

Introduction

Synthetic genomics provides powerful tools for elucidating biological functions and evolutionary processes by enabling the design and synthesis of entire genomes [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, genome synthesis has progressed from viral genomes [3, 4], bacterial chromosomes [5–17] to plant [18] and animal genomic regions [19–21], advancing from kilobase to megabase scales. While significant progress has been made, the overall engineering cycle (DBTL) remains labor-intensive and time-consuming [22]. Among these steps, slow or inefficient assembly directly delays downstream functional testing and iterative refinement. Therefore, accelerating DNA assembly not only reduces hands-on time but also shortens the entire design–build–test–learn cycle in synthetic genomics [1, 23].

Biofoundries have emerged as cornerstone infrastructure in synthetic biology, integrating automation, high-throughput workflows, and computational design to accelerate the engineering of biological systems [23, 24]. Since the establishment of the Global Biofoundry Alliance (GBA) in 2019—uniting 16 leading biofoundries—the field has witnessed transformative progress [25]. The Edinburgh Genome Foundry now processes over 2000 DNA assembly reactions weekly, 20-fold higher throughput than manual methods [23, 26, 27]. The DNA-Bot/AssemblyTron platform [28, 29], developed by the London Biofoundry, integrates BASIC assembly technology with the Opentrons® OT-2 robotic system to deliver a cost-effective, open-source solution for automated DNA assembly. The iBioFAB from University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign builds up to 1000 TALEN constructs daily and reduces costs to <$3 per construct [30]. The PlasmidMaker platform automatically synthesized 101 plasmids (ranging from 5 to 18 kb) in three days [31]. Numerous other biofoundries, including those not yet peer reviewed but documented in press releases, videos, and company websites, specialize in developing tools and technologies for automated DNA assembly. Examples include Singapore’s SynCTI Foundry (https://syncti.org/synthetic-biology-foundry/), Ginkgo Bioworks (https://www.ginkgo.bio/), Amyris Inc. [32], the DAMP Lab [33] and others. These advancements underscore biofoundries’ capacity to streamline routine DNA construction, yet a critical frontier remains unaddressed: scaling toward large DNA fragment automated assembly. Current biofoundry DNA assembly pipelines rely primarily on in vitro ligation or recombination methods, which exhibit sharp efficiency declines with increasing fragment size. While cell-free systems can assemble plasmids up to 100 kb [34], success rates for larger constructs remain prohibitively low. This bottleneck limits the goals for synthetic genomes. Thus, advancing biofoundry capabilities toward large-scale DNA assembly is not merely an incremental step but a necessary evolution to unlock the full potential of biological engineering.

Numerous cell host-based strategies for large DNA assembly have been developed. For example, SIRCAS allows iterative replacement of genomic regions in Salmonella via recombineering [35], while the SwAP-In or REXER strategy enables stepwise replacement of genomic segments in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Escherichia coli [6, 36, 37]. Additionally, episomal-based methods have been optimized for assembling large DNA fragments in various hosts. Entirely synthetic Mycoplasma genomes (0.5–1 Mb) were first assembled into yeast episomes from shorter in vitro fragments before transfer into Mycoplasma cells [5, 38]. OGAB enables one step assembly of multiple DNA fragments with a designed order and orientation in Bacillus subtilis plasmids [39–42], and is now offered for commercial-scale synthesis of 100 kb human DNA (https://www.synplogen.com/en/technology/). BASIS achieved 1 Mb human genome assembly in E. coli, leveraging its rapid growth and well-characterized genetics [43]. Benefiting from the host’s native recombination machinery and avoiding in vitro ligation inefficiencies, in vivo assembly in microbial hosts offers a path to automation, but it has not been established yet because of the cumbersome in vitro DNA operations, the preparation of competent cells, and the incompatibility of the operation process with automated equipment.

Since the advent of DNA recombination technology in the 1970s [44], circular plasmid vectors have been core tools in molecular biology, enabling gene cloning, genetic manipulation, and genetic engineering. However, as synthetic biology advances, circular vectors face bottlenecks in cloning large fragments and complex sequences. Bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) stability decreases significantly for plasmids >300 kb [45, 46], and oversized circular plasmids are prone to breakage during extraction with low yields, limiting validation to sequencing rather than rapid assays. By contrast, linear plasmids are a naturally occurring class of plasmids [47], offering unique function. Scientists have attempted to develop engineered vectors and methods using linear plasmids. Ronald Godiska et al. developed the linear “pJAZZ” vector, demonstrating the advantage for cloning of repetitive or unstable sequences in E. coli [48, 49]. Zhong et al. reported CALBIA, which uses a linear phage-derived vector and RecA-mediated recombination to clone megabase-sized human chromosomal DNA in bacteria [50]. Though insufficient application, linear plasmids exhibit better stability, and easier validation [e.g. via pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)] for large DNA fragments.

Here, we developed Programmed DNA Assembly by Cas9 and Conjugative Transfer (PACT), an iterative method for assembling large DNA constructs into a linear plasmid backbone via single-crossover recombination. Because PACT operates independently of the RecA+, it can effectively utilize RecA− E. coli strains to efficiently assemble DNA fragments characterized by high repeat content. As PACT eliminates the need for in vitro DNA extraction, enzymatic digestion, or protoplast formation, we integrated PACT into the Tianjin University Biological Foundry for DNA Fabrication (TJU-DNAFab), creating an automated workflow (aPACT) capable of high-throughput assembly of large DNA molecules. As proof of concept, we assembled the designed Arabidopsis thaliana mitochondrial (350 kb) and chloroplast (120 kb) genomes. PACT and aPACT advance scalable DNA assembly, facilitating synthetic genomics in complex multicellular organisms.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids construction

Strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The E. coli DH10B was used as the donor strain, with either E. coli MG1655 or DH10B serving as recipient strains for PACT experiments. Two engineered plasmids were central to this system: (i) RK2-Z3, a derivative of the RK2 plasmid hosted in the donor strain, modified by deletion of kanamycin and tetracycline resistance markers; and (ii) pCas [51], maintained in recipient strains, which enables constitutive Cas9 expression and arabinose-inducible production of λRed recombinase to enhance homologous recombination efficiency. The RK2-Z3 plasmid provided essential conjugation functions for DNA transfer between strains, while the pCas plasmid facilitated DNA assembly through CRISPR–Cas9 coupled with λRed recombinase.

The recipient vectors were constructed through a multi-step assembly strategy. The TelN gene and tosRL sequence were commercially synthesized (SynbioB, China). The BAC backbone and chloramphenicol resistance gene (Cat) were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Japan) with primers listed in Supplementary Table S2. Homologous arms specific to the donor strain were similarly amplified via PCR. Subsequently, five DNA fragments, including the synthesized TelN and tosRL cassettes, BAC backbone, Cat marker, and donor-specific homology arms, were assembled using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assembled products were transformed into E. coli DH10B competent cells. Positive clones were selected on LB agar plates containing 34 μg/ml chloramphenicol and verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology).

The donor vectors of violacein biosynthetic reporter system were constructed from four initial vectors pDon1, pDon2, pDon3, and pDon4. The backbones of pDon1 to pDon4 and donor DNA bricks were amplified via PCR. Then assemble them also by using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly kit. The assembled products were transformed into E. coli DH10B competent cells and screened on LB agar plates supplemented with spectinomycin (50 μg/ml) or apramycin (50 μg/ml).

Conjugative transfer of RK2-Z3 to donor strains

The RK2-Z3 plasmid was introduced into strains harboring donor plasmid via bacterial conjugation [52]. Overnight cultures of RK2-Z3 strain were grown in LB medium supplemented with 100 μg/ml ampicillin, while donor strains were cultured in LB containing either 50 μg/ml spectinomycin or 30 μg/ml apramycin. Bacterial cells (1 ml each) were harvested by centrifugation at 10 000 × g for 1 min, washed twice with fresh LB medium, and resuspended in 80 μl LB. Donor strains and RK2-Z3 bearing E. coli were mixed at a 1:4 (v/v) ratio, spotted onto LB agar plates, and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to facilitate conjugation. Cell mixtures were subsequently streaked onto selective LB agar plates containing both ampicillin (100 μg/ml) and either spectinomycin (50 μg/ml) or apramycin (30 μg/ml). Plates were incubated at 37°C overnight.

PACT

Donor and recipient bacterial strains were inoculated as single colonies into 3 ml of LB medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and incubated overnight at 30°C. The following day, cultures were subcultured at a 2% ratio (60 μl) into fresh 3 ml antibiotic-containing LB medium. For recipient cells, the medium was further supplemented with 10 mM L-arabinose as an inducer. The subculuture were followed by incubation at 30°C with shaking for 3.5 h. After incubation, cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with 1 ml fresh LB medium, and resuspended in 100 μl LB. Serial dilutions (ranging from 10−5 to 10−7) of the recipient bacterial culture were plated on LB agar supplemented with chloramphenicol. The colonies that grew on these plates were counted to determine the total recipient bacterial population. A conjugation mixture was prepared by combining 30 μl of recipient with 30 μl of donor (Donor/Recipient = 1:1) in a 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tube. To assess the impact of donor-to-recipient ratios on conjugation efficiency, recipient cells were maintained at a fixed volume of 30 μl, while donor volumes were systematically varied as follows: 10 μl (0.33:1), 15 μl (0.5:1), 30 μl (1:1), 60 μl (2:1), and 90 μl (3:1). Aliquots of the mixture were spotted onto LB agar plates in 5–8 discrete droplets and incubated at 30°C for 2 h. Bacterial cells were subsequently resuspended by gently rinsing the agar surface with 1 ml of antibiotic-free LB medium. Serial dilutions (10- and 100-fold) of the resuspended cells were prepared, and 50 μl aliquots were plated onto selective LB agar plates. Transconjugants were enumerated after 16–20 h of incubation at 30°C. Violet colonies represented the correct assembly. The assembly efficiency was calculated as follows: Assembly efficiency = (Correct assembly count) / (Total recipient bacterial population).

Illumina sequencing of digital DNA assemblies

Twenty four Digital DNA assemblies and 1 mix DNA of donors (as control) were carried out for Illumina Sequencing. Each library was constructed from 200 ng DNA. Initial fragmentation via sonication yielded 350 bp length of fragments. These fragments underwent end repair, dA-tailing, and ligation with Illumina compatible adapters. Size selection and PCR enrichment preceded purification using the AMPure XP system (Beverly, USA). Library quality control was performed on the Agilent 5400 (Agilent, USA) with quantification via qPCR (final concentration: 1.5 nM). Libraries meeting quality thresholds were proportionally pooled according to effective concentration and sequenced on Illumina platforms (PE150). Quality-filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA v0.7.17-r1188 [53] (parameter: mem -t 4 -k 32 -M), generating primary BAM files. PCR duplicates were removed using SAMtools [54] (parameter: rmdup). Variant discovery was conducted with GATK v4.0.5.1 [55] (HaplotypeCaller --pair-hmm-gap-continuation-penalty 10 --genotyping-mode DISCOVERY -stand-call-conf 30). Raw variants were filtered using these thresholds:

SNPs: QD < 2.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQ < 30.0 || HaplotypeScore > 13.0 || MappingQualityRankSum < −12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < −8.0.

InDels: QD < 2.0 || FS > 200.0 || ReadPosRankSum < −20.0.

Analysis of the sequence features of organellar genomes

Sequence features of A. thaliana mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes were examined using previous method [56]. Briefly, to detect interspersed repeats, the construct sequence underwent self-alignment via BLAST (v2.16.0) blastn, configured as follows: -word_size 6, -reward 1, -penalty -1, -gapopen 3, -gapextend 2, -evalue 5, -perc_identity 70. GC content variation was assessed over a 51 bp sliding window to pinpoint regions with extreme values. Potential secondary structures were evaluated using the primer3-py package (v2.0.3) for Python, utilizing its default parameters but setting the hairpin_window_size to 41.

Construction of donor plasmids

The donor plasmids were assembled via S. cerevisiae in vivo homologous recombination [38]. Overlapping DNA fragments were amplified by PCR using Arabidopsis genomic DNA as the template and primers listed in Supplementary Table S2. To enhance plasmid stability in yeast, 1–2 autonomously replicating sequences (ARSs) were incorporated into each donor plasmid, distributing 9 ARSs across mitochondria genome and 5 across chloroplast genome (Supplementary Table S3). The vector backbones were also generated through PCR. Subsequently, the purified donor fragments (100 ng each) and corresponding vector backbones (100 ng each) were mixed and co-transformed into S. cerevisiae protoplasts [38]. Homologous recombination (driven by > 60 bp overlapping sequences between fragments and vectors) facilitated the assembly of the DNA fragments into the donor plasmids. Finally, transformed yeast colonies were selected on synthetic dropout media, and correct colonies were screened for validation via PCR.

The yeast assembled donor plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH10B for amplification and PFGE analysis

Yeast cultures were grown overnight in 3 ml selective medium, followed by cell harvesting via centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min. Cell walls were enzymatically digested using 1 ml zymolyase-20T buffer (20 μg/ml zymolyase-20T, 1 M sorbitol, 10 mM sodium phosphate/EDTA, pH 7.5) at 30°C for 60 min. Plasmid DNA was subsequently extracted using the HiPure Plasmid/BAC EF Kit (Magen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Five microliters of purified plasmids were electroporated into E. coli DH10B using Micropulser system (Bio-Rad). Transformed cells were plated on LB agar containing 34 μg/ml chloramphenicol and incubated at 37°C for 16 h. Single colonies were selected for plasmid propagation in LB medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (34 μg/ml), and bacterial plasmids were re-extracted using the HiPure Plasmid/BAC EF Kit. For structural validation, 50 ng of each plasmid was digested with NotI at 37°C for 30 min and analyzed by PFGE using a CHEF Mapper XA System (Bio-Rad). Separation was performed in 1% agarose gels at 14°C with 6 V/cm for 16 hr, employing switch times from 0.5 to 1.5 s.

Bacterial agarose plug preparation and PFGE analysis

Bacterial cells were harvested from 0.5 ml overnight cultures by centrifugation at 10 000 rpm for 1 min. The cell pellet was sequentially washed with 1 ml of ddH2O and 1 ml of 50 mM EDTA (pH 8.0). Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in 500 μl of cell resuspension buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.2), centrifuged again, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 μl of the same buffer. The suspension was equilibrated at 50°C for 5 min. Next, 100 μl of pre-equilibrated (50°C) 2% low-melting agarose in cell resuspension buffer was added. The mixture was gently vortexed, transferred into plug molds, and solidified on ice or at 4°C for 30 min. After solidification, the agarose plugs were carefully ejected from the plug molds. The plugs were washed once with 1 ml of wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM EDTA). To digest cellular proteins, plugs were incubated in 1 ml of Proteinase K reaction buffer (100 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 0.2% sodium deoxycholate, 1% sodium lauryl sarcosine, 1 mg/ml Proteinase K) at 50°C overnight. Residual protease activity was neutralized by washing the plugs four times with 1 ml of wash buffer (30–60 min per wash, room temperature with gentle agitation). Processed plugs may be stored in wash buffer at 4°C for several months.

About 1/4 yeast agarose plug was performed PFGE analysis by Bio-Rad CHEF MAPPER system in a 1% agarose gel using 0.5× TBE buffer with circulation at 14°C. The procedures were run for 20 h at 6.0 V/cm. Switch times were optimized based on DNA fragment size: 1 to 25 s for groups syn_athC01-syn_athC05 and 10 s to 60 s for groups syn_athM01-07 and WT_athM01-09.

Linear DNA purification for sequencing

The linear DNA of syn_athC05 and syn_athM07 was purified prior to sequencing. Briefly, bacterial agarose plugs were prepared as mentioned above. Then large linear DNA molecules were separated from circular genomic DNA via PFGE, and the linear DNA was run into low melting agarose gel (Bio-rad, #1613111). The linear DNA fraction was excised from the low-melting-agarose gel and digested with β-agarase I (NEB, #M0392) to release linear DNA. The DNA was recovered according to a standard DNA recovery procedure (https://www.neb.cn/zh-cn/protocols/dna-purification-from-agarose-gels-using-beta-agarase-i-m0392).

Result

Design and mechanism of PACT

For a DNA assembly method to be compatible with automation, it must combine simplicity, efficiency, and robustness. Previous studies have applied linear vectors for DNA cloning [49, 50]. PACT builds on this concept by employing a linearized vector system stabilized by the phage N15 telomere system (telN/tosRL) to maintain plasmid integrity in recipient cells [48, 57]. Donor DNA fragments are carried on circular plasmids containing an origin of transfer for bacterial conjugation (oriT), a single guide-RNA expression cassette for Cas9 (sgRNA), sgRNA target site (ST), a spectinomycin resistance marker (aadA), and the tosRL telomere-forming site. These are all essential DNA parts for PACT (Fig. 1A). The assembly proceeds via three key steps (Fig. 1B): (i) donor plasmids conjugatively transfer into recipient cells, where telN protelomerase converts tosRL into telR and telL ends, generating linear DNA; (ii) Cas9, guided by sgRNA, cleaves both donor and recipient vectors at ST sites; (iii) λRED recombinase, induced by arabinose, mediates a single homologous recombination event, integrating donor DNA into the recipient’s linear vector. This entirely in vivo process requires only bacterial culturing, conjugation and serial diluted plating, bypassing DNA extraction, digestion, and purification (Fig. 1C).

Enhanced assembly efficiency and large DNA assembly capability

To evaluate assembly efficiency, we used the violacein biosynthetic pathway as a reporter (VibiR system), splitting the pathway into two fragments (2923 and 2087 bp), and placing them separately in donor and recipient vectors (Fig. 2A). Violet colony formation indicated successful assembly. Assembly efficiency increased with conjugation time (Supplementary Fig. S1), with a 2-h duration selected for subsequent experiments. Using E. coli MG1655 (RecA+) as the recipient enhanced efficiency by 3.9-fold compared with RecA-deficient DH10B [E. coli MG1655 with assembly efficiency of (1.85 ± 0.12)*10−2, while E. coli DH10B of (4.80 ± 0.45)*10−3] (Fig. 2B), highlighting the contribution of homologous recombination.

The value displayed above each column corresponds to the assembly efficiency, which was calculated by dividing the violet colony count by the total recipient bacterial population [(correct assembly count) / (total recipient bacterial population)]. Unlike conventional circular vectors that require double-crossover events, PACT enables single-crossover integration, thereby markedly improving efficiency. Compared with a circular vector control (cir-PACT) (Supplementary Fig. S2), PACT achieved ~30-fold higher efficiency [(6.11 ± 0.46) × 10−4] versus [(1.85 ± 0.12) × 10−2)] (Fig. 2B), demonstrating its ability to combine high efficiency with high accuracy.

We next examined the impact of donor-to-recipient ratios on conjugation and assembly efficiency. By fixing the number of recipient cells and varying donor input, we observed no significant difference in violet colony counts at ratios of 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1 (Fig. 2C). This suggests that donor input is flexible as long as it is not lower than recipient levels, simplifying practical implementation.

To test PACT’s capacity for large-fragment assembly, we constructed donor vectors carrying inserts of 23.4, 43.4, 63.4, and 83.4 kb. As expected, efficiency declined with increasing DNA fragment length, but sufficient colonies were consistently obtained (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Correct assemblies were confirmed by PFGE (Supplementary Fig. S3C–F). For the largest donor (83.4 kb), PACT achieved an efficiency of (2.60 ± 0.10) × 10−4 (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S3F).

Taken together, these results establish that PACT offers simple operation, high fidelity, and enhanced efficiency, while remaining capable of assembling large DNA fragments up to at least 83 kb in a single step.

High-fidelity iterative DNA assembly with PACT

We next investigated whether PACT could support iterative DNA assembly. Toward this end, we engineered four donor vectors with distinct sgRNA-Marker cassettes (Fig. 3A). The donor DNA bricks can be assembled into the recipient vector in proper order by 100–500 bp overlaps. The resulting constructs could serve as new recipients for subsequent rounds, enabling sequential integration of multiple fragments into a recipient vector (Fig. 3B). Using advanced VibiR assays, violacein pathways split into three to five fragments (Supplementary Fig. S4) were successfully assembled in two to four consecutive cycles (Fig. 3C–E), with efficiencies comparable to one-step PACT. To further validate iterative assembly, we tested a 210 kb digital DNA sequence (digital DNA) [58, 59] (Fig. 3F and G), divided into four donors (80, 40, 40, and 40 kb). Sequential assembly yielded high-fidelity constructs. Among 24 sequenced clones, 15 had correct sequences (15/24). Three types of SNP appeared in three assembled fragments, and the same type of indel occurred in the other six assembled fragments (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Table S5). These results establish that iterative PACT supports iterative assembly of large DNA with both high efficiency and fidelity, despite the usage of MG1655 (RecA+) as the recipient cells and employed Cas9 cleavage.

Assembly of wide type A. thaliana mitochondrial genome with high repeat content

Based on the validation that PACT enables the assembly of large DNA fragments, we hypothesized that combining PACT with conventional assembly methods—such as in vivo recombination in yeast—could be used to rapidly generate donor DNA, which could then be assembled into fragments of several hundred kilobases via PACT (Fig. 4A). To test this hypothesis, we selected the Arabidopsis mitochondrial genome as our assembly target. With a length of 367 808 bp and containing 109 repeat units ranging from 50 to 6590 bp (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S6), this genome presents a major challenge for assembly, as these repeats are prone to erroneous recombination during DNA assembly. We reasoned that although using the RecA-deficient E. coli strain DH10B as the host for PACT may reduce assembly efficiency, its low recombination activity could enhance the stability of DNA containing repetitive sequences. Based on this premise, we divided the mitochondrial genome into 9 donor DNA fragments (Fig. 4B), each rapidly constructed via PCR amplification and yeast in vivo recombination. Using DH10B as the host strain, we iteratively assembled the complete Arabidopsis mitochondrial genome via PACT, followed by PFGE analysis (Fig. 4C). The positive assembly rate for each round exceeded 50% (ranging from 14/24 to 8/8) (Supplementary Table S7), except for the second Donor-MF2 assembly, which dropped to 1/8. Subsequent sequencing revealed that this region was prone to be swapped by a transposon element originating from the DH10B genome (Fig. 4D), with this insertion event observed in all six sequenced assemblies. Despite this, the DH10B-hosted assembly demonstrated greater stability than that using E. coli MG1655 as the host, where the assembled mitochondrial genome exhibited structural instability and DNA deletions (Supplementary Fig. S6). These results demonstrate that PACT can utilize low-recombination E. coli strains such as DH10B as hosts to efficiently assemble DNA fragments with high repeat content.

Automated workflow of PACT (aPACT)

TJU-DNAFab is a platform aiming to rapid, high-throughput assembly of large DNA (Supplementary Fig. S7A). It integrates automated pipetting, cell culture, plating, film sealing and removing by a robotic manipulator (MEGAROBO PF400). The simplicity and iterative nature of PACT make it readily compatible with automation. We therefore integrated PACT into the TJU-DNAFab robotic platform, establishing aPACT, a high-throughput, unattended workflow (Fig. 5A). The system handles pipetting, cell culture, conjugation, and plating in 96-well and multi-well plate formats. Each well pair serves as an independent assembly reaction, enabling dozens of parallel experiments (Fig. 5B). Using VibiR assays, aPACT performed 48 independent assemblies in a single run, yielding consistent efficiencies (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Large DNA constructs (60 and 80 kb) were also assembled with ~100 colonies per reaction. Increasing arabinose induction time did not further enhance efficiency, indicating robust system performance under standard conditions (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. S7C, and Supplementary Table S8). Together, these results demonstrate that aPACT enables automated assembly of large DNA fragments in parallel and at scale.

Assembly of Arabidopsis thaliana chloroplast and mitochondrial genome by aPACT

To further validate the capability of aPACT for assembly of large DNA, we first reassembled the 210 kb digital DNA using aPACT (Supplementary Fig. S8A). Three parallel plate-wells were set up for each assembly cycle. The results showed a high number of colonies and a high positive rate in each assembly cycle (Supplementary Fig. S8B), demonstrating the feasibility of aPACT for large DNA assembly.

To showcase the potential application of aPACT, we applied it to the assembly of A. thaliana mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, which were slightly redesigned to distinguish with wide type. For the design of the chloroplast genome, 21 introns and 2 large sequences in inverted repeat regions were deleted. Meanwhile, the stop codons of 32 genes were replaced with TAA (Supplementary Fig. S8C and Supplementary Table S9). For the design of the mitochondrial genome, 49 repeat elements were deleted and 9 ARS sequences were added as watermarks (Supplementary Fig. S8D and Supplementary Table S3). Although we simplified the sequences, their sequences remained more complex than the digital DNA, due to the high repetitive content, secondary structures, and extreme GC variation (Fig. 6A).

For the chloroplast genome (120 kb), five donor fragments ranging in size from 18 to 25 kb (Fig. 6B) were sequentially assembled over five cycles, yielding high-efficiency assemblies across multiple replicates (Fig. 6C and D, Supplementary Fig. S8E, and Supplementary Table S10). Ultimately, the complete 350 kb mitochondrial genome was successfully assembled (Fig. 6G, Supplementary Fig. S8F, and Supplementary Table S10). These results demonstrated that aPACT can assemble highly complex, repeat-rich eukaryotic organellar genomes, reaching lengths of several hundred kilobase-pairs, and extending its applicability beyond artificial constructs to biologically relevant large DNA molecules.

Discussion

PACT combines linear vector technology, bacterial conjugation, Cas9, and the λRed recombination system, endowing DNA assembly with the following advantages. (i) The use of linear vectors enables DNA assembly through only one single crossover. In contrast, traditional methods using circular vectors require double crossovers for DNA assembly. This design enhances the assembly efficiency by 30-fold. (ii) PACT does not rely on RecA. As a result, PACT can use low-recombination E. coli strains as hosts to efficiently assemble DNA fragments with high repeat content. (iii) PACT DNA assembly does not require long homology arms (100–500 bp). This is particularly beneficial for de novo genome synthesis projects, as it minimizes the wasteful DNA synthesis associated with creating long homology arms, thereby reducing both cost and overall assembly time. (iv) By eliminating labor-intensive in vitro steps (e.g. DNA extraction, enzymatic digestion, purification) and bypassing the need for protoplast preparation or specialized competent cells, aPACT achieves compatibility with automation platforms. When integrated with robotic systems, aPACT supports the continuous assembly of DNA constructs exceeding 350 kb, representing a significant improvement over previously reported automated methods. We have systematically benchmarked PACT against similar existing DNA assembly methods to delineate its distinct features within the current technological landscape (summarized in Table 1).

Despite these advances, aPACT has several limitations. First, unlike methods such as Gibson assembly, CasHRA[60] or yeast in vivo recombination, it cannot yet assemble multiple DNA fragments simultaneously in a single reaction. Our preliminary attempts to assemble three fragments (one recipient plus two donors) resulted in a sharp decline in efficiency (data not shown), underscoring the current limitations of multi-fragment assembly in automated workflows. Second, we also applied PACT to assemble the A. thaliana chloroplast genome (~154.5 kb). Although smaller in size, assembly was unsuccessful due to the presence of a pair of large (~26 kb) inverted repeats. This outcome identifies a current limitation of the PACT method in handling large inverted repeat structures. Third, while this study demonstrated the successful parallel assembly of 48 constructs in a single batch, large-scale industrial production experiments remain to be validated. Further optimization of throughput and error correction mechanisms will be critical for future applications.

In addition, aPACT has not yet achieved fully end-to-end genome synthesis and assembly from short oligonucleotides to megabase-scale constructs. To realize full automation, several key challenges remain: (i) Integration of DNA sequence design and decomposition software; (ii) Automated assembly of medium-length donor DNA fragments; (iii) Automated validation of long DNA constructs. In the present work, the design and decomposition of organelle genome still rely on traditional biological design software (e.g. SnapGene), with significant reliance on manual operations and empirical judgment. Notably, artificial intelligence (AI) has already enabled the creation of AI-designed viruses [61]. Leveraging AI to automate long DNA sequence design and decomposition represents a promising approach to address this gap. The preparation of donor DNA fragments still relies on conventional methods such as Gibson assembly or yeast recombination. Some other simple methods may be applicable to automated platform for the rapid assembly of 40–50 kb DNA. For example, the iPac technology (in vitro Packaging-assisted DNA assembly) is a straightforward approach that utilizes the bacteriophage in vitro packaging system to construct ~40–50 kb long DNA from five to ten DNA fragments [62]. Regarding the automated validation of long DNA fragments, while electrophoresis has been instrumented (e.g. Bio-Rad’s CHEF Mapper XA System; BioOptic’s Qsep Series), the cumbersome sample preparation steps prior to electrophoresis and the lack of integration with upstream DNA to full automation. Assembly processes remain major hurdles. Taken together, fully end-to-end autonomous genome synthesis and assembly still require extensive further research to overcome these interconnected challenges.
