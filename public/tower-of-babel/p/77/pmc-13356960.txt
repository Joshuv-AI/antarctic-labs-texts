US race/ethnic disparities in diabetes technologies: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 950 000 individuals
Authors: Maria L M Rego, Maxim Barnett, Maria Helena Siqueira Tavares de Melo, Ana Rivadeneira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo provide a descriptive summary and to quantitatively examine racial and ethnic disparities in the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) among people living with diabetes in the United States.Research Design and MethodsWe followed the Cochrane and PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD420251124678). Search was conducted on PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane up to August 2025. Observational studies were included if they (1) were conducted in the United States, (2) evaluated the use of CGM/CSII across different races/ethnic groups, and (3) reported odds ratios (ORs) for between-group comparisons. Three-level random-effect meta-analyses examined OR in the use of CGM/CSII across different groups: Non-Hispanic White (NHW; reference group) vs Black/African American, Hispanic, Asian, and Other. Subgroup analyses were conducted for children vs adults, and type 1 vs type 2 diabetes.ResultsTotally, 1737 studies were screened, and 18 studies were included (N = 955 556 individuals). Most participants were NHW (70.9%). Approximately 40% of individuals had type 2 diabetes, and 98.9% were adults. Black/African Americans (OR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.38-0.60), Hispanics (OR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.50-0.69), and “Other” (OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.58-0.83) were less likely to use CGM, relative to NHW individuals, while Asians were equally likely. Black/African Americans (OR = 0.31, 95% CI = 0.23-0.42) and Hispanics (OR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.42-0.55) were less likely to use a CSII when compared to NHW. No analysis was conducted for “Asian” or “Other.” Limitations include moderate/substantial heterogeneity and reliance on electronic medical record data.ConclusionRacial and ethnic disparities exist in the use of diabetes technologies. Its recognition is essential to advance solutions that promote equitable care in diabetes.

The American Diabetes Association recommends the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) for individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes requiring insulin [1]. In addition, continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) and automated insulin delivery systems are recognized as the primary chosen strategy to optimize glycemic control [1]. Prior randomized clinical trials comparing CGM use with the conventional multiple daily blood glucose checking and insulin injection for 6 months showed that the CGM use decreased mean HbA1c by 0.3% to 0.6% compared to conventional strategies [2-4]. Some of the studies also showed decreased duration of hypoglycemic episodes [2, 3], and overall treatment satisfaction. In randomized clinical trials, the use of these devices has been associated with improvement in glycemic metrics, including reductions in HbA1c and hypoglycemia and increases in time in range. This, in turn, improves glycemic control, which reduces the risk of diabetes-related complications [5]. Despite these benefits and clear indications, racial and ethnic disparities in access to these technologies have been investigated in the United States. Indeed, recent important publications have highlighted the urgent need to discuss and address CGM and CSII access to mitigate racial and health inequities [6-11]. Persistent disparities in diabetes technology use are influenced not only by financial and insurance barriers but also by structural and implicit biases, educational and system-level factors, and limited clinician and patient support, all of which must be addressed to ensure equitable diabetes care.

Two recent systematic reviews synthesized evidence from approximately 30 studies and endorsed persistent disparities in access to diabetes technologies across racial and ethnic groups, with individuals who are non-Hispanic White (NHW) being more likely to have access to them, relative to other racial and ethnic groups [11, 12]. For example, one of the reviews, involving 36 articles, revealed several factors related to disparities in diabetes technology, including race/ethnicity, insurance type, and some demographic characteristics, such as income, education, age, and language [11]. These reviews were important in searching for potential factors related to health inequity in the United States, which is essential to guide future research. However, despite identifying these gaps and stimulating discussion, a quantitative synthesis summarizing these findings remains unexplored. Conducting a quantitative analysis on this issue is critical as it will allow for a more precise and objective understanding of the magnitude of disparities, thus informing strategies to promote equitable access and establish a baseline for future evaluation.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to provide (1) a descriptive summary of the current literature and (2) a quantitative investigation of racial and ethnic disparities in the use of diabetes technologies (ie, CGM and CSII) among people living with diabetes in the United States. Importantly, this study aligns with the US Department of Health and Human Services’ Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, which has emphasized as a central priority in the Healthy People 2030 initiative the elimination of health disparities and the promotion of equitable opportunities for all individuals to achieve optimal health [13].

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were performed following the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook for Systematic Review of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement guidelines [14, 15]. The present study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251124678).

Data source and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central. The search strategy is detailed in Table S1 [16], and it was run on August 13, 2025. The references from all included studies, previous systematic reviews, and meta-analyses were also searched manually for additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they (1) were observational studies conducted in the United States, (2) included individuals with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, (3) evaluated the initiation or use of CGM devices or CSII across different races and/or ethnic groups, (4) reported odds ratios (ORs) as well as any variance measure (eg, 95% confidence intervals) for between-group comparisons, and (5) were published up to August 11, 2025, without limiting the initial date. Studies were excluded if they were conducted outside the United States, did not report race/ethnicity-specific comparisons, or were case reports, conference abstracts without sufficient data, or reviews.

Data extraction

The search results were exported into Covidence [17] for duplicate removal and study triage. Screening was conducted in 2 stages by 2 authors: first by title and abstract, followed by full-text review to determine final eligibility. After selection, data were extracted into an Excel spreadsheet. To characterize each study, we extracted the author, journal, year of publication, total and by race/ethnicity sample size, type of diabetes technology assessed (CGM and/or CSII), age, time from diabetes diagnosis, OR and 95% CI, and variables included in the adjusted statistical models for each study. If data were missing, we contacted authors to obtain them. If we were unsuccessful, we left the variable with the missing data blank for this study. Two authors (M.R. and M.M.) independently extracted the data following predefined search criteria and quality assessment.

Endpoints/subgroups analyses

The primary outcome of this meta-analysis was the likelihood of initiating or currently using CGM or CSII among individuals with diabetes in the United States. We focused specifically on differences across racial and ethnic groups as defined in the original studies (eg, NHW, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Other). When necessary, mathematical strategies [18] were used to adjust reference groups and to ensure consistency across studies. For example, when non-Hispanic Black was the reference group, it was switched to NHW to be consistent with the majority of studies [19]. The same strategy was done for studies reporting the likelihood of not being on CGM or a CSII; they were transformed to the chance of being on these devices [20, 21]. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether associations differed by population characteristics (ie, children/adolescents vs adult samples) and diabetes type (ie, type 1 vs type 2).

Risk of bias

The methodological quality of eligible trials was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies [22]. Eight questions were completed (yes, no, unclear, and not applicable) for each individual study. The percentage of questions answered as “yes” was calculated, and the study was classified as high risk of bias if 20% to 50% items scored yes, moderate risk of bias if 50% to 80% items scored yes, and low risk of bias if 80% to 100% items scored yes [23]. Two authors completed the risk of bias assessment (M.M. and M.R.) (Table S2 [16]). Disagreements were resolved through a consensus after discussing the reasons for the discrepancy.

Data synthesis and analysis

A 3-level random-effects meta-analysis was performed, with OR as the outcome measure and 95% CI as the variance. This approach is necessary because each study provides multiple comparisons. As such, the 3-level meta-analysis [24] allows variation in effect sizes at the level of participants (level 1), outcomes within studies (level 2), and between studies (level 3). Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test and I2 statistics. If Q was <0.05, heterogeneity was assumed to be present. The I2 was categorized as follows: I2 = 0% to 40% (low heterogeneity), I2 = 30% to 60% (moderate heterogeneity), I2 = 50% to 90% (substantial heterogeneity), and I2 = 75 to 100% (considerable heterogeneity). The statistical significance in level 2 (within-study) and level 3 (between-study) variances was performed using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) with the full model as reference. Statistical significance was set at P-value <.05. In addition, bias was assessed by visually inspecting funnel plots of effect sizes as a function of standard errors and by conducting Egger's regressions [25], where the SE serves as an independent variable, and the effect size serves as the outcome variable. To identify outliers and influential cases, we computed Cook's distances (Di) [26]. Specifically, if Di of a given study was visually distinguished from the other studies, it was considered an influential case. In the presence of outliers, sensitivity analyses were conducted with and without the outlier. All analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.4.3) using the package metafor [27].

Results

Study selection and literature retrieval

The initial search yielded 2307 results. After removal of duplicate records and ineligible studies, 1737 remained and were fully reviewed based on the inclusion criteria. Of these, a total of 18 studies were included [19-21, 28-42], comprising 955 556 individuals (Fig. 1).

Systematic review

Study characteristics and risk of bias

A total of 676 336 (70.9%) patients were NHW; 151 440 (15.9%) were Black/African American; 14 987 (1.6%) were Asian; 57 256 (6%) were classified as “Other,” which included Native American, Pacific Islander, multiracial, and unknown; and 80 861 (8.5%) were Hispanic. Nine studies included only adults [28, 29, 31, 33, 35-37, 41, 42], 7 studies included only children [19, 20, 30, 32, 34, 38, 40], 1 study included both age groups but with statistical analysis separated for each age group [39], and 1 study included both age groups with statistical analysis for all the individuals together [21]. In total, 10 741 (1.1%) children and adolescents and 942 861 (98.9%) adults were included. Most of the studies included only individuals with type 1 diabetes [19-21, 29, 30, 32, 34-42], 3 included type 1 and type 2 diabetes [28, 31, 33], and 1 included only type 2 [42]. Across the studies included, the proportion of participants using CGM or CSII varied by race/ethnicity. Among NHW individuals, the use of these technologies ranged from 1% to over 70%, with most studies clustering between 20% and 60%. Specifically, 3 studies reported 1% to 12%; 4 reported 20% to 30%; 3 reported 30% to 40%; 5 reported 50% to 60%; and 3 reported >70%. For Black/African American participants, technology use was generally lower. Five studies reported ≤10% use; 4 reported 10% to 20%; 3 reported 20% to 30%; another 3 reported 30% to 40%; 2 reported 50% to 60%; and 1 reported >90% use. Among Hispanic participants, technology use was also comparatively limited. Five studies reported <10% use; 4 reported 15% to 30%; 3 reported 30% to 50%; 3 reported 50% to 60%; and 2 reported >70%. For Asian individuals, 1 study reported 10% use; 1 reported 20% use; 2 reported between 20% and 30% use; and 1 reported >80% use. For “Other,” 2 studies reported up to 10% use; 4 reported between 10% and 20% use; 3 reported between 20% and 36% use; and 1 reported >80% use. Risk of bias analysis revealed that 9 studies had a low risk of bias [20, 28, 30-33, 35-37], 8 had a moderate risk of bias [19, 21, 29, 38-42], and 1 has a high risk of bias [34] (Table S2) [16].

Meta-analysis

Likelihood of being on CGM for NHW vs Black/African American individuals

Fourteen studies [19-21, 28, 29, 31, 33, 35, 38-42], comprising 17 effect sizes, examined differences in the likelihood of using CGM between NHW and Black/African American individuals. Kanbour et al [36] compared Black individuals with non-Black individuals, so this study was not included in this analysis. Black/African Americans were significantly less likely to use CGM (OR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.38-0.60, P < .001; Fig. 2A). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (Q(16) = 304.32, P < .001; I2 = 76.22%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within-study variance was statistically significant (LRT = 81.54; P < .001), while the effect of between-study variance was not significant (LRT = 0.55; P = .459). The funnel plot suggests an asymmetrical distribution (Fig. 2B), and Egger's regression test was statistically significant (Z = −2.24, P = .006). Cook's D analysis revealed 1 outlier [35] (Di = 0.23). Sensitivity analysis without the outlier did not change the results (OR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.34-0.58, P < .001).

Likelihood of being on CGM for NHW vs Hispanic individuals

Twelve studies [19-21, 29, 31, 32, 38-42], comprising 14 effect sizes, compared the likelihood of being on CGM between NHW and Hispanic individuals. Lipska et al [33] conducted an analysis comparing Hispanic individuals with non-Hispanic individuals, so it was not included in this analysis. Hispanics individuals were significantly less likely to use CGM (OR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.50-0.69, P < .001; see Fig. 3A). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (Q(13) = 279.51, P < .001; I2 = 65.61%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within-study variance was statistically significant (LRT = 203.97; P < .001), while the effect of between-study variance was not significant (LRT = 0.00; P = 1.00). The funnel plot suggests an asymmetrical distribution (Fig. 3B), although Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = −0.66, P = .41). Cook's D analysis did not reveal any outliers.

Likelihood of being on CGM for NHW vs Asians and “Other” individuals

Six studies [31-33, 35, 41, 42], comprising 7 effect sizes, compared the likelihood of CGM use between NHW and Asian individuals. Asian individuals were equally likely to use CGM, relative to NHW (OR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.76-1.01, P = .064). Low heterogeneity was observed (Q(6) = 44.61, P < .001; I2 = 13.79%) revealed low heterogeneity. The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within-study variance was statistically significant (LRT = 34.12; P < .001), while the effect of between-study variance was not significant (LRT = 0.00; P = 1.00). The funnel plot suggests an asymmetrical distribution, although Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = 0.72, P = .164). Cook's D analysis revealed 1 outlier study with 2 outlier effect sizes [38] (Di = 0.69 and 1.21). Sensitivity analysis without the outliers showed significant differences in CGM use between NHW and Asian individuals, with the latter group less likely to be on CGM (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.77-0.98, P = .024).

Seven studies [28, 29, 31, 33, 40-42], comprising 14 effect sizes, compared the likelihood of CGM use between NHW individuals and those classified as “Other.” Individuals in the “Other” category were significantly less likely to use CGM (OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.58-0.83, P < .001). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (Q(13) = 114.66, P < .001; I2 = 41.55%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within-study variance was statistically significant (LRT = 77.98; P < .001), while the effect of between-study variance was not significant (LRT = 0.00; P = 1.00). The funnel plot suggests a symmetrical distribution and Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = −0.23, P = .63). Cook's D analysis revealed 1 outlier [38] (Di = 2.21). Sensitivity analysis without the outlier did not change the results (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.58-0.85, P < .001).

Likelihood of being on a CSII for NHW vs Black/African American individuals

Seven studies, comprising 8 effect sizes, compared the likelihood of using a CSII between White and Black/African Americans [20, 21, 30, 34, 38, 39]. Kanbour et al conducted the analysis comparing Black individuals with non-Black individuals and was not included in this analysis [36]. Black/African Americans were significantly less likely to use a CSII (OR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.21-0.44, P < .001; Fig. 2C). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (Q(7) = 80.45, P < .001; I2 = 80.36%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within- and between-study variance was not statistically significant (LRT = 1.40; P = .237; LRT = 0.00; P = 1.00, respectively). The funnel plot (Fig. 2D) suggests a symmetrical distribution, and Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = −1.87, P = .37). Cook's D analysis did not reveal any outliers.

Likelihood of being on a CSII for NHW vs Hispanic individuals

Six studies, comprising 9 effect sizes, compared the likelihood of using a CSII between NHW and Hispanics individuals [20, 21, 30, 35, 38, 39]. Hispanic individuals were significantly less likely to use a CSII (OR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.40-0.58, P < .001; Fig. 3C). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (Q(6) = 18.69, P = .005; I2 = 65.21%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within- and between-study variance was not statistically significant (LRT = 0.00; P = 1.00; LRT = 0.94; P = .331, respectively). The funnel plot (Fig. 3D) suggests a symmetrical distribution, and Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = 2.60, P = .320). Cook's D analysis revealed 2 outliers (Di = 0.24 and 0.28) [20, 30]. Sensitivity analysis without the outlier did not change the results (OR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.39-0.58, P < .001).

Likelihood of being on a CSII for NHW vs Asian and “Other” Individuals

Only 2 studies compared the likelihood of using a CSII between non-Hispanic NHW and Asian individuals [30, 35] and between NHW and “Other” individuals [30, 39]. Thus, no meta-analysis was done.

Subgroup analysis

Children and adolescents

Eight studies (1.1% of the total sample) included children and adolescents. Three assessed CGM only [19, 32, 40], 2 assessed CSII only [30, 34], and 3 included both [20, 38, 39]. A subgroup analysis was conducted to examine any diabetes technology use (CGM and/or pump). Across 8 studies, comprising 10 effect sizes, Black/African American youth were significantly less likely to use diabetes technologies, relative to their NHW peers (OR = 0.31, 95% CI = 0.23-0.43, P < .001). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (Q(9) = 29.73, P < .001; I2 = 63.45%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within- and between-study variance was not statistically significant (LRT = 0.15; P = .698; LRT = 1.05; P = .306, respectively). The funnel plot suggests an asymmetrical distribution, although Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = −0.96, P = .40). Cook's D analysis revealed 2 outliers [30, 39] (Di = 0.46 and 0.27). Sensitivity analysis without them did not change the results (OR = 0.32, 95% CI = 0.26-0.40, P < .001).

Across 7 studies [19, 30, 32, 34, 38-40], comprising 9 effect sizes, Hispanics individuals were significantly less likely to use any diabetes device compared to NHW individuals (OR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.42-0.59, P < .001). Heterogeneity was not assumed to be present (Q(7) = 11.50, P = .118; I2 = 30.83%). The LRT tests indicated that the influence of within- and between-study variance was not statistically significant (LRT = 0; P = 1; LRT = 0.77; P = .379, respectively). The funnel plot suggests an asymmetrical distribution, although Egger's regression test was not statistically significant (Z = −0.42, P = .697). Cook's D analysis revealed 1 outlier study with 2 outlier effect sizes [16] (Di = 0.62 and 0.34). Sensitivity analysis without the outliers did not change results (OR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.48-65, P < .001). Only 2 studies included a comparison between NHW and Asian individuals, as well as NHW and “Other” race/ethnicity children and adolescents. Thus, no meta-analysis was conducted for these groups.

Adults

Subgroup analysis for the adults was done separately for CGM and CSII. Across 8 studies [28, 29, 31, 33, 35, 39, 41, 42], comprising 10 effect sizes, Black/African Americans were less likely to use CGM compared to NHW individuals (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.53-0.75, P < .001, I2 = 70.6%). Across 6 studies [29, 31, 35, 39, 41, 42], comprising 7 effect sizes, Hispanics individuals were less likely to use CGM compared to NHW (OR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.52-0.81, P < .001, I2 = 89.29%). Across 5 studies [31, 33, 35, 41, 42], comprising 6 effect sizes, Asian and NHW adults had the same likelihood to use CGM (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.76-1, P = .054, I2 = 29.86%). Finally, across 7 studies [28, 29, 31, 33, 39, 41, 42], comprising 11 effect sizes, those in the “Other” category were less likely to be using CGM (OR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.51-0.83, P < .001, I2 = 44.21%).

For CSII use, comparisons with NHW were limited to 2 studies for Black/African American and for Hispanic groups and to 1 study for each Asian and “Other” group. Thus, an analysis was done comparing NHW with non-NHW individuals. Across 3 studies [35, 36, 39], comprising 7 effect sizes, those in the non-NHW group were less likely to use CSII (OR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.41-0.64, P < .001, I2 = 61.35.22%).

Subgroup analysis: type 1 and type 2 diabetes

For CGM use among individuals with type 1 diabetes and across 12 studies [19-21, 28, 29, 32, 33, 35, 38-41], comprising 13 effect sizes, Black/African Americans were less likely to be on CGM compared to NHW (OR = 0.40, 95% CI = 0.32-0.51, P < .001, I2 = 65.53%). Across 10 studies [19-21, 29, 32, 35, 38-41], comprising 11 effect sizes, Hispanics individuals were less likely to be on CGM compared to NHW (OR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.45-0.66, P < .001, I2 = 56.1%%). Two studies were removed from the Hispanic analysis because the reference group for comparison was “not Hispanics” and could not be classified as NHW [28, 33]. Across 3 studies, comprising 3 effect sizes [32, 35, 41], Asian individuals were less likely to be on CGM than NHW individuals (OR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.68-0.98, P = .032, I2 = 0.00). Only 2 studies evaluated “Other” and NHW, so no analysis was conducted. For CSII use, no studies included type 2 diabetes; thus, there was no subgroup analysis based on diabetes type for CSII use. The main results include only type 1 as no study included type 2 diabetes in their sample.

For CGM use among individuals with type 2 diabetes, only 3 studies were included [28, 33, 42]. A meta-analysis was conducted comparing NHW with non-NHW group. Individuals in the non-NHW category were less likely to use CGM, relative to NHW (OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.57-0.83, P < .001, I2 = 91.67%).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to quantitatively analyze the racial and ethnic disparities in the use of diabetes technologies in the United States. Data from 18 studies and 953 602 individuals showed that Black/African American, Hispanic, and “Other” races had lower odds of using CGM, relative to NHW. Similarly, Black/African American and Hispanic individuals were less likely to use a CSII, relative to NHW. Sensitivity analyses showed that results remain unchanged after excluding outliers, except for CGM use among the Asian group compared to NHW, which showed that Asian individuals are less likely to be on CGM compared to NHW. Subgroup analyses revealed similar disparity patterns among children and adolescents, and across type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

These findings are critical because one of the key advantages of conducting a quantitative analysis, compared with a purely systematic review, is the ability to quantify the magnitude of an observed issue. By using quantitative measures, differences in diabetes technology use between groups can be directly compared and expressed in measurable terms (in our case, OR). The present meta-analysis not only highlights the existence of disparities in diabetes device use, as previous systematic reviews have done [11, 12], but also quantifies the magnitude of these differences. Quantitative estimates are extremely valuable in public health, as they allow interventions and strategies to be evaluated and compared over time, thereby providing an important reference point for future research and policy efforts. This framework aligns with prior reviews that underscore the importance of creating a baseline picture of the racial/ethnic disparities scenario regarding diabetes technology use as a key to offer insight for quality improvement initiatives [10, 11]. Importantly, these ideas align closely with the focus of the US Department of Health and Human Services and the Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion of the United States, in promoting health to minorities [13].

Our findings align with previous reviews and large population data studies reporting racial and ethnic inequities in diabetes technology access. Among several factors linked to disparities in CGM and CSII in the United States, race and ethnicity were the most studied ones and affect both diabetes types [11]. A recent systematic review of 23 studies, including large cohorts from the United States and the United Kingdom, evaluated racial and ethnic disparities and found that NHW individuals had more access to diabetes technologies, compared to Hispanic and Black individuals [12]. Subsequently, this same study found that disparities were greater among older individuals compared to younger ones. Our findings are in accordance with these results, supporting racial and ethnic disparities in the use of CGM and CSII, but differ in that children/adolescents had the largest disparities in our age subgroup analyses. This might be explained by other variables such as parental involvement in device management and provider prescribing practices, but this is beyond the scope of this study.

Although a systematic review [11] examining US disparities in diabetes technologies (n = 36 studies) highlighted the significant amount of studies examining race/ethnicity disparities, compared to other disparities (eg, health insurance)—thus justifying the present study—the disparities in diabetes technologies have several contributors. For example, the use of these devices is associated with their cost, provider education, familiarity with them, coverage insurance policies, perceived discrimination, and stigma [6, 10], as well as age, gender, income, and education [10]. This is important because the same individual is subject to different types of inequities, and understanding the personal, healthcare, geographic, economic, educational, and social aspects that can interfere with their care is the beginning of understanding the tools and support required by the patient to ensure optimal care for patients [10]. These social and demographic determinants of health are associated with diabetes-related outcomes and may play a role in conjunction with the pillar of race and ethnicity [43]. Moreover, it is important to recognize that long-standing disparities contribute to the root of racial and ethnic disparities, such as marginalized identities, living in a deprived area, socioeconomic status, and health insurance access [10], hence the importance of the present study.

Future directions should include research focused on deeply understanding why different races/ethnicities affect accessibility to diabetes devices. In other words, clinicians, researchers, and patients would benefit from understanding if this is a structural problem, including insurance type and offer, or biases from prescribers who are not offering access to these devices. Perhaps it might even be a combination of both, such that it places marginalized groups from racial/ethnical minorities to be perceived as having less access to digital literacy and/or having less opportunity to comprehend how to manage diabetes and how to benefit from these tools. In addition, virtual strategies, such as the Onduo Virtual Diabetes Clinic [44], showed improvement in diabetes care, especially for those using intermittent CGM. This could be a strategy to increase education, access, and benefits for diabetes technologies across different racial and ethnic groups, especially if done not only individually but also in group sessions, including people who can culturally relate to each other, which can help with the adaptation and adherence to these devices [10]. Furthermore, having a diverse healthcare team can facilitate communication and effective education, promoting better adherence and diabetes control [10]. In addition, using protocols that standardize the steps to interpret and monitor blood sugar and make it available, online or through download, may facilitate the use of the devices and simplify their use by the patients [45, 46]. Finally, an evaluation over time of these disparities would reveal whether this is a worsening, improving, or unchanged trend. Acknowledging this pattern is valuable to question actions that might be helpful or might be required to promote health equity. Future studies for the following years might be able to deeply investigate this trend over time.

Limitations

Despite the large sample size and subgroup analyses, this study has some limitations. First, heterogeneity was moderate to substantial, reflecting variation among the studies. Second, most studies relied on electronic medical records, which are subject to misclassification and/or inaccuracy. Third, race/ethnicity categories were not uniform, and using a broad category as “Other” could have masked within-group differences. Fourth, age disparities could be related to type 1 or type 2 distribution; however, the limited amount of studies for each subgroup (eg, Asian adults with type 1 diabetes) limited the feasibility of this subgroup analysis. Fifth, most of the included studies in this meta-analysis were not nationally representative of the US population, and many of them were restricted to a small geographical area or health system (such as local hospitals). Although many studies adjusted for multiple covariates, the reported covariates were not consistent across all studies, leaving residual confounding and limiting the generalization of the results. Finally, studies included in this review were conducted between 2018 and 2024, representing a narrow and contemporary time frame within the current generation of diabetes technologies. As such, meaningful assessment of temporal trends in disparities was not feasible.

Conclusion

Addressing this problem remains a challenge. Establishing its existence, as many previous studies have done, is helpful, but gathering previous data in a quantitative analysis is an important step to have a better picture of the problem and its magnitude. This allows discussion for interventions and guides where actions need to be prioritized. Clearly, this problem is multifactorial, and evaluating other potential associated factors is essential to promote healthy equity. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence on racial and ethnic disparities in access to CGM and CSII in the United States. Overall, we found that 9 studies were of low risk of bias, 8 were of moderate risk of bias, and 1 was at high risk of bias. Although indications for these devices are present in guidelines and overall access is increasing, being from a race group other than NHW and non-Hispanic ethnicity decreases the likelihood of being on these devices.
