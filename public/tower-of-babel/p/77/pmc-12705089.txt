Real-World Outcomes of 3-Month Olfactory Training on Post-COVID-19 Parosmia
Authors: Xinni Xu, Kristóf Peter Ludányi, Jerry Hadi Juratli, Fruzsina Németh, Dávid Nagy, Helga Kraxner, Thomas Hummel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-COVID-19 parosmia can be protracted and psychologically distressing. The effect of olfactory training (OT) on parosmia is still unclear. The primary objective was to investigate the effect of 3 months of OT on COVID-19-related parosmia. The secondary objectives were to study whether improvement in olfactory quantitative function and compliance to OT influenced improvement in parosmia.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted from October 5, 2022, to February 7, 2023, at a tertiary smell and taste clinic. Patients with persistent post-COVID-19 parosmia were instructed to perform OT twice daily for 3 months. Improvement in parosmia was measured using self-rated intensity and pleasantness scales, a structured parosmia questionnaire, and the Sniffin’ Sticks Parosmia Test (which measures hedonic direction and range). Quantitative olfactory function was measured with Sniffin’ Sticks to determine the threshold, discrimination, and identification (TDI) score. Pre- and post-OT assessment of parosmia and quantitative olfactory function was performed. Compliance to OT was calculated as a percentage of the number of OT sessions performed over the expected number of training sessions over 3 months.ResultsThirty-eight patients (mean age 41.8 [±13.3] years, 12 [31.6%] males) were recruited. After 3 months, 52.6% (n = 20) reported perceived improvement in parosmia. There was improvement in perceived pleasantness (−2.0 [−3.0 to −1.0] vs. −3.0 [−4.0 to −2.0], p = 0.027), hedonic range (2.4 [±1.3] vs. 1.9 [±1.6], p = 0.023), and TDI (27.9 [±7.4] vs. 25.2 [±7.2], p = 0.001) from baseline. All measures of parosmia did not differ significantly between patients who achieved the minimum clinically important difference of ≥5.5 in TDI scores versus those who did not. Patients who had >70% compliance to OT displayed more significant improvements in hedonic range (1.1 [±1.1] vs. 0.2 [±1.3] p = 0.04) compared to those who were less compliant.ConclusionCOVID-19-related parosmia and olfactory dysfunction improved with OT over 3 months. Improvement in olfactory function does not necessarily improve parosmia perception. However, compliance with OT appears to have an effect on improvement in parosmia.

Introduction

Quantitative olfactory loss is a well-known hallmark of the SARS-CoV-2 virus responsible for the COVID-19 disease [1]. Parosmia, a qualitative olfactory dysfunction in which distorted odor perception occurs in the presence of an odor [2], is also a common sequelae of COVID-19, with a prevalence rate as high as 40% [3]. It may also appear after a period of apparent olfactory recovery [4].

The mismatch between the memory of an odor and the actual odor experience in parosmia can be psychologically distressing, especially as most of the odor distortions tend to be unpleasant [5]. Parosmia can impact eating habits, sense of well-being, and overall quality of life [6, 7]. While some patients notice an improvement in parosmia at 6 months after its onset [8], others may continue to experience it well beyond 2 years of contracting COVID-19 [9].

Managing patients suffering from parosmia has some challenges. The first is that evidence-based treatment options for parosmia are limited. Given that parosmia frequently occurs together with quantitative olfactory dysfunction, treatment of parosmia is typically in line with that of quantitative olfactory loss [10]. Olfactory training (OT) has been shown to be effective in treating COVID-19-related olfactory disorders [11]. However, the effect of OT on COVID-19-related parosmia is unclear. The second difficulty is in measuring and tracking changes in parosmia. As parosmia is a qualitative problem and parosmia patients are likely to have some degree of preserved olfactory function, measuring threshold and recognition alone is insufficient when it comes to olfactory assessment [12]. Subjective assessments are important in parosmia. This can be in the form of patient self-assessments, but they are subject to different biases. There are limited psychophysical tools to measure qualitative olfactory perception. The SSParoT is one of them [13], although it is not yet widely adopted in clinical settings. More objective measurements of parosmia, such as using resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging, have not been established and are mainly for research purposes [14].

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the effect of 3 months of OT on COVID-19-related parosmia in a real-world setting using current instruments of parosmia assessment. The secondary objectives were to study whether improvement in olfactory function and compliance to OT influenced improvement in parosmia perception.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This was a prospective cohort study conducted at the tertiary Smell and Taste Clinic at Semmelweis University Department of Otorhinolaryngology and Head and Neck Surgery from 5 October 2022 to 7 February 2023. Consecutive patients with parosmia were identified and instructed to undergo OT for 3 months. Parosmia was defined as a qualitative olfactory dysfunction, whereby there was distorted perception of one or more odor stimuli [10]. The inclusion criteria were individuals of 18 years and older, COVID-19 diagnosis proven on COVID-19 antigen tests or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests, and onset of parosmia after contracting COVID-19. Patients with parosmia from other causes, such as Parkinson’s disease and idiopathic parosmia, were excluded. All patients underwent parosmia and olfactory assessments before starting OT and at a 3-month follow-up. We used the STROBE reporting guideline [15] to draft this manuscript, and the STROBE reporting checklist [16] when editing, included in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549019).

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures were improvement in parosmia-related parameters: subjective improvement, intensity and pleasantness of the parosmia, hedonic range, and hedonic direction. Secondary outcome measures were effect of improvement in olfactory function (measured by the threshold, discrimination, and identification [TDI] composite score on parosmia) and effect of compliance to OT on parosmia and olfactory function.

Parosmia Assessments

Patients were asked to rate the intensity of their parosmia from a scale of 1 to 10 (10 = maximum intensity) and pleasantness of parosmia from a scale of −5 to +5 (−5 = very unpleasant, 0 = neutral, +5 = very pleasant). A structured parosmia questionnaire was administered. This consisted of 4 statements: (1) Because of my olfactory problem, food tastes different than it should taste. (2) I always have a bad odor in my nose, regardless if any odor source is present. (3) Odors which are pleasant to other people are unpleasant for me. (4) The biggest problem is not that I do not or weakly perceive odors, but that they smell different than they should [17]. Patients rated how much they identified with each statement with 1 out of 4 available options: “This is always the case”, “This is often the case”, “This is rarely the case”, and “This is never the case”. Each statement was allocated 1, 2, 3, and 4 points respectively. The sum of scores from the 4 statements were tallied.

Patients also underwent the Sniffin’ Sticks Parosmia Test (SSParoT) [13]. This is a psychophysical test that evaluates hedonic olfactory perception with 2 measurements: hedonic range and hedonic distance. Patients were presented with 4 pairs of pleasant and unpleasant odors (e.g., peppermint and fish). They rated each odor on a 9-point hedonic scale (−4 = extremely unpleasant; 0 = neutral; +4 = very pleasant). Hedonic range describes the perceived range between 2 oppositely valenced odors (the difference between the ratings of the 2 odors). Hedonic distance indicates the overall hedonic perception of odors (mean hedonic ratings of all odors).

Olfactory Assessment

Olfactory testing was conducted using the Sniffin’ Sticks (Burghart Messtechnik GmbH, Holm, Germany), which consists of odor threshold, discrimination, and identification testing [18]. The sum of results from these three tests gave a composite TDI score (minimum 1, maximum 48), which indicated the global olfactory performance. Patients were classified as normosmic (TDI ≥30.75), hyposmic (TDI between 16.25 and 30.50), or anosmic (TDI ≤16) [19]. The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for TDI was taken to be 5.5 [20].

Olfactory Training

All patients received intervention in the form of OT. This consisted of deliberate and conscious sniffing of 4 odors (lemon, rose, eucalyptus, and clove) twice a day for at least 15 s each [21]. Patients were instructed to do this daily over a period of 3 months and to record every training session on a diary. Compliance to OT was calculated as a percentage of the number of OT sessions performed over the expected number of training sessions over the 3-month period. OT of >70% was considered to be compliant.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows version 26.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) with statistical significance set at 2-sided p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) when normality and homogeneity assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) was used. Changes in numerical variables were assessed using the paired t test when normality and homogeneity assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. To measure the effect of olfactory function on parosmia, participants were stratified into whether they achieved the MCID or not. Participants who were lost to follow-up at 3 months were excluded from analysis.

Results

Participant Demographics

There were 38 patients (mean age 41.8 [SD 13.3] years, 12 [31.6%] males). The mean duration of parosmia was 15.4 (SD 5.8) months. The quality of parosmia was unpleasant in most patients (91.7%); for the remainder, they were in the neutral range (Table 1). The most common stimuli for parosmia were onions (n = 18, 47.4%), followed by meat (n = 14, 36.8%) and coffee (n = 12, 31.5%). More than half the patients identified with the statements “Because of my smell problem, food tastes different than it should”, “Many smells that are pleasant to other people are unpleasant to me”, and “The biggest problem is not that I don’t detect odors or detect them faintly, but that they smell differently than they should” (Table 1). The mean baseline TDI score was 25.2 (SD 7.2), which was in the hyposmia range. All patients returned for follow-up after 3 months of OT.

After 3 months of OT, the cohort showed significant improvement in discrimination (10.5 [SD 2.8] vs. 11.5 [SD 3.2], p = 0.031), identification (9.0 [SD 2.5] vs. 10.0 [SD 2.5], p = 0.003), and TDI scores (25.2 [SD 7.2] vs. 27.9 [SD 7.4], p = 0.001) (Table 1). Twelve (31.6%) patients achieved the MCID in TDI score.

Improvement in Parosmia

Twenty (52.6%) patients reported an improvement in parosmia after 3 months, whilst 18 (47.4%) patients reported no change. There was significant improvement in the perception of its unpleasantness (−2.0 [IQR −3.0 to −1.0] vs. −3.0 [IQR −4.0 to −2.0], p = 0.027) and hedonic range (2.4 [SD 1.3] vs. 1.9 [SD 1.6], p = 0.023) compared to baseline. However, when patients with no perceived improvement in parosmia were compared with those who perceived improvement in parosmia, there were no significant differences in any of the measures of parosmia or olfactory function (online suppl. Table 2).

Effect of Improved Olfactory Quantitative Function on Parosmia

Patients who achieved the MCID tended to have a shorter duration of parosmia compared to patients who did not (12.7 [SD 6.5] vs. 16.6 [SD 5.2] months, p = 0.053). There were no significant differences in any of the measures of parosmia between patients who achieved the MCID versus those who did not (online suppl. Table 3).

Effect of Compliance to OT on Parosmia and Olfaction

The median compliance to OT was 37.5 (7.0–75.0) %. Twelve patients (31.6%) complied with OT for at least 70% of the study period (Table 2). These patients had significant improvement in hedonic range (1.1 [SD 1.1] vs. 0.2 [SD 1.3], p = 0.040) compared to those who did OT <70% of the time (shown in Fig. 1a). There were no other significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of other parosmia and olfactory function outcome measures.

Discussion

The recovery process for parosmia can be protracted [22]. An online survey of 434 patients with COVID-19-related olfactory impairment reported that at 6 months after its onset, 51% had partial or no improvement in parosmia, compared to only 9% who perceived resolution in parosmia [3]. In a prospective cross-sectional study of 148 patients with COVID-19-induced parosmia, 57% continued to experience parosmia at 6 months since its onset [8]. In our cohort, the mean duration of COVID-19-related parosmia was 15 months. We found that parosmia generally improved after 3 months of OT. Over half the patients reported subjective improvement in parosmia. There was improvement in perceived (un)pleasantness, percentage of changed odors, and hedonic range. In terms of quantitative olfactory function, the cohort also had significant improvement in discrimination, identification, and TDI scores.

Quantifying parosmia in measurable terms is challenging. In most clinical contexts, assessment of parosmia relies primarily on the patient interview. However, this is not ideal as patients may struggle with verbalizing olfactory experiences and descriptions can be heterogenous [23]. The use of a parosmia-specific structured questionnaire is helpful, in that a low global score, or agreement with the statement that odors smell different than expected, can help to quantify parosmia [17]. When it comes to tracking changes in parosmia over time, this appears to be even more difficult. Even though half the cohort indicated they perceived improvement in parosmia after 3 months, this was not reflected on any of the parosmia assessments, including the structured questionnaire scores, when compared to patients who did not perceive any improvement in their parosmia.

Compared to psychophysical measurements of quantitative olfactory dysfunction, there are far fewer psychophysical tools to measure parosmia. Apart from the SSParoT, another potential test is the SCENTinel 1.1, which is a rapid, self-administered olfactory test that is reportedly able to detect parosmia (in addition to detecting odor, intensity and identification) by rating the pleasantness of a test odor and an imagined, universally unpleasant odor, thereby creating a hedonic score [24]. Both have yet to be widely adopted in clinical studies. The studies that have used SSParoT reported mixed findings. One study of 112 patients with post-COVID-19 parosmia reported that patients scored significantly differently on hedonic range and hedonic direction compared to the normative data from the original SSParoT study [25]. However, another study of 63 patients with post-viral olfactory dysfunction found that the short version SSParoT did not discriminate between patients with and without parosmia [26]. In our cohort of patients, the baseline mean hedonic range (1.9) and hedonic direction (0.4) were higher than the respective proposed cutoff values of −1.5 and −2.0 taken from the 10th percentile scores of normal subjects [13]. There are several possible reasons for this. The test assumes that patients with parosmia would rate a pleasant or neutral odor as unpleasant. In reality, triggers for parosmia are usually specific odors, which may differ from person to person [4, 5, 27]. In our study, the most common odors that triggered parosmia were onions, meat, and coffee. Yet none of these odors were test odors in the SSParoT. It is possible that patients with odor-specific parosmias may indicate relatively normal scores on the test if the trigger odors are not part of the test. The test may not be relevant in the rare cases where typically unpleasant odors are perceived as pleasant [28, 29]. On the other hand, there was a significant improvement in hedonic range after 3 months of OT. Therefore, the SSParoT may have some utility in observing the overall trend of parosmia in comparison with baseline data.

The role of OT in improving post-infectious quantitative olfactory loss, including COVID-19-related olfactory impairment, is well established [11, 21, 30, 31]. However, there are few studies that examined the role of OT in treating parosmia specifically. In a study of 75 patients with post-COVID-19 parosmia who were stratified to undergo modified OT versus no OT over 9 months, both groups demonstrated improvement in parosmia scores, although improvement in the modified OT group was significantly better [32]. In a double-blind randomized study comparing the effects of OT versus placebo in COVID-19-related olfactory dysfunction, it found that the rate of parosmia decreased in the OT group but not the placebo group [33]. In another study, patients with COVID-19-related parosmia who were randomized to receive OT supplemented with oral ultra-micronized palmitoylethanolamide and luteolin (PEA-LUT), with or without alpha-lipoic acid, had significantly higher rates of parosmia resolution compared to patients who received OT alone [34]. However, it should be noted that PEA-LUT is not unanimously convincing in its effect on olfaction. A study in which 50 patients with post-viral olfactory dysfunction randomized to receive OT with PEA-LUT or OT alone observed that the additional improvement in olfactory function in the OT/PEA-LUT group may not be clinically meaningful [35]. In our study, the proportion of patients who perceived improvement in parosmia, as well as parosmia assessment scores, were not significantly different between patients who achieved the MCID improvement in TDI scores versus those who did not. Hence, the present results do not support the idea that improving olfactory function translates to improvement in parosmia.

Compliance to OT in our cohort was poor at 37.5%. This is a practical issue that has been similarly observed in other studies [36, 37]. Patients tend to discontinue treatment when treatment durations increase, or if little improvement is perceived [38]. Patients who did not attempt OT had significantly lower improvement in threshold scores and their hedonic distance trended toward a negative score compared to those who performed OT. In addition, among patients who did not attempt OT, none of them achieved the MCID improvement in TDI scores. On the other hand, when OT was attempted, 37.5% of them managed to achieve the MCID. Moreover, patients who performed OT >70% of the time had significantly improved hedonic range compared to those who were less compliant to OT. This suggests that OT is not only beneficial in the recovery of olfactory function, it also may improve parosmia. However, given the low compliance rate, it is hard to comment if OT played a direct role in improving parosmia or whether the outcomes were simply the result of natural resolution. A large-scale questionnaire survey of 2,031 participants reported that parosmia was less likely to last for more than 2 years and was more likely to improve compared to phantosmia and quantitative olfactory disorders [4].

There are several limitations of this study. The sample size is small and may have been underpowered to detect significant changes in some of the findings that showed a trend toward significance. The study design did not include a control group with either placebo or no OT for comparison. Spontaneous recovery in parosmia and quantitative olfactory function may have contributed to the observed improvements. Patients were only seen at baseline and at the end of 3 months, which may not have been sufficient to encourage compliance with OT. However, this is a real world issue in managing patients with parosmia in a busy clinical setting.

Conclusion

Patients with post-COVID-19 parosmia generally had improvement in parosmia after 3 months of OT. Improvement in olfactory function did not appear to influence improvement in parosmia. Patients who were more compliant with OT saw an improvement in hedonic range. The current tools for measuring parosmia appear to be limited in terms of ability to track changes in parosmia over time and may not always agree with patients’ subjective assessment. This will need to be validated with further studies and over a longer period of time.
