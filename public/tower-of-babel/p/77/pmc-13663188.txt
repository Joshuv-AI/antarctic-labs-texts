Can brain-to-tumour outer interface radiomics improve the efficiency of MRI in predicting the brain invasion of meningiomas?
Authors: Zongmeng Wang, Dingfu Wei, Weiwei Xia, Ye Li, Xiaofang Zhou, Mingwei Sun, Sihui Liu, Haixia Li, Yang Song, Jie Wu, Yuting Chen, Yunjing Xue, Lin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain invasion serves as an independent diagnostic criterion for the diagnosis of WHO grade 2 meningiomas. This study aimed to evaluate the value of radiomic models constructed with features extracted from different regions of the brain-to-tumor interface to predict potential brain invasion of meningiomas.MethodsThe cohort of this retrospective multicenter study included 915 consecutive patients from three centers with histopathologically confirmed meningiomas. Brain invasion status was determined separately using predefined criteria based primarily on histopathological findings, with operative records used as supplementary information. Nine radiomic models were developed using radiomic features extracted from nine different regions of interest: 3–5 mm regions of the brain-to-tumor interface (BTI), brain-to-tumor inner interface (BTII), and brain-to-tumor outer interface (BTOI). The features were derived from T1W, T2-FLAIR, and CE-T1W images. A logistic regression classifier was trained for model development. Model discrimination was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, while clinical utility and incremental predictive value were quantified using decision curve analysis (DCA) and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index.ResultsAs compared to models constructed with features from the 3–5 mm BTII and BTI regions, the BTOI radiomic models demonstrated enhanced predictive capability for meningioma brain invasion. The area under the curve (AUC) values for the 3-, 4-, and 5-mm BTOI models were 0.845, 0.845, and 0.905 with the test cohort, and 0.818, 0.825, and 0.874 with the external validation cohort, respectively. DCA confirmed the clinical superiority of the BTOI models, which provided the highest net benefit across various threshold probabilities. Moreover, the IDI index indicated that the BTOI features offered statistically significant incremental value for invasion risk stratification as compared to the BTII and BTI models (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe BTOI radiomic models demonstrated superior performance in predicting brain invasion in meningiomas compared with models constructed from the BTI and BTII regions. These findings suggest that the peritumoral brain tissue adjacent to the tumor may contain particularly informative imaging characteristics related to invasion and provide imaging-based support for optimizing ROI delineation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40644-026-01078-w.

Introduction

Meningiomas are the most prevalent primary intracranial tumors in adults, accounting for approximately 41.7% of all cases [1]. Among the various histological features of meningiomas, brain invasion has emerged as a critical determinant of clinical management and is independently associated with tumor recurrence, shorter progression-free survival, higher incidence of seizures, and increased need for adjuvant radiotherapy [2–5]. Consequently, the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System (CNS) has established brain invasion as an independent diagnostic criterion for grade 2 meningiomas [6]. Despite established clinical significance, preoperative identification of brain invasion remains challenging. Although conventional radiographic features, such as irregular tumor shape and peritumoral edema, have been proposed as indicators of brain invasion, specificity remains controversial and highly reader-dependent, thereby limiting routine clinical utility [7, 8].

Radiomics has emerged as a valuable tool for the transformation of medical images into high-dimensional quantitative data to elucidate the underlying pathophysiology of tumors [9]. Traditional radiomic approaches in meningioma research have predominantly concentrated on extracting features from intratumoral regions, while a clinicoradiomic model based on these features demonstrated favorable performance to predict brain invasion [10]. However, this intratumor-focused methodology is inherently limited by the inability to capture imaging data from the peritumoral region, which is crucial to identify brain invasion of meningiomas. Moreover, accumulating evidence underscores the necessity of incorporating peritumoral tissue into radiomic analyses to facilitate the precise evaluation of meningioma aggressiveness [11]. In line with this evidence, recent studies have verified that radiomic features derived from the brain-to-tumor interface (BTI) region can effectively predict brain invasion by meningiomas [12, 13]. Notably, the BTI is a spatially heterogeneous zone rather than a homogeneous layer. Histopathologically, brain invasion is characterized by the presence of tumor tissue within the adjacent brain parenchyma without an intervening leptomeningeal layer, with tumor cells infiltrating as irregular tongue-like protrusions [14]. This microscopically distinct boundary naturally creates two different biological compartments: an inner subregion representing the peripheral rim and invasive leading edge of the tumor, and an outer subregion representing the adjacent brain parenchyma, including microscopic tumor infiltration and reactive changes. Furthermore, glioma research has firmly established that the peritumoral zone exhibits distinct regional heterogeneity. Wang et al. [15] demonstrated that the peritumoral zone can be stratified into functionally distinct subregions, while Pan et al. [16] confirmed that radiomic performance varies significantly across different peritumoral widths. These consistent pathological and radiological findings strongly support the biological plausibility that the BTI can be stratified into distinct subregions. Hypothetically, dividing this interface into inner and outer subregions at varying widths may produce more specific and discriminative imaging features than analyzing the interface as a whole. However, to date, no study has explored this stratification or identified the optimal subregion and width for radiomic analysis of meningiomas.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate and compare the predictive performance of radiomic signatures extracted from spatially distinct subregions of the BTI at multiple widths, with the primary objective to identify the optimal peritumoral region of interest (ROI), based on both subregion and width, for accurate preoperative prediction of brain invasion by meningiomas.

Materials and methods

Study population

The protocol of this retrospective, cross-sectional, diagnostic multicenter study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the requisite for informed patient consent was waived. This retrospective study investigated the medical records of 1,273 patients with pathologically confirmed meningiomas retrieved from Center 1 between January 2013 and April 2022, along with 112 from Center 2 between August 2021 and August 2022, and 179 from Center 3 between July 2017 and May 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of meningioma according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System; (2) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed within 1 week prior to surgery; and (3) availability of complete clinical baseline data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and/or surgery and (2) missing/poor quality T1-weighted (T1W), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), or contrast-enhanced (CE)-T1W MR images. All included meningiomas were histopathologically confirmed. The presence or absence of brain invasion was strictly determined based on histopathological evaluation. Brain invasion was defined as the histopathological evidence of meningioma cells infiltrating the adjacent brain parenchyma without an intervening connective tissue layer [17]. Cases were classified as non-invasive only when no histopathological evidence of brain parenchymal infiltration was identified, and this was corroborated by operative records describing no definite brain invasion. Intraoperative findings were used only as supplementary information and were not considered sufficient to establish brain invasion. To improve label reliability, cases with suspected intraoperative brain invasion but lacking definitive histopathological confirmation were excluded from the study. Finally, 729, 65, and 121 patients from Centers 1, 2, and 3, respectively, were included for analysis (Fig. 1).

Image acquisition and normalization

T1W, T2-FLAIR, and CE-T1W images were obtained with 1.5T or 3.0T scanners. The detailed protocols are shown in Supplementary Material 1. Before manual segmentation, T1W, T2-FLAIR, and CE-T1W images were standardized via Z-score normalization to ensure a standard normal distribution of image intensities. ComBat harmonization was applied to reduce variability in feature distributions across different scanners. T1W and T2-FLAIR MR images were subsequently registered to the corresponding CE-T1W images using the Statistical Parametric Mapping plugin unit (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) of MATLAB software.

ROI segmentation

To ensure data validity and accuracy, MR images were randomly and independently segmented by two neuroradiologists (with 10 and 15 years of experience) using 3D Slicer software (v5.0.2; https://www.slicer.org/). Segmentation was performed in a blinded manner to the histopathological results. ROIs were delineated on axial CE-T1WI images for the tumor region, excluding edema. The volume of interest was generated by fusing segmented tissue on each slice. To define the interface subregions, boundary modification algorithms were applied to the original tumor volume of interest using Roter Interaction Analysis System software (https://pewter-papyrus-421.notion.site). The overall BTI region was defined by simultaneously shrinking the tumor margin inwards and expanding it outwards by 3–5 mm. Specifically, the brain-to-tumor inner interface (BTII) region was defined by solely shrinking the tumor margin inwards by 3–5 mm, while the brain-to-tumor outer interface (BTOI) region was defined by solely expanding the tumor edge outwards by 3–5 mm. Manual correction was performed to exclude any irrelevant brain or non-brain structures inadvertently captured during the boundary expansion. The corresponding ROIs are shown in Fig. 2.

Radiomic feature extraction

FeAture Explorer software (v0.5.2; https://apps.juniper.net/feature-explorer/) was used to extract standardized medical imaging data, and the characteristics were extracted in strict compliance with the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative guidelines [18] based on the original and transformed images. The radiomic features of the BTI, BTII and BTOI regions (3–5 mm), including first-order, shape, and texture features, were extracted and quantified. The three features described global information, such as the grayscale mean value and variance, local information, such as the shape and edge of the ROI, and mutual information between pixels inside the ROI and neighborhood. The texture features include mainly the gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level run length matrix, gray-level size zone matrix, gray-level dependence matrix, and neighborhood gray-tone difference matrix.

Feature selection

The feature selection pipeline followed a sequential multistep process. First, intraclass correlation coefficients were computed to quantify the interreader agreement for radiomic features [19]. To address class imbalance in the training dataset, the synthetic minority oversampling technique was applied to balance positive and negative samples [20]. Subsequent preprocessing included Z score normalization of the feature matrix, followed by dimensionality reduction through iterative removal of features with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.990 to ensure statistical independence. Finally, recursive feature elimination was employed to rank and select features, thereby optimizing the feature subset for model development while preserving discriminative capacity.

Model construction

Patients from Center 1 were assigned to the training and test sets, while patients from Centers 2 and 3 were used as the external validation set. Logistic regression was used as the classifier to construct the model. To determine the hyperparameters of the model, 5-fold cross-validation was applied to the training dataset. The hyperparameters were set according to the model performance with the external validation dataset. All of the above processes were implemented in Python (v3.7.6; https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-376/) using FeAture Explorer software (v0.5.2). Nine 3–5 mm models of the BTI, BTII, and BTOI were constructed using radiomic features extracted from the corresponding regions. A flowchart of the model construction process is shown in Fig. 3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.2; https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/old/) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 22.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were compared with the Student’s t-test (normal distribution) or the Mann‒Whitney U test (nonnormal distribution). Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test. Model performance was evaluated by analysis of the receiver operating characteristic curve. The area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated at cut-off thresholds maximizing Youden’s index, and the 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated via 1000-bootstrap resampling. Models were compared using DeLong’s test for AUC discrimination and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) for incremental value assessment. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided probability (p) value < 0.05.

Radiomic feature selection

In total, 290 radiomic features (14, 51, 72, 42, 48, 48, and 15 defined as shape, first-order, gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level dependence matrix, gray-level size zone matrix, gray-level run length matrix, and neighborhood gray-tone difference matrix, respectively) were extracted from T1W, T2-FLAIR and CE-T1W MR images across each region. After the features were selected using intraclass correlation coefficients, Z score normalization, Pearson correlation coefficient analysis, and recursive feature elimination, 4, 7, and 22 features were ultimately included in the construction of the BTI 3-, 4-, and 5-mm models, respectively, while 5, 2, and 3 features were selected to construct the BTII 3-, 4-, and 5-mm models, respectively, and 12, 12, and 22 features were selected for the BTOI 3-, 4-, and 5-mm models, respectively. A detailed composition of the feature sets is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Model comparison and performance evaluation

DeLong’s test was used to compare the AUC estimates of the discrimination performance among the different models. The detailed results are shown in Table 3. As compared to the BTII models, the BTOI models demonstrated superior discriminatory accuracy with both the training and test sets (p < 0.05), and significant superiority was observed for two comparisons with the external validation set (p < 0.05). IDI analysis further revealed consistent net reclassification benefits for the BTOI models over the BTII and BTI models with the training, test, and external validation sets (IDI: 5.29%–37.71%, 7.36%–32.11%, and 3.92%–16.63%, respectively; Table 4). The DCA results corroborated these findings, demonstrating that the BTOI models provided the greatest net benefit across a broader range of clinical threshold probabilities (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, nine radiomic models were developed using the features of the 3–5 mm BTI, BTII, and BTOI regions and validated by independent analyses to predict brain invasion of meningiomas. The BTOI-derived models demonstrated superior predictive performance, with the BTOI 5-mm model achieving the highest discriminatory accuracy with both the internal test set and the multicenter external validation cohort. These findings suggest that the BTOI 5-mm region represents the optimal region to predict brain invasion of meningiomas.

Radiomic analysis has emerged as a promising noninvasive strategy to identify brain invasion of meningiomas, as increasing evidence highlights the diagnostic value of peritumoral and interface regions beyond intratumoral characteristics [10–13]. Initially, Zhang et al. [10] demonstrated that radiomic signatures derived from the tumor parenchyma enabled risk stratification for brain invasion, thereby establishing the fundamental role of radiomics in this field. However, models relying solely on intratumoral features fail to capture the complex tissue interactions at the BTI, where microscopic invasion actively occurs. Subsequent studies redirected attention toward the BTI, recognizing its superior biological relevance to invasive behavior. Joo et al. [11] were the first to propose that features of the BTI could serve as predictive biomarkers, providing a critical foundation for subsequent interface radiomics. Building upon this foundation, Xiao et al. [12] further optimized interface segmentation by investigating different volumetric widths, underscoring that ROI definition directly impacts model accuracy. Recently, Li et al. [13] developed an interface-based model specific to grade 2 meningiomas by incorporating clinical semantic features, further confirming the clinical value of interface radiomics. Despite these significant advances, a critical methodological limitation remains, as all prior studies treated the BTI as a single, anatomically homogeneous region. However, none distinguished the pure tumor compartment (BTII) from the anatomical brain–tumor boundary (BTI) and the specific zone where invasive tumor cells intermingle with brain parenchyma (BTOI). The present study addresses this conceptual gap by subdividing the interface into three well-defined, biologically distinct subregions. By systematically comparing the predictive performance across multiple thicknesses and validating the results with an independent multicenter cohort, this study provides a more precise approach to interface radiomics, directly linking ROI selection to the pathology of meningioma brain invasion.

Aggressive meningiomas, like other invasive tumors of the CNS (e.g., glioblastomas), exhibit microscopic infiltration beyond radiographically evident margins [17, 21]. The superior performance of BTOI-based radiomic models can be directly explained by the distinct tissue composition of each subregion. Confined to the internal margin of the tumor, the BTII consists of peripheral tumor tissue but lacks specificity for the dynamic tumor-brain interactions that define actual invasion. By comparison, the BTI symmetrically encompasses the tumor boundary, but its unstratified mixture of tumor and brain parenchyma dilutes invasion-specific signals, leading to suboptimal predictive performance. In contrast, the BTOI specifically isolates the tissue immediately outside the macroscopic tumor margin, representing the true histopathological invasion front—a site where infiltrating tumor cells actively interact with reactive brain parenchyma, characterized by neuronal injury and astrogliosis. Previous studies have documented characteristic metabolic alterations in this zone, such as reduced N-acetylaspartate and choline alongside elevated lipids on proton MR spectroscopy, which reflect neuronal loss, astrocytic dysfunction, and cell membrane degradation [22–25]. Furthermore, molecular abnormalities, including the overexpression of Ki-67, RASGRF1, and DTX1, are also concentrated at this specific interface [26]. Crucially, these structural, metabolic, and molecular changes alter the local tissue microenvironment, manifesting as subtle textural and intensity variations on MRI. By specifically targeting this active invasion front, the BTOI model effectively captures these distinct radiomic patterns.

Histopathological diagnosis of meningioma brain invasion is intrinsically limited by sampling error, as microscopic analysis misses invasive foci if the adjacent brain tissue is unsampled [27, 28]. According to Pižem et al., brain tissue was assessable in only 41.2% of routinely sampled meningiomas [29]. Since invasion cannot be ruled out in the remaining 58.8% of unassessable cases, this proportion represents a major source of false negatives, establishing a pragmatic lower-bound histopathological sensitivity of roughly 41% [29]. This inherent label noise in the reference standard inevitably biases model evaluation. Specifically, when the model correctly identifies a truly invasive tumor pathologically mislabeled as non-invasive, the prediction is statistically penalized as a false positive, leading to an underestimation of the true specificity of the model. Despite this penalty, the BTOI model maintained high and stable performance across internal and external validation cohorts, highlighting the strong robustness of BTOI radiomic features to capture true invasion signatures, even with imperfect histopathological labels. This impressive cross-center stability can be partly attributed to our rigorous data harmonization and feature selection strategies. Although our cohorts included intrinsic variations in magnetic field strengths, sequence types, and scanner vendors, we effectively mitigated these batch effects through Z-score standardization and ComBat harmonization prior to model construction. Furthermore, applying recursive feature elimination on a training set with mixed acquisition parameters inherently filtered out protocol-sensitive variables, retaining only radiomic features that demonstrated robust stability across varying magnetic field strengths and sequence types. Notably, the model achieved favorable performance using the 22 selected features. The consistent results across multicenter validation suggest that these features may reflect reproducible imaging patterns related to brain invasion. However, the potential risk of overfitting cannot be completely ruled out. Nevertheless, recent statistical studies [30, 31] support that such multidimensional signatures can outperform highly simplified models to capture complex spatial pathophysiology like that of the BTI. Ultimately, integrating this BTOI signature with standardized surgical sampling and MRI-based risk stratification offers a promising pathway to overcome the inherent limitations of histopathological diagnosis.

There were several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective study, which may have introduced biases, such as inherent confounders and selection bias. Second, although the external validation cohort included patients from two tertiary hospitals, the overall sample size of positive cases (brain invasion) was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of these findings. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further validate the performance of the BTOI radiomic model. Third, inter-reader variability in manual tumor segmentation may impact the stability of radiomic feature measurements. Although only features with high inter-reader reproducibility were retained for model development, the implementation of automated segmentation algorithms is essential to standardize the radiomic workflow and improve the clinical applicability of the model.

Conclusions

In this multicenter radiomics study, features extracted from the BTOI region demonstrated superior performance for predicting brain invasion in meningiomas compared with those derived from the BTI and BTII regions. These findings suggest that the peritumoral brain tissue adjacent to the tumor may contain particularly informative imaging characteristics related to invasion. Our results provide imaging-based support for optimizing ROI delineation in radiomics studies of meningioma brain invasion and may improve preoperative assessment of brain invasion risk.
