Development and characterization of rat hindlimb ischemia models mimicking peripheral arterial disease severity
Authors: Yingxiang Liang, Connor Mullen, Emma R. Young, Mohamed Zaghloul, Yuhan Guo, Shijie Jin, Madeleine Yang, Jingyu Yan, Xiaochao Xia, Batool Arif, Tiffany Ly, Daniel Li, Theodore Lee, Adam Boukind, Jonathon Blake Schofield, Mohamed A. Zayed, Justin M. Sacks, Xiaowei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a progressive atherosclerotic disorder that reduces blood flow to the lower extremities, leading to skeletal muscle degeneration, functional decline, and limb loss in advanced stages. To enable rigorous preclinical evaluation of regenerative therapies across disease stages, we developed and quantitatively characterized three rat hindlimb ischemia models representing graded arterial insufficiency: iliac artery ligation (Iliac), femoral artery ligation and excision (Femoral), and combined femoral plus popliteal artery ligation and excision (Fem/Pop). Laser Doppler perfusion imaging demonstrated an immediate reduction to approximately 60–65% of baseline in Iliac, 40–50% in Femoral, and less than 30% in Fem/Pop limbs. Perfusion recovery occurred earliest in the Iliac model, with delayed recovery in the Femoral and Fem/Pop models, while overall temporal recovery trends were comparable by mixed-effects analysis. Histological and quantitative morphometric analyses at the chronic endpoint revealed progressively severe muscle remodeling with increasing ischemic burden, including increased centrally nucleated fibers and fibrosis, most pronounced in the Fem/Pop model. Oxidative stress markers trended higher with increasing ischemia severity but did not differ significantly among models. Among the three models, Fem/Pop produced the most sustained clinical ischemia and structural remodeling, while regenerative, inflammatory, and biochemical endpoints exhibit heterogeneity at chronic stages, enabling stage-appropriate evaluation of vascular and regenerative therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43361-7.

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) affects an estimated 202 million individuals globally and remains a leading cause of chronic limb-threatening ischemia and amputation1–3. As a manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis, PAD is marked by progressive narrowing and occlusion of arteries, ultimately reducing perfusion to the lower extremities4–6. In advanced stages, patients experience intermittent claudication, ischemic ulcers, and necrosis, often requiring surgical revascularization or limb amputation2. Despite current pharmacologic and surgical approaches, the long-term preservation of muscle function and tissue viability remains a major clinical challenge7.

A major barrier to the development of effective PAD therapies is the lack of standardized and scalable preclinical models that accurately recapitulate the progressive nature and pathophysiology of human disease8,9. Although rodent hindlimb ischemia models have been instrumental in advancing PAD research, several methodological limitations persist. Many conventional models rely on combined arterial and venous ligation, which induces rapid necrosis and venous congestion rather than isolated arterial insufficiency. While these approaches accelerate tissue loss, they fail to reflect the gradual reduction in arterial perfusion that characterizes clinical PAD. In addition, ischemic severity varies widely across studies due to inconsistent ligation levels and limited quantitative validation of perfusion deficits. Other models introduce additional injury components, such as nerve disruption or extensive tissue trauma, which confound interpretation of ischemia-specific vascular and skeletal muscle responses10–14. Consequently, most existing models represent either overly severe or poorly defined ischemic states and do not support systematic evaluation of therapies across disease stages. There remains a critical need for well-defined preclinical models that generate reproducible, graded levels of ischemia, enabling stage-appropriate testing of vascular and regenerative therapeutic strategies8,9,15.

To address these gaps, we developed and characterized three rat hindlimb ischemia models that use artery-only ligation and excision to produce reproducible, severity-dependent tissue injury. These models, including iliac artery ligation (Iliac), femoral artery ligation and excision (Femoral), and combined femoral plus popliteal artery ligation and excision (Fem/Pop), were designed to represent increasing levels of arterial compromise at distinct anatomical sites. The Iliac model produces mild, proximal ischemia with preserved collateral flow. The Femoral model induces more distal occlusion and moderate ischemia. The Fem/Pop model targets multiple arterial segments, generating the most profound and persistent perfusion deficits. Using a combination of clinical ischemia scoring, laser Doppler perfusion imaging (LDPI), and histological and biochemical analyses, we quantitatively defined the functional and structural consequences of each ischemic condition on skeletal muscle viability and regenerative capacity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare rat hindlimb ischemia models with graded arterial occlusion at different anatomical levels within a standardized experimental framework. Together, these models establish a scalable preclinical platform for stage-specific evaluation of vascular and regenerative therapeutic strategies in PAD.

Materials and methods

Animal use and ethical statement

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Washington University in St. Louis (protocol 24–0145). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, and all experiments were conducted in compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Male Sprague–Dawley rats (300–350 g) were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water. For surgery, rats were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (1–3% in oxygen via precision vaporizer) and then administered ketamine/dexmedetomidine (75 mg/kg and 0.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). Adequate anesthetic depth was confirmed by absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex and by physiologic monitoring throughout the procedure. Analgesia was provided with sustained-release buprenorphine (0.5 mg/kg, subcutaneous; single administration providing up to 72 h coverage).

Surgical models of hindlimb ischemia

Three rat models of artery-only hindlimb ischemia were established to represent increasing ischemic severity (Fig. 1). In the Iliac model, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the common iliac artery. The left common iliac artery was isolated from surrounding connective tissue and ligated using 6 − 0 silk suture. This model aimed to simulate proximal arterial occlusion without extensive distal ischemia, modeling early-stage PAD.

In the Femoral model, a longitudinal skin incision was made along the medial aspect of the left thigh to expose the femoral neurovascular bundle. The femoral artery was carefully dissected away from the adjacent femoral vein and nerve under an operating microscope. Proximal and distal ligations were placed using 6 − 0 silk sutures, and the intervening arterial segment, approximately 1.5 cm in length, was excised to prevent revascularization via spontaneous recanalization. In addition, all visible arterial side branches, including the superficial epigastric, lateral circumflex femoral, and genicular arteries, were identified and ligated to minimize collateral flow. This model induces moderate ischemia by removing a primary arterial supply to the hindlimb while preserving venous drainage.

In the Fem/Pop model, the Femoral model procedure was extended to include the distal popliteal artery. After excising the femoral artery segment and ligating all its branches, the incision was continued distally to expose the popliteal artery behind the knee. The popliteal artery was then ligated proximally and distally and excised in its entirety. All associated side branches, including the superior and inferior genicular arteries, were also ligated to ensure complete interruption of distal perfusion.

To verify complete interruption of distal perfusion and minimize potential collateral flow, LDPI was performed on postoperative day (POD) 1. The perfusion ratio between the ischemic and contralateral limbs consistently dropped below 50%, confirming effective arterial occlusion and reproducible induction of severe ischemia. This model results in sustained ischemia affecting the calf and foot musculature, modeling features of severe arterial insufficiency relevant to PAD2,4.

Following each procedure, wounds were closed with 5 − 0 absorbable sutures. Postoperative recovery was closely monitored, with particular attention to limb function, wound healing, and general activity. At POD 35, rats were euthanized by barbiturate overdose using pentobarbital euthanasia solution (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal), followed by extended CO₂ exposure as a secondary method to ensure death, per the approved protocol. Unless otherwise stated, 5 animals per group were used for longitudinal LDPI and endpoint tissue analyses. Clinical ischemia index scoring was performed daily by three blinded evaluators and averaged per animal.

Laser Doppler perfusion imaging and clinical ischemia index

Hindlimb perfusion was quantitatively assessed using LDI (Moor Instruments, UK), which provides a noninvasive, real-time evaluation of microvascular blood flow in superficial tissues15,16. Rats were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature and minimize physiological variability during imaging. Hair was removed from the hindlimbs prior to baseline acquisition to ensure consistent laser penetration and signal quality. Perfusion imaging was conducted at baseline (Day 0, pre-surgery) and on POD 1, 6, 9, 14, 20, 23, 28, and 35. Each session included a series of triplicate scans to minimize operator variability and account for spontaneous fluctuations.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined over the plantar footpads of both hindlimbs, as these areas provide consistent signals representative of distal perfusion. Circular ROIs were drawn at the plantar surface of the ischemic and contralateral limbs, carefully avoiding background and edge artifacts. Mean flux values (perfusion units) were calculated using the manufacturer’s software. For each group and time point, LDI% values from all animals were averaged. ROIs and color scales were standardized across all measurements to ensure consistency.

In parallel with perfusion imaging, a clinical ischemia index was used to qualitatively evaluate visible signs of hindlimb ischemia on a daily basis. This index provided a rapid and noninvasive means of assessing tissue viability based on external appearance. Each limb was assigned a score according to a modified 4-point scale, defined as:1 Normal for 0: Skin color and texture indistinguishable from the contralateral limb, with intact nail beds and no swelling;2 Mild pallor for 1: Visible blanching or reduced redness of distal digits, occasionally with slight cooling, but without ulceration or scab formation;3 Ulceration for 2: Localized skin breakdown, small ulcer or darkened patch involving toes or distal paw, indicating partial tissue loss;4 Necrosis/mummification for 3: Extensive blackening, scabbing, or tissue desiccation extending beyond digits, often with loss of soft tissue contour.

To minimize inter-rater variability, all evaluators were trained with representative reference photographs illustrating each grade, including borderline examples between 0 and 1 and 1–2. Ambiguous cases were discussed in a pilot session before formal scoring to ensure consensus on threshold distinctions. Scoring was performed by three blinded independent evaluators, and averaged within each group for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Following euthanasia at POD 35, the gastrocnemius muscle was harvested and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h. The gastrocnemius muscle was selected because it is the most commonly analyzed and most ischemia-sensitive muscle in rodent hindlimb ischemia models, exhibiting more pronounced degeneration, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling than the tibialis anterior17. Consistent with this, the recent spatial mapping study comprehensively characterized injury, regeneration, and angiogenesis zones in the mouse hindlimb and confirmed that the gastrocnemius is a major site of ischemic injury, while also demonstrating regional variability within the muscle itself18. Tissues were processed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for evaluation of general muscle morphology, including myofiber integrity and nuclear localization19. Images of muscle cross-sections were captured at 20× magnification, pre-processed, and myofibers segmented using a custom MATLAB script developed in-house. At least 200 fibers per animal were analyzed. To ensure reproducibility across the entire dataset, all images were processed using identical preprocessing, thresholding, and watershed parameters. In addition, each segmented image underwent manual visual verification by the authors to confirm accurate fiber boundary detection and to identify rare cases requiring correction. This combined automated–manual workflow ensured high internal consistency and reliable morphometric quantification across all experimental groups. The detailed MATLAB code is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Central nuclei are defined as nuclei located within the center of the myofiber rather than at the periphery, expressed as the number of centrally nucleated fibers per mm², commonly interpreted as a marker of regeneration after ischemic injury. Equivalent diameter is the diameter of a circle with an area equal to that of the myofiber, used as a shape-independent index of fiber size. Area (µm²) is defined as cross-sectional area of each myofiber, indicating hypertrophy or atrophy. Perimeter (µm) is the length of the myofiber boundary, reflecting surface complexity. Roundness is calculated as 4π × Area / (Perimeter)2, with values closer to 1 indicating more circular fibers. Solidity is the ratio of the myofiber area to the area of its convex hull, reflecting boundary smoothness and regularity. These morphometric indices collectively provide a quantitative assessment of muscle fiber size, shape, and regeneration status following ischemic injury.

Fibrotic tissue content was evaluated using Picrosirius Red (PSR) staining, which specifically highlights collagen deposition. Three to five representative regions per tissue were analyzed for each animal. Higher collagen content was interpreted as indicative of chronic fibrosis and impaired regenerative remodeling20.

For immunohistochemistry, paraffin sections underwent deparaffinization and citrate-based antigen retrieval (pH 6.0, 95 °C, 20 min), followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies [e.g., CD31, CD68, CD86, CD206, HIF-1α, isolectin B4 (IB4)]. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FluoView 1000 spectral confocal microscope. All image acquisition and quantitative analyses were conducted in a blinded manner using standardized thresholds.

CD31⁺ capillary density was quantified in five random high-power fields (20×) per section using Fiji/ImageJ (version 1.54 g; Java 1.8.0_345). Images were split into channels, thresholded, and binarized. Capillaries were defined as discrete CD31⁺ round-to-oval vascular profiles within a fixed particle-size range, while larger arteriolar and venular structures were excluded. Pixel-to-distance calibration was performed using the embedded scale bar, and capillary density was expressed as vessels per mm². A detailed step-by-step analysis workflow with annotated representative images is provided in Supplementary Material S2. For macrophage phenotyping, immunostaining was performed using antibodies against CD68 (pan-macrophage), CD86 (M1-like), and CD206 (M2-like) macrophages. For assessment of hypoxia-associated signaling and perfusion-accessible microvasculature, tissue sections were stained for HIF-1α and IB4. As part of all quantitative analyses, identical threshold settings and particle-analysis parameters were applied across all experimental groups to ensure unbiased and reproducible comparisons.

Oxidative damage in ischemic muscles

To assess oxidative damage in ischemic muscle tissue, levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and protein carbonyl content were quantified from harvested gastrocnemius muscle lysates. MDA, a byproduct of lipid peroxidation, was measured using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay. Briefly, muscle tissue was homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and supernatants were reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95 °C for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm, and MDA concentration was calculated against a standard curve generated with known concentrations of 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane.

Protein carbonyl content, a marker of protein oxidation, was measured using a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based assay. Protein extracts were incubated with DNPH, and the resulting hydrazone derivatives were quantified spectrophotometrically at 370 nm. Carbonyl concentrations were normalized to total protein content as determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. These oxidative stress markers were compared across experimental groups to evaluate the extent of lipid and protein oxidation under different ischemic conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of Q–Q plots, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test prior to ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed in Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Inter-rater reliability of the clinical ischemia index was assessed using a two-way mixed-effects model [ICC(3,k)], representing average-measures consistency among three fixed raters. Agreement was interpreted as poor (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (0.75–0.9), or excellent (> 0.9). In addition, pairwise quadratic-weighted κ coefficients were calculated to evaluate agreement between individual raters.

Results

Development of graded arterial occlusion models in rats

We established and validated three rat hindlimb ischemia models reflecting graded levels of arterial occlusion. All procedures were technically reproducible and well tolerated, with > 95% survival. To prevent spontaneous reperfusion through collateral vessels, all visible side branches, including the superficial epigastric, lateral circumflex femoral, and genicular arteries, were ligated during the Fem/Pop procedure. The Fem/Pop model, which includes additional excision of the popliteal artery, was designed to mimic critical limb ischemia by disrupting both proximal inflow and distal outflow to the hindlimb.

Progressive ischemic injury by clinical scoring and perfusion imaging

To assess ischemic severity, we monitored limb appearance using a clinical ischemia index and measured tissue perfusion using LDPI. The ischemia index revealed a clear gradient of injury severity among the models (Fig. 2). The Femoral model displayed moderate ischemic signs, including occasional ulceration, whereas the Fem/Pop model exhibited persistent tissue breakdown and necrosis, consistent with critical limb ischemia. In particular, on POD 1, mean values (± SEM) were 0.22 ± 0.10 in the Iliac group, 0.28 ± 0.17 in the Femoral group, and 0.14 ± 0.14 in the Fem/Pop group. By POD 6, the Femoral group increased markedly to 2.18 ± 0.23, while the Iliac and Fem/Pop groups remained low at 0.20 ± 0.14 and 1.06 ± 0.15, respectively. At POD 9, both the Femoral (3.0 ± 0.0) and Femoral/Popliteal (3.0 ± 0.0) groups peaked, while the Iliac group stayed low at 0.23 ± 0.08. By POD 14, values decreased to 1.12 ± 0.23 (Femoral) and 2.26 ± 0.46 (Fem/Pop), with Iliac still low at 0.50 ± 0.29. By POD 28, external ischemic scores improved across all groups, although the Fem/Pop limbs frequently retained residual scarring.

To evaluate scoring consistency among evaluators, we calculated the inter-rater reliability for the clinical ischemia index. Inter-rater reliability was excellent overall [ICC(3,k) = 0.929, 95% CI (0.905–0.948), P < 0.001] and remained high within groups [Iliac: 0.696 (0.479–0.832); Femoral: 0.932 (0.891–0.960); Fem/Pop: 0.932 (0.889–0.961); all P < 0.001]. Pairwise quadratic-weighted κ ranged 0.721–0.951, confirming robust agreement among evaluators.

LDPI confirmed the ischemia index trends with quantitative perfusion measurements (Fig. 3). On POD 1, perfusion in the ischemic limb dropped to 51.5 ± 20.5% of baseline in Iliac, 47.7 ± 13.5% in Femoral, and 29.2 ± 11.7% in Fem/Pop. By POD 9, recovery was near-complete in Iliac (85.6 ± 6.9%) and partial in Femoral (79.8 ± 24.9%), but remained lower in Fem/Pop (62.4 ± 13.6%). A mixed-effects model (REML) revealed a significant effect of time [F(9, 40) = 10.54, P < 0.0001] but no significant effect of group [F(1.39, 41.81) = 0.23, P = 0.711] or time × group interaction [F(12.54, 41.81) = 0.61, P = 0.823], indicating comparable temporal recovery patterns among groups. Final perfusion levels at POD 35 exceeded baseline in all groups (117.2 ± 27.0% in Iliac, 144.0 ± 52.9% in Femoral, and 149.6 ± 93.3% in Fem/Pop). However, statistical testing showed no significant difference from baseline in Iliac (t = 1.09, P = 0.32) or Femoral (t = − 1.94, P = 0.11) groups, and only a borderline increase in the Fem/Pop group (t = 2.57, P = 0.05). These results indicate earlier perfusion recovery in Iliac, with delayed recovery in Femoral and Fem/Pop, while overall temporal recovery patterns were statistically comparable across groups.

Histological evidence of muscle atrophy and fibrotic remodeling

Histological evaluation of gastrocnemius muscles revealed progressive tissue degeneration corresponding to the severity of ischemia (Fig. 4). H&E staining showed preserved myofiber architecture in Iliac limbs, moderate atrophy and mononuclear infiltration in the Femoral model, and extensive necrosis with disrupted myofibers in the Fem/Pop model. PSR staining demonstrated minimal interstitial fibrosis in the Iliac and Femoral muscles but marked collagen deposition in the Fem/Pop group.

Vascular and inflammatory responses

To evaluate angiogenesis and inflammation, we performed immunohistochemistry for CD31, CD68, CD86, and CD206. CD31⁺ capillary density was modestly reduced in the Femoral group and significantly reduced in the Fem/Pop group compared with Iliac (Fig. 5; Iliac: 75.7 ± 31.6 #/mm²; Femoral: 37.0 ± 30.9 #/mm²; Fem/Pop: 26.1 ± 10.9 #/mm²; P < 0.05), indicating impaired angiogenic responses under more severe ischemic conditions.

To further characterize tissue microenvironment under graded ischemic conditions, immunostaining for HIF-1α and IB4 was performed (Figure S1). Representative images demonstrated HIF-1α expression within ischemic muscle regions, reflecting activation of hypoxia-responsive signaling pathways. Quantitative analysis revealed comparable levels of HIF-1α signal among the Iliac, Femoral, and Fem/Pop groups (P = 0.903), with no clear severity-dependent gradient. In parallel, IB4 staining was used to visualize perfusion-accessible microvasculature. Quantification of IB4⁺ vessel density showed no statistically significant differences among groups (P = 0.622), although substantial inter-animal variability was observed, particularly in the Iliac group. These findings suggest that while hypoxia-responsive signaling and perfusion-accessible microvasculature presence persist at this chronic time point, neither parameter scales linearly with the degree of arterial occlusion.

CD68⁺ macrophages were sparse across all groups, with a qualitatively greater presence in Femoral and Fem/Pop models. Among macrophage subsets, CD86⁺ pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages were enriched in the Fem/Pop group, whereas CD206⁺ pro-regenerative (M2-like) macrophages were present in all groups but proportionally reduced in the Fem/Pop group (Fig. 6). These findings indicate a shift toward a sustained pro-inflammatory immune milieu in severe ischemia, which may contribute to impaired muscle regeneration and increased fibrotic remodeling.

Oxidative stress in severe ischemia

To assess biochemical markers of oxidative damage, MDA level and protein carbonyl content were measured in ischemic muscles (Fig. 7). Protein carbonyl concentration showed higher values in Femoral and was highest in Fem/Pop rats (Iliac: 6.8 ± 4.8 nmol/mg; Femoral: 11.0 ± 2.9 nmol/mg; Fem/Pop: 13.3 ± 5.3 nmol/mg), consistent with increased oxidative stress under sustained ischemia. However, one-way ANOVA detected no significant between-group differences for either protein carbonyl content (F = 1.31, P = 0.31) or MDA (F = 1.23, P = 0.33). These trends suggest a potential contribution of oxidative stress during chronic ischemia but do not demonstrate statistically significant group differences in the present dataset.

Discussion

We successfully developed and characterized three rat models of hindlimb ischemia that recapitulate progressive levels of perfusion loss, tissue damage, and regenerative impairment. Each model offers distinct advantages tailored to specific experimental objectives. The Iliac model induces mild, reversible ischemia, making it well-suited for studying early-stage interventions and subclinical tissue responses. The Femoral model creates moderate, sustained ischemia with partial regenerative activity, ideal for evaluating functional biomaterials and moderate-risk therapies. In contrast, the Fem/Pop model produces severe ischemia with more frequent necrosis and fibrosis, representing a high-grade injury state suitable for testing potent angiogenic or antifibrotic interventions. Clinically, these correspond approximately to early-stage PAD with intermittent claudication (Iliac), intermediate-stage PAD with functional limitation and ulcer risk (Femoral), and advanced chronic limb-threatening ischemia with high amputation risk (Fem/Pop). Notably, the divergence among endpoints underscores the importance of selecting ischemia models based on the specific biological or therapeutic question being addressed.

A key innovation of this study is the exclusive use of artery-only occlusion to induce hindlimb ischemia. In contrast, many conventional rodent models involve combined ligation of arteries and veins, which accelerates necrosis but introduces additional confounding factors such as venous congestion, edema, and impaired drainage21–24. These effects do not mirror the pathophysiology of human PAD, which is characterized predominantly by progressive arterial stenosis with intact venous outflow2,13,25. By isolating arterial occlusion at defined anatomical levels, the iliac, femoral, and popliteal arteries, we aimed to mimic the gradations of arterial flow reduction observed in clinical PAD, while maintaining venous and lymphatic patency. This design enables a more accurate assessment of ischemic perfusion deficits, muscle degeneration, angiogenic responses, and regenerative outcomes under conditions that closely align with patient disease progression.

The Fem/Pop model, in particular, represents a significant advancement by incorporating popliteal artery excision. This distal occlusion disrupts blood flow to the lower leg and foot, effectively modeling critical limb ischemia. In contrast to femoral-only models that allow partial compensation via collateral circulation, the inclusion of the popliteal artery eliminates this compensatory mechanism, resulting in more sustained and severe ischemia26. This allows for rigorous evaluation of therapies intended to enhance microvascular growth, tissue remodeling, or myofiber preservation in severely ischemic tissues3,27–29.

These artery-only models address a major gap in PAD research by offering a scalable, reproducible, and physiologically relevant platform for studying tissue responses and therapeutic interventions4,14. The integration of perfusion imaging, clinical ischemia scoring, and histological analysis enhances the translational applicability of the system9,15,16. Ongoing work includes applying a biomaterial matrix within the Fem/Pop model and validating its performance in a large-animal porcine model to further assess scalability and clinical potential. Beyond biomaterial testing, this graded ischemia platform can facilitate preclinical evaluation of diverse therapeutic modalities, including gene therapies aimed at enhancing angiogenic signaling, cell-based strategies such as mesenchymal stem cell or endothelial progenitor cell delivery, and the assessment of small-diameter vascular grafts or tissue-engineered conduits under controlled ischemic stress. The ability to model distinct disease stages further enables precision-medicine approaches, where therapeutic efficacy can be tailored to early, intermediate, or advanced PAD.

Beyond their utility for in vivo therapeutic evaluation, these graded artery-only models provide essential reference data for developing physiologically relevant in vitro platforms that mimic PAD. Most existing cell culture or organ-on-chip models of ischemia rely on arbitrary hypoxia levels or injury durations that are not benchmarked against validated in vivo conditions. By integrating our in vivo measurements of perfusion loss, oxidative stress, inflammatory cell composition, fibrosis, and regenerative timelines across mild, moderate, and severe ischemia, in vitro systems can be calibrated to replicate the microenvironment of each disease stage. Such calibration would enable more accurate modeling of oxygen and nutrient gradients, reactive oxygen species levels, extracellular matrix composition, and immune–vascular cell interactions. This alignment between in vivo and in vitro models has the potential to improve predictive accuracy for therapeutic testing and to accelerate translation of candidate interventions into clinically relevant settings.

Although our models demonstrate clear gradients in perfusion loss and tissue necrosis, interpretation of central nuclei density requires caution. Iliac and Fem/Pop groups exhibited substantial inter-animal variability in myofiber central nucleation, resulting in a non-monotonic pattern (Iliac ≈ Fem/Pop > > Femoral) that does not strictly follow the expected severity gradient. This variability likely reflects heterogeneous regional sampling within the gastrocnemius, asynchronous regenerative activation, and differences in the balance between degeneration and repair at the chronic 35-day endpoint. Accordingly, we do not interpret central nucleation as a direct severity marker, but instead emphasize perfusion, necrosis, and fibrosis as more robust indicators of graded ischemic injury. Consistent with this view, recent spatial mapping studies have shown that only discrete regions of the gastrocnemius reproducibly undergo ischemic damage and vascular remodeling after arterial ligation, highlighting substantial intra-muscle and inter-animal heterogeneity and explaining variability in histological and angiogenic readouts18. Collectively, these findings underscore that no single endpoint captures ischemia severity; rather, the models diverge across perfusion, structural, inflammatory, and oxidative dimensions.

Several limitations and future directions warrant consideration in this study. First, the current study focused on a 35-day endpoint, limiting insight into long-term tissue remodeling and functional recovery. Extended timepoints and repeated behavioral assessments are necessary to define chronic outcomes and the durability of therapeutic effects. Second, while our analyses included histological evaluation, vascular quantification, and oxidative stress markers, more granular mechanistic insight could be gained through single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, or proteomic profiling to map immune, stromal, and myogenic cell populations. Additionally, although we observed differences in capillary density and fibrosis, direct quantification of collateral vessel formation using micro-computed tomography or advanced 3D imaging techniques would more comprehensively characterize the vascular response. Finally, these models currently reflect an acute-onset ischemia, while human PAD typically develops gradually due to progressive atherosclerosis4,5,9,13. Incorporating models of chronic arterial narrowing or endothelial injury may enhance relevance to early PAD9,13. It will also be important to including both sexes to capture sex-specific differences in vascular and regenerative biology.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed and validated a set of artery-only rat hindlimb ischemia models that span increasing degrees of arterial occlusion and tissue remodeling, providing a scalable and physiologically relevant platform for PAD research. By selectively ligating and excising the iliac, femoral, and popliteal arteries while preserving venous outflow, these models generate reproducible gradients in perfusion loss, muscle degeneration, vascular remodeling, and regenerative responses. Rather than relying on a single measure of disease severity, this framework captures how clinical appearance, perfusion recovery, histological remodeling, inflammatory polarization, and oxidative stress diverge across stages of ischemia. Within this spectrum, the Fem/Pop model induces sustained severe ischemia and structural muscle remodeling consistent with advanced PAD, while earlier models better reflect mild and intermediate disease states. Together, these graded ischemia models provide a robust preclinical foundation for stage-specific testing of regenerative biomaterials, angiogenic and antifibrotic therapies, and for benchmarking in vitro systems against well-defined in vivo ischemic conditions.
