Large Fibroma of the Hard Palate: A Rare Clinical Presentation
Authors: Molka Ben Salah, Marwa Garma, Sameh Sioud, Habib Hamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFibromas of the oral cavity are benign proliferations of fibrous connective tissue, most commonly occurring in areas subject to chronic trauma, such as the buccal mucosa, tongue, and lips. Fibromas of the hard palate, particularly those of large size, are extremely rare. A 61‐year‐old female presented with a slow‐growing, painless lesion on the midline of the hard palate that caused discomfort during mastication. Clinical examination revealed a firm, pedunculated, pale pink, cauliflower‐like lesion measuring 2.5 × 3.6 cm. CT imaging showed no underlying bone involvement. An excisional biopsy was performed under local anesthesia, and hemostasis was achieved using ferric sulfate gel. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of fibroma. Postoperative healing was uneventful, with minimal discoloration at the surgical site. This case highlights the rare presentation of a large palatal fibroma, underscores the importance of considering uncommon sites in diagnosis, and demonstrates the safe use of ferric sulfate gel for intraoperative hemostasis.

Introduction

Fibromas of the oral cavity are benign proliferations of fibrous connective tissue and represent the most common soft‐tissue hyperplastic lesions encountered in clinical practice, accounting for 20% to 91.7% of cases [1, 2]. Oral irritation fibroma, often considered a reactive lesion rather than a true neoplasm, typically develops in response to chronic mechanical irritation or low grade local trauma, including but not limited to chronic irritation from plaque and calculus accumulation, chronic cheek trauma, hormonal imbalance, and ill‐fitting prostheses [3, 4]. Trauma from malocclusion was also reported [5].

It frequently affects sites prone to masticatory forces, such as the buccal mucosa, tongue, and lips in 41.9%, 12.9% and 32.2% of cases, respectively [3].

Clinically, irritation fibromas usually appear as slow‐growing, asymptomatic, sessile or pedunculated nodules, often measuring less than 1.5 cm in diameter [3, 6, 7]. Thus, larger lesions may raise diagnostic challenges and require careful evaluation.

Diagnosis is primarily clinical but must be confirmed histopathologically, as other lesions such as pyogenic granuloma, squamous papilloma, and ossifying fibroma can present with overlapping features. Imaging may also be necessary in selected cases, especially in anatomically challenging locations such as the palate, to exclude underlying bone involvement.

Treatment of fibromas typically involves surgical excision using a scalpel, electrocautery, or laser [3, 6, 7]. Furthermore, although evidence is limited, some studies have supported the use of ferric sulfate for controlling bleeding in soft tissues and during mucosal biopsies [8, 9].

The present case aims to contribute to the literature by describing an unusually large fibroma located in the palate, highlighting its atypical site and size as well as its surgical management using a ferric sulfate‐based hemostatic gel for controlling bleeding after excision.

Case History

A 61‐year‐old female, treated for hypothyroidism and a candidate for bisphosphonate therapy for osteoporosis, presented to the department of medicine and oral surgery with a chief complaint of a persistent oral lesion that had been gradually increasing in size over the past 3 years. She reported discomfort while chewing, particularly hard foods. There was no history of trauma to the affected area or any associated symptoms, such as pain, bleeding, or a burning sensation. No previous dental interventions were reported.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

On clinical examination, a single mass was observed arising from the midline of the hard palate and extending posteriorly and laterally (Figure 1). It was oval in shape, pale pink in color, with irregular surface and margins exhibiting a cauliflower‐like appearance, measuring 2.5 × 3.6 cm. On palpation, it was slightly pedunculated and firm in consistency. The lesion was not fluctuant, did not blanch on pressure, and was rubbery in consistency. The rest of the palatal mucosa appeared normal in appearance. Apart from poor oral hygiene, no abnormalities were noted. Neck examination was unremarkable, with no enlargement of cervical lymph nodes. The lesion exhibited no signs of malignancy: no underlying induration or suspicious heterogeneous macroscopic appearance.

Pre‐Operative intraoral photograph showing the lesion.

A head and neck CT scan revealed a well‐defined hypodense lesion measuring 2.5 × 3.6 cm overlying the mid‐palatal area. The underlying bony cortex was intact (Figure 2), ruling out peripheral ossifying fibroma.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Histopathological examination revealed a polyp lined by squamous epithelium exhibiting mild parakeratotic hyperkeratosis. The core of the polyp consisted of fibrous connective tissue rich in fibroblasts. This features were suggestive of Fibroma ruling out pyogenic granuloma and squamous papilloma. Thus, histopathological confirmation remains essential for definitive diagnosis.

After 3 months, no sign of recurrence was observed. Since then, the patient has been lost to follow‐up due to her medical condition. Local causes leading to this condition should be identified and removed to prevent recurrence. In our case, no direct trauma was identified but the patient was advised to undergo scaling and root planing to eliminate potential local irritative factors.

Surgical excision is the standard treatment approach for oral fibroma and other hyperplastic benign lesions. Nonetheless, if malignancy signs are identified, MRI to assess internal tissue characteristics and incisional biopsy followed by definitive resection after histopathological diagnosis represent the appropriate approach.

The ferric sulfate hemostatic gel was safely applied for adequate hemostasis. However, the short‐term follow‐up precluded assessment of whether the observed discoloration would fade. In addition, given its discreet positioning in the palate, this ferric sulfate adverse effect qualifies as a minor complication.

Disscussion

Fibromas are common benign fibrous overgrowths of the oral soft tissues, accounting for 20% to 91.7% of cases [3, 6, 10]. Kadeh H et al. detected irritation fibromas in 20% of 451 lesions in an Iranian population [2]. Soyele et al. observed fibromas in 39.7% of 78 cases in a Nigerian population [10]. In accordance with the results of the above studies, Ayaz M et al. reported fibrous hyperplasia as the predominant benign oral hyperplastic lesion, constituting 40% of total cases [11]. Rajeh et al. found the highest prevalence (91.7%) among 48 oral reactive hyperplastic lesions in a Yemeni population [1]. In the literature, fibromas are commonly termed irritation fibromas, focal fibrous hyperplasia, fibrous epulis, or fibroepithelial polyps [3]. In the present case, we preferred the designation «fibroma» due to the lack of certain identifiable direct chronic irritation or mechanical trauma.

These lesions are usually observed in regions exposed to chewing forces, mainly buccal mucosa, gingiva, and tongue. According to Halim et al., buccal mucosa was the primary site for oral fibromas (41.9%), followed by gingiva (32.3%) and lip/tongue (12.9% each) [3]. Toida et al. similarly noted tongue (n = 66) and buccal mucosa (n = 42) predominance, with only 7 palatal cases among 129 lesions [6]. Soyele OO et al. noted only one case on the palate out of 31 cases of Focal Fibrous Hyperplasia [10]. Conversely, Rajeh et al. in Yemen and Ayaz M et al. in Pakistan did not find palatal fibromas in their retrospective cross‐sectional study [1, 11].

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed and Scopus, over the last 20 years, from 2006 to 2026, for case reports or clinical images. The Boolean equation (oral fibroma OR irritation fibroma OR focal fibrous hyperplasia OR fibrous epulis) AND (palate OR palatal) identified only 10 reported cases. Thus, palatal location of fibromas remains exceptionally rare compared to the predominant involvement of buccal mucosa, gingiva, and tongue across multiple studies.

Fibromas usually measure less than 1.5 cm in greatest dimension [3, 6]. In our case, the fibroma was measuring 2.5 × 3.6 cm. Traumatic irritants may include calculus, foreign bodies, maladapted prostheses, dental malocclusion, and hormonal imbalance [3, 4]. The latter potential etiology has primarily been linked to menstrual fluctuations in the literature [3, 4]. Regarding our patient, hypothyroidism‐induced hormonal imbalances might have promoted fibroma development. Other authors have hypothesized that the tongue's upward movement may have caused low‐grade trauma to the palate over time, creating a persistent stimulation [13]. In our patient, no direct traumatic etiology was objectified with certainty; however, she was advised to undergo scaling and root planing to eliminate potential local irritative factors.

Fibromas are more common in women than in men. Halim D et al. found a strong female predominance up to 71% among oral fibroma patients [3]. Additionally, this female predilection was supported by Soyele OO et al. and Kadeh H et al. [4, 10]. By contrast, Okbah et al. reported a higher frequency of fibroma in the male gender than in females [4]. This strong female predominance might be attributed to the predisposing role of hormones in the formation of such lesions.

It is usually seen in the third and fourth decades of life as reported by several Asian studies: Kumar et al. in India, Ayaz M et al. in Pakistan and Kadeh H et al. in Iran [2, 11, 21]. But in our case, the patient was a 61‐year‐old female, which is consistent with the results reported by Soyele O et al. who found that oral fibroma lesions are more common in the fifth, sixth and seventh decades of life, although they can occur across a broad age range, from 10 to 70 years [10]. By contrast, pediatric presentations under age 10 have also been reported [18].

Surgical excision using scalpel is the most common and conventional procedure for fibromas [6, 7, 22]. Dental electrocautery is also a valuable surgical tool for precise tissue dissection and hemostasis [12, 19, 23]. In addition, several authors advocate diode laser use due to enhanced healing, decreased postoperative pain, and improved hemostasis [7, 13]. This report describes the use of a ferric sulfate hemostatic gel. This agent promotes hemostasis through protein agglutination, facilitating safe and efficient soft tissue management [8]. It is commonly employed in endodontic procedures for hemostasis during pulpotomy or in restorative dentistry for gingival retraction [8, 9]. The available literature offers limited evidence on the effectiveness of ferric sulfate for controlling intraoperative bleeding in oral surgery, highlighting the need for further investigations to substantiate these findings. However, its application is advised for mucosal lacerations and gingival hemorrhage [9]. In the presented case, the ferric sulfate agent provided effective hemostatic control. Though, according to some reports, ferric sulfate may occasionally lead to mucosal temporary discoloration, as observed in our case, and can delay wound healing if residues are left in contact with bone [8, 24]. Furthermore, the limited observation period in our case precludes asserting the transient nature of this discoloration.

Definitive diagnosis of fibroma is established by histopathological examination of the excised lesion, which in our case showed a polyp lined by squamous epithelium exhibiting mild parakeratotic hyperkeratosis. The core of the polyp consisted of fibrous connective tissue rich in fibroblasts.

Recurrence is rare and typically attributed to incomplete excision, persistence of etiologic factors, or reinjury of the site [5, 13, 22]. However, short‐term follow‐up in this case precludes definitive exclusion of recurrence.
