Advancing Support for Electronic Cigarette Control Policies in the United States: The Impact of Theory-Based Messaging Strategies
Authors: Julia M. Alber, Lorraine Jackson, Ruta Viela, Owen Jessup, Lauren Sandoval, Katharine Santilena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDespite public health efforts to educate on the harms of e-cigarettes, they remain popular in the United States (U.S.) particularly among youth and young adults. Tobacco control policies are needed to more effectively decrease access among these populations. This study focused on testing theory-based messages to promote public support for e-cigarette control policies among a sample of U.S. adults.MethodsA survey panel company was used to recruit U.S. adults (N = 301) for an online survey in April 2021. Participants were randomly exposed to four messages each from one of three conditions: 1) attitude-based messages, 2) perceived norms-based messages, and 3) control messages. After viewing each message, participants rated the message on perceived effectiveness measures. Upon viewing all messages, participants rated support for e-cigarette control policies.ResultsCompared to the control condition, the attitudes-based condition was associated with higher perceived effectiveness (p < .01) and positively related to higher support for taxing e-cigarettes (p = .01).ConclusionsResults may inform research and practice into the types of communication strategies that best resonate with the public and are more likely to increase support for e-cigarette control policies. However, research is needed to better understand the impact of theory-based messages on policy support over time.

Introduction

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) are the most commonly used tobacco product among young people in the United States (U.S.) (Office of the Surgeon General, 2018). Exposure during youth can harm the developing brain and increase the risk for traditional cigarette use (Office of the Surgeon General, 2018). Despite these risks, e-cigarettes remain popular due to their appealing flavors and their small size, reduced odor, and lack of visible exhaust that allow discreet use (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022). In 2022, over 2.5 million U.S. youth reported past 30-day use (Cooper et al., 2022). Given the extent of use and health implications, it is critical to implement strategies to decrease access.

U.S. cities, counties, states, and federal agencies can adopt varying e-cigarette policies. One framework for these policies is the “four P's” of marketing: product, price, promotion, and place (Warner et al., 2022). Product policies address packaging, color, or size. For example, Michigan banned flavored e-cigarettes while California banned the sale of all flavored tobacco products (State of Michigan Health and Human Services, 2019; California Senate, 2020). Another strategy is to address the “price.” Some states have instituted laws that put an excise, or special tax, on e-cigarette products (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021). Promotion policies may ban marketing or develop public health campaigns. Place policies restrict where e-cigarettes are sold, from limiting sales near schools to banning e-cigarette sales entirely, as Arroyo Grande, CA, did in 2025 (City of Arroyo Grande, 2025). Despite these efforts, broader and more comprehensive policies are still needed nationwide.

Public and stakeholder support is critical for adopting and enforcing tobacco control policies, as broad backing can create pressure across local, state, and federal levels (Winickoff et al., 2016). The Integrated Behavioral Model (IBM) offers a framework to understand factors influencing support for e-cigarette policies. IBM posits that behavior, such as supporting a health policy through advocacy action or voting, is shaped by three constructs: attitudes, perceived norms, and perceived control (Glanz et al., 2015). Attitudes are favorable or unfavorable beliefs an individual holds towards a behavior. For example, beliefs about the effects e-cigarette control policies may have on the health of the public may be valued as favorably or unfavorably, representing an attitude. The second construct, perceived norms, refers to what the individual believes their family, friends, or doctors would think of them engaging in a behavior. This could be beliefs about whether they think their friends and family may support e-cigarette control policies. The final construct, perceived control, is an individual's perception of the degree of difficulty or ease of carrying out that behavior due to environmental constraints. Perceived control can be determined by examining the extent to which the individual believes certain actions are within their own control. For example, believing that they can easily find and afford to buy e-cigarettes in their area.

IBM has previously been used to understand factors that affect e-cigarette policy support. In a cross-sectional survey, attitudes and perceived norms were found to be significantly related to support for some types of e-cigarette policies (Alber et al., 2021). Because policy support is not a direct behavior, we applied the IBM by conceptualizing support intentions as a proxy for action, consistent with prior applications in contexts where advocacy or voting behaviors are difficult to measure (Alber et al., 2021). There is a need to understand how the model could be applied to policy-focused messages.

Public support for health-related policies is critical for promoting policy adoption and compliance (Majmundar et al., 2019). Yet, there is limited research on how to promote health-related policy support using messaging. Most existing studies have centered around promoting negative attitudes towards tobacco-related products among the public and specific populations (Cornacchione Ross et al., 2017). However, there is some research on the effects of using media and health communication strategies for promoting health-related policies in general. For example, in a literature review of storytelling interventions, researchers found evidence that these types of interventions may have positive impacts on health-related policies (Fadlallah et al., 2019). Another systematic review found that media campaigns may have a positive effect on increasing policymakers' awareness and creating tools to influence policy formation; however, there was a lack of evidence on their overall effect (Bou-Karroum et al., 2017). Previous research found that a theory-based messaging campaign showed increased compliance towards a tobacco-free policy (Fallin et al., 2012; Rayens et al., 2015).

While there has been research on public reactions to policy changes and exposure to e-cigarette advertising on social media (Collins et al., 2019), factors underlying policy support are not well understood (Majmundar et al., 2019). Additionally, while there has been progress in passing some e-cigarette control policies, these are not all comprehensive policies and are likely to receive significant pushback from interested groups (e.g., tobacco retailers, tobacco industry). This could result in stakeholders finding loopholes in the current laws or repealing new laws. For example, U.S. Food and Drug Administration prohibits prefilled e-cigarette cartilage in any flavor but menthol and tobacco flavors are exempt (Woodcock, 2021). Going forward, advocacy among the public for e-cigarette control policies will be necessary to increase pressure among stakeholders to pass comprehensive policies. Thus, there is a need to examine and understand: What communication strategies and message features seem to best resonate with the public to increase support for e-cigarette control policies?

Purpose of study

The overall purpose of this study is to develop and test messages that increase support for e-cigarette control policies aimed at increasing the difficulty for youth to acquire e-cigarettes (e.g., flavor bans, restricting access, increased local taxes, etc.). The specific aims of this study include comparing perceived effectiveness and e-cigarette policy support between attitudes-based messages, perceived norms-based messages, and control messages.

Methods

Procedures

An online survey panel (Qualtrics Survey Panels) was used to recruit a sample of U.S. adults in April 2021. Eligibility criteria included: 1) being 18 years or older, and 2) currently living in the U.S. Quota sampling was used to ensure the sample was representative of the U.S. population on sex, race, and ethnicity (United States Census Bureau, 2019). The panel survey company directly provides incentives to the participants. Approval from California Polytechnic State University's Institutional Review Board, including confirmation that it met the required protections of human subjects, was received prior to data collection.

In the online survey, participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: 1) attitudes-based messages, 2) perceived norms-based messages, and 3) control messages. This experimental approach is often used in message testing studies (Kim and Cappella, 2019). Within each condition, participants viewed four messages. Each message used the same four backgrounds, non-animated images, and font. Messages within each condition were shown in random order (Kim and Cappella, 2019). Measures

Participants completed demographic questions, including an open-ended age question and multiple-choice questions that assessed annual income, education, sex, and race/ethnicity adapted from prior sources (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2014; National Center for Health Statistics, 2015; Alber et al., 2021). Education level was coded as 0) high school or less and 1) above high school, while annual income was coded as 0) $49,999 or less and 1) $50,000 or above. Current smoking status and e-cigarette use were measured by asking participants if they smoke cigarettes or used e-cigarettes every day, some days, or not at all. Both current smoking status and e-cigarette use were coded for analysis as 0) not at all and 1) every day or some days.

After viewing, participants gave feedback on each message by providing an overall message rating and responding to perceived effectiveness measures (Alber et al., 2020). The overall message rating measure asked participants “How much do you like or dislike this message?” on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1) dislike a great deal to 7) like a great deal. Perceived effectiveness measures included eight items related to the persuasiveness of the messages (e.g., “this message was persuasive,” “this message made me want to support the ban of e-cigarettes”) on a Likert scale from 1) strongly disagree to 7) strongly agree. The items were averaged to create an overall perceived effectiveness score for each message. For analysis, the perceived effectiveness score (Cronbach's α = 0.96) and overall message rating (Cronbach's α = 0.93) were averaged across the four messages within each condition to create a score for each condition. Higher values reflected higher message ratings and perceived effectiveness.

Support for e-cigarette policies was measured across 10 items that asked participants to indicate their level of agreement to statements about policies on a 7-point Likert scale from 1) strongly disagree to 7) strongly agree. Each item and their alignment with the four P's of marketing are listed in Appendix B. The items were averaged together to create an overall policy support score (Cronbach's α = 0.93). A higher value reflected more support for e-cigarette control policies.

Statistical analysis

Frequency and central tendency statistics were calculated for participant characteristics (e.g., age, sex) and outcome variables (i.e., overall message rating, perceived effectiveness, policy support) by condition. Multiple linear regressions were conducted to explore which conditions (i.e., attitudes-based versus control, perceived norms-based versus control) were associated with message rating, perceived effectiveness, and policy support while controlling for individual characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education, income, current smoking status, e-cigarette use, race/ethnicity; Czaplicki et al., 2020). Listwise deletion was used to handle missing data. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics 26 was used to analyze the data.

Support for E-cigarette policies

Central tendency statistics for all policy outcomes can be found in Appendix B. Regression analyses did not reveal that any condition was related to the overall policy support score. Current e-cigarette user (b = −0.20, p < .01), age (b = 0.26, p < .01), and being female (b = −0.12, p = .03) were significantly related to overall e-cigarette policy support, F(10,278) = 6.31, p < .01, R2 = 0.18. Only one condition was significantly related to one individual policy. The attitudes-based message condition (b = 0.16, p < .01) was positively related to higher support for local governments taxing e-cigarettes, F (10,278) = 5.52, R2 = 0.16, p < .01.

Discussion

Overall, this study provided some evidence that attitudes-based messages may be better perceived by audiences and have the potential to impact policy support. However, more robust, long-term studies are needed to further understand the utility of IBM in developing effective messages for e-cigarette control policy support. Despite limitations to the study, including the cross-sectional design and short-term exposure to messages, this study provides a foundation for future research to further develop and test theory-based messages aimed at increasing public support for health-related policies.

Results from this study found that using attitudes-based messages related to higher perceived effectiveness while perceived norms-based messages did not. This is similar to other studies that have used messages examining attitudes and social factors that can influence e-cigarette beliefs. For example, one study found that a “health” condition that focused on the negative effects of e-cigarettes related to more positive attitudes towards e-cigarettes as an alternative to traditional cigarettes (Pokhrel et al., 2018). However, the researchers found the “social” condition, not the “health” condition, led to lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes compared to traditional cigarettes. While the outcome variable differed, the conditions were similar and the outcomes both reflected a perception towards e-cigarettes. In another study, researchers found that lower social acceptance of e-cigarettes and increased negative attitudes were related to supporting the limitation of places where e-cigarettes could be purchased and used (Alber et al., 2021).

The current study found that the attitudes-based messages had higher perceived effectiveness scores compared to the perceived norms-based messages. There are a few explanations why attitude-based messages about potential harms (e.g., e-cigarettes are bad for the environment) may be perceived as more persuasive than socially focused messages. One possibility is that perceived norm-based messages (e.g., “most scientists think e-cigarettes are bad for the environment ”) may feel externally imposed, potentially triggering psychological reactance (i.e., a motivation to resist persuasion when autonomy feels threatened). Another possible explanation is recipients might interpret perceived norm-based messages as strategic persuasion attempts (“They are trying to make me conform”), while attitude-based statements may seem more like an informational claim about facts. This potential difference in perceived intent could influence resistance to persuasion and perceived effectiveness.

While significantly related to higher perceived effectiveness, the attitudes-based condition was only significantly related to one policy and not overall policy support. Regarding the “four P's” of marketing, the local government's e-cigarette tax represents “price.” While this may explain greater support for price-related policies, other factors could account for the nonsignificant results for other policy types. First, the study may have been underpowered to detect small effects. In a meta-analysis of vaping prevention messages, effect sizes were moderate for beliefs (d = 0.14–0.39) but higher for perceived effectiveness (d = 0.55–0.57; Ma et al., 2023). Because effect sizes for e-cigarette messaging are often in this range, our study's sample size may not have been sufficient to detect comparable effects across multiple policy outcomes. However, the sample size was similar to previous tobacco control messaging studies that have ranged from 18 to 120 evaluators per message (Kim and Cappella, 2019). Second, measuring outcomes in a single setting (i.e., a one-time survey) limited our ability to assess long-term effects. Although perceived effectiveness has been shown to predict intentions and behavior (Cappella, 2018), without follow-up data we could not evaluate whether these effects persisted over time. Third, the non-significant findings could have been due to a ceiling effect given the high level of support for the policies and that the sample was mostly non-tobacco users. Fourth, the limited variance between messages and the control condition may have reduced their impact. To help control for variation, the same images were used, and the policy mentioned was consistent across messages. Greater variation in the wording of text may have led to more significant findings such as including information about the level of policy (e.g., local, state, federal) and who was promoting or supporting it.

Past research has emphasized the importance of perceived credibility in message design for tobacco control (Kowitt et al., 2016; Lazard et al., 2018). For example, one study found that the use of embedded links to credible sources was preferred by participants for online tobacco education (Lazard et al., 2018). Additionally, giving additional information, such as explaining the impact of e-cigarettes or providing specific statistics, may provide additional context and rationale for the audience to make judgments on policy support. One study found that using different types of messages, such as risk messages and compensation messages, was effective in persuading an audience about the regulation of nicotine products (Duong et al., 2021). In another experimental study, researchers exposed participants to messages about different types of harm related to smoking and vaping (Grummon et al., 2020). It is possible adding certain types of content (e.g., risk messages) in combination with the IBM to guide message development may have a more significant impact on policy support.

Additionally, there may have been participant factors not accounted for in the analyses. For example, social media use and engagement in social issues in general may have been factors that affected policy support responses. Researchers found a relationship between online engagement and lower support for e-cigarette policies (Majmundar et al., 2019). The researchers did not find this relationship with other factors, such as tobacco marketing exposure. This current study found that certain factors, such as e-cigarette use, were related to policy support which echoes some existing research. For example, a previous study found that support for e-cigarette regulation was stronger among current smokers (Wackowski and Delnevo, 2015). Future research could incorporate additional control variables into the analyses to better understand other factors that may impact support of e-cigarette control policies.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. This study used a cross-sectional study method and did not examine long-term effects. Additionally, public support was not measured directly as an action or behavior rather their overall support of the behavior. Participants were exposed to messages only once, which may have reduced impact. The survey panel, though quota-sampled for certain demographics, may not represent all U.S. adults. One strength is that this study did apply a theory that has been previously tested for tobacco prevention and control. Finally, the control condition used in this study included information about e-cigarette policy support and may not have served as a true control. Using a control condition, such as showing no messages or messages on an unrelated topic, may have yielded different results. Finally, the perceived effectiveness measure combined message perceptions and effects perceptions into one measure rather than measuring them separately. This may limit the ability to interpret the results related to perceptions of the message itself and perceptions of the effects of those messages (Noar et al., 2020). More research that includes additional message exposure and long-term evaluations are needed to better understand the effectiveness of using theory-based messaging to promote support for e-cigarette control policies.

Conclusions

Given the popularity of e-cigarette use in the U.S. among youth and the potential negative health implications of e-cigarette use, it is critical to continue to promote strategies that can decrease access and use. Policies at the local, state, and federal level have the potential to make significant, wide-scale impacts. However, there is limited research on how theory and messaging can be used to increase public support for these types of policies. This study provided evidence that attitudes-based messages may be perceived better than perceived norms-based messages. The attitudes-based message increased support for local e-cigarette taxes (a “price” policy), but more long-term research with repeated exposure is needed to see if messaging can influence support across all four P's of marketing. Furthermore, this study suggests that some policies receive more general support than others (e.g., warning labels about risks versus banning all sales of e-cigarettes). Before introducing controversial policies to the public, initially focusing on policies that fall within the public's latitude of acceptance or latitude of noncommitment may provide opportunities for initiating persuasion regarding policy support. This study contributes to the literature exploring how the IBM can be applied to health messages, particularly regarding e-cigarette public policy. Health messages can play an integral role in influencing public policy support and should continue to be explored with contemporary audiences.
