Aging shifts hippocampal reactivation from selective reinstatement to category-level misbinding during episodic memory
Authors: Destaw B Mekbib, Ian M McDonough
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpisodic memory is the ability to bind elements of an experience into coherent associations. This ability declines with age, often producing errors that can interfere with daily life. Memory distortions might arise from unstable hippocampal representations that cannot be maintained across encoding, storage, and later retrieval. However, whether aging disrupts the temporal continuity of these representations across memory phases has not been directly tested. Here, adults aged 20–74 completed functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scanning during an associative memory task involving face–object and face–scene pairings, followed by post-encoding rest and a cued retrieval test. Using multivoxel correlation structure analyses, we quantified hippocampal pattern similarity across each memory stage. Increasing age was associated with increased category errors and reduced representational similarity across all transitions. Higher encoding–retrieval similarity predicted better associative accuracy in younger adults and was less predictive in middle-aged and diminished in older adults. Uniquely among older adults, greater cross-phase similarity predicted elevated category-level binding errors, suggesting a shift from selective reactivation to misbinding with age. Baseline hippocampal organization and encoding-specific connectivity supported accurate reinstatement only in younger adults. Aging disrupts hippocampal stability, possibly representing a core mechanism of age-related decline in associative memory across the adult lifespan.Keywords: episodic memory, fMRI, hippocampus, recapitulation, representational similarity

Introduction

Episodic memory is the ability to encode, store, and retrieve associations among multiple elements of past experiences and critically depends on the hippocampus (Takashima et al. 2006; Mekbib and McDonough 2025). fMRI studies demonstrate that patterns of hippocampal activation observed during encoding re-occur during both post-encoding rest and retrieval, possibly reflecting neural reinstatement processes (Ritchey et al. 2013; Lutz et al. 2024). The present study investigated how functional connectivity in the hippocampus during these memory stages contributes to both stabilized and distorted memory traces in young, middle-aged, and older adults.

Prior work in young adults has revealed similarity in hippocampal multivoxel patterns between encoding and post-encoding rest (Gruber et al. 2016; Schapiro et al. 2018; Tambini and D’Esposito 2020; King et al. 2022). These similarity patterns often predict subsequent associative memory performance, suggesting that spontaneous reactivation of newly encoded representations during rest supports memory consolidation. Likewise, encoding–retrieval neural similarity has been linked to memory performance, indicating that reinstating encoding-related activation can support successful memory reconstruction (Ritchey et al. 2013; Tompary et al. 2016a). However, the role of the post-encoding rest to retrieval transition has not been assessed (Mekbib and McDonough 2025). This transition may not simply reflect the passage of time, but how representations that undergo offline processing during rest are subsequently expressed during retrieval. Thus, post-encoding rest–retrieval similarity provides a novel window into whether representations reactivated during post-encoding rest are preserved, reorganized, or transformed before being accessed at retrieval. Examining this transition therefore allows us to determine whether memory-related representations remain stable across phases or evolve in ways that may contribute to memory errors.

Aging provides a natural test for examining these mechanisms. Aging is associated with hippocampal atrophy and network reorganization that can alter the contributions of hippocampally mediated memory processes (Raz and Rodrigue 2006). Specifically, the atrophy–compensation hypothesis proposes that age-related structural decline may be accompanied by compensatory changes in brain function to support cognition (Park and Reuter-Lorenz 2009). Older adults also exhibit reduced distinctiveness of neural representation and weaker hippocampal engagement during memory processing (Raz and Rodrigue 2006; Pauley et al. 2023). For example, older adults show reduced encoding–retrieval neural similarity, suggesting diminished reinstatement of encoding-related representations during retrieval (Hill et al. 2021; Chamberlain et al. 2022).

These neural alterations parallel well-documented memory declines across the adult lifespan. Aging disproportionately impairs associative memory—the ability to remember relationships between features in an event—while memory for single features or items remains relatively preserved (Naveh-Benjamin 2000; Badham et al. 2024). According to the Associative Deficit Hypothesis, older adults show reduced efficiency in forming and binding new associations, in part due to disruptions in attentional control and medial temporal lobe function, including the hippocampus (Naveh-Benjamin 2000; Ranganath and Ritchey 2012). In contrast, the Hyper-binding Hypothesis proposes that reduced attentional control can lead to the encoding of excessive or irrelevant details, resulting in broader or less selective associative representations and increased susceptibility to memory errors (Campbell et al. 2010). For example, measures of attentional control are related to memory performance (St-Laurent et al. 2014; Wixted et al. 2018), and may therefore contribute to age-related differences in how newly learned associative representations are formed and expressed.

As stated earlier, functional organization may contribute to age-related differences in memory that impact all stages of memory processing. One approach for assessing regional organization is regional homogeneity (ReHo). ReHo reflects the local synchronization of neural activity. By assessing intrinsic functional organization within the hippocampus prior to or during encoding, one can assess how variability in such organization influences how memory representations are formed and subsequently expressed across phases.

To better understand how the neural representation changes across memory stages during adulthood, we used fMRI with multivoxel correlation structure (MVCS) analyses to examine how hippocampal patterns observed during (i) encoding are recapitulated during post-encoding rest and (ii) encoding and post-encoding rest are reinstated during retrieval across the adult lifespan. Participants encoded face–object and face–scene pairs, completed a post-encoding rest period, and subsequently performed a cued associative memory test. We hypothesized that (i) hippocampal representational similarity across encoding, post-encoding rest, and retrieval would decline with age, reflecting reduced stability and specificity of mnemonic representations; (ii) greater representational overlap among memory phases would predict higher associative memory accuracy; and (3) reduced or non-specific similarity would be associated with increased associative errors, particularly in older adults. Additionally, we tested whether hippocampal atrophy, baseline hippocampal organization, encoding quality, or attentional control might explain age-differences in hippocampal reinstatement patterns and associative memory performance.

Materials and methods

Participants

Eighty right-handed adults between 18 and 74 years of age were initially enrolled, including 20 younger adults, 28 middle-aged adults, and 32 older adults (see Table S1 for demographics). Participants completed the St. Louis University Mental Status (M = 26.49, range = 21–30). All participants had between 12 and 18 years of education and were excluded if they reported neurological or psychiatric disorders, substance abuse, or MRI contraindications. Participants were also excluded if more than 50% of volumes in any run were censored, resulting in the exclusion of 19 subjects (3 younger, 7 middle-aged, 9 older). For details, see Supplementary Materials and Table S2. The final sample consisted of 61 adults: 17 younger (18–30 years), 21 middle-aged (50–60 years), and 23 older adults (61–74 years). This age distribution reflects the study design, with younger adults recruited as a baseline group and middle-aged and older adults recruited as part of a cognitively normal cohort at varying dementia risk.

Study procedure

The procedures have been previously reported (McDonough et al. 2019). Data collection was distributed across two to three in-person sessions. In the first session, participants provided informed consent, undertook cognitive testing and health assessments. A second session was scheduled for the completion of health-history surveys and psychosocial questionnaires. The final session involved magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning in which participants completed a baseline resting-state scan (R1), followed by two associative encoding runs (EN1, EN2). After the acquisition of high-resolution T1-weighted structural images, a second resting-state scan (R2) was conducted. The session concluded with two retrieval runs (RE1, RE2). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Alabama (#16–011-ME).

Magnetic resonance imaging procedure

Resting-state

Each resting-state scan lasted for 5 min (175 volumes). During these scans, participants were instructed to close their eyes, remain awake, and stay as still as possible.

Paired associative encoding

All participants completed EN tasks involving face-object and face-scene stimuli (Fig. 1). During each trial, an emotionally neutral face was displayed above either an object or a scene for 3 s. To facilitate learning, participants were instructed to imagine the person interacting with the object or within the scene. After viewing each pair, participants rated how likely they were to remember the pair using a three-point scale: likely to remember, possibly will remember, or unlikely to remember. Inter-trial intervals were separated by a jittered fixation cross ranging from 1.72 to 17.20 s (mean = 4.6 s). This phase consisted of two runs, each with 32 trials per run, lasting ~8 min.

Overview of the experimental paradigm. Participants completed a 5-min baseline resting-state scan followed by two associative encoding runs and a 6-min T1-weighted anatomical scan, which provided a brief period during which recently learned associations could spontaneously reactivate prior to the post-encoding rest scan. Participants then completed a 5-min post- encoding resting-state scan and two 4 min retrieval cue scans. During encoding, face–object and face–scene pairs were shown for 3 s each, and participants imagined the person interacting with the item before making a three-point memory rating. Trials were separated by jittered fixation intervals and distributed across two 8-min runs. During retrieval, each studied face cue was presented with four previously viewed pictures (two objects and two scenes), with all lures drawn from pairs associated with different faces. Participants withheld responses when unable to recall the target, enabling measurement of complete forgetting. Retrieval responses were categorized as correct, incorrect-same category, incorrect-different category, or no response, yielding three behavioral measures: total correct, category error bias, and forgetting.

Associative memory retrieval

The retrieval task consisted of 64 trials lasting 5.16 s. In each trial, a previously seen face was presented and participants were asked to identify the correct associated object or scene from four response options. The options consisted of two previously viewed object images and two previously viewed scene images. All four images were previously seen, and each could serve as either a correct match or a lure across different trials. Each of the image options was seen multiple times such that a given image might be a lure on one memory trial, but a correct answer on a different memory trial. Therefore, correct performance required recollection of the specific face-item pairing during EN, making task accuracy a reflection of associative recollection. Participants were told to respond while the images were on the screen but could respond during the fixation period if needed. The intertrial intervals ranged from 1.72 to 10.32 s (mean = 3.7 s). The memory test was divided into two runs, lasting for 5 min.

Participants’ responses during the associative memory retrieval task were classified as correct, incorrect, or no response. Incorrect responses were further subdivided into related errors, in which the chosen lure matched the target category (eg selecting an object lure when the correct answer was an object), and unrelated errors, in which the chosen lure was from the non-target category (eg selecting a scene when the correct answer was an object). Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition and preprocessing

MRI data were acquired using a 3 T Siemens PRISMA scanner at the UAB Civitan International Neuroimaging Laboratory. High-resolution T1-weighted structural MP2RAGE scans were acquired using (parallel acquisition acceleration type = GRAPPA; acceleration factor = 3, TR = 5000 ms, TE = 2.93 ms, TI1 = 700 ms, TI2 = 2030 ms, flip angle 1 = 4°, flip angle 2 = 5°, Field of View of 256 mm with a matrix size of 256 × 256 × 176 slices, in-plane resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 mm2). The functional scans used T2*-weighted EPI sequences (interleaved axial slices, 2.5 mm thickness, TR = 1720 ms, TE = 35.8 ms, flip angle = 73°, FOV of 260 mm with a matrix = 104 × 104×56 slices, in-plane resolution = 2.5 × 2.5 mm2, multi-band acceleration factor = 4).

The imaging data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK). High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were reoriented to match the orientation of the standard Montreal Neurological Institute 152 template and segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid tissue classes using SPM’s unified segmentation approach. For the functional data, preprocessing was performed separately for each of the six functional runs. The functional volumes were corrected for head motion using a rigid-body realignment algorithm, and six motion parameters (three translations and three rotations) were estimated for use in nuisance regression during subsequent denoising steps. Each participant’s mean functional image across all runs was then co-registered to their own T1-weighted anatomical image using normalized mutual information to ensure precise alignment. All functional runs were resampled into the same native anatomical space and orientation and remained in native space. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio while minimizing spatial blurring, functional data were modestly smoothed using a 3 mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. Low-frequency signal drifts below 0.008 Hz were removed from all runs.

Multivoxel correlation structure analyses

MVCS analyses were used to quantify hippocampal multivoxel patterns within the three memory phases. Bilateral hippocampi were defined anatomically using FreeSurfer v6.0, and additional preprocessing steps were applied to remove nuisance signals from the BOLD data (see Supplementary Material). For each memory phase and participant, we extracted the time series from all n hippocampal voxels and computed pairwise Pearson correlations between all voxel time courses, yielding an n × n MVCS matrix for each memory phase. Correlation values were Fisher z-normalized to improve normality. Each MVCS matrix is interpreted as reflecting the multivoxel representational structure of the hippocampus during a given phase (Tambini and Davachi 2013; King et al. 2022). To quantify similarity across phases, we computed the representational similarity index (RSI) by correlating MVCS matrices between pairs of phases (EN–R2, R2–RE, EN–RE). Higher RSI values reflect greater similarity in hippocampal multivoxel structure across memory phases. Analyses were conducted separately for each phase pair to allow examination of phase-specific effects. Analyses between hippocampal-cortical RSI are reported in the Supplementary Material.

Brain–behavior relationships

Statistical analyses investigating brain-behavior relationships were implemented in R (version 4.4.1). Multiple regression models were used for all analyses using behavior as the outcome variable, RSI, age (continuous), and their interaction as predictors. Separate models were conducted for each RSI pair (EN–R2, R2–RE, EN–RE). Follow-up analyses of significant interactions were conducted using simple slopes.

Results

Behavioral results

Age strongly predicted the proportion of total correct trials (r = −0.625, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2A), replicating findings of lower associative memory performance with increasing age. Young adults (M = 0.36, SD = 0.02, Table S1) correctly identified a greater proportion of trials than both middle-aged (M = 0.24, SD = 0.02; t = 4.388, P = 0.0001; Fig. 2B; Table S1) and older adults (M = 0.20, SD = 0.02, Table S1; t = 5.830, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2B). Middle-aged adults did not differ significantly from older adults (t = 1.435, P = 0.33; Fig. 2B). Age did not significantly predict category error bias (r = 0.182, P = 0.161; Fig. 2C) or forgetting (r = 0.029, P = 0.826; Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that age-related differences reflect a general reduction in associative memory accuracy rather than a reliable shift toward a specific type of error.

Behavioral outcomes. (A) Total correct associative memory responses declined with increasing age. (B) Younger adults showed higher accuracy than middle-aged and older adults, who did not differ from each other. (C) Forgetting rates did not differ significantly across age groups. (D) Category error bias likewise showed no reliable age differences.

Phase-matched hippocampal pattern similarity

A repeated-measures ANCOVA with memory phase as a within-subject factor and age as a continuous covariate revealed a main effect of phase on RSI, F(2,118) = 133.024, P < 0.001 (Fig. 3A). EN–RE similarity (M = 0.543, SD = 0.013) was greater than both EN–R2 (M = 0.424, SD = 0.013; t = 10.748, P < 0.0001) and R2–RE similarity (M = 0.399, SD = 0.012; t = 15.243, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3B). Age significantly predicted RSI, F(1,59) = 9.188, P = 0.0036, with higher age associated with lower hippocampal RSI across memory phases (Fig. 3C). Significant negative correlations were observed between age and EN–R2 (r = −0.31, P < 0.001), EN–RE (r = −0.28, P < 0.001), and R2–RE similarity (r = −0.41, P < 0.001), indicating reduced representational overlap with increasing age. Because the sampled age distribution included a gap between ~30–50 years, these continuous age effects should not be interpreted as evidence of smooth linear lifespan trajectories. Thus, we additionally examined RSI differences using categorical age groups (young adults: 20–30, middle-aged adults: 50–63, older adults: 63–74). These analyses produced similar patterns, with young adults generally showing higher RSI than older adults across memory phases, whereas differences between middle-aged and older adults were less consistent (Fig. S1). The phase × age interaction was not significant, F(2,118) = 1.014, P = 0.366, suggesting a general rather than phase-specific impact of age on hippocampal representational similarity. For the cortical analyses, EN-R2 hippocampus-occipital RSI also reliably declined with age (Fig. S2).

(B) RSIs were highest between encoding and retrieval, and lower between encoding–rest and rest–retrieval. (C) Hippocampal RSIs declined with age across all phase comparisons. Hippocampal multivoxel pattern similarity as a predictor of memory performance

In a series of regression analyses, we found EN–RE RSI significantly predicted total accuracy (β = 0.023, P = 0.044; Fig. 4A). The age x EN–RE interaction approached significance (β = −0.021; P = 0.058; Fig. 4B), indicating that the benefit of higher RSI on total accuracy diminished across the adult lifespan. Younger adults showed the strongest positive association between RSI and total accuracy (β = 0.04, P = 0.01), middle-aged adults showed a weaker effect (β = 0.02, P = 0.04), and older adults showed a non-significant trend (β = 0.001, P = 0.093).

(C) Higher EN–RE RSI was linked to lower rates of forgetting in younger adults, but not in middle-aged or older adults. Given that the total correct score does not account for the type of error being committed (same category, different category, or forgetting), we tested whether any of the age-related decreases in accuracy could be explained by the type of error committed. First, we observed a significant interaction between age and EN–RE RSI in predicting forgetting (β = 0.04, P = 0.008). Higher EN–RE RSI was associated with lower forgetting rates in young adults (β = −0.06, P < 0.0001), but these trends were not observed either in middle-aged (β = −0.02, P > 0.17) or older adults (β = 0.02, P > 0.32; Fig. 4C). Second, we also observed a significant interaction with age for category error bias across the three memory phases: EN–R2 (β = −0.037 = 0.006), EN–RE (β = −0.029, P = 0.012), and R2–RE (β = −0.034, P = 0.008). Only among older adults, higher hippocampal RSI was associated with a greater different-category error and this bias was exacerbated in later stages of memory: EN–R2 (β = −0.03, P = 0.02; Fig. 4D), EN–RE (β −0.04, P = 0.01; Fig. 4E), and R2–RE (β = −0.05, P < 0.001; Fig. 4F). For the cortical analyses, hippocampus-insula RSI and hippocampus–precuneus RSI interacted with age to predict multiple performance measures in the same direction as the hippocampal RSI, mostly between EN-R2 phases (Fig. S3).

Testing the atrophy-compensation hypothesis

We used hippocampal volume as a proxy for atrophy and conducted regression analyses with hippocampal RSI as the outcome measure and hippocampal volume, age, and their interaction as the independent variables. If atrophy is related to hippocampal RSI, we expected hippocampal volume to be correlated with RSI to a greater extent in older than younger adults. If individual differences rather than atrophy per se are related to hippocampal RSI, we expected hippocampal volume to be related to RSI across all ages.

Hippocampal volume decreased with age (r = −0.55, P < 0.0001). Hippocampal volume predicted R2–RE RSI (β = 0.287, P = 0.045), but not EN–R2 RSI (β = 0.201, P = 0.182), EN–RE RSI (β = 0.16, P = 0.292), nor interactions with age across memory phases (β ≤ 0.011, P ≥ 0.337). This pattern indicates that hippocampal volume is modestly related to later-stage representational similarity but the relationship between hippocampal volume and RSI does not vary with age (Fig. S4).

Because hippocampal volume decreased with age and was related to RSI in some phases, we next tested whether controlling for hippocampal volume explained the effects of RSI on memory outcomes. When controlling for hippocampal volume, the previously observed relationships between RSI and memory outcomes remained unchanged except for a slight reduction in effect size with total accuracy (EN–RE main effect: β = 0.022, P = 0.052; EN–RE x age interaction effect: β = −0.021, P = 0.058). The age and RSI interaction continued to predict category error bias across phases (EN–R2: β = −0.037, P = 0.007; EN–RE: β = −0.028, P = 0.013; R2–RE: β = −0.034, P = 0.009) and forgetting (β = 0.04, P = 0.009), with no differences in effect sizes. Specifically, older adults with higher RSI showed greater different-category error bias across memory phases (β > = −0.05, P < = 0.02), whereas younger adults with higher EN–RE RSI showed reduced forgetting (β = −0.06, P < 0.0001). These findings suggest that age-related differences in memory performance are not primarily explained by hippocampal atrophy but instead reflect differences in how representations are expressed across phases.

Testing predictions of baseline functional reorganization

We next examined whether baseline hippocampal organization accounted for the observed effects by calculating mean ReHo (mReHo) during pre-encoding rest. mReHo decreased with age (r = −.40, P = 0.001). Accounting for age, R1-mReHo predicted EN–R2 RSI (β = 0.298, P = 0.03) and EN–RE RSI (β = 0.318, P = 0.02), but not R2–RE RSI (β = 0.238, P = 0.0711), and no interactions with age were observed across memory phases (β ≤ 0.126, P ≥ 0.355; Fig. S5). This pattern indicates that baseline hippocampal organization contributes similarly to representational similarity similarly across all ages.

Because mReHo decreased with age and was related to RSI in some phases, we next tested whether controlling for mReHo explained the effects of RSI on memory outcomes. After controlling for R1-mReHo, total accuracy was no longer significant (EN–RE main effect: β = 0.018, P = 0.124; EN–RE x age interaction effect: β = −0.019, P = 0.08). In contrast, age and RSI interaction continued to predict the category error bias (EN–R2: β = −0.038, P = 0.005; EN–RE: β = −0.04, P = 0.008; R2–RE: β = −0.045, P = 0.007) and forgetting (EN–RE: β = 0.04, P = 0.008). Specifically, higher RSI was associated with different category error bias in older adults across memory phases (β > = −0.04, P < = 0.03), and higher EN–RE RSI was associated with reduced forgetting in younger adults (β = −0.06, P = 0.01). These findings suggest that baseline functional organization might explain the effects of EN-RE RSI on total accuracy but not on other memory errors.

Testing encoding and attention accounts

We next examined whether encoding-related hippocampal organization and attentional processes accounted for the observed effects. Encoding mReHo decreased with age (r = −.42, P = 0.001). Encoding mReHo predicted EN-RE RSI (β = 0.37, P = 0.006), indicating that stronger hippocampal organization during encoding facilitates encoding-to-retrieval RSI patterns (Fig. S6). However, encoding mReHo did not significantly predict EN-R2 RSI (β = 0.233, P = 0.088), R2-RE RSI (β = 0.197, P = 0.133), nor interactions with age across memory phases (β ≤ 0.0995, P ≥ 0.427). When controlling for age and encoding mReHo, the main effects of RSI on total accuracy was no longer significant (EN–RE: β = 0.021, P = 0.084), and the RSI and age interaction did not reach significance (EN–RE: β = −0.021, P = 0.067). In contrast, the interaction between RSI and age remained significant in predicting category error bias across phases (EN–R2: β = −0.038, P = 0.004; EN–RE: β = −0.030, P = 0.008; R2–RE: β = −0.037, P = 0.005) and forgetting (EN–RE: β = 0.042, P = 0.006). Specifically, older adults with higher RSI showed greater different-category error bias across memory phases (β > = −0.05, P < = 0.02), whereas younger adults with higher RSI showed reduced forgetting (β = −0.07, P < 0.0001). Finally, we examined whether attentional processes could explain these effects. Our attentional control measures were not correlated with age, did not predict RSI across memory phases (Fig. S7 and Fig. S8) and all effects remained significant after controlling for these variables (see Supplementary Material).

Overall, these findings suggest that encoding quality (via encoding mReHo) might explain the effects of EN-RE RSI on total accuracy but not on other memory errors. In contrast, attentional control did not account for any age-related differences in hippocampal RSI on memory outcomes. Overall, the brain and attention measures collectively account for 31.53% of the age-related variance in total accuracy, 60.32% of the age-related variance in category error bias, but too little variance was available for forgetting to attempt to explain age-related variance (see Table S3).

Discussion

We used MVCS during fMRI to examine how hippocampal representational dynamics changed across encoding, post-encoding rest, and retrieval across the adult lifespan. Aging was associated with reduced hippocampal similarity across all mnemonic phases. In younger adults, greater encoding–retrieval similarity predicted higher associative accuracy, whereas in older adults, it predicted cross-category errors. Age differences in baseline hippocampal functional organization and neural encoding quality significantly reduced the effects on total accuracy, but the differential age effects of RSI on forgetting and category error bias could not be explained by hippocampal volume, baseline functional organization, or attentional control.

Hippocampal RSI decreased systematically with age across EN–R2, EN–RE, and R2–RE. To our knowledge, prior studies have no simultaneously examined hippocampal representational similarity across all three mnemonic transitions using MVCS-based RSI framework across the adult lifespan (St-Laurent et al. 2014; Wixted et al. 2018; Trelle et al. 2019). EN–RE similarity was the strongest and predicted higher accuracy in younger adults. This finding aligns with prior multivoxel pattern similarity work showing that successful retrieval is related to encoding-related hippocampal patterns that preserve the relational structure of the original memory trace (Ritchey et al. 2013; Tompary et al. 2016b; Liang and Preston 2017). However, this positive association weakened in middle-aged adults and was largely absent in older adults, consistent with evidence that neural dedifferentiation starts in middle-age to reduce the distinctiveness and stability of mnemonic representations (St-Laurent et al. 2014; Zheng et al. 2018).

In older adults, hippocampal RSI demonstrated a qualitatively different pattern. Greater similarity across all memory phases predicted increased category misbinding errors, suggesting that persistent hippocampal similarity reflects reduced representational specificity rather than effective verbatim reinstatement. This pattern is consistent with theories proposing that aging biases memory toward broader, overlapping representations (Naveh-Benjamin et al. 2003). Similarly, hyper-binding accounts propose that reduced inhibitory control leads to overlapping associative links, increasing representational overlap across related events (Campbell et al. 2010; Campbell and Davis 2024). For example, older adults continue to maintain neural states despite clear event boundaries whereas young adults switch neural states across event boundaries (Lugtmeijer et al. 2025). Such perseveration of neural states across events serves as a mechanism underlying hyper-binding across trials because the trial-level event boundaries might be less distinct with increasing age. Our findings integrate these views by demonstrating that excessive hippocampal similarity across mnemonic phases—when not aligned with behaviorally relevant associations—can actively promote cross-category memory errors.

These findings occurred in the context of similarity patterns across correct and incorrect trials. This choice allowed us to test whether age-related declines in hippocampal similarity were associated with poorer associative memory at the possible cost of inflating the age effects. Restricting analyses to correct trials would address a different question: When memory succeeds, how is successful retrieval instantiated neurally? We contend that this approach complicates interpretation because memory accuracy reflects variation in both correct and incorrect responses. If using a neural measure derived from correct trials only, a mismatch occurs that may obscure the neural processes that contribute to individual differences in memory performance.

We next examined whether pre-existing differences in hippocampal integrity or organization could account for these age-related effects in our memory outcomes. The brain measures showed moderate to large declines with age in our sample and resting mReHo reduced the aging effects for total accuracy. Few task-related fMRI studies have investigated whether modulations of brain activity during a task are dependent on the existing brain organization. This study is the first to show that greater pre-existing hippocampal functional organization are related to later RSI patterns in the hippocampus that, in turn, support associative memory performance. In contrast, hippocampal volume did not show strong relationships with encoding-retrieval, encoding-post-encoding rest RSI, nor memory outcomes. Neither of these patterns explained the interactions with hippocampal RSI and memory errors, which aligns with evidence that representational quality during new learning, rather than absolute atrophy or pre-existing hippocampal organization, drives false memories in aging (Yassa and Stark 2011; Koen et al. 2019).

We examined whether age-related differences in hippocampal RSI could be explained by local hippocampal coordination during encoding or attentional control during encoding. Using mReHo, we found that encoding hippocampal synchrony consistently predicted EN–RE RSI. Moreover, when independently controlling for mReHo at each time point, the main effect of EN–RE RSI on total accuracy was no longer significant. These findings indicate that intrinsic and task-driven hippocampal synchrony are key mechanisms underlying successfully hippocampal RSI. These findings support evidence that network coherence promotes more stable mnemonic traces and more effective hippocampal replay (Schlichting and Preston 2015; Murty et al. 2017). Importantly, however, mReHo did not interact with age and was unrelated to age differences in memory errors. These dissociations suggest that intrinsic hippocampal organization as a proxy for higher neural encoding supports effective reinstatement but cannot prevent age-related distortions in mnemonic representations (Yassa and Stark 2011).

Our findings extend the Hyper-binding Hypothesis beyond encoding and attentional processes to post-encoding transformations of hippocampal representations. Importantly, these transformations persist even after controlling for encoding quality and attentional differences. We propose that aging can alter the mnemonic representation during early consolidation by reactivating partial or overlapping information across events, leading to progressively altered representations at retrieval for some items. While prior work has shown that aging disrupts hippocampal differentiation (Yassa and Stark 2011), the current findings demonstrate how this disruption unfolds dynamically over time, transforming possible reinstatement from selective to overgeneralized representation during immediate consolidation processing.

The present study has several limitations. First, we focused on functional connectivity in the hippocampus because of a relative dearth of aging studies on RSI patterns during rest in this region (Mekbib and McDonough 2025). Additional research is needed to understand how RSI in the cortex impact the contiguity of mnemonic representations. Second, multiple methods have successfully been used to measure post-encoding and retrieval reinstatement such as multivoxel pattern analysis that could be explored in older adults. Third, pattern similarity does not uniquely index reactivation, as it may also reflect similarities in cognitive processing rather than shared representations. Fourth, we censored fMRI volumes due to high levels of movement. Although consistent with prior research, newer methods like projection scrubbing offer a complementary approach (Phạm et al. 2023). Fifth, we did not directly compare similarity between pre-encoding rest and task-related phases, limiting our ability to determine whether R2 uniquely reflects RSI patterns akin to reinstatement or whether similar patterns are present at baseline and vary with age. Lastly, although our analyses emphasized age-related differences, there was substantial overlap in behavioral performance across groups, suggesting that individual variability may also contribute to the observed patterns.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that age-related episodic memory differences might arise from representational changes in the hippocampus over memory phases. In younger adults, strong evidence was found for encoding-retrieval similarities to support accurate memory. In middle-aged and older adults, such similarity patterns were misdirected, contributing to increased different-category errors. Baseline hippocampal organization and encoding-specific connectivity supported accurate hippocampal similarity in younger adults but could not offset maladaptive patterns in middle-aged and older adults. These findings provide a phase-specific framework for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying age-related memory deficits, highlight the novel role of rest-retrieval similarity in memory errors, and suggest potential avenues for future work aimed at enhancing hippocampal representational fidelity and reducing hyper-binding in aging populations.
