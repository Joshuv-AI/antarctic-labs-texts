Multimorbidity quantification from the perspective of precision management: challenges and strategies

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal healthcare systems face growing challenges from multimorbidity as populations age rapidly. Finally, the seamless integration of multimorbidity quantification with healthcare information systems is essential to optimize personalized management and improve treatment outcomes.

Introduction

With the growing incidence of chronic diseases and an ageing population, the importance of multimorbidity has gained widespread attention [1–3]. The dominant focus on single diseases creates barriers to gaining insights and developing effective strategies for managing multiple conditions, leading to several negative outcomes, including lower quality of life, functional impairment, increased healthcare utilization, and fragmented care [4]. In low-income countries, complex multimorbidity and weak healthcare systems complicate disease diagnosis and treatment [5]. Quantifying multiple conditions is a fundamental component of standardizing multimorbidity research, as it is essential for identifying high-risk patient populations, refining intervention strategies, and enhancing disease management.

However, the current quantitative methods for assessing multimorbidity still have several limitations. Firstly, these methods fail to capture the dynamic interactions between diseases, which often involve shared genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that vary across individuals [6–8]. The simple summation of individual diseases fails to account for the interactions between them, which can lead to different health outcomes based on the distinct combination of contributing factors. Additionally, multimorbidity patterns also exhibit considerable variation across different populations, reflecting unique environmental backgrounds, as well as economic and social factors [9,10]. Consequently, traditional methods do not adequately capture the complex ways in which diseases co-occur or account for critical elements such as disease severity, temporal relationships, or the cumulative impact of multiple conditions on health. To more accurately quantify the burden of multimorbidity, a refined approach to quantifying multimorbidity is required to more precisely capture the complexity of disease co-occurrence and its impact on patient health outcomes.

Precision medicine refers to medical care based on a patient’s genetic background and other molecular or cellular analyses, categorizing patients into subpopulations based on factors such as susceptibility, disease biology, prognosis, or therapeutic response [11]. Precision management, focused on accurate patient identification and personalized disease management strategies and interventions, represents a paradigm shift in the management of multimorbidity.

Accurate quantification of multiple conditions is essential for effective precision management in multimorbidity. While summation methods can be useful in certain contexts, a more refined approach is necessary to capture the complexity of disease co-occurrence. By integrating individualized data and developing precise quantification models, healthcare providers can better identify high-risk populations, guide early intervention, and improve patient outcomes. A comprehensive assessment of disease burden facilitates the evaluation of severity, elucidates pathophysiological interactions, and supports the development of comprehensive treatment strategies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and quality of life for patients [12].

Integrating multimorbidity quantification with precision management presents significant potential; however, it faces several challenges. The variability in clinical features and treatment responses across different diseases complicates the management of multimorbidity patients. Additionally, the application of existing clinical data in multimorbidity management still requires further investigation and validation.

This paper provides a comprehensive review of recent studies (2011–2024) on multimorbidity and comorbidity, retrieved from databases including Web of Science, PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and WanFang, using relevant keywords up to September 2024. It offers a detailed exploration of the definition of multimorbidity, emphasizing its distinction from comorbidity, and examines commonly used assessment tools. Additionally, the paper addresses the challenges in developing and applying multimorbidity quantification methods, highlighting the need for population-specific models, the integration of modifiable risk factors, and the analysis of disease interactions. It underscores that quantifying multimorbidity is fundamental to effective management, with personalized care approaches that optimize early intervention, reduce disease burden, and improve treatment outcomes. By aligning multimorbidity measurement with the principles of precision management, healthcare systems can enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of interventions tailored to individual patient needs.

Defining multimorbidity and its distinction from comorbidity

Over the past few decades, there has been no consensus on how to define and measure the terms ‘multimorbidity’ and ‘comorbidity’ [13,14]. Feinstein [15] defines comorbidity as ‘any distinct additional clinical entity that has existed or may occur during the clinical course of a patient who has the index disease under study’. In 2008, the WHO defined multimorbidity as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions within an individual [16]. (Figure 1). The UK National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) recommends using ‘multiple long-term conditions,’ as this term more accurately reflects disease chronicity, management potential, and condition interactions [17].

Common patterns, pathogenesis, and environmental factors of multimorbidity.

Research has identified key comorbidity patterns, including cardio-metabolic disorders, mental health conditions, and musculoskeletal issues. The development of multimorbidity is influenced by a variety of factors, including socioeconomic status, natural environment, early-life conditions, and lifestyle choices. It results from interconnected mechanisms linked to ageing, inflammation, and other pathologies.

Building on prior studies, we have further elaborated the definition of multimorbidity, which may enhance understanding of the term, as depicted in Figure 2(a–c). Firstly, time span and sequence are critical considerations. The term ‘time span’ refers to the duration during which two or more conditions are evaluated for co-occurrence, either [1] the diseases co-occur at the same time point [2]; the diseases occur during the same period but do not coexist at any specific moment. Temporality is fundamental to establishing causal inference in multimorbidity research. Advanced analytical methods, such as multi-state models and group-based trajectory modelling, enable the uncovering of dynamic disease sequences. Concurrently, causal analytical methods like Mendelian randomization help distinguish direct effects from confounding [18]. The integration of these approaches with multimorbidity indices offers a powerful strategy for elucidating mechanistic pathways and informing sequential, personalized treatment strategies. For instance, studies have shown that both moderately and rapidly increasing multimorbidity are associated with accelerated cognitive decline in older adults [19].

Secondly, it is crucial to recognize that diseases without a shared cause should co-occur at rates consistent with their independent prevalence. Conversely, diseases sharing common causes or biological interactions are expected to co-occur more frequently than would be predicted under the assumption of independence. The interactions of diseases involve underlying mechanisms [20], the impact of shared risk factors [21–23], cause-effect associations [24,25], and the possibility of coincidental co-occurrence of the diseases, as illustrated in Table 1. Many factors contributing to multimorbidity seem to share similar biological pathways, facilitating organ crosstalk at systemic, tissue, cellular, and molecular levels [26,27]. Research indicates that oxidative stress and chronic inflammation mechanisms, which lead to cellular senescence, disrupted cell signalling, and apoptosis, play pivotal roles in disease progression.

Validated multimorbidity assessment tools

Although numerous indices have been developed to evaluate multimorbidity and its impact on health, to date, no gold standard has been established for its assessment. The application of multimorbidity quantification tools requires careful consideration of the methodologies used to develop relevant indicators, taking into account factors such as the study objectives, as well as the source and type of research data [28–32] (Table 2). For instance, some tools, such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [28] and the Kaplan and Feinstein Comorbidity Index (KFI) [31], were originally developed based on mortality outcomes, and their applicability to other outcomes needs further validation. Furthermore, while the CCI and ECI have been validated in low- and middle-income countries, the applicability of other tools in such settings requires further validation [33].

As the population ages, disease profiles have evolved. For example, HIV has transitioned from an acute fatal infection to a chronic manageable condition that may additionally increase the risk of other chronic diseases [34]. However, its weighting in traditional comorbidity indices such as the CCI has not been fully updated to reflect this change. In addition, the assessment of comorbidities, which relies on professional medical diagnoses, ensures that comorbidity indices are primarily applied within hospital settings for accurate and comprehensive evaluations.

Notably, it is crucial to recognize that while some assessment tools employ the term “comorbidity,” their fundamental approach aligns more closely with the concept of multimorbidity. Unlike comorbidity, multimorbidity does not rely on identifying an index disease. Instead, it involves a comprehensive assessment of all conditions from the patient’s perspective.

More recent tools address these limitations by moving beyond mortality-based designs and incorporating broader dimensions of health system use. Modern multimorbidity assessment systems, such as the Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) [35], Adjusted Morbidity Groups (AMG) [36], and Clinical Risk Groups (CRGs) [37], are designed to quantify disease burden in a more comprehensive manner. Unlike earlier tools, they integrate diagnostic complexity, longitudinal healthcare utilization, and predictive modelling of health outcomes. These systems also avoid the assumption of a single index disease and are more suitable for population-level health management and policy planning.

While these newer tools offer significant advantages, including adaptability to various healthcare systems, integration of healthcare resource utilization patterns, and better alignment with the complexity of real-world patients, they also present notable challenges. These include reliance on high-quality administrative or clinical data, potential variability in performance across different regions, and the requirement for robust technical infrastructure to ensure effective implementation.

Frailty, multimorbidity, and disability: overlaps and distinct care needs

Frailty, multimorbidity, and disability are distinct yet interrelated conditions that significantly impact the health and care needs of older adults. Frailty is characterized by increased vulnerability to stressors due to multisystem decline, involving physical, cognitive, and psychosocial components [38]. Key features include unintentional weight loss, fatigue, weakness, slow gait, and low physical activity. Disability is defined as impaired ability to perform essential activities for independent living and quality of life [39].

The relationship between these conditions is bidirectional. Multimorbidity contributes to the development of frailty, while frailty and disability can complicate the management of comorbidities. Frailty is critical prognostic factors that can be used to stratify risks and predict outcomes, even among patients with similar disease profiles. Studies show 68% of frail individuals have multiple chronic conditions, and 21% also experience disability, highlighting the compounded burden. Sarcopenia can accelerate functional decline, increasing disability risk [40,41].

While comorbidity requires disease-specific treatment, frailty and disability need distinct approaches. Frailty benefits from holistic interventions (e.g. nutrition, strength training), and disability focuses on rehabilitation and support [42,43]. An integrated assessment is crucial to address these concurrent needs and minimize disease severity, maintain function, and prevent further decline and dependence.

Challenges in multimorbidity quantification

Precision identification: developing population-specific models

The global distribution of multimorbidity not only highlights the widespread nature of these diseases but also emphasizes the significant impact of sociodemographic factors, including age, gender, and race, on the prevalence of diseases [8,44]. Multimorbidity patterns, which vary significantly across populations, are strongly and persistently associated with socioeconomic disadvantage throughout the lifespan, an association further compounded by the complex interplay between physical and mental health conditions [45,46]. Developing population-specific multimorbidity quantification models is crucial for advancing precision medicine and optimizing personalized interventions.

The geographic distribution of multimorbidity varies significantly, reflecting unique environmental backgrounds, economic and social factors, and the local burden of disease [9,10,47]. Socioeconomic status is a key determinant, with lower status consistently correlating with higher multimorbidity rates [48]. Economically disadvantaged communities often experience limited healthcare access, elevated occupational hazards, increased life stress, and reduced health awareness [49,50]. These factors collectively amplify the risk of chronic diseases. Frameworks like the social determinants of health (SDoH) provide insights into how socioeconomic conditions contribute to health disparities [51]. Educational attainment and occupation type also play critical roles, with higher education linked to better health awareness and behaviours [52].

Advancing the precision identification of multimorbidity requires a transition from generalized descriptions to technically robust strategies for constructing population-specific models. Future work should focus on systematically adapting models to diverse populations by incorporating multi-source data, such as electronic health records and insurance claims, while accounting for variations in data structure and cohort characteristics [53]. We also recommend that studies integrate more precise geographic and environmental variables to derive portable and reproducible multimorbidity measures. Although emerging tools such as Phenome-wide Multi-Institutional Multimorbidity Explorer (PheMIME) [54] demonstrate promise in visualizing cross-population disease interactions, further research remains essential to develop generalizable models that incorporate contextual and socioeconomic determinants to effectively support personalized interventions.

In-depth analysis: revealing disease interactions

Multimorbidity exhibits distinct clustering characteristics, and simply summing individual diseases does not capture their interactions. The single disease count approach also fails to capture the impact of disease severity or the combination of diseases on mortality risk. Identifying multimorbidity patterns is essential for providing insights into prevention, treatment, and prognosis improvement of the disease. Therefore, multimorbidity quantification should move beyond simple aggregation and explore methods that capture interrelationships among diseases, facilitating targeted healthcare strategies and improved outcomes [6–8].

Commonly recognized multimorbidity patterns in existing literature include cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, psychological, respiratory, cancer, metabolic, allergic, neuropsychiatric, and so on [51,55–57]. The clusters most consistently identified across studies are cardio-metabolic and mental health conditions. Additionally, clusters involving musculoskeletal and allergic conditions have also been observed (Table 3). Although about five multimorbidity patterns have been validated, their stability across subsamples remains insufficient, and the variability observed between studies underscores the complexity of multimorbidity beyond these well-established patterns [58].

While existing research has extensively described cross-sectional multimorbidity patterns, understanding their longitudinal progression remains limited. Mental health conditions, especially in older, male, or socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals, often lead to additional diseases more rapidly than conditions like cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes (T2D), and heart failure (HF) [59]. Jensen and colleagues identified 15 disease trajectory clusters, with gout and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) critical to the development of the accumulation of multimorbidity and adverse outcomes [60]. These findings emphasize the importance of early intervention in multimorbidity progression to improve care and health outcomes.

Beyond descriptive clustering, we emphasize causal approaches to dissect disease interactions [61]. Unlike temporal diagnosis sequences and associational methods, which tend to over-represent common conditions and obscure clinically meaningful patterns, causal analytics such as Mendelian randomization and Bayesian networks help elucidate mechanistic pathways and support personalized interventions [18]. For instance, a novel “Body Clock” derived from Bayesian multi-organ disease models outperforms traditional frailty indices in predicting mortality and disability, and has been integrated into a Health Octo Tool to capture ageing heterogeneity [62]. Such integration of multimorbidity indices with causal reasoning reflects a growing convergence in the field, enabling improved detection of non-random clusters, uncovering of disease mechanisms, and reorganization of healthcare delivery.

Proactive intervention: including modifiable risk factors

Given the current lack of cure for most diseases, the primary goal of multimorbidity quantification is to predict the risk of adverse outcomes [28–31]. However, multimorbidity quantification should not only address the disease burden but also integrate modifiable risk factors to guide proactive intervention strategies.

Understanding how environmental factors affect multimorbidity risk can support the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies to address the global burden of multiple health conditions [63]. Environmental factors influence span the spectrum of life from mode of childbirth and early-life exposures (including breastfeeding and antibiotic exposure in infancy) to exposures later on in adulthood (including smoking, major life stressors, diet and lifestyle) [64] (Table 4).

Researchers are encouraged focus on diseases that co-occur due to shared risk factors, whether biological or environmental [65–73]. This highlights the potential for ‘upstream’ strategies in the primary prevention of age-related multimorbidity, through either universal or targeted interventions that address shared risk factors such as adiposity, smoking, obesity, blood pressure, and glycaemic control [69,71,73]. Unlike genetic risk factors, many environmental factors are modifiable. Adopting modifiable healthy lifestyles has been linked to long-term benefits, even for individuals aged 80 or older, regardless of whether multimorbidity is present from middle age onward [74,75]. Moderate exercise and a high micronutrient diet are linked to reduced multimorbidity risk, with mechanisms like metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and microbial imbalances playing a role [76–79].

The inclusion of modifiable risk factors is essential for proactive intervention; however, real-world implementation faces several challenges. Fragmented healthcare systems, concerns about data privacy, and resource constraints, especially in low- and middle-income countries, can hinder the effective integration of these factors into practice [80]. Successful implementation depends on key system maturity criteria, including data interoperability, electronic health record completeness and quality, and trust from both the public and clinicians in AI-assisted decision-making. Addressing these structural and contextual barriers is crucial for translating insights into actionable interventions.

Optimized management: integrating with healthcare systems

The rising prevalence of multimorbidity diminishes quality of life, increases mortality rates, and escalates healthcare costs in both primary and secondary care settings [2,81–85]. Physicians should assess the benefits and risks of treatment options, prioritizing those that minimize adverse interactions [84]. Effective management of multimorbidities requires integrating healthcare information, linking multimorbidity quantification with systems like ICD coding and electronic health records (EHRs) to enhance clinical decision-making, improve care quality, and reduce costs.

Treatment plans must consider disease interactions to avoid exacerbating existing conditions. Medications, especially polypharmacy, play a significant role in developing multimorbidity. For instance, antipsychotics increase the risk of diabetes and dyslipidaemia, while anticholinergic drugs raise the risk of cardiovascular events and dementia [86,87]. Additionally, treatments like oral steroids for polymyalgia rheumatica can lead to side effects such as diabetes, cataracts, and osteoporosis. Polypharmacy increases the potential for drug-drug and drug-condition interactions, further complicating multimorbidity management [88].

In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines focus on safe and effective medication management, and Australia supports simplifying medication regimens and regular reviews to align with changing care needs [89,90]. However, isolated prescribing practices complicate medication schemes, hinder adherence, and increase mortality risks [91,92].

To optimize multimorbidity management, it’s essential to integrate multimorbidity quantification into healthcare systems. A unified framework must connect disease-specific data with comprehensive healthcare platforms, such as EHRs and decision support systems. Emerging tools like predictive modelling, AI-driven clinical decision support, and risk stratification dashboards show promise in enhancing care delivery [93,94]. These technologies can provide clinicians with real-time, personalized insights to guide diagnosis, treatment prioritization, and follow-up planning.

Such integration standardizes treatment protocols, reduces fragmentation, and helps clinicians prioritize interventions. Successful integration requires system-level prerequisites: seamless data interoperability, AI-ready infrastructure and workforce, and equity monitoring to prevent care disparities, ensuring standardized care and improved clinical decision-making.

Notably, the increased sensitivity of diagnostic technologies has led to an exacerbation of the risk of “overdiagnosis.” Many incidental “abnormalities” may never manifest symptoms throughout an individual’s life, yet being labelled as “disease” can lead to unnecessary anxiety and interventions.

Outlook

Multimorbidity poses significant challenges, underscoring the need for innovative, population-specific approaches to quantify and manage complex health conditions. Future research should focus on developing models that integrate sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and environmental factors to better understand disease interactions, and to improve prevention, treatment, and prognosis. Incorporating modifiable risk factors into these models is crucial for proactive intervention. However, implementing these strategies faces substantial challenges, particularly in fragmented healthcare systems, including data privacy concerns and issues with system interoperability. Policy initiatives must prioritize the integration of multimorbidity quantification tools with healthcare systems, utilizing AI to optimize clinical care and reduce costs, while promoting interdisciplinary collaboration to improve population health outcomes.
