Impact of Exercise on Clonal Hematopoiesis
Authors: Jessica M. Scott, Brandon L. Tsai, Caitlin Stewart, Stefan E. Eng, James R. White, Kelly L. Bolton, Ines Vaz-Luis, Julie Havas, Antonio Di Meglio, Anne-Laure Martin, Sandrine Boyault, Ruilin Wang, Jean Baptiste Micol, Luis A. Diaz, Paul C. Boutros, Lee W. Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClonal hematopoiesis (CH) is more prevalent in cancer patients and is associated with excess all-cause mortality (ACM) risk compared to patients without CH. Interventions to mitigate risk are not available.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to investigate whether exercise reduces CH prevalence and excess ACM risk of CH in cancer patients.MethodsWe integrated genomic sequencing data with detailed exercise and clinical annotation from 2 retrospective studies: 3,539 patients with solid tumors from the MSK-IMPACT (Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets) and 1,142 patients with primary breast cancer from CANTO (CANcer TOxicities). Patients were classified as nonexercisers (ie, 0 metabolic equivalent of task-h/week) or exercisers (ie, >0 metabolic equivalent of task-h/week). We then conducted a remotely-supervised structured exercise therapy study in 4 patients with cancer and CH.ResultsIn multivariable analysis, CH prevalence was not different between exercisers and nonexercisers in MSK-IMPACT (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.8 to 1.13; adjusted Firth’s logistic regression) or CANTO (OR: 1.28; 95% CI: 0.89-1.84; adjusted Firth’s logistic regression). Patients with CH had a higher unadjusted hazard of ACM relative to patients without CH (HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.23-1.62). Relative to non-CH nonexercisers, CH-exercisers had a lower adjusted hazard of ACM (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.55-0.87). In the prospective exercise study, cardiovascular risk factors were improved; no changes in peripheral blood somatic variant allele frequency were observed.ConclusionsExercise was not associated with CH burden but does reduce excess ACM of CH in patients with cancer. Further research is needed to investigate the exercise–CH relationship in cancer and other populations. Trial Registration Clinical trials.gov number: NCT03996239.

Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is the expansion of hematopoietic cells harboring somatic mutations and strong predictor of all-cause mortality (ACM) in multiple clinical settings.1,2 CH is elevated in patients with cancer3 and has been associated with incremental (excess) ACM risk compared to patients without CH.4

Despite its importance, strategies to lower CH prevalence or offset excess ACM risk of CH are lacking.5 Consensus guidelines emphasize a recommendation for regular exercise for individuals with CH,6 but supporting evidence is not available in any setting. Here we report an observational-clinical costudy of exercise on CH in patients with cancer. We hypothesized that exercise would lower CH prevalence and attenuate excess mortality risk of CH.

Methods

Observational studies

A detailed description of the methods is provided in the Supplement. In brief, we integrated genomic sequencing data (that included CH identification) from patients with detailed exercise exposure data from 2 retrospective studies: 3,539 patients with solid tumors from the Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets (MSK-IMPACT)7 cohort and 1,142 patients with primary breast cancer from the CANcer TOxicities (CANTO)8 cohort.

MSK-IMPACT Cohort

Patients with 25 different cancer types sequenced at MSK Cancer Center from March 2001 to May 2023 were included in this study. Patients reported smoking and alcohol use and had a tumor and blood sample (as a matched control) obtained at diagnosis sequenced using the MSK-IMPACT clinical sequencing assay, a hybridization capture-based, next-generation sequencing platform.9 MSK-IMPACT is an institutional prospective tumor-sequencing protocol (NCT01775072). The present study (integration of MSK-IMPACT with exercise data) was approved by the MSK Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (NCT03208374).

Patients were determined to have CH if their blood sample had variants fulfilling any of the following criteria: 1) truncating variants in NF1, DNMT3A, TET2, IKZF1, RAD21, WT1, KMT2D, SH2B3, TP53, CEBPA, ASXL1, RUNX1, BCOR, KDM6A, STAG2, PHF6, KMT2C, PPM1D, ATM, ARID1A, ARID2, ASXL2, CHEK2, CREBBP, ETV6, EZH2, FBXW7, MGA, MPL, RB1, SETD2, SUZ12, ZRSR2 or in CALR exon 9; 2) any truncating mutations (nonsense, essential splice site, or frameshift indel) in known tumor suppressor genes as per the Cancer Gene Census, OncoKB, or the scientific literature; 3) translation start site mutations in SH2B3; 4) TERT promoter mutations; 5) FLT3-ITDs; 6) in-frame indels in CALR, CEBPA, CHEK2, ETV6, EZH2; 7) any variant occurring in the Catalogus of Somatic Mutations in Cancer “hematopoietic and lymphoid” category ≥ 10 times; 8) any variant reported as somatic at least 20 times in Catalogus of Somatic Mutations in Cancer; 9) any variant noted as potentially oncogenic in an in-house data set of 7,000 individuals with myeloid neoplasm greater than or equal to 5 times; and 10) any loci (defined by the amino-acid location) reported as having at least 5 missense mutations and at least one exact mutational match in TopMed.

Postdiagnosis exercise was assessed using the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire (GLTEQ)10 or International Physical Activity Questionnaire.11 The GLTEQ only assesses exercise performed during free time (ie, not work or house work-related) over the past 30 days. The GLTEQ assesses mild, moderate, and vigorous exercise but in the present analysis only moderate and vigorous exercise were analyzed. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire assesses the frequency and duration of moderate and vigorous exercise, walking, and sedentary behavior over the past 7 days. Only moderate and vigorous exercise were analyzed. As such, the research question focuses on analysis of exercise (ie, structured physical activity in leisure time with the intent of improving health) as opposed to general physical activity. In both questionnaires, the frequency and average duration of moderate and vigorous exercise was assessed and capped at 240 minutes/week (4 hours) each. Exercise intensity was weighted by an estimate of the 5 metabolic equivalent of task (MET) for moderate exercise and 9 MET for vigorous exercise.12 The weighted estimates were summed to yield a maximum total of 56 MET hours per week (MET-hours/week). Patients were classified as exercisers (>0 MET-hours/week; n = 2,366; 67%) and nonexercisers (0 MET-hours/week; n = 1,173; 33%). The definition of regular exercise (ie, at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise or 75 minutes of vigorous exercise) results in patients performing a considerable amount of exercise (eg, 100-149 minutes) being defined as nonexercisers. Given this, and the fact that we only analyzed moderate and vigorous exercise (excluded mild intensity exercise), we adopted a stricter definition of nonexercise (ie, those reporting 0 MET-h/wk), consistent with our prior work.13 The median duration between MSK-IMPACT testing and exercise survey completion was 606 days.

CANTO cohort

Patients (age equal or higher than 40 years at diagnosis of breast cancer) were included from the CANTO cohort (NCT01993498) between February 2012 and March 2018. All patients provided written informed consent at the time of inclusion. The study was approved by the ethics committee (ID-RCB:2011-A01095-36,11-039).

Blood samples were obtained at diagnosis. CH was assessed by next generation sequencing with unique molecular identifiers (HaloPlexHS; Agilen Technologies) for 17 genes (including DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, PPM1D, ATM, JAK2, and TP53) without previous solid or hematological cancers. Quality controls of reads were performed on raw FastQ files with FastQC (v0.11.9). Reads trimming for low 3’ terminal base quality (< 20), poly-G tails, sequences with 3 or more undetermined bases, and removing of Illumina adapter sequences was performed using fastp (v0.20.1). Quality reports were gathered with MultiQC (v1.9). Mapping to the reference genome build hg19 was performed with Burrow-Wheeler Aligner mem (v0.7.17) with default parameters, except for “-M -A2 -E1”. Umi-based reads deduplication and sequence correction was performed using LocatIt (v2.0.5; Agilent Software) using default parameters, except for “-m 2 -d 1 -U -R -IB -OB -r -c 2,500”. Deduplicated binary alignment map was filtered out for unpaired reads using samtools (v1.9), and for reads with more than 33% local mismatches using bamUtil (v1.0.15). Variant calling was performed using Varscan (v2.4.4) with a minimum variant read depth of 3 for single nucleotide polymorphisms and 2 for indels and deactivating the default strand bias filter (which is incompatible with the amplicon technology used). Annotation of identified variants was performed using Annovar (v2018-04 to 16) with the annotation databases: refGene, exac03, gnomad211_exome, kaviar_20150923, avsnp150, clinvar_20190305, intervar_20180118, cosmic87_coding_20181113, cosmic87_noncoding_20181113, cadd13, dbnsfp33a, and an in-house base of genomic mononucleotidic repeats of length ≥5. Filtering of annotated variants was performed under R (v3.6.3), keeping those respecting these criteria: = / = synonymous, ExAC_ALL frequency <0.005, VAF >1%, and refGene function = exonic or splicing.

Postdiagnosis exercise was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire-16 (World Health Organization). The questionnaire assessed duration and intensity of work, transportation, and leisure-time physical activity over the past 7 days. Consistent with our approach adopted in the MSK-IMPACT cohort, only moderate and vigorous exercise were analyzed in MET-hours/week with patients classified as exercisers (>0 MET-hours/week; n = 363; 32%) and nonexercisers (0 MET-hours/week; n = 779; 68%).

Intervention study

We conducted a single-center, proof-of-concept study of exercise therapy in 4 nonexercising adult cancer patients with CH identified at MSK. The study was approved by the MSK Institutional Review Board (NCT03996239). All patients provided written informed consent.

At pretreatment, each patient completed a baseline symptom-limited cardiopulmonary exercise test to evaluate peak oxygen consumption. We then leveraged a digital, decentralized platform to conduct all study procedures remotely in the patients’ primary residence.14 All exercise sessions were conducted with remote, real-time, one-on-one supervision and monitoring by study exercise physiologists using two-way video conferencing. Exercise therapy was standardized as individualized treadmill walking delivered for 300 minutes each week over 5 unique sessions per week for up to 12 consecutive months (ie, 120-249 unique planned exercise sessions). All sessions were conducted between ∼55% to 95% of each patient’s baseline VO2 peak following nonlinear dosing (ie, dose continually altered and progressed).15 Postintervention changes in cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk profile were evaluated by change in VO2 peak, interstitial glucose levels, resting heart rate, resting systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and body composition. Baseline and postintervention somatic variant allele frequency (VAF) in peripheral blood were assessed to quantify changes in CH variants.7

Statistical analysis

The association between CH prevalence and exercise status was evaluated using adjusted Firth’s logistic regression (AFLR) based on the model: CH status ∼ exercise status + age + survey + smoking + alcohol. Sex was included as a covariate where appropriate. Analyses were further stratified into large and small clone size using a median dichotomy of the max VAF of CH variants. Differences in ACM were evaluated using both univariate and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression. Due to median follow-up and cohort characteristics (ie, a higher proportion of patients with advanced disease), ACM was only examined in the MSK-IMPACT cohort.

CANTO was not an appropriate data set to examine mortality, given this cohort comprises women with early-stage breast cancer and short median follow-up to date. All clinical interventional study data were summarized using descriptive statistics only.

Results

Association between exercise and CH prevalence

The MSK-IMPACT cohort included patients between March 2001 and May 2023, whereas CANTO included patients from February 2012 and March 2018. The median patient age in MSK-IMPACT was 62 years (IQR: 18 years) and 56 years (IQR: 16 years) in CANTO (Supplemental Table 1). CH variants were most common in DNMT3A, TET2, and PPM1D, as expected and 40% had mutations in multiple CH genes (Supplemental Figure 1).

In MSK-IMPACT, CH prevalence was 35% (821/2,366) in exercisers and 41% (481/1,173) in nonexercisers; this difference was not significant in multivariable analysis (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.8-1.27, AFLR) (Figure 1A). No association was detected when CH patients were stratified by clone size16 (small clones: VAF <5.2%, large clones: VAF ≥5.2%) (Figure 1A). We conducted further analysis in the most well-powered cancer type: breast cancer. Exercise was not associated with CH, independent of clone size, nor when stratified by clinical subtype (Figure 1C, Supplemental Figure 2). These findings were validated in the CANTO cohort. CH prevalence was 18% (67/363) in exercisers and 13% (105/779) in nonexercisers; this was not significantly different in multivariable analysis (OR: 1.28; 95% CI: 0.89-1.84: AFLR) nor as a function of clone size or subclinical type (Figure 1C, Supplemental Figure 2). Results from Firth’s logistic regression were consistent with conventional logistic regression (Supplemental Figure 3).

ACM based on CH and exercise status

Patients were categorized into 4 groups based on CH and exercise status: group 1, non-CH–nonexercisers (referent group, n = 692); group 2, non-CH–exercisers (n = 1,545); group 3, CH–nonexercisers (n = 481); and group 4, CH–exercisers (n = 821). After a median 4.5 years follow-up, 804 ACM deaths were observed. Adjusted ACM risk differed across the 4 groups (Figure 1D; P < 0.001). Compared with non-CH–nonexercisers, CH–nonexercisers had a nonsignificant higher hazard of ACM (HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 0.97-1.58), non-CH–exercisers had a lower hazard of ACM (HR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.51-0.79), and CH–exercisers (group 4) had a lower hazard of ACM (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.55-0.87) (Figure 1D, Central Illustration). In pan-cancer analysis, patients with CH had a higher unadjusted hazard of ACM relative to patients without CH (HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.23-1.62) (Supplemental Figure 6A), and relative to nonexercisers, exercisers had a lower hazard of ACM (HR: 0.64; 95% CI; 0.56-0.74) (Supplemental Figure 6B).

Discussion

The fitness advantage conferred to CH mutant clones highlights the interplay between hematopoietic stem cell genotype-phenotypes, the hematopoietic microenvironment (niche), and the systemic (host) milieu.17 Although promoting system-wide and tissue-specific robustness,18 contrary to our hypothesis, the present findings do not support the notion that exercise suppresses CH clonal expansion. The lack of association of exercise on CH were confirmed in our prospective “proof-of-concept” clinical trial of long-term structured exercise therapy; however, these preliminary findings should be confirmed in larger prospective trials. The potential modifying effect of exercise may be limited in the context of the vast genotoxic damage caused by anticancer therapy.4 Prior work, however, also found no association between self-reported healthy lifestyle (normal body mass index, nonsmoking, regular exercise, and high diet quality) and CH prevalence in women from the general population.19 Whether the higher CH prevalence and distinct mutational spectrum due to anticancer therapy or a malignancy limits the potential modifying effect of exercise on CH, or whether exercise-induced perturbations can influence clonal evolution warrants further investigation.

Evidence-based treatment guidelines to attenuate the excess mortality risk of CH, either in the general population or in those with cancer, are not available. Our findings showing exercise attenuation of excess mortality risk of CH provides empirical evidence to support recommendation of exercise as an aspect of consensus CH management guidelines in cancer.6 The mechanisms underpinning reduced risk are not known; however, findings from our clinical trial suggest that exercise may modulate CH-related CVD sequelae.20 Future work investigating the effects of exercise therapy on the inflammatory milieu in larger CH patient cohorts with and without cancer are needed to validate our findings.

Study limitations

Limitations of our study require consideration. Self-reported assessment of exercise has well-known limitations. In addition, leisure time and nonleisure time physical activity such as transport-related physical activity was not reported, which may have differential associations with health outcomes. In the MSK-IMPACT cohort, exercise was evaluated within 2 years of the pathology date. Although exercise levels can change over time, self-reported exercise is shown to be a proxy of long-term exercise.12 Although ACM was analyzed adjusting for known CH covariates, cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and lipid status were not available for inclusion in adjusted models. The relationship between exercise and ACM in patients with CH is postulated to be mediated via a reduction in cardiovascular mortality; however, cardiovascular mortality data were not available to confirm this. Given inherent limitations of observational studies we conducted a “proof-of-concept” clinical trial; however, evaluation of different exercise therapy regimens is required, and only data from randomized controlled trials can evaluate causality. Finally, generalizability of our findings are restricted to patients with cancer, a population with higher CH prevalence and distinct mutational spectrum. Relatedly, the prevalence of CH was lower in the CANTO compared with the MSK-IMPACT data sets because CH was measured at the point of diagnosis before the administration of any anticancer therapy (anticancer therapy is a major promoter of CH) and the median age was lower in the CANTO sample. Finally, it is possible that the observed nonsignificant associations between exercise and CH may be because of small sample size and differences between data sets.

Conclusions

In summary, findings from this synchronous observational-coclinical trial indicate that exercise is not significantly associated with CH burden or CH mutational landscape, but attenuates excess mortality in patients with cancer.
