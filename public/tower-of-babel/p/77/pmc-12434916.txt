Prevalence and contributing factors of computer vision syndrome among university students in Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Saeed Barzegari, Zahra ArabKermani, Zahra Mahmoudvand, Ibrahim Arpaci, Fatemeh Shabani, Amir Hossein Najafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComputer vision syndrome (CVS) is a common condition in the digital era, causing fatigue in users exceeding four hours of screen time daily. It manifests as blurred vision, itching, dryness, and eye fatigue. This study examined the prevalence and risk factors for CVS among university students.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 230 students using simple sampling. A structured questionnaire included: (1) demographic data, (2) digital device use and ergonomic habits, and (3) CVS symptom frequency and severity based on the validated CVS-Q© scale.ResultsA total of 76.1% of the participants were found to have CVS, with a higher prevalence among female students (80.2%) compared to male students (70.7%). 87.4% of the subjects (n = 201) experienced one or more symptoms of CVS. Most of them experience headaches (60.4%, n = 139), eye irritation (55.7%, n = 128), tearing (50.9%, n = 116), and discomfort in the eyes (49.6%, n = 114). Female students scored a higher mean of CVS than male students (p = .004). Risk factors were a lack of adequate rest between screen watching periods (p ≤ .001) and failure to observe the 20-20-20 rule (p = .015).ConclusionThe study indicates that the prevalence of CVS is extremely high among the students, and, therefore, there is a need for ergonomic interventions to be implemented widely and used for prevention or alleviation of symptoms of CVS.Keywords: Computer vision syndrome, Blurred vision, Double vision, Ergonomic

Background

Over the last decade, the use of digital tools for recreational and educational purposes has significantly increased across all age groups [1]. While these tools offer various benefits for entertainment, socialization, education, and learning, excessive and prolonged use can lead to a range of visual and eye-related symptoms known as “computer vision syndrome” (CVS) or digital eye strain (DES) [2]. Symptoms associated with CVS include double vision (diplopia), blurred vision, poor near vision, slow focusing, eye fatigue, pain in and around the eyes, tiredness, burning sensations, dryness, a gritty feeling, redness, and tearing [3]. CVS is a common issue among computer users. 90% of the users who spend more than three hours a day in front of the screen suffer from CVS [4]. Research estimates indicate that approximately 60 million people worldwide suffer from CVS, with one million new cases emerging each year [3, 5]. Reports suggest that the prevalence of CVS in children ranges from 12.1 to 94.8%, while in adults it ranges from 35.2 to 97.3% [6–8]. This wide variability may be due to differences in age groups, study designs, definitions of CVS, duration and intensity of screen use, and even geographic and cultural factors across populations. Given this wide prevalence, it is essential to identify the contributing factors that lead to the onset and progression of CVS symptoms.

Several factors can contribute to the development and worsening of CVS. These include poor lighting, low screen contrast, inadequate workplace illumination, prolonged staring at digital screens without blinking or taking breaks, uncorrected refractive errors, and improper viewing distances [9, 10]. These complications can significantly impact users’ quality of life [11]. Today, electronic tools like computers, tablets, iPads, and smartphones have become essential to university education. The development of these technologies has significantly transformed teaching methods and made information and study resources more accessible [12]. Students can now easily access resources and books on their devices, which reduces their reliance on paper-based materials [13]. However, this shift in lifestyle raises health concerns. The prevalence of CVS has increased among computer users, specifically medical students, who spend extensive hours studying and researching in front of a screen [12]. This raise concerns not only about students’ ocular health but also about potential impacts on academic performance and long-term well-being.

The use of digital learning technologies has become common in today’s education, leading students to spend significant time using them [12]. This increased screen time raises the risk of CVS, which can negatively affect quality of life and academic performance [7]. One common preventive recommendation is the “20-20-20 rule,” which advises that every 20 min, individuals should take a 20-second break to look at something 20 feet away [14]. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the prevalence of this issue to understand its scope and develop appropriate solutions [7]. Although several studies have investigated CVS among university students in Iran, most have either focused on general student populations or lacked detailed assessments of ergonomic and behavioral risk factors. Moreover, few studies have specifically examined medical and paramedical students, a group that often experiences prolonged screen exposure due to intensive academic workloads. The present study addresses these gaps by focusing on students at Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences, offering population-specific insights that may inform targeted prevention strategies.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to assess the prevalence of CVS and identify its associated ergonomic and behavioral risk factors among medical and paramedical students.

Materials and methods

Population and sampling

This study was conducted at Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences in Sari, Iran. All students from Amol medical and paramedical faculties were included in the study using a convenience sampling method. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study and the confidentiality of their responses. Only those who voluntarily agreed and provided written informed consent were included in the study. Students who voluntarily agreed to participate were included after being informed about the study objectives and provided written informed consent. There were no strict exclusion criteria; however, Students who declined to participate or submitted substantially incomplete questionnaires, such as those missing entire sections were excluded from the final analysis. However, questionnaires with minor missing data in demographic fields were retained, provided that the core CVS-related sections were fully completed. Data collection was conducted in person after receiving ethical approval from the IRB of the “Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences” (Ethics Code: IR.MAZUMS.REC.1403.158).

At the time of the study, approximately 270 students were enrolled in the medical and paramedical faculties of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (Amol campus). A total of 250 students completed the questionnaire, of which 230 were fully and validly completed and met the inclusion criteria. As the entire accessible student population was approached, a census-style convenience sampling method was employed, and no formal sample size calculation was performed.

Measurement tool

Demographic questions

The first part consisted of demographic questions, including age, gender, marital status, place of residence, field of study, and academic year.

CVS_Q

We used the Persian version of the Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire (CVS-Q FA©), which was translated, culturally adapted, and validated by Qolami et al. [14]. The original questionnaire was developed by Seguí et al. in 2015 [15]. The questionnaire contained 16 questions about various eye symptoms and problems, each assessed separately for frequency and severity. Each symptom was evaluated using two separate scales. Frequency was assessed via a three-point Likert scale (never, sometimes, often/always), while severity was rated using a two-point scale (moderate, intense), consistent with the Persian version of the CVS-Q described by Qolami et al. [14]. The symptoms evaluated included eye burning, eye itching, the sensation of a foreign body in the eye, watery eyes, excessive blinking, eye redness, pain in the eye area, a feeling of heaviness in the eyelids, blurred vision, dry eyes, double vision, impaired near-distance vision, seeing colored halos around objects, increased sensitivity to light, a sensation of reduced vision, and headaches. The CVS score for each respondent is calculated by summing the products of the values assigned to repetition (never = 0, sometimes = 1, always = 2) and severity (moderate = 1, intense = 2). The score from the questionnaire ranges from 0 to 64, and a score of 6 or higher indicates that the individual suffers from CVS [14].

Digital device use and ergonomic habits

The third part included questions on the use of computers and other digital devices. This section addresses daily screen time, frequency and duration of breaks, viewing distance, use of glasses or contact lenses, awareness of the 20-20-20 rule, and any chronic conditions that may affect vision. These questions were developed ad hoc by the research team based on previous literature.

Statistical methods and variables

First, the data were entered into SPSS software, where the total scores of the Computer Vision Syndrome questionnaire were calculated for each individual. The “Kolmogorov-Smirnov” test was conducted to analyze the distribution of the CVS score variable. Given the non-normal distribution of the data, the non-parametric “Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis” tests were employed to assess the mean differences between groups. Additionally, Spearman’s test was utilized to evaluate the correlations.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 230 students participated in the study, with an average age of 21 years (21.18 ± 1.52), ranging from 18 to 25 years. More than half of the participants were female (57%), and a vast majority were single (96.2%) and lived with their parents (57%). Regarding the field of study, nearly two-fifths of the students were enrolled in nursing (40.9%), followed by about one-fifth in emergency medicine (18.3%). The remaining participants were distributed among health information technology (14.3%), medicine (13.9%), and laboratory sciences (12.2%). Students from the first to fifth academic years were almost evenly represented. The most frequently used digital device was the mobile phone, reported by nearly nine out of ten students (87.8%). The primary purposes for using digital devices included education, recreation, and social networking, noted by more than four-fifths of participants (81.1%) (Table 1).

Recurrence and severity of CVS symptoms

The study’s results indicated that 87.4% (201 participants) had experienced at least one CVS symptom. Among the participants, 95.4% of women and 84.8% of men reported experiencing at least one of the symptoms. The most prevalent symptoms included headaches (60.4%), burning eyes (55.7%), tearing (50.9%), and eye pain (49.6%). Less common symptoms were double vision (14.4%), difficulty focusing on close objects (17.4%), and seeing colored halos around objects (17.8%) (Table 2).

The average CVS score was 10.33 ± 13.27. For men and women, the scores were 9.01 ± 10.99 and 10.95 ± 15.00, respectively. The prevalence of CVS among the students was found to be 76.1%. Crosstab analysis indicated that no statistically significant gender difference in CVS prevalence was found (p =.096), although females reported higher rates (80.2%) compared to males (70.7%). The findings of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated a non-normal distribution of CVS scores (p ≤.001), so non-parametric tests were employed. Moreover, the average CVS score among female students was significantly greater than that of male students (p =.004).

Mean differences in CVS scores

This study investigates the average differences in total CVS scores across different groups. The results indicate that the average CVS score is unrelated to the most commonly used digital tool (p =.907). However, several factors were associated with increased CVS scores among participants: longer duration or years of using digital devices (p =.022), fewer breaks taken while using these devices (p ≤.001), and not adhering to the 20-20-20 rule (p =.015). It should be noted that this question referred to general use throughout the day and was not limited to studying or academic activities.

Participants experiencing pain in the joints of their fingers (p =.015), wrists (p =.013), and necks (p ≤.001), and those with headaches reported statistically significant higher average CVS scores compared to those without pain in these areas (p =.039). Additionally, individuals who reported difficulties in holding objects (p ≤.001) and those with eye diseases (p ≤.001) showed statistically significant differences in their average CVS scores compared to others. Discussion

Prevalence

The present study investigated the factors contributing to CVS among 1,403 Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences students. The findings revealed that the prevalence of CVS among participants was 76.1%, while 87.4% of participants reported experiencing at least one symptom. This prevalence falls within the higher range reported in previous studies. For instance, similar or slightly lower rates have been observed among bank employees in Ethiopia (73%) and users in Nigeria (74%), while comparable or higher rates have been reported among students in India (77.5% and 80.3%) and office employees in Sri Lanka (67.4%). These findings suggest that CVS prevalence remains consistently high across different populations with prolonged digital device exposure [16–19].

Several studies in Iran and neighboring countries have explored the prevalence of Computer Vision Syndrome (CVS), reporting variable rates depending on the population and methodology. For instance, a study among university staff and graduate students in Tehran reported a CVS prevalence of 48.7%, with a significant correlation between longer screen time and increased symptom severity [20]. Similarly, research conducted in Isfahan among professional computer users showed that 79% experienced burning eyes, 66% had dry eye, and 65% reported asthenopia, highlighting the ocular and musculoskeletal burden associated with digital device use [21].

In comparison, the prevalence reported among students studying health sciences in Saudi Arabia was 97.3% [6], while Thai students participating in virtual classes experienced a rate of 97.9% [22]. These latter results are higher than those found in the current study. The study team attributes the differences in prevalence to variations in sample sizes and the inclusion of additional symptoms, such as neck and shoulder pain, which are indicators of CVS. In the present study, the prevalence of CVS was significantly higher among female students compared to males. This finding is consistent with previous research reporting a higher prevalence of CVS among females [19, 23–25]. Several behavioral and ergonomic factors may contribute to this gender difference. For instance, females may engage more frequently in near-work activities or take fewer screen breaks during prolonged digital device use. Additionally, differences in posture, workstation ergonomics, screen height, and lighting preferences could increase CVS-related symptoms in females. Subratty et al. [24] also found that women reported occupational overuse symptoms, including visual discomfort, more frequently than men, possibly due to gender-based roles involving extended screen exposure. Similarly, Ranasinghe et al. [19] identified female gender as a significant predictor of CVS. Our results also indicated a higher prevalence of CVS among female students, which may suggest the need for targeted preventive strategies.

Symptoms

In this study, the most reported symptom among students was headaches, occurring in 60.4% of cases. This was followed by eye irritation at 55.7%, eye-watering at 50.9%, and eye pain at 49.6%. In contrast, the study performed by Ranasinghe et al. reported headaches in 45.7% of participants, dry eyes in 31.1%, and pain around the eyes in 28.7% [19]. Similarly, a study found that 41.8% of participants reported headaches, eye pain at 31.6%, and eye pressure at 26.7% [26].

Multiple studies have identified headaches as the most common symptom [6, 19, 27], which aligns with our findings. However, the prevalence of headaches reported in this study is higher than in other research. For example, the rates were 53.3% among students at Emirates University and 43.2% among students from medical and engineering universities in Chennai [18, 28], both of which are lower than the results of our study. Headaches are often associated with other CVS symptoms, though many patients may not recognize them as a direct visual issue [19].

Comparing the CVS score among groups

The findings of this study indicate that the average CVS score did not differ significantly based on the primary digital tool used by participants. However, the study revealed several factors contributing to higher CVS scores among participants: an increase in the duration or number of years using digital devices, a decrease in rest periods while using these tools, and a failure to adhere to the 20-20-20 rule.

Our findings indicate that prolonged use of digital devices, reduced frequency of breaks, and not following the 20-20-20 rule are associated with higher CVS scores. These associations are consistent with the trends reported in previous studies, although causality cannot be established due to the cross-sectional nature of this research. Taking breaks during extended computer work is essential for reducing eye strain, as shifting the focus of one’s eyes from the screen helps relax the eye muscles. In contrast, using digital devices for more than seven hours daily exacerbates these symptoms [6, 17, 23, 25, 27]. Overall, the results of the current study did not show a statistically significant relationship between daily computer use and CVS; however, other factors such as total years of use, taking fewer breaks, and ignoring the 20-20-20 rule were significantly associated with higher CVS scores. The current study revealed that individuals with eye diseases are more likely to experience CVS than those without such conditions. Multiple studies have indicated that a history of eye diseases and using glasses and corrective or contact lenses are associated with a heightened risk of CVS [18, 23, 27]. In our study population, we found a significant association between eye diseases and the occurrence of CVS.

A study performed by Logaraj et al. found a significantly increased risk of CVS for users who viewed their screens at less than 20 inches [29]. Similarly, research by Chiemeke and his colleagues indicated that more pronounced visual symptoms occurred when users viewed their screens from less than 10 inches away [30]. The studies by Hassan et al. and Shantakumari et al. noted that the prevalence of headaches in students decreased significantly when they viewed their screens from a distance greater than 50 cm [28, 31]. However, While the overall analysis did not reveal a statistically significant association between viewing distance and CVS across all groups, pairwise comparisons indicated significant differences between participants who used devices at < 40 cm and > 76 cm, and between those at 40–76 cm and > 76 cm; this aligns with a survey conducted in Sri Lanka among computer workers [19]. It is worth noting that the “American Optometry Association” recommends a viewing distance of 20 to 28 inches [14].

The results of this study indicate that individuals experiencing ergonomic issues—such as joint pain in the fingers, wrists, neck, and headaches—have statistically significantly higher average CVS scores compared to those who do not experience pain in these areas. In a related study, Hassan et al. reported that many engineering students are unaware of the correct sitting posture. They emphasized that the height of their workstations and awareness of ergonomic principles are significantly associated with reducing CVS symptoms [19, 32]. Additionally, the present study’s findings reveal that even though users are knowledgeable about ergonomic practices, they still experience a higher prevalence of CVS due to neglect and non-implementation of these methods. Therefore, more significant efforts should be made to teach students how to use electronic devices properly.

The study’s strengths and suggested directions

This study has several strengths that contribute to its value and relevance. First, it utilized a validated and symptom-specific questionnaire, ensuring reliable measurement of CVS based on both frequency and severity of symptoms. Second, the sample size was adequate and included participants from multiple disciplines and academic years, enhancing the generalizability of the findings within the university context. Moreover, the study addressed both visual and physical symptoms associated with prolonged digital device use, offering a more comprehensive understanding of CVS manifestations. Future research should consider longitudinal designs to assess causality and symptom progression over time. Additionally, integrating clinical eye examinations alongside self-reported data may improve diagnostic accuracy. Expanding the research to include students from other universities and educational settings can also provide broader insight into the prevalence and risk factors of CVS.

Limitation

The study was a cross-sectional analysis using a convenience sampling of university students. The results relied entirely on self-reported symptoms, which may be subject to recall bias and reporting inaccuracies. No clinical eye examinations were performed. Additionally, individuals who had not recently undergone an eye examination and those with a history of eye disease were not excluded from the study. Furthermore, as participants were exclusively from medical and paramedical faculties, who are more likely to use digital devices extensively for academic purposes, there is a potential for selection bias related to higher screen exposure in this population.

Conclusion

The findings revealed that CVS symptoms were more prevalent among female students, those with longer years of digital device use, and individuals who did not adhere to the 20-20-20 rule. Additionally, lack of regular breaks, inability to adjust screen brightness, and pre-existing eye diseases were significantly associated with higher CVS scores. Musculoskeletal discomfort, such as pain in the fingers, wrists, and neck, as well as headaches and difficulty holding objects, were also linked to increased CVS symptoms. The most frequently reported symptoms included headaches, burning eyes, watery eyes, and eye pain.

Given that CVS can negatively affect students’ productivity and well-being, it is essential to promote ergonomic practices and raise awareness regarding the proper use of digital tools. Universities should implement targeted interventions such as ergonomic education programs, scheduled screen breaks, and strategies for optimizing screen settings to reduce the risk and severity of CVS symptoms. Future research, including longitudinal studies and clinical eye assessments, is recommended to validate and expand upon these findings.
