Ultrasound-activated nanoregulator enables Cu2+ directional enrichment and enhances cuproptosis via energy-gated feedback loop
Authors: Haiyan Huang, Rui Zhang, Yu Luo, Kexin Zhang, Bingbing Duan, Gulizhaer Ainiwa, Yiting Ma, Yamei Miao, Lingxiao Zhang, Xiaofeng Zhu, Xiang Li, Kaiyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modulating intratumoral copper homeostasis to trigger cuproptosis is a promising copper‑based anticancer strategy, but its clinical translation is hindered by the absence of precise spatiotemporal control over intracellular copper levels and ATP7A‑mediated active copper efflux. Here we report an ultrasound-activated copper molybdate nanoregulator, HCu1.5MoO5 (HCMO), that releases copper on demand and reprograms copper flux in tumor cells under ultrasound. Once internalized, ultrasound activates HCMO to release copper ions, providing a sustained intracellular copper supply. In parallel, HCMO also generates reactive oxygen species that impair mitochondrial function and depress cellular ATP, thereby attenuating the activity of the ATP-dependent exporter ATP7A and narrowing copper efflux. This dual-axis imbalance, in situ copper supplement, and efflux limitation significantly disrupt cellular copper homeostasis, interrupt the tricarboxylic-acid cycle, induce mitochondrial dysfunction, further enhance copper accumulation, and eventually cause irreversible cuproptosis. Together with apoptosis and necroptosis, the coordinated damage releases damage-associated molecular patterns and induces immunogenic cell death. Overall, this spatiotemporally programmable strategy links materials, physical fields, and immunity in a closed framework to coordinately disrupt copper homeostasis, offering a generalizable route to on-demand cuproptosis therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04290-9.

Introduction

Cuproptosis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancers [1–3], by overwhelming intracellular copper homeostatic buffers and directly ligating lipoylated enzymes of the tricarboxylic-acid (TCA) cycle, while destabilizing iron-sulfur (Fe-S) proteins [4–6]. The resulting proteotoxic stress and bioenergetic collapse define a death program distinct from apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis [7, 8]. Recent work further shows that copper dysregulation can elicit immunogenic cell death (ICD) [9, 10], creating opportunities to couple local tumor control with systemic anti-tumor immunity [11–13].

Clinically, however, cuproptosis has been difficult to trigger precisely [14–16]. Conventional approaches lack spatiotemporal precision; they cannot guarantee that copper dysregulation occurs specifically in tumor cells on demand. Moreover, tumor cells actively maintain copper homeostasis through the ATP-dependent exporter ATP7A [17, 18], which prevents excessive accumulation of intracellular copper. Systemic delivery of copper ionophores lacks tumor selectivity [19, 20], whereas gene-silencing strategies that disable ATP7A face delivery and stability constraints [21–23]. These gaps motivate a modality that can (i) raise the intracellular copper supply at the tumor site [24, 25] and (ii) shut down ATP7A-mediated efflux at the same time [26, 27].

Ultrasound (US) technology stands out as a powerful candidate to bridge this gap. As a clinically established non-invasive modality, US offers superior tissue penetration, enabling precise treatment for deep-seated tumors [28, 29]. US can trigger piezocatalytic processes to generate toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) in situ, to induce oxidative stress [30]. However, the catalytic efficiency of piezoelectric sonosensitizers is limited by the electronic band structures. Conventional piezoelectric materials typically possess relatively wide bandgaps, such as TiO₂ (≈3.05 eV) [31], Fe-UBTO (≈3.16 eV) [32], and Mn-ZnO (≈2.95 eV) [33], or moderate bandgaps like BiOCl (≈2.39 eV) [34] and BMO-MXene (≈2.67 eV) [35]. These wide energy gaps impose substantial barriers for electron excitation and carrier transport, thereby restricting the yield of ROS generation.

Herein, we develop an US activated copper molybdate nanoregulator with small bandgap (≈1.75 eV), HCu1.5MoO5 (HCMO), that generated ROS efficiently and reprogrammed copper flux inside tumor cells under US (Scheme 1). Upon cellular internalization, through the activation of US, HCMO synchronously (a) generates ROS, including hydroxyl radical (•OH) and superoxide (•O2−) to impair mitochondrial respiration and lower ATP production, thereby reducing the activity of the ATP-dependent Cu2+ exporter ATP7A, closing the copper efflux; and (b) releases Cu2+ inside acidic environment to provide a sustained intracellular Cu2+ source, which disrupts the mitochondrial TCA cycle, provoking aggregation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and loss of Fe-S cluster, such as ferredoxins (FDX-1) and lipoyl synthase (LIAS). This further disables ATP7A and enhances Cu2+ accumulation, establishing a positive feedback loop. Together, HCMO triggers a one-two punch, an internal Cu2+ overload coupled with the suppression of the cell’s Cu2+ export capacity, synergistically forcing tumor cells into an irreversible cuproptosis. In parallel, mitochondrial injury co-activates apoptosis and necroptosis. Together with cuproptosis, this coordinated damage amplifies the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that mature dendritic cells (DCs) and enhance anti-tumor immune response. This work provides a spatiotemporally programmable route to induce cuproptosis on demand, overthrowing tumor cells’ Cu2+ homeostasis, providing a universal strategy for targeted Cu2+ homeostasis-based tumor treatment.Scheme 1(a) Synthesis of HCMO; (b) its ultrasound-activated Cu2+ directional enrichment and enhanced cuproptosis via energy-gated feedback loop

Results and discussion

HCMO was obtained through a mild, urea-assisted aqueous co-precipitation route, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Following ultrasonic exfoliation, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the as-prepared HCMO formed rod-like shapes (Fig. 1b). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) showed an average hydrodynamic diameter of ≈297 nm with a narrow distribution (PDI ≈0.24, Fig. S2), and a slightly negative zeta potential ≈ − 4.5 mV (Fig. S3). Then, the lattice crystallinity and composition were investigated. High-resolution TEM showed lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of ≈0.375 nm assignable to the (0 1 1) plane of HCu1.5MoO5 (Fig. 1c). The XRD pattern of the exfoliated HCMO matched the reference pattern of HCu1.5MoO5 (JCPDS 89–6870) (Fig. 1d). Elemental mapping showed a homogeneous distribution of Cu, Mo and O across HCMO (Fig. 1e). The XPS survey (Fig. S6) also verified the presence of Cu, Mo and O. The high-resolution spectra of Cu 2p further showed Cu 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks at ≈934.75 and ≈954.57 eV, respectively, with shake-up satellites, indicating that Cu was at Cu(II) state (Fig. 1f). The high-resolution spectra of Mo 3d showed a Mo 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 doublet at ≈232.37 and 235.50 eV, respectively, consistent with Mo(VI) state (Fig. 1g). The O 1 s signal centered at ≈530.84 eV corresponded to lattice O2− (Fig. S7).

To investigate if HCMO can produce ROS under US, its piezoelectric properties were investigated, including the valence band (VB), bandgap, and conduction band (CB) of HCMO [36–38]. XPS spectrum revealed that the VB is at ≈2.23 eV (Fig. 1h). The bandgap of HCMO was calculated from the UV–vis absorption using Tauc analysis, which was ≈1.75 eV (Fig. 1i), together giving a CB value of ≈0.48 eV. In addition, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) provided direct evidence of piezoelectric characteristics in HCMO, showing distinct butterfly-shaped displacement-voltage curves and hysteresis loops (Fig. 1j). Under US, periodic mechanical strain polarized HCMO, separated charge carriers and drove carrier transport, which was captured as synchronous on/off ultrasonic current transients (Fig. 1k). Under US, the internal piezoelectric field caused band energy increased on the CB side and dropped on the VB side, which also caused band bending [39]. As a result, HCMO enabled the oxidation of H2O/OH− to •OH and the reduction of O2 to •O2−, establishing dual radical generation under US (Fig. 1l).

To evaluate the ability of HCMO to generate ROS, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy using DMPO as the spin trap was implemented to identify the generation of •OH and •O2−. Additionally, colorimetric oxidation of 3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) and 1,3-Diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) probe was also applied to detect the •OH and •O2− generation (Fig. 1m). As shown in Fig. 1n, a characteristic 1:2:2:1 quartet of DMPO-OH was observed for HCMO + US, whereas HCMO or US alone produced negligible signals, indicating efficient •OH under US. Colorimetric oxidation of TMB to ox-TMB further corroborated •OH generation. HCMO + US yielded a time-dependent increase in ox-TMB that scaled with US duration (Figs. 1o and S8) and HCMO concentration (Fig. S9). Furthermore, ESR also resolved the sextet after HCMO + US treatment, confirming •O2− formation (Fig. 1p). In parallel, as shown in Fig. 1q, HCMO + US showed a US time-dependent depletion of DPBF absorbance (Fig. S10). Depletion of DPBF absorbance was also observed with increased HCMO dose (Fig. S11). Together, these data suggested that the US can activate HCMO to produce •OH and •O2− concurrently.

We next tested the Cu2+ release from HCMO by using inductively coupled plasma (ICP). As shown in Fig. S12, ICP analysis showed that HCMO releases Cu2+ more rapidly under mildly acidic conditions (pH ≈5–6) than at neutral pH, and US further accelerated the Cu2+ release. This pH-dependent and US enhanced behavior implies that HCMO could preferentially release Cu2+ within the acidic environment of tumor cells.

Motivated by the US-activated ROS generation and Cu2+ release, we then investigated the cellular uptake of HCMO by labeling HCMO with Cy2 and monitoring the internalization by MB49 at various time points. Time-lapse microscopic imaging revealed progressively intensified cytoplasmic signal from 4–12 h (Fig. S13). We next evaluated the cytotoxicity of HMCO. Cell-counting kit (CCK-8) assays showed that HCMO has minimal effects on cell viability for both MB49 and L929 cells for different incubation periods (Figs. 2a and S14–15), indicating good in vitro biocompatibility without the activation of US. On the other hand, upon US irradiation, HCMO displayed marked anti-tumor activity. Treating MB49 cells with HCMO + US sharply reduced viability compared to HCMO, US, and control groups (Fig. 2b). Additionally, a clear concentration dependence was also observed across different concentrations of HCMO when combined with US (Fig. S16). As previously found that HCMO generated ROS under US, the DCFH-DA probe was used to examine the intracellular ROS level. As shown in Figs. 2c and S17, the DCFH-DA probe exhibited the brightest green fluorescence in the HCMO + US group, indicating a significant amount of ROS within the cells, while HCMO or US alone produced only a weak signal. Such accumulation of ROS could induce cell death; thus, the live/dead staining using Calcein-AM/PI showed a widespread PI-positive cell in the HCMO + US group, indicating significant cell death after the treatment (Figs. 2d and S18). Flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI further showed a robust increase of Annexin V+ apoptotic populations under HCMO + US, whereas other groups remained largely Annexin V⁻/PI⁻ (Figs. 2e and S19). Together, these data verify that the US functions as an external “on-switch” that turns on HCMO’s therapeutic potential by triggering intracellular oxidative pressure.

Our previous work has identified that accumulation of intracellular ROS could lead to mitochondrial dysfunction [28, 40], thus we next examined whether HCMO compromises mitochondrial bioenergetics and ATP production. JC-1 staining revealed a pronounced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) after the HCMO + US treatment, evidenced by a red-to-green fluorescence shift, while other groups maintained polarized mitochondria (Figs. 2f and S20). Bio-TEM further showed evidence of damage in mitochondria, including cristae fragmentation and vacuolization (Fig. 2g). Consistent with this structural mitochondrial damage, intracellular ATP levels dropped significantly in the HCMO + US group (Fig. 2h).

Because the copper exporter ATP7A is an ATP-driven exporter, thus, this ATP deficit could reduce the activity of the ATP7A. Therefore, the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by HCMO creates an energy crisis to inhibit the copper efflux. Consistently, Western blot (WB) analysis revealed a marked downregulation of ATP7A protein expression following HCMO + US treatment (Figs. 2i and S21–22), likely attributed to a combination of synthesis inhibition and stress-induced degradation. This blockade of efflux, synchronized with the on-demand release from HCMO, led to a substantial intracellular Cu2⁺ overload, as evidenced by the intense fluorescence of the Cu2⁺ probe (Rhodamine B hydrazide) in the HCMO + US group (Fig. S23). This disruption of copper homeostasis triggered the canonical hallmarks of cuproptosis, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated DLAT aggregation (Figs. 2j and S24) and decline in FDX-1 intensity after HCMO + US treatment (Figs. 2k and S25). Corroborating these morphological changes, WB analysis confirmed the oligomerization of DLAT and the concurrent depletion of Fe-S cluster proteins (FDX-1 and LIAS) (Fig. 2i),. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HCMO + US drives a ROS-mediated ATP shortage to suppress ATP7A-mediated Cu2+ efflux, while simultaneously supplying Cu2+ inside cells, thereby driving irreversible cuproptosis.

Given that severe mitochondrial stress is a convergence point for multiple lethal signaling pathways, we hypothesized that the HCMO-induced bioenergetic collapse would propagate beyond cuproptosis. We first interrogated the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. As shown in Fig. 3a, compared with the control, US, or HCMO groups, the HCMO + US treatment markedly increased cytosolic cytochrome-c (Cyt-c) level expression from the WB test, indicating the execution of apoptosis. In parallel, receptor-interacting protein kinase-3 (RIPK3) was also found to be upregulated in the WB test, suggesting the engagement of necroptosis (Figs. S26–27). Immunofluorescence imaging corroborated these findings, where HCMO + US treatment showed elevated fluorescence signal in Cyt-c, cleaved caspase-3 (c-Cas-3), and RIPK3 staining compared to other groups (Figs. 3b and S28–30). Because mitochondrial damage could be immunogenic [29, 41], we next examined the release of DAMPs, including high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1) and calreticulin (CRT). HCMO + US caused pronounced release of HMGB1 from the cell nucleus, with a significant fluorescence signal drop in nuclear (Fig. 3c–d), and markedly increased surface exposure of CRT (Fig. 3e–f).

These released DAMPs could induce ICD; therefore, we extracted bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) from mice and co-cultured them in vitro with supernatants from tumor cells subjected to various treatments to investigate the initiation of innate immune activation. Flow cytometry revealed that HCMO + US significantly augmented the proportion of CD80+/CD86+ mature BMDCs (Fig. 3g–h), raising the maturation rate to ≈22.9% in the HCMO + US group, compared to ≈7.4%, ≈9.8%, and ≈12.6% in the control, US, and HCMO groups, respectively. To further verify the functional activation of DCs, we quantified the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the supernatant. As depicted in Figs. S31–32, the supernatant of the HCMO + US treated group displayed significantly elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) compared to other groups, these pro-inflammatory cytokines would be necessary for T cell priming. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HCMO + US orchestrates a multimodal cell death program driven by mitochondrial collapse. By simultaneously triggering cuproptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, this strategy transforms dying tumor cells into immunogenic activation by releasing DAMPs. This efficient promotes DC maturation, converting local therapy to the initiation of adaptive immune response.

Systemic safety is a prerequisite for any clinical translation of nanomaterials. Before validating the therapeutic efficacy, we investigated the bio-safety of HCMO. Hemolysis assay showed negligible erythrocyte lysis across the tested concentration range (Fig. S33). Routine hematology and serum biochemistry remained indistinguishable between the control and HCMO groups (Figs. S34–35), indicating no acute disruption of hematopoietic indices or liver-kidney function. Histopathology of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) revealed no overt structural lesions by hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. S36). These findings support the bio-safety of HCMO.

We next quantified the in vivo distribution of HCMO in subcutaneous MB49 bladder tumor-bearing mice. Using a fluorescently labeled probe, ex vivo imaging demonstrated time‐dependent tumor accumulation, reaching a maximum ≈12 h after intravenous dosing (Figs. S37–38). Guided by this result, the US was scheduled 12 h post‐injection to maximize the therapeutic effect of HCMO.

Next, we established a subcutaneous MB49 bladder tumor model to evaluate therapeutic efficacy. MB49 tumor-bearing mice (initial tumor volume ≈60–80 mm3) were randomized into four groups (control, US, HCMO, HCMO + US). HCMO with PBS or PBS alone (for the control group) was administered via the tail vein on days 0 and 7. US was applied to the US and HCMO + US groups 12 h after the injection. Mice were sacrificed on day 14 (Fig. 4a). Individual growth curves revealed continuous tumor growth in the control, US, and HCMO groups, whereas tumors in the HCMO + US group grew slowly or transiently stalled (Fig. 4b). Consistently, the relative tumor volume from HCMO + US group is significantly lower than other groups (Fig. 4c). Macroscopic tumor photographs and endpoint tumor weights corroborated the strongest suppression in the HCMO + US group (Fig. 4d–e). To investigate the in vitro mechanism, Tumors were collected, and their cross-sections were stained with H&E, Ki-67, TUNEL, and DCFH-DA probe (Fig. 4g). Compared with the control, US, and HCMO groups, the HCMO + US group caused (i) the most pronounced architectural disruption by H&E, (ii) the lowest proliferative index by Ki-67, (iii) the strongest TUNEL positivity indicative of apoptosis, and (iv) the highest intratumoral ROS signal. These results align with the therapeutic effects of HCMO + US observed in vitro. More importantly, immunofluorescence analysis of the tumor sections confirmed cuproptosis in vivo. As shown in Figs. S39–40, the tumors treated with HCMO + US exhibited DLAT aggregation and reduction in FDX-1 expression compared to the control and monotherapy groups, confirming Cu2+ overload and the subsequent cuproptosis cascade were triggered in vivo.

We then profiled intratumoral T-cell recruitment and activation by examining the CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. S42). Within the CD3+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte gate, HCMO + US significantly increased CD4+ T-cell frequency to ≈20.3% (control ≈6.5%, US ≈7.6%, HCMO ≈9.8%) (Fig. 5c,f), and CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell infiltration to ≈30.7% (control ≈5.1%, US ≈6.5%, HCMO ≈11.2%) (Fig. 5d,g). These data support that the combined cuproptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis can elicit ICD effectively.

To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms associated with HCMO, we performed RNA-seq on tumors from the control and HCMO + US groups. A volcano plot revealed extensive differential expression, with ≈372 genes upregulated and ≈70 genes downregulated in the HCMO + US group (Fig. 5h). As shown in Fig. 5i, hierarchical clustering of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) confirms a distinct transcriptional state induced by treatment. GO enrichment highlighted immune-activation programs, including antigen processing and presentation (MHC I/II complex), response to interferon (and its positive regulation), adaptive immune response, chemokine-mediated signaling, and lymphocyte activation (Fig. 5j). Notably, terms linked to cellular response to Cu2+/oxidative stress and regulation of metabolic enzyme complexes were also enriched, consistent with a metallo-metabolic trigger that couples Cu2+ dysregulation to immune activation. KEGG analysis further identified broad engagement of immune activation, including T-cell receptor signaling, antigen processing and presentation, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT, chemokine signaling, and Toll-like receptor pathways, accompanied by coordinated rewiring of mitochondrial/energy-metabolism circuits (Fig. 5k). We further mapped representative DEGs to enriched terms and pathways using a chord diagram (Fig. 5l) and a Sankey plot (Fig. 5m). The chord diagram links the top GO categories to their contributing genes and highlights extensive gene sharing among immune‑activation terms, including antigen processing/presentation, response to interferon, chemokine‑mediated signaling, and lymphocyte activation. The Sankey plot also revealed broad engagement of immune circuits, such as T‑cell receptor signaling, antigen processing/presentation, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, JAK–STAT, chemokine, and Toll‑like receptor pathways, while simultaneously routing genes into stress/energy‑metabolism pathways such as MAPK and cAMP signaling. Together, these results were consistent with the observed DCs maturation and the enhanced CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells infiltration.

Conclusion

This study established an ultrasound-activated HCMO nanoregulator that executed two synchronized intracellular controls: (i) ROS-compromised mitochondria and depleted ATP, down-shifting the ATP-dependent exporter ATP7A and limiting Cu2+-efflux; and (ii) US enabled a sustained release of intracellular Cu2+. The resulting dual-axis imbalance pushed Cu2+ beyond the stability threshold, driving toward irreversible cuproptosis. In parallel, mitochondrial damage co-activated apoptosis and necroptosis, and cooperatively enhanced ICD, promoting DCs maturation and effector T-cell infiltration. This strategy integrates materials, physical stimulus, and immunity within a single programmable platform for targeted and on-demand Cu2+ homeostasis regulation, facilitating the application of cuproptosis in tumor therapy. Despite these promising results, several limitations warrant further investigation. While the short-term biocompatibility of HCMO was confirmed, its long-term metabolic pathway and potential chronic toxicity in major organs require more comprehensive evaluation to ensure clinical safety. Additionally, the therapeutic performance of HCMO in other deep-seated or large-volume tumors might need further modification and optimization. Future work will focus on the precision delivery of HCMO to various tumor types and the exploration of its synergistic potential with other clinical modalities like chemotherapy or immunotherapy.

Methods

Materials

Copper dinitrate and urea were sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd, and DMPO was acquired from Dojindo Laboratories. Ammonium molybdate were purchased from Aladdin Ltd. DLAT and FDX-1 antibody was obtained from Sanying Ltd. MB49 and L929 cells were acquired from American type culture collection. The ATP assay kits were provided by Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd, CRT and HMGB1 antibodies were supplied by Proteintech Group, Inc. CD45-FITC, CD11c-Violet 450, CD80-APC, CD86-PE/Cy7, CD3-Violet 450, CD4-Percp/Cy5.5, and CD8a-APC antibodies were obtained from Elabscience Biotechnology Co. Ltd.

Synthesis of HCu1.5MoO5

Dissolve 18 mmol of ammonium molybdate in 300 mL of deionized water and stir at 85 °C. Subsequently, add 0.1 g of urea to the ammonium molybdate solution and stir for 10 min. Subsequently, 18 mmol of copper dinitrate was added and mixed into the ammonium molybdate solution under continuous stirring at 85 °C. The solution was cooled to room temperature over 5 h, washed with water, dried at 50 °C for 24 h, and then ultrasonic exfoliated at 600 W for 6 h to obtain the final product.

Characterization

Characterization techniques utilized in this study include AC-HAADF-STEM conducted with a Titan Cubed Themis G2 300; XRD utilizing a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer; XPS performed with a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha; ultrasonic current measurement using a CH1760E electrochemical analysis instrument; ESR spectra acquired with a Bruker EMX1598 spectrometer; zeta potential and DLS measurements carried out with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90; UV–vis absorption spectra recorded using a UV-1901 spectrophotometer; diffuse reflectance; flow cytometry analyses conducted with an Agilent NovoCyte flow cytometer; and fluorescence microscopy using a Thermo EVOS M5000.

Cell culture

L929 and MB49 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units mL⁻1 penicillin, and 100 µg mL⁻1 streptomycin, under 5% CO₂ at 37 °C.

Immunofluorescence experiments

MB49 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The medium was then replaced with a complete medium containing HCMO (100 µg mL⁻1), and the cells were co-cultured for 12 h. As in the previous experiment, various treatments were applied under the same conditions, followed by overnight culture. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. They were then blocked with an immunofluorescence-blocking solution and incubated with a primary antibody against DLAT, FDX-1, c-Cas-3, Cyt-c, RIPK 3, CRT, and HMGB1 at room temperature for different periods. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with a secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488) for 1 h, and then stained with Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min. Finally, DLAT was detected using a fluorescence microscope.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy evaluation

Male C57BL/6 mice (3–4 weeks old) were selected for the establishment of the tumor model. MB49 cells (2 × 106) suspended in PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right hind limb of each mouse. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 60 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4): (1) Control, (2) US, (3) HCMO (4) HCMO + US. The mice were then administered HCMO intravenously. For the US treatment groups, tumors were irradiated using an ultrasonic therapy apparatus (Intelect Mobile Ultrasound, Model 2776, DJO, France) with parameters set at 1.0 MHz, 1 W cm⁻2, and 50% duty cycle for 10 min, conducted 12 h post-injection. Tumor size and body weight were monitored every two days throughout the treatment period. All the procedures were approved by Shanghai Chengxi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval NO. CX052511102).

Statistical analysis

All the data are shown as the mean ± SD. Unless stated otherwise, experiments were implemented in triplicate. The significance of the difference was decided through one-way ANOVA (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.
