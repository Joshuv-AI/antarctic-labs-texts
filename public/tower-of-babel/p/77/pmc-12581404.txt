Expression profile of cystatin gene family in alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) related to biotic and abiotic stress response
Authors: Jierui Wu, Qian Ai, Rui Dai, Chengmei Zhou, Zhiqiang Zhang, Lan Yun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCystatins are cysteine protease inhibitor in plant and animal that not only regulates the intracellular transport of endogenous proteins in plants but also plays an important role in plant defense against biotic and abiotic stresses. However, the cystatin gene family in alfalfa remains poorly characterized.ResultIn this study, 55 cystatin genes distributed across 18 chromosomes in alfalfa were identified. GO enrichment analysis indicated that the 55 MsCYS genes were primarily associated with cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity. These genes were classified into six distinct subgroups, with each containing one or two typical cystatin domains. Analysis of cis-acting regulatory elements suggested the potential involvement of MsCYS genes in stress responses. Transcriptome analysis revealed that nearly half of the MsCYS genes were not expressed across the examined tissues and stress conditions and those that are expressed showed higher expression levels in stems and leaves than in roots. Expression levels of MsCYS genes were higher in stems and leaves than in roots. Under stress caused by thrips, MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 were significantly up-regulated. Real-time RT-PCR results corroborated the transcriptome data. The expression of MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 increased markedly, reaching 5.5-fold and 8.3-fold that of the control levels. After 14 days of thrips infestation. The cysteine content in alfalfa leaves increased significantly, which was associated with the up-regulation of MsCYS genes. Additionally, exogenous application of jasmonic acid enhanced the expression of certain MsCYS genes, thereby conferring resistance to thrips in alfalfa. Exogenous salicylic acid also up-regulated the expression of certain MsCYS genes and modulated insect defense.ConclusionOur study identified 55 members of the cystatin gene family in alfalfa and analyzed their phylogenetic relationships, structural characteristics, and putative functions in resistance to biotic and abiotic stress. This work establishes a robust foundation for further functional characterization of each cystatin gene member, which provides novel insights for improving the stress tolerance of alfalfa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12161-0.Keywords: Alfalfa, Cystatin gene family, Biotic stress, Abiotic stress, Expression

Introduction

Cystatins are a family of cysteine protease inhibitors found in plants that play regulatory roles in sulfur metabolism and protein turnover [1]. They lack disulfide bonds and putative glycosylation sites, and function as reversible inhibitors of C13 legumain peptidase and papain-like cysteine proteases [2, 3]. The inhibitory mechanism is mediated by three conserved motifs: the QxVxG active site in the central loop, one or two glycine residues near the N-terminus, and the A/PW motif near the C-terminus [4]. Furthermore, plant cystatins typically adopt an α-helical fold and contain a conserved LARFAV motif, which contributes to the formation of a hydrophobic core within the molecule [5].

Plant cystatins function by regulating cysteine protease activity, thereby controlling protein turnover [6]. They play crucial roles in seed germination, maturation, programmed cell death, and responses to various abiotic stresses [7, 8]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that plant cystatins contribute to plant defense against pests, fungi, and viruses [9, 10]. Overexpression of cystatin genes in Arabidopsis thaliana and Beta vulgaris significantly enhanced tolerance to salt, drought, and cold stress [11, 12]. Cystatins also play a pivotal role in the development and senescence of legume nodules [13]. For instance, investigation of Glycine max cystatin gene suggested that GmCYS16 may positively regulate nodulation in soybean [14]. Although genomic studies of cystatin genes have been conducted in species such as Malus pumila Mill and Hordeum vulgare, elucidating their roles in plant development and stress responses [6, 15], a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the cystatin gene family in alfalfa has not been reported.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa), the most widely cultivated legume forage crop, is characterized by its high protein content and excellent palatability, making it a crucial feed source for livestock throughout the world [16]. Meanwhile, its yield and quality are substantially compromised by various biotic and abiotic stresses [17]. To reduce losses caused by stress, investigating the growth, development, especially stress response mechanisms of alfalfa, has become research focus of breeders. MsCYS1, a cystatin gene isolated from young alfalfa leaves, is predominantly expressed in stems and leaves, where it plays a critical role in regulating endogenous protease activity and thus influences early stem and leaf development [18]. Beyond modulating endogenous cysteine proteases, cystatins also inhibit proteases in herbivorous insects. To better understand the functions of cystatin genes in alfalfa growth, development, and stress responses, we performed a genome-wide identification of the cystatin gene family in this species. Our results lay the foundation for further functional characterization of each cystatin gene member and offer valuable insights for improving stress resistance in alfalfa.

Materials and methods

Identification of the MsCYS genes in alfalfa

We retrieved the alfalfa genome sequence from the NCBI database [19], and the cystatin domain (Accession No. PF00031) from the Pfam database. Subsequently, we used both the cystatin domain and the identified Arabidopsis thaliana cystatin gene as queries to identify putative cystatin genes in the GenBank non-redundant protein database and the alfalfa genome [19]. All candidate alfalfa cystatin genes identified through these methods were further subjected to a conserved domain search in the NCBI database to verify the presence of the cystatin domain.

The amino acid sequences of the identified MsCYS proteins were analyzed using the ProtParam tool to determine their size [20], molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), instability coefficient, aliphatic index and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) [21]. Subcellular localization of the MsCYS proteins was predicted using the Cell-PLoc 2.0 software [22]. Protein secondary structures were predicted with the SOPMA software [23].

Analysis of phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs and gene structure

Following multiple sequence alignment of the cystatin protein sequences from Medicago sativa [24], Medicago truncatula [25], Arabidopsis thaliana [19] and Oryza sativa [26], we performed a phylogenetic analysis using MEGA 7.0 software [27]. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed with the maximum likelihood (ML) method and 1000 bootstrap replicates. To identify conserved motifs in the MsCYS proteins, we employed the MEME Suite [28]. Gene structure information was extracted from the alfalfa genome annotation file (GFF) using TBtools software and subsequently visualized [29].

Chromosome distribution and gene duplication

To determine the chromosomal distribution of the MsCYS gene family in alfalfa, we used TBtools to visualize gene locations based on the genome-wide annotation data of alfalfa [30]. We also employed a multicollinearity analysis toolkit to examine gene duplication events between Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula, as well as between Medicago sativa and Arabidopsis thaliana [29]. Furthermore, TBtools was used to calculate non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates for the identified duplicate gene pairs [29].

Promoter cis-acting regulatory element analysis

The 2000-bp genomic sequences upstream of the translation start site of the MsCYS genes were retrieved from the CADL Genome Blast Server. Cis-acting regulatory elements involved in plant growth, development, stress responses, and hormone signaling were identified using the PlantCARE online database [31].

Plant materials cultivation

The plant material used in this study was Medicago sativa L. ‘Caoyuan No.4’, whose seeds were obtained from the College of Grassland Science at Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. This insect-resistant variety was developed by Inner Mongolia Agricultural University through multiple cycles of field selection, and demonstrated improved tolerance to thrips infestation [32]. Thrips are pervasive pests that impact alfalfa production in China and many other major growing areas [33]. These insects inflict damage by feeding on phloem sap, leading to nutrient loss, growth inhibition, reduced biomass yield, and in severe cases, plant death [34].

Alfalfa plants were grown in pots containing a mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite at a 2:1 ratio. Each pot housed a single plant and was maintained in a greenhouse under controlled conditions, with day/night temperatures set at 30 ± 5 ℃/20 ± 5 °C. Water was supplied every two days as needed. After 45 days of growth under standardized conditions, fifteen uniformly grown seedlings per treatment group were selected for subsequent stress treatments.

Plant stress treatment

The stress caused by thrips was induced following the method established by Tu [34]. After 45 days of growth, uniformly developed seedlings were selected and infested with 100 thrips per pot, applied directly to the leaves. Each pot was then enclosed with a 300-mesh nylon bag. Sampling was conducted at 0 d, 7 d, and 14 d post-infestation. The raw transcriptome sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI database under accession number PRJNA1053830. For simulated drought stress, a 15% PEG-6000 solution was prepared using Hoagland nutrient solution. After 45 days of growth, plants were subjected to the drought treatment, and samples were collected at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, and 24 h. For hormone treatments, plants were sprayed five times with 0.5 mmol/L salicylic acid and 100 µmol/L methyl jasmonate after 45 days of growth. The treated plants were then covered with transparent plastic bags to minimize hormone volatilization. Samples were taken at 0 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h after treatment. For tissue-specific expression analysis, root, stem, and young leaf samples were collected from alfalfa plants. Fifteen plants were assigned to each treatment group, with three biological replicates each. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis [35].

Analysis of MsCYS genes family expression patterns

Transcriptome data of alfalfa under different tissue conditions (SRP055547) and drought stress (SRP145557) were downloaded from the NCBI database [36, 37]. Transcriptome data under stress caused by thrips were generated through independent sequencing in this study, and the raw data have been deposited in the NCBI database under the accession number PRJNA1053830. Expression patterns of the MsCYS genes across various tissues, drought conditions, and stress caused by thrips were analyzed, and a heatmap was constructed to visualize the results.

GO functional annotation and cysteine content determination

We utilized transcriptome data from unstressed conditions (PRJNA1053830, control group) to identify all 55 MsCYS genes and then performed Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and enrichment analyses to elucidate their potential biological functions. Additionally, cysteine content in alfalfa leaves was measured at 0 d, 7 d, and 14 d after thrips infestation with an amino acid analyzer [38].

RNA isolation and real-time RT-PCR analysis

Based on phylogenetic analysis and expression profiling in response to stress caused by thrips, nine MsCYS genes representing six distinct subgroups were selected for real-time RT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the OmniPlant RNA Kit (Cwbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop ND1000 spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the EasyQuick RT MasterMix (Cwbio, Beijing, China) with total RNA as the template. The resulting cDNA was used as template for gene expression analysis. Real-time RT-PCR was performed on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using 2 × MagicSYBR Mixture, with the alfalfa β-actin gene (GenBank: JQ028730.1) as the internal reference [39]. Primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool [40] (Tab S1). The experiment included three biological replicates and two technical replicates per sample. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method [41].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 21.0 software [42]. Differences were considered statistically significant at p-value < 0.05. Figures were prepared using SigmaPlot 14.0 [43].

Results

Identification and sequence characteristics of MsCYS genes in alfalfa

Genome-wide identification of CYS family members in alfalfa was conducted using HMM profiles (PF00031) and BLAST searches, with seven CYS genes from Arabidopsis thaliana serving as query sequences [19, 44]. A total of 55 CYS genes were identified. The encoded proteins ranged from 68 amino acids (MsCYS11) to 240 amino acids (MsCYS49), with molecular weights varying from 7.847 kDa to 26.831 kDa. The predicted isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 4.67 (MsCYS45) to 7.05 (MsCYS3 and MsCYS6). Based on instability indices, MsCYS19, MsCYS31, and MsCYS37 (values >40) were classified as unstable proteins, while the remaining 52 MsCYS proteins were deemed stable. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) was below zero for 36 MsCYS proteins, indicating hydrophilic characteristics, whereas the other 19 proteins exhibited hydrophobic properties. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that all MsCYS proteins are cytoplasmic (Table 1). Secondary structure prediction revealed that the MsCYS proteins are predominantly composed of α-helices and random coils, with α-helix content ranging from 29.59% to 46.22% and random coil content between 32.77% and 51.92% (Table S2).

MsCYS genes identified in alfalfa and their sequence characteristics

Phylogenetic analysis of MsCYS genes in alfalfa

To investigate the evolutionary relationships among MsCYS proteins, a neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed using the amino acid sequences of Medicago sativa, Medicago truncatula, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Oryza sativa. The 55 MsCYS proteins were classified into six distinct groups according to phylogenetic clustering: Group 1 (12 members), Group 2 (5 members), Group 3 (18 members), Group 4 (10 members), Group 5 (6 members), and Group 6 (4 members). Most MsCYS proteins were distributed in Groups 1, 3, and 4. Orthologous gene pairs such as MsCYS41/MtCYS13 and MsCYS14/MtCYS4 were identified within Group 1. Group 3 consisted solely of MsCYS and three MtCYS proteins, while Group 4 included MsCYS proteins and one AtCYS5 protein. The phylogenetic analysis revealed that the genetic relationship between Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula cystatin genes is much closer than that between Medicago sativa and Oryza sativa or Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 1).

Conserved motifs, domains and gene structure analysis of the MsCYS genes

To identify conserved motifs among the MsCYS proteins, motif analysis was conducted using the MEME program. A total of 15 conserved motifs were identified, with lengths ranging from six amino acids (motif 10) to 50 amino acids (motif 9) (Fig. 2E). MsCYS proteins within the same subgroup exhibited similar motif distribution patterns. Notably, all MsCYS proteins contained the QxVxG active site (motif 2) and a PW motif near the C-terminus (motif 4). A total of 52 MsCYS proteins contained an A motif near the C-terminus (motif 5), while the remaining 3 may have lost this motif during evolution. A total of 48 MsCYS proteins possessed two glycine residues near the N-terminus (motif 6). A total of 52 MsCYS proteins contained the LARFAV motif (motif 1 and motif 7), whereas the other three may have undergone nucleotide substitutions during evolution (Fig. 2B, Table S3). Domain analysis confirmed that all 55 MsCYS proteins contain cystatin domains, further supporting their classification within the cystatin gene family (Fig. 2C). Exon-intron structure plays a pivotal role in the evolutionary dynamics of gene families. The 55 MsCYS genes typically contain one to four exons, with a predominant occurrence of single-exon structures, potentially resulting from structural adaptations during evolution (Fig. 2D).

Chromosomal distributions and collinearity analysis of MsCYS genes

Chromosomal localization analysis revealed an uneven distribution of the 55 MsCYS genes across 18 chromosomes in alfalfa. Most MsCYS genes were located on chr4.2 (9 genes), chr4.3 (6 genes), chr8.2 (6 genes), and chr8.1 (5 genes). In contrast, only one MsCYS gene was identified on each of chr2.3, chr4.4, and chr5.1 (Fig. 3). Gene duplication serves as a major driving force in genome evolution and genetic diversification. A total of 36 fragment duplication pairs of MsCYS genes were identified across 17 chromosomes, with no duplication events detected on chr7.2. MsCYS11 and MsCYS12 both showed collinearity with four other MsCYS genes (Fig. 4). To investigate selective constraints acting on duplicated MsCYS gene pairs, the ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates (Ka/Ks) was calculated (Table S4). Generally, Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection, Ka/Ks = 1 suggests neutral evolution, and Ka/Ks < 1 implies purifying selection. The Ka/Ks ratios for all 25 duplicated gene pairs were < 1, indicating that the MsCYS gene family has undergone predominantly purifying selection.

Identification of MsCYS collinear gene pairs in alfalfa. The collinear gene pairs are linked by black lines

To further investigate the phylogenetic relationships of cystatin genes in alfalfa, we performed synteny analysis between the Medicago sativa and Arabidopsis thaliana genomes. This analysis identified five collinear gene pairs: MsCYS3/AtCYS4, MsCYS6/AtCYS4, MsCYS43/AtCYSA, MsCYS44/AtCYSA, and MsCYS49/AtCYSB. Similarly, synteny analysis between the Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula revealed 48 collinear gene pairs. These results suggest the presence of evolutionarily conserved homologous genes across species, which may retain similar functions and regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 5).

Identification of cis-acting regulatory elements in the MsCYS promoters

Cis-acting regulatory elements in the 2000-bp promoter region upstream of the transcription start site of MsCYS genes were analyzed. These promoter regions were found to contain numerous regulatory elements involved in various biological processes, including plant hormone responsiveness (abscisic acid, salicylic acid, methyl jasmonate, and gibberellin), growth and development (light signaling and auxin response), and stress responses (drought and low temperature). Photosynthesis, a fundamental process in plants, depends critically on light signal perception and transduction, which regulates numerous physiological activities. Methyl jasmonate, an endogenous plant hormone, modulates responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses. Abscisic acid, another key phytohormone, is instrumental in mediating adaptation to drought and high-salinity conditions (Fig. 6). In summary, the MsCYS gene family is implicated in regulating phytohormone signaling, coordinating developmental processes, and responding to diverse environmental stresses.

Expression pattern analysis of MsCYS genes in different tissues

To further investigate the roles of MsCYS genes in growth and development, we analyzed their expression patterns across different tissues using publicly available transcriptome data from previous studies. A total of 24 MsCYS genes showed no expression in roots, stems, or leaves. MsCYS41 and MsCYS43 were highly expressed in roots but exhibited relatively lower expression in stems and leaves, suggesting their primary involvement in root growth and development. The remaining 29 MsCYS genes showed significantly higher expression levels in stems or leaves compared to roots (Fig. 7A). To validate the transcriptome data, nine highly expressed MsCYS genes representing all six phylogenetic groups were selected for tissue-specific expression analysis in roots, stems, and leaves using real-time quantitative PCR. The results were consistent with the transcriptome data, confirming their reliability. These nine genes were expressed in all three tissues but showed elevated expression in stems and leaves. Notably, MsCYS6 expression in stems was approximately 13-fold higher than in roots (Fig. 7B). Overall, these findings indicate that MsCYS genes display tissue-specific expression patterns, with the majority being highly expressed in stems and leaves.

Expression profile of MsCYS genes under stress caused by thrips

Thrips, a type of phytophagous insect, can significantly reduce both the yield and quality of alfalfa. To investigate the expression patterns of MsCYS genes under thrips infestation, we infested alfalfa leaves with thrips and performed transcriptome sequencing after 0, 7, and 14 days to assess how this treatment influences the expression of the MsCYS gene family. Among the 55 MsCYS genes, 27 genes were not expressed, 10 genes were up-regulated, 14 genes were down-regulated, and 4 genes showed an initial increase followed by a decrease in expression. Among these genes with expression levels, five genes exhibited high expression: MsCYS3, MsCYS6, MsCYS14, MsCYS49, and MsCYS54. MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 were progressively up-regulated with prolonged thrips feeding, whereas MsCYS3, MsCYS6, and MsCYS14 were significantly down-regulated under the same conditions (Fig. 8A). Real-time RT-PCR results under thrips infestation were highly consistent with the transcriptome data. After 14 days of treatment, the expression of MsCYS3, MsCYS6, MsCYS12, and MsCYS48 was significantly down-regulated. In contrast, the expression of MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 was markedly up-regulated, reaching levels of 5.5-fold and 8.3-fold higher than that of control, respectively (Fig. 8B). These genes are suggestively key regulators in activating alfalfa’s defense response against thrips infestation.

Functional annotation analysis of MsCYS genes family

To further investigate the functional roles of the MsCYS gene family, GO annotation analysis was conducted using transcriptome data from unstressed alfalfa leaves. The GO analysis covers three major categories: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Within biological processes, the MsCYS genes were primarily associated with response to stimulus (5 genes), biological regulation (3 genes), and cellular process (3 genes). For cellular components, most annotations were assigned to cell part (3 genes) and membrane part (3 genes). In the molecular function category, all 55 MsCYS genes were annotated as molecular function regulators, and 2 genes were linked to catalytic activity (Fig. 9A). GO enrichment analysis indicated that the 55 MsCYS genes were significantly enriched in biological processes related to cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, endopeptidase inhibitor activity, and peptidase inhibitor activity (Fig. 9B). Given that cystatin directly influences cysteine levels in plants, we quantified cysteine content in alfalfa leaves under thrips stress. The results revealed a significant increase in cysteine content with prolonged stress duration. Compared to the control group, cysteine content increased by 42.25% after 7 days of thrips infestation and doubled after 14 days (Fig. 9C).

Expression profile of MsCYS genes under drought stress

Analysis of cis-acting regulatory elements in the MsCYS genes indicated a notable abundance of drought stress response elements. Consequently, we further investigated the expression patterns of these genes under drought stress. A total of 28 MsCYS showed no expression under these conditions. Overall, the drought stress expression profiles aligned with their tissue-specific expression patterns. Among them, MsCYS2, MsCYS4, and MsCYS7 were not expressed in roots, stems, or leaves and were only detected at trace levels during the early stage of drought stress, with no expression observed at later time points. In contrast, MsCYS22 and MsCYS37, though expressed at low levels in leaves under control conditions, were not expressed during drought stress. Cluster analysis revealed three major expression patterns among the MsCYS genes in response to drought. The first pattern was characterized by absence of expression throughout the stress period. The second pattern showed an initial increase, followed by a decrease, and then a subsequent rebound in expression. The third pattern exhibited an initial up-regulation followed by down-regulation (Fig. 10A). Real-time RT-PCR results indicated that the expression levels of several MsCYS genes fluctuated at early (2 h) or middle (8 h) stages of drought stress, a trend consistent with the transcriptome data. Among the up-regulated genes, MsCYS6 showed the highest expression level, with an approximately 5-fold increase after two hours of drought exposure (Fig. 10B).

Expression profile of MsCYS genes in response to plant hormones

Previous studies have shown that overexpression of the wheat cystatin gene TaMDC1 in tomato confers broad-spectrum resistance to bacterial pathogens, a defense response mediated by methyl jasmonate and salicylic acid [45]. In this study, we also identified an abundance of salicylic acid and jasmonic acid response elements within the promoter regions of the MsCYS genes. These observations prompted us to explore the potential functions of MsCYS genes, particularly their roles in hormone-mediated signaling pathways. After treatment with 100 µmol/L jasmonic acid, the nine selected MsCYS genes showed varying degrees of up-regulation, with fluctuating expression levels observed after three hours interval. Notably, MsCYS49 exhibited the strongest response, with expression levels nearly 14-fold higher than that of the control (Fig. 11A).

Following application of 0.5 mmol/L salicylic acid, the expression of MsCYS3, MsCYS6, MsCYS8, and MsCYS20 was significantly up-regulated, showing temporal variation at 3, 6, and 24 h post-treatment. Among these, MsCYS6 displayed the highest expression at 24 h, reaching nearly 5-fold than that of the control. Discussion

Plant cystatins are crucial not only for storage protein mobilization during seed germination and plant growth [46], but also for regulating endogenous protein turnover and inhibiting cysteine proteases in herbivorous insects [7]. Therefore, we comprehensively analyzed the characteristics, phylogeny, and expression patterns of the cystatin gene family in alfalfa under various stress conditions, leading to the identification of key candidate genes involved in plant stress responses.

A total of 55 MsCYS genes were identified in alfalfa, representing a substantial expansion of the cystatin family compared to other species. For example, only 13 and 18 cystatin genes have been reported in barley and sorghum, respectively [6, 47]. This variation in gene number may reflect differences in adaptive evolution among species. The encoded proteins of 55 MsCYS genes range from 68 (MsCYS11) to 240 (MsCYS49) amino acids in length, with molecular weights between 7.847 kDa and 26.831 kDa, and isoelectric points (pI) ranging from 4.67 (MsCYS45) to 7.05 (MsCYS3 and MsCYS6). Except for MsCYS19, MsCYS31, and MsCYS37, which were predicted to be unstable proteins, the remaining 52 proteins were classified as stable proteins. A total of 36 MsCYS genes encode hydrophilic proteins, while the other 19 encode hydrophobic ones. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that all MsCYS proteins are cytoplasmic. Previous studies have demonstrated that the majority of plant cystatins possess a relatively small molecular weight, typically ranging from 12 to 16 kDa. However, due to the presence of an extended C-terminal tail, plant cystatins exhibit an increased molecular weight [3].

Phylogenetic analysis in this study indicated that the identified alfalfa cystatin proteins share high sequence homology with those from Medicago truncatula. By comparing the protein sequence of 55 MsCYS genes, we identified three characteristic motifs typical of cystatins: the central QxVxG active site (motif 2), two glycine residues near the N-terminus (motif 6), and the PW motif near the C-terminus (motif 4) [48, 49]. Additionally, the conserved LARFAV motif was widely present within the MsCYS gene family (motif 1 and motif 7) [5]. All 55 MsCYS proteins contained motif 2 and motif 4, while 47 contained motif 6. This variation may result from nucleotide deletions during evolution. Cystatins containing cysteine residues but lacking the LARFAV motif have been reported in certain algae [19], and members of the cystatin superfamily have also been identified in bacteria [50], supporting the hypothesis of independent parallel evolution of cysteine protease inhibitors in animals and plants. This evolutionary trajectory suggests a common ancestral origin, with plant cystatins having diverged through mechanisms such as gene duplication, alternative splicing, and adaptive evolution, leading to the diversity of cystatins encoded in plant genomes [51, 52]. Domain analysis confirmed that all 55 MsCYS genes contain the cystatin domain. These genes are distributed across 18 chromosomes in alfalfa, with the majority located on chr4.2 (9 genes), chr4.3 (6 genes), chr8.2 (6 genes), and chr8.1 (5 genes). In contrast, only one single MsCYS gene was found on each of chr2.3, chr4.4, and chr5.1, possibly due to tandem duplication events [53]. Intraspecific collinearity analysis revealed 25 pairs of segmentally duplicated genes within this family. Ka/Ks analysis showed that all duplicated gene pairs had a ratio less than one, indicating that the MsCYS gene family has undergone predominant purifying selection during evolution.

Cis-acting regulatory elements play an essential role in modulating hormone responses during plant development and resistance to various stresses [54]. In this study, we analyzed the 2000-bp promoter sequences upstream of the transcription start sites of MsCYS genes in alfalfa. The promoter region of MsCYS harbors numerous responsive regulatory elements, including those involved in plant hormone response (abscisic acid, salicylic acid, methyl jasmonate, gibberellin), growth and development (light signaling, auxin), and stress response (drought, low temperature). These results suggest that the MsCYS genes are not only involved in growth and development but also play a significant role in responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses.

Initially, we examined the tissue-specific expression patterns of the 55 MsCYS genes. The results showed that 43.64% of these genes were expressed at low levels in roots, stems, and leaves, while 52.73% exhibited higher expression in stems and leaves compared to roots, consistent with previous findings [18]. Plant cystatins can act as defense proteins against diseases, insect pests, and pathogens [55]. Insecticidal cystatins have been isolated from barley, tomato, and potato [56–59], and antifungal cystatins have been identified in wheat [60]. Given the substantial impact of thrips on alfalfa yield and quality, we performed transcriptome sequencing under thrips stress. The data revealed that 27 MsCYS genes were not expressed. 10 were up-regulated, 14 were down-regulated, and 4 showed an initial increase followed by a decrease in expression. MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 were highly expressed and up-regulated under thrips infestation, suggesting their potential as key regulators in alfalfa’s defense response. We also focused on the expression pattern of MsCYS gene under abiotic drought stress. The clustering analysis revealed the presence of three distinct expression patterns. The first pattern was characterized by no expression. The second pattern involved an initial increase in expression followed by a decrease and then a subsequent increase once more. The third pattern was marked by an initial rise in expression followed by a decline. Cystatins play a crucial regulatory role in the plant’s response to abiotic stress. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, the expression levels of AtCYS1 and AtCYS2 were significantly up-regulated under high temperature stress [8]. Additionally, AtCYSa and AtCYSb are induced by various abiotic stresses including salt, drought, oxidation, and cold stress [11]. The expression of AtCYS5 was induced during Arabidopsis thaliana seed germination under high temperature conditions and exogenous ABA treatment. Moreover, the overexpression of AtCYS5 significantly enhanced the stress resistance in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana plants [61]. Overall, the MsCYS genes that were silent in tissues remained silent or lowly expressed under thrips and drought stress. The six groups we identified included MsCYS genes with silent or stress-regulated functions. To verify the transcription data’s reliability, we selected nine highly expressed MsCYS genes for real-time RT-PCR validation. Their expression levels were elevated in stems and leaves. MsCYS6 stood out with a significant increase in expression in stems, reaching approximately 13-fold higher than in roots. After 14 days of stress caused by thrips, the expression of MsCYS3, MsCYS6, MsCYS12, and MsCYS48 was significantly reduced. Conversely, MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 expression increased markedly, reaching 5.5-fold and 8.3-fold more than the control levels, respectively. The expression levels of MsCYS3, MsCYS6 and MsCYS14 were markedly elevated under drought stress conditions. MsCYS6 displayed the highest level of expression with an approximately 5-fold increase after two hours of drought stress. The real-time RT-PCR data generally aligned with the transcriptome results.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of MsCYS gene functions, we conducted GO annotation and enrichment analysis using transcriptome data obtained from unstressed conditions. The results of the GO annotation indicated that all 55 MsCYS genes are annotated as molecular function regulators. Furthermore, the GO enrichment analysis revealed that these 55 MsCYS genes are predominantly enriched in biological processes linked to cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity. The cystatin superfamily of protease inhibitors regulates cysteine proteases across various biological systems [7]. Inhibitory cystatins form tight, reversible complexes with cysteine proteases. They act as pseudo-substrates, entering the active site cleft of target enzymes to inhibit their activity [62]. Cystatins directly affect cysteine content in plants. We focused on the alterations of cysteine levels under stress caused by thrips, and the results demonstrated a significant increase in cysteine content in alfalfa during stress caused by thrips infestation. Our research investigated the correlation between MsCYS genes and insect resistance. Since salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate are known as key regulators of plant insect resistance response [63, 64], we applied exogenous salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate to examine MsCYS gene expression under hormone treatment. Following treatment with 100 µmol/L jasmonic acid, nine MsCYS genes were up-regulated to varying degrees. MsCYS49 displayed the highest expression level, exhibiting an up-regulation of nearly 14-fold compared to the control group. After treatment with 0.5 mmol/L salicylic acid, the expression levels of MsCYS3, MsCYS6, MsCYS8 and MsCYS20 were significantly up-regulated.

Based on our findings, we propose the following hypothesis: when thrips feed on alfalfa leaves, the plant responds by up-regulating the expression of genes such as MsCYS49 and MsCYS54. This elevated expression enhances cystatin activity, which modulates cysteine content in the leaves and activates downstream defense responses. Additionally, cystatin may inhibit proteases in the thrips’ digestive system, thereby disrupting their nutrient absorption and metabolism [54]. Furthermore, exogenous application of jasmonic acid and salicylic acid can induce the expression of MsCYS genes, improving alfalfa’s resistance to thrips. In summary, this study provides novel and unique insights into the response of the alfalfa cystatin gene family to various stresses, particularly thrips infestation. However, further functional studies are required to validate the roles of these candidate genes.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified 55 MsCYS genes in the alfalfa genome, distributed across 18 chromosomes. These genes were classified into six distinct subgroups. GO enrichment analysis indicated that the MsCYS genes are primarily associated with cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity. Transcriptome analysis revealed that nearly half of the MsCYS genes were not expressed across the examined tissues and stress conditions, and those that are expressed showed generally higher expression levels in stems and leaves than in roots. Under thrips infestation, MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 were significantly up-regulated. Real-time RT-PCR results corroborated the transcriptome data: the expression of MsCYS49 and MsCYS54 increased significantly after 14 days of thrips infestation. Concurrently, cysteine content in alfalfa leaves rose substantially, which correlated with the up-regulation of MsCYS gene expression. Moreover, exogenous application of jasmonic acid induced the expression of MsCYS genes, thereby enhancing resistance to thrips. Salicylic acid treatment also up-regulated a subset of MsCYS genes and contributed to the modulation of plant defense response. In summary, this study offers novel insights into the biological functions of the MsCYS gene family and the mechanisms of alfalfa resistance to biotic and abiotic stress.
