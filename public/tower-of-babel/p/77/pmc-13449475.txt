Loneliness despite social integration: subjective loneliness without social isolation as a risk factor for psychotic-like experiences
Authors: Norio Sugawara, Takahiro Tabuchi, Keita Tokumitsu, Norio Yasui-Furukori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychotic-like experiences (PLEs) are associated with subjective loneliness, as well as social isolation. However, few studies have examined how different combinations of loneliness and social isolation relate to overall PLEs and specific psychotic symptom domains. The aim of this study was to examine the associations between combinations of loneliness and social isolation and PLEs in a population-based sample in Japan.MethodsData were derived from the 2025 wave of the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey (JASTIS), including 25,424 adults aged 15–84 years. PLEs were assessed using the Japanese version of the PRIME Screen–Revised (PS-R). Loneliness was measured using the University of California, Los Angeles Loneliness Scale version 3, Short Form 3-item (UCLA-LS3-SF3), and social isolation was assessed using the Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS-6). Logistic regression analyses with inverse probability weighting were conducted to examine associations between four categories of loneliness/social isolation and overall PLEs as well as six psychotic symptom domains.ResultsThe weighted prevalence of PLEs was 4.3%. Compared with participants with both loneliness and social isolation, those with neither loneliness nor social isolation showed lower odds of overall PLEs (OR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.42–0.63), as did those with social isolation without loneliness (OR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.51–0.74). In contrast, participants with loneliness without social isolation had higher odds of overall PLEs (OR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.24–1.71). Symptom-domain analyses indicated heterogeneous patterns across domains.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that loneliness without social isolation is particularly associated with PLEs, highlighting the importance of subjective loneliness as a correlate of PLEs. This suggests that reliance on objective indicators of social isolation alone may overlook individuals at elevated risk, emphasizing the importance of attending to subjective loneliness in population-based mental health research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00685-y.Keywords: Psychotic-like experiences, Loneliness, Social isolation, General population, Symptom domains

Introduction

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) are subclinical manifestations of psychotic phenomena, such as hallucination-like perceptual abnormalities, delusion-like beliefs, and suspicious or unusual thought content, that occur in individuals without a diagnosed psychotic disorder [1]. These experiences are typically less frequent, less severe, and less persistent than clinical psychotic symptoms and are conceptualized within a psychosis continuum framework, ranging from transient subclinical experiences to persistent and severe psychotic disorders [2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that PLEs are relatively common in the general population, with lifetime prevalence estimates of approximately 5.8% [3]. Accumulating evidence further suggests that PLEs are associated with adverse mental and physical health outcomes [4, 5], as well as impaired social functioning [6], highlighting their clinical and public health relevance even in the absence of a diagnosable psychotic disorder.

There is growing evidence that PLEs are closely linked to social experiences, particularly loneliness [7]. Population-based studies have consistently shown that individuals reporting PLEs experience higher levels of loneliness compared with those without such experiences [8, 9], and recent meta-analytic findings demonstrate a robust association between psychotic experiences—especially paranoia-related symptoms—and loneliness [7]. Although these findings support a strong association, the causal direction between loneliness and PLEs remains to be clarified. Nonetheless, several theoretical frameworks posit that loneliness may play a role in the development and maintenance of PLEs, potentially through mechanisms such as heightened social threat perception, negative social cognition, and emotional distress [10, 11].

Importantly, loneliness is conceptually and empirically distinct from social isolation [12]. Loneliness refers to the subjective perception of a discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships, whereas social isolation reflects an objective lack or paucity of social contacts and social participation. Although loneliness and social isolation are moderately correlated and often co-occur, they represent separable constructs [13, 14]. Individuals may feel lonely despite having frequent social interactions, or conversely may not feel lonely despite being socially isolated. This yields four social profiles defined by the presence or absence of loneliness and social isolation, which may have different implications for PLEs. Emerging evidence suggests that PLEs may be more strongly associated with subjective loneliness than with objective social isolation [15, 16], underscoring the importance of considering both constructs simultaneously. Nevertheless, few studies have explicitly examined how different combinations of loneliness and social isolation relate to PLEs, leaving an important gap in understanding the social context in which PLEs arise. Among individuals experiencing loneliness, social interactions may not necessarily function as protective but may instead be perceived as socially threatening or distressing, possibly due to heightened vigilance to social threat and negative cognitive biases in social information processing [17, 18].

Moreover, PLEs do not constitute a unitary construct but instead comprise heterogeneous symptom dimensions that may differ in their underlying mechanisms and social correlates [2]. Accumulating evidence indicates that the association between social experiences and PLEs is not uniform across symptom domains. In particular, loneliness appears to be more consistently and strongly linked to paranoia-related experiences than to other psychotic-like symptoms [7]. In contrast, hallucination-like experiences may be more closely related to social isolation and may arise through distinct socio-cognitive and affective pathways, such as the social deafferentation hypothesis [19]. These findings suggest that examining PLEs solely as a binary or aggregate construct may obscure important symptom-specific associations. Furthermore, the relationship between social disconnection and other PLE domains, such as grandiosity, has been relatively underexplored, highlighting the need for analyses at the level of individual symptom domains when investigating social determinants of PLEs.

The aim of this study was to examine the associations between combinations of loneliness and social isolation and PLEs in a population-based sample in Japan. Using a four-category classification based on the presence or absence of loneliness and social isolation, we investigated their associations with overall PLEs as well as with specific psychotic symptom domains. We hypothesized that individuals with loneliness in the absence of social isolation would show the strongest associations with PLEs among the four groups.

Methods

Design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis to examine how different combinations of loneliness and social isolation relate to PLEs, using data derived from the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey (JASTIS), a nationwide web-based survey [20]. The analytic dataset originated from an online panel survey that has been implemented annually since 2015 [20–22]. The 2025 survey wave was administered between February and March. Participants were sampled from a large commercial research panel comprising approximately 2.2 million registered users. For the 2025 wave, stratified random sampling was applied to assemble a nationally representative cohort of 28,000 individuals aged 15–84 years from all 47 prefectures of Japan. Sampling quotas were predefined according to sex, age category, and prefecture, and recruitment within each stratum was terminated once the target number—derived from the 2019 national census—had been reached. This procedure was intended to preserve demographic representativeness at the national level. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to survey participation. The survey platform required participants to answer items in sequence and did not allow item skipping, thereby preventing missing responses. Of the 46,312 individuals who had taken part in JASTIS or the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey (JACSIS) during 2015–2024 and remained contactable via the survey company, all were approached for participation. Completed questionnaires were returned by 25,799 respondents, resulting in a response rate of 55.7%. In parallel, to augment the follow-up sample, the same questionnaire was fielded as an additional survey targeting newly registered panel members. Invitations for this supplementary recruitment were issued in two batches—75,462 on March 29, 2025, and 49,829 on March 30, 2025—for a combined total of 125,291 invitations. A total of 2,201 responses were received from these new panel members (response rate ≈ 1.8%). Consequently, 28,000 responses were deemed valid overall. Participation was voluntary, and withdrawal was permitted at any time without penalty. Ethical oversight and regulatory compliance were ensured throughout. Specifically, the study procedures conformed to the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects and were consistent with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration (as amended in 2024). Approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the Osaka International Cancer Institute (Approval No. 20084) on June 19, 2020. In addition, the survey agency operated in accordance with the Act on the Protection of Personal Information in Japan. Respondents received “Epoints,” which may be redeemed for online shopping or cash.

Post-collection data integrity checks were then implemented using predefined criteria. We removed respondents with extremely short completion times (less than 600 s), those who did not pass an attention-check item, those indicating use of all listed medication categories, those reporting every health condition listed, and those stating that more than 15 family members lived in the same household. After applying these exclusions, the analytic dataset comprised 25,424 participants (Fig. 1).

Outcomes and variables

PLEs

PLEs were assessed using the Japanese version of the PRIME Screen–Revised (PS-R) [23]. The PS-R consists of 11 items assessing attenuated positive psychotic symptoms, each rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (definitely disagree) to 6 (definitely agree). The 12th item of the original PS-R, which assesses insight into illness, is omitted in the Japanese version because it does not refer to attenuated positive symptoms [23]. Specifically, participants were classified as having overall PLEs if they met at least one of the following conditions: (i) endorsement of at least one item rated 6 (definitely agree) with symptom duration of one year or longer; (ii) endorsement of two or more items rated 6 regardless of symptom duration; (iii) endorsement of two or more items rated 5 (somewhat agree) with symptom duration of one year or longer; or (iv) a total PS-R score of 39 or higher.

The 11 PS-R items were further categorized into six psychotic symptom domains: perplexity and delusional mood (items 1 and 5), first-rank symptoms (items 3, 6, and 11), overvalued beliefs (items 2 and 4), suspiciousness/persecutory ideas (item 7), grandiose ideas (item 8), and perceptual abnormalities (items 9 and 10) [23–25]. Endorsement of each symptom domain was determined based on prior studies, with items scored as 5 or 6 considered positive [24, 25]. For analyses of the six psychotic symptom domains, endorsement additionally required that the symptom had persisted for at least one year. A symptom domain was considered present if one or more items within the domain met these criteria.

Loneliness and social isolation

The UCLA Loneliness Scale version 3, Short Form 3-item (UCLA-LS3-SF3) was employed to evaluate loneliness [26]. This instrument asks participants how frequently during the past 30 days they experienced (1) a lack of companionship, (2) feeling left out, and (3) feeling isolated. The Japanese version of the UCLA-LS3-SF3 has demonstrated established validity and reliability. Each item was rated on a four-point scale (‘never’ = 0, ‘rarely’ = 1, ‘sometimes’ = 2, ‘always’ = 3) [27]. Total scores ranged from 0 to 9, with higher values reflecting greater loneliness severity. Scores of 4–9 were categorized as loneliness [28, 29].

Social isolation was defined as the absence of social relationships. To assess this construct, the Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS-6) was used [30]. The Japanese version of the LSNS-6 has been validated and shown to be reliable [31]. This instrument was originally designed to screen for social isolation in community-dwelling adults. Possible scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater social connectedness. Consistent with prior studies, scores of 0–11 were classified as social isolation [30].

Based on the presence or absence of social isolation and loneliness, participants were further classified into four mutually exclusive categories: neither social isolation nor loneliness, loneliness without social isolation, social isolation without loneliness, and both social isolation and loneliness [12].

Covariates

The following variables were treated as covariates in the analyses: age; sex (female or male); marital status (married, never married, widowed, or divorced); educational attainment (high school or below, college or above, or others, which included participants who did not specify their educational attainment); self-rated health (good/very good, fair, or poor/very poor); household income (JPY, income < 4 million, 4 million ≤ income < 8 million, 8 million ≤ income, or data not available); and self-reported history of psychiatric illness.

Statistical analyses

To address selection bias inherent in web-based sampling, we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) to align our internet survey sample with the demographic structure of the general population [32]. The 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC), conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), served as our reference population. Merging the CSLC and internet survey datasets enabled us to model the probability of survey participation. Participants were stratified by sex and age into 14 groups, and within each stratum, propensity scores (PSs) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression. Predictor variables for the PS model were selected based on availability in both datasets and included factors such as geographic region, marital status, education, household, self-rated health, and smoking status. Some covariates were excluded for specific subgroups based on prior research and data availability. For respondents aged 15–19 years, marital and smoking status were omitted due to their uniformity and lack of data collection on youth smoking post-2013. Among females in this age range, area of residence was also excluded, given minimal regional variation in internet access within this demographic [33]. For women aged 70 and above, education was not included in the model due to historical disparities in access to higher education.

We first described participant characteristics by the presence or absence of PLEs. Categorical variables were compared descriptively as proportions. All analyses, including participant characteristics, were conducted using IPW to ensure representativeness. To examine the associations between social isolation/loneliness categories and PLEs, including overall PLEs and six categories of psychotic symptoms, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses. The four-category variable based on social isolation and loneliness (no isolation/no loneliness, no isolation/loneliness, isolation/no loneliness, and isolation/loneliness) was included as the primary independent variable, with the group characterized by both isolation and loneliness used as the reference category. To further clarify the gradient of risk, we additionally conducted supplementary analyses using the group with neither isolation nor loneliness as the reference category. All models were adjusted for age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, self-rated health, household income, and history of psychiatric illness. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated all models without weighting, presented in the Supplement. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. All analyses were performed with SPSS for Windows (version 30.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA).

A similar pattern was observed for perplexity and delusional mood, in which participants with loneliness without social isolation exhibited higher odds (OR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.01–1.67, p = 0.043) compared with the reference group, while both categories without loneliness showed reduced odds (No isolation/ No loneliness, OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.25–0.54, p < 0.001; Isolation/ No loneliness, OR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.30–0.58, p < 0.001). Regarding first-rank symptoms, loneliness without social isolation did not show differences relative to the reference group, whereas social isolation without loneliness (OR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.36–0.58, p < 0.001) and neither loneliness nor social isolation (OR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.32–0.53, p < 0.001) showed lower odds. For overvalued beliefs, loneliness without social isolation remained associated with higher odds (OR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.17–2.01, p = 0.002), whereas neither of the groups without loneliness (with or without social isolation) showed a statistically significant association. For suspiciousness/persecutory ideas, loneliness without social isolation was not associated with differences relative to the reference group, whereas social isolation without loneliness (OR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.24–0.55, p < 0.001) and neither loneliness nor social isolation (OR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.12–0.36, p < 0.001) showed lower odds relative to the reference group. For grandiose ideas, loneliness without social isolation showed higher odds (OR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.11–2.17, p = 0.009) compared with the reference group, whereas both categories without loneliness did not. For perceptual abnormalities, loneliness without social isolation did not show differences relative to the reference group, whereas both categories without loneliness (No isolation/ No loneliness, OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.17–0.48, p < 0.001; Isolation/ No loneliness, OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.23–0.53, p < 0.001) showed lower odds. In addition, analyses using the group with neither social isolation nor loneliness as the reference category were conducted to further examine the gradient of risk, and the results are presented in Table S14.

In sensitivity analyses using unweighted models (Table S15), the overall pattern of associations was largely consistent with the IPW results, except that the associations in which loneliness without social isolation showed higher odds than the reference group for perplexity and delusional mood and for overvalued beliefs in the weighted models were no longer observed. Analyses using the group with neither social isolation nor loneliness as the reference category were also conducted in the unweighted models and are presented in Table S16. All models were adjusted for age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, self-rated health, household income, and history of psychiatric illness.

Discussion

This study investigated the associations between combinations of loneliness and social isolation and PLEs in a large, representative Japanese population sample. Our primary finding was the robust and consistent association between loneliness without social isolation and overall PLEs. Individuals reporting subjective loneliness despite objective social integration had significantly higher odds of PLEs than those experiencing both loneliness and social isolation. Notably, this pattern suggests that the coexistence of objective isolation does not necessarily correspond to higher odds beyond subjective loneliness alone. To our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to explicitly examine how the interplay between objective social isolation and subjective loneliness relates to both overall PLEs and specific psychotic symptom domains.

Our findings suggest that subjective loneliness constitutes the fundamental correlate of PLEs across most symptom domains, whereas objective social isolation alone appears insufficient to account for elevated risk. These findings can be further understood within the framework of heterogeneous social disconnection profiles. While individuals with both loneliness and isolation may reflect a state of global social deprivation, and those with isolation without loneliness may indicate adaptation to limited social contact, loneliness without social isolation may represent a qualitatively distinct condition characterized by a discrepancy between objective social embeddedness and subjective relational dissatisfaction. In four domains—first-rank symptoms, suspiciousness/persecutory ideas, perceptual abnormalities, and perplexity and delusional mood— loneliness consistently differentiated individuals from those without loneliness, underscoring the primacy of perceived relational discrepancy over the mere quantity of social contacts. Although social deafferentation has been proposed as a pathway linking social isolation to hallucination-like experiences, our findings did not show a specific elevation of perceptual abnormalities among individuals with social isolation alone. This discrepancy may reflect differences between clinical and general-population samples, or the possibility that subjective distress rather than objective isolation is more relevant in this population-based context. Notably, for overall PLEs and, in weighted models, for grandiose ideas, perplexity and delusional mood and overvalued beliefs, the association was strongest among individuals experiencing loneliness despite social integration, suggesting that the combination of subjective social pain and ongoing social exposure may intensify self-referential processing and maladaptive meaning attribution [34, 35]. The attenuation of this pattern for perplexity and delusional mood and overvalued beliefs in unweighted analyses further suggests that selection processes inherent in web-based surveys may have obscured the risk gradient among socially integrated yet lonely individuals, whereas population weighting revealed clearer associations in these domains. These discrepancies between weighted and unweighted models may reflect correction for selection biases inherent in web-based surveys. For example, individuals with poorer health or limited digital access may be underrepresented in unweighted samples due to healthy user bias or digital divide effects. By aligning the sample with the general population, IPW may have enhanced the visibility of risk patterns among socially integrated yet lonely individuals, thereby clarifying associations that were attenuated in unweighted analyses. In contrast, grandiose ideas exhibited a distinct pattern in which loneliness without isolation showed a uniquely stronger association. A similar tendency was also observed for overvalued beliefs, although the strength of the association was less consistent. This pattern may reflect compensatory self-enhancement processes, whereby individuals with unmet relational needs attempt to regulate a vulnerable self-concept through exaggerated positive self-representations in social contexts [36], although this interpretation requires further empirical verification. Such processes may arise particularly when individuals remain embedded in social contexts yet experience unmet relational needs, creating a discrepancy between external social connectedness and internal relational dissatisfaction. Together, these overlapping patterns indicate that loneliness operates as a broad vulnerability factor for PLEs, while the psychological configuration of being socially connected but subjectively lonely may be associated with higher odds of specific symptom expressions. These domain-specific patterns can also be interpreted within established theoretical frameworks of psychosis. From a cognitive perspective, loneliness may bias information processing toward threat-related interpretations, particularly relevant to paranoia-related experiences [18]. From an affective standpoint, loneliness-related distress may amplify negative emotional states that contribute to aberrant salience attribution [37, 38]. From an interpersonal perspective, discrepancies between perceived and actual social connectedness may intensify self-referential processing, particularly among socially embedded yet lonely individuals [39]. Consistent with this view, loneliness without social isolation may represent a particularly distressing state in relation to PLEs.

Several socio-cognitive mechanisms have been proposed to explain how loneliness may contribute to the emergence of PLEs. Loneliness may facilitate PLEs via heightened social threat perception—particularly paranoia [7] —as well as negative social cognition, including maladaptive self- and other-schemas [40]. In addition, affective processes such as depressive symptoms and social anxiety may also contribute to this association. Loneliness is closely linked to depressed mood and heightened social evaluative concerns[37], which in turn have been implicated in the development of paranoia and other psychotic-like experiences [41]. Reduced corrective social feedback may further bias salience and meaning attribution processes, promoting internally driven interpretations of ambiguous experiences [42]. Although loneliness is consistently linked to adverse mental health outcomes, the role of social isolation appears less straightforward. Perceived discrepancy between desired and actual relationships, rather than social isolation per se, may confer greater psychological vulnerability [34, 35]. When such discrepancy occurs despite objective social integration, it might intensify self-referential processing and social evaluative concerns, thereby amplifying affective and cognitive pathways linked to PLEs. Recent experience sampling studies showed that negative affect during isolation, rather than isolation itself, predicted subsequent PLEs [11, 16]. Our findings further support this view, demonstrating that subjective loneliness—especially in the absence of objective isolation—is more strongly associated with PLEs.

Taken together, our findings have important clinical and public health implications. Specifically, they underscore the necessity of assessing subjective loneliness even among individuals who appear socially connected, as outward indicators of social integration may obscure substantial internal relational distress. This distinction is critical for early identification, as individuals who are embedded in social networks but experience loneliness may otherwise be overlooked in conventional screening approaches that prioritize objective isolation. Importantly, loneliness represents a potentially modifiable target. As highlighted by Lim et al. [43], a range of psychosocial interventions—including interventions aimed at improving relationship quality rather than quantity, addressing maladaptive social cognition, enhancing positive affect through positive psychology–based approaches, and reducing social anxiety—have demonstrated feasibility and acceptability in individuals with psychosis, with concomitant reductions in loneliness and psychotic symptoms. Such approaches emphasize strengthening existing social ties, modifying negative interpersonal schemas, and fostering positive emotional experiences, rather than solely increasing social contact frequency. Applied to the context of PLEs, these interventions may help attenuate socio-cognitive and affective pathways linking loneliness to symptom expression. Therefore, incorporating routine assessment of loneliness—particularly among socially integrated individuals—may facilitate more targeted and effective psychosocial strategies, ultimately contributing to improved management and prevention of PLEs.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse or bidirectional associations between loneliness and PLEs cannot be excluded. Second, the internet-based survey design may have introduced selection bias. Although IPW improved population representativeness, some bias likely persists, and generalizability should be interpreted with caution. In particular, the marked discrepancy in response rates between follow-up participants and newly recruited respondents raises the possibility of self-selection bias. Individuals who chose to participate may differ systematically from non-respondents in ways not fully captured by the weighting variables. Although IPW was applied to mitigate such biases by aligning the sample with a reference population, this approach relies on the assumption that all relevant confounders are adequately measured and included in the model. Residual bias may therefore remain, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Third, all measures were self-reported, raising the possibility of recall bias and shared method variance. Fourth, despite adjustment for multiple covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., personality traits or subthreshold affective symptoms) may remain. Finally, the extent to which these findings can be generalized beyond the Japanese context should be considered with caution. Cultural differences in social behavior, interpersonal expectations, and emotional expression may influence both the experience and reporting of loneliness and psychotic-like experiences. Future studies in diverse cultural settings are needed to determine the cross-cultural robustness of these associations.

Conclusion

In this large population-based study, loneliness without social isolation was most strongly associated with overall PLEs, indicating that subjective loneliness—rather than objective isolation—may constitute a key correlate of psychotic-like experiences. Symptom-specific analyses further revealed heterogeneous patterns across domains, suggesting that the interplay between perceived relational disconnection and social context may differentially shape the expression of specific psychotic-like symptoms. The attenuation of certain associations in unweighted models also suggests that estimates for socially integrated yet lonely individuals may be sensitive to selection processes inherent in internet-based surveys.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of distinguishing subjective loneliness from objective social isolation in both research and practice. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal and potentially bidirectional relationships between loneliness and PLEs, and to determine whether loneliness without social isolation represents a precursor, consequence, or maintaining factor of specific symptom domains. Furthermore, future research should investigate effective intervention strategies targeting subjective loneliness, particularly among individuals who appear socially connected but experience persistent relational dissatisfaction, in order to inform prevention and early intervention efforts for PLEs.
