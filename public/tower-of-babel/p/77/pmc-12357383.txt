Transcriptomic analysis identifies key genes associated with nutrient deprivation responses in infectious bursal disease virus infection
Authors: Jiaxing Wang, Qiangzhou Wang, Yuyu Ping, Xuan Huang, Ting Yang, Zongping Liu, Hao Bai, Guobin Chang, Shihao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) causes a highly contagious and immunosuppressive disease that severely impacts the poultry industry. However, the molecular basis of IBDV-induced immunosuppression remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigate key genes implicated in this process, focusing on the interplay between metabolism and immune regulation. IBDV infection enhanced glucose and glutamine uptake in HD11 cells. Transcriptome analysis across glucose-starved, amino acid-starved, IBDV-infected, and control groups identified key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to immune and metabolic pathways. Specifically, 612 DEGs were associated with glucose starvation, 998 with amino acid starvation, and 496 shared between both conditions. Enrichment analysis revealed involvement in cytokine signalling, amino acid biosynthesis, and antiviral responses. qPCR validated ENO2 and MAT1A expression changes, and a regulatory network analysis highlighted their potential roles in nutrient stress and IBDV pathogenesis. This study presents a novel strategy to identify genes involved in nutrient deprivation during IBDV infection, highlighting ENO2 and MAT1A as key contributors to immune cell dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11918-x.

Introduction

Infectious bursal disease (IBD), caused by the infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), is a highly contagious and immunosuppressive disease that poses a significant threat to the poultry industry [1]. The economic burden of IBD is substantial, driven by increased mortality rates, reduced growth performance, and the costs associated with vaccination strategies [2]. Current understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying IBDV-induced immunosuppression remains limited, and the prevention and control of IBDV remain significant challenges. IBDV is known to target and destroy actively dividing B cells that carry IgM [3]. Additionally, macrophages have been shown to be susceptible to IBDV infection, with infected macrophages producing a variety of cytokines and chemokines that may significantly influence the virus’s protective and/or pathogenic mechanisms [4].

Immune cells, including macrophages, primarily rely on glycolytic metabolism to execute their biological functions, such as the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to combat microorganisms [5]. LPS-activated macrophages, for instance, enhance glycolysis while suppressing oxidative metabolism [6]. The upregulation of glucose utilization and the increase in glycolytic flux are crucial for meeting the energy requirements of proinflammatory macrophages [7, 8]. Given their lack of independent metabolic capacity, viruses are entirely dependent on the host cell’s metabolic machinery. Therefore, the virus requires a substantial supply of intermediate metabolites and energy to hijack cellular metabolism, meeting its needs for proliferation [9–12]. Glucose, glutamine, and fatty acid metabolism, which are central to cellular metabolism, are key targets for viral manipulation [13]. IBDV infection has been reported to stimulate several metabolic pathways in host cells, including glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and lipid metabolism [14]. The nutrient-deprived environment induced by IBDV infection may impair immune cell function, contributing to immunosuppression, but the key metabolic genes involved in this process are not yet identified.

In this study, we first confirmed that IBDV infection significantly enhances glucose and glutamine uptake in HD11 cells. We then established four distinct groups: glucose-starved, amino acid-starved, IBDV-infected, and untreated controls, for transcriptome sequencing. Through comparative analysis, we identified the DEGs associated with nutrient stress in the context of IBDV infection and their signalling pathways. Notably, two key genes, Enolase2 (ENO2) and Methionine Adenosyltransferase 1 A (MAT1A), were significantly altered in response to nutrient deprivation and IBDV infection. These findings provide new insights into the metabolic mechanisms underlying IBDV-induced immunosuppression and offer potential targets for improved prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

The chicken macrophage-like cell line HD11 were graciously provided by Professor Xin’an Jiao at Yangzhou University and was routinely maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; HyClone, Utah, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Ozafan, Nanjing, China) and 1% (v/v) Penicillin‒Streptomycin (PS; Thermo, Waltham, USA). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2.

IBDV (LM strain) was kindly provided by Professor Jue Liu from Yangzhou University. Experiments related to IBDV infection were conducted in the Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory of the Epigenetics Center at Yangzhou University, following the guidelines outlined in the Epigenetics Center’s laboratory pathogen operation manual. This manual includes a comprehensive set of safety measures, such as processing infectious wastewater through chemical disinfection procedures and sterilizing contaminated waste that has come into contact with infectious materials using an autoclave.

Western blot and antibody

Lysate harvesting and western blot analysis was performed as previously described [15]. The proteins were subsequently separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with a quick blocking solution at room temperature for 30 min, followed by incubation with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with the primary antibody, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The VP2 monoclonal antibody, generously provided by Professor Shijun Zheng from China Agricultural University, was diluted to 1:1000. β-actin (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was diluted to 1:5000. The secondary antibodies, goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP and goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (NCM, Suzhou, China), were diluted to 1:10,000.

Glucose and glutamine detection assay

HD11 cells were infected with IBDV for a duration of 12 h, following which the culture supernatants were collected. The glucose and glutamine concentrations in both the cell lysates and the culture medium were determined using enzymatic assay kits procured from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (NJJCBio, Nanjing, China) and Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology (MLBio, Shanghai, China), respectively. The mock-infected cells served as a negative control for these assays.

Sample collection and preparation

HD11 cells were plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells/ml in 12-well plates. For the AAS group, the culture medium was replaced with Krebs-Ringer’s Solution (Procell, Wuhan, China) containing 1% (v/v) PS and incubated for 4 h [16]. For GS group, the medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM (Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 1% FBS and 1% (v/v) PS, and cells were incubated for 4 h [17]. In the IBDV infection group, cells were infected with IBDV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 in DMEM for 2 h, followed by replacement with DMEM containing 1% FBS DMEM, and incubation for 24 h. Mock- infected cells served as the control. Each treatment was carried out in three independent biological replicates.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from HD11 cells using the FreeZol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries were synthesized and sequenced by Guangzhou Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The data analysis workflow included raw data filtering based on an absolute count threshold of 1, followed by differential expression analysis using a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 and a log2-fold change (FC) greater than 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotation and enrichment analyses were performed using the OmicShare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/), with the top 15 terms selected for visualization based on their FDR values.

RT‒qPCR

RT-qPCR was performed using the First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for RT-qPCR and PerfectStart Green SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and gene-specific primers were synthesized by GENEWIZ. β-actin was used as an endogenous control, and the relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method [18]. The gene-specific primers used for mRNA analysis are provided in detail in Table S1.

Prediction and visualization of protein interaction networks

The protein interaction networks for ENO2 and MAT1A were predicted using the STRING database and visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.2). A medium confidence score threshold of 0.400 was applied, allowing for a maximum of 50 interactions. The network was curated to highlight direct and indirect interactions among the proteins of interest.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 5. Two-tailed paired Student’s t-tests were used to determine the significance of differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

IBDV infection enhances nutrient uptake in HD11 cells

To elucidate the impact of IBDV on nutrient uptake in host cells, we infected chicken HD11 macrophages with the IBDV LM strain. IBDV replication was evaluated at 12 and 24 h post-infection using Western blotting and RT-qPCR (Fig. 1a, b). Following this, the levels of glucose and glutamine in both the infected cells and their culture medium were measured at 12 h post-infection. The results revealed a significant increase in glucose and glutamine concentrations in IBDV-infected cells compared to the uninfected controls, indicating that nutrient uptake by host cells is enhanced during IBDV replication (Fig. 1c, d).

Transcriptomic analysis reveals differential gene expression patterns

Our findings demonstrate that IBDV infection triggers excessive uptake of glucose and amino acids, thereby creating a nutrient-deprived microenvironment for other immune cells. To explore which metabolic genes in the cellular expression profile of IBDV-infected cells are involved in nutrient deprivation responses, we performed transcriptome sequencing on the CTRL, AAS, GS, and IBDV groups (Fig. 2a). A total of 17,148 raw data points were obtained from the transcriptome sequencing results. Principal component analysis (PCA) on the four datasets indicated a significantly increased correlation between the GS and AAS groups, while the correlation between the control and IBDV groups was relatively low (Fig. 2b).

Differential expression analysis of the CTRL vs. AAS, CTRL vs. GS, and CTRL vs. IBDV groups revealed 1,315 upregulated and 1,022 downregulated genes in the amino acid starvation group, 792 upregulated and 595 downregulated genes in the glucose starvation group, and 1,964 upregulated and 1,118 downregulated genes in the IBDV group (Fig. 2c). Heatmaps for these DEGs and GO and KEGG pathway analysis were performed (Figs. 2d and 3). GO functional annotation of IBDV-infected HD11 cells revealed enrichment in pathways related to cell motility, localization, and stress responses (Fig. 3a). The enrichment results for glucose and amino acid starvation were significantly enriched in plasma membrane, extracellular, and single-organism cell process categories (Fig. 3b, c). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the IBDV-infected group revealed significant enrichment in antiviral cytokines, cytokine receptors, toll-like receptors, and other immune-related pathways (Fig. 3d). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that glucose starvation primarily affected amino acid biosynthetic metabolism and cytokine signalling pathways, while amino acid starvation mainly affected cytokine signalling pathways associated with viral protein interactions (Fig. 3e, f).

Gene expression profiling of DEGs associated with amino acid starvation and IBDV infection

To explore the specific interactions between IBDV infection and amino acid metabolism, we performed differential gene expression analysis comparing the CTRL group with the AAS and IBDV groups. A Venn diagram revealed 998 overlapping DEGs between the amino acid starvation and IBDV groups (Fig. 4a). GO analyses indicated that the DEGs were enriched in biological processes (BPs) related to cellular and single-organism processes, Molecular functions (MFs) associated with binding and catalytic activities, and cellular components (CCs) mainly in cell and cell part categories (Fig. 4b and Table S2). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted immune-related pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions with viral proteins, Toll-like receptor signalling pathways, and TNF signalling pathways (Fig. 4c and Table S3). Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct clustering among the control, AAS, and IBDV groups (Fig. 4d and Table S4).

Gene expression profiling of DEGs associated with glucose starvation and IBDV infection

Next, to further elucidate the specific interactions between IBDV infection and glucose metabolism, we conducted differential gene expression analysis comparing the CTRL group with the GS group and the IBDV group. A Venn diagram revealed 612 overlapping DEGs between the GS and IBDV groups (Fig. 5a). Subsequent GO enrichment analysis indicated that the BPs associated with the enriched DEGs were predominantly cellular processes and single-organism processes. MFs were enriched primarily in binding and catalytic activities, while CCs were enriched in cell and cell part categories (Fig. 5b and Table S5). The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that these DEGs predominantly influence the biosynthesis of amino acids and cytokine-cytokine receptor response signalling pathways (Fig. 5c and Table S6). Heatmap analysis using differentially expressed metabolic genes revealed distinct clustering among the control, GS, and IBDV groups (Fig. 5d and Table S7).

Gene expression profiling of DEGs between IBDV infection, glucose starvation, and amino acid starvation

To further clarify which nutrient deprivation response-related genes are involved in the IBDV infection process, we performed an overlap screening of DEGs from the AAS, GS, and IBDV groups (Fig. 6a and Table S8). This screening identified 496 shared genes. We then created a heatmap to visualize the expression patterns and differences of these DEGs across different treatment groups (Fig. 6b and Table S8). Subsequently, we conducted GO functional annotation and KEGG enrichment analysis for these DEGs. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the enriched mRNAs were associated with BPs such as cellular processes, single-organism processes, and biological regulation. In terms of MFs, the main enrichments were in binding and catalytic activities, while for CCs, the enrichments were predominantly in cell parts and cells (Fig. 6c and Table S9). The KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were primarily enriched in cytokine and cytokine receptor signalling pathways, as well as amino acid biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 6d and Table S10).

Screening and validation of amino acid biosynthesis genes expression

To understand the role of nutrient deprivation during IBDV infection, we analyzed metabolic gene expression patterns based on our findings. KEGG pathway analysis identified the amino acid biosynthesis pathway as the second most enriched, containing a total of 8 DEGs (Table S10): ENO2, MAT1A, Glutamate Pyruvate Transaminase 2 (GPT2), Pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 1 (PYCR1), fructose 1,6-bisphosphate aldolase B (ALDOB), Cystathionine beta-synthase (CBSL), Glutamate-ammonia ligase (GLUL), and 3-phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH). The volcano plots indicate that these candidate genes exhibit significantly different expression patterns across AAS, GS, and IBDV groups (Fig. 7a-c). Through RT-qPCR validation, we discovered that only ENO2 and MAT1A displayed both significantly elevated expression in IBDV-infected cells (Fig. 7d) and marked differential expression between control groups (Fig. 7e). The concordance between qPCR and RNA-seq data confirmed ENO2 and MAT1A as the most responsive metabolic regulators to IBDV infection, highlighting their potential roles in virus-induced nutrient stress responses.

Establishment of the protein‒protein interaction network

Subsequently, we constructed individual protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for ENO2 and MAT1A (Fig. 8a, b). The results indicated that ENO2 exhibits strong interactions with other members of the ENO family, including ENO1 and ENO4, as well as direct interactions with ALDOB, ALDOC, TPI1, PGAM1, BPGM, and PKLR. These genes are primarily involved in key metabolic processes such as gluconeogenesis, glycolysis, and amino acid biosynthesis [19–24]. In the MAT1A interaction network, a strong association was observed with its homolog MAT2B. Notably, MAT1A also directly interacted with members of the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) family— DNMT3B, DNMT1, and DNMT3A. These genes are implicated not only in metabolic regulation but also in immune cell function, particularly through epigenetic mechanisms [25, 26]. The interaction between MAT1A and DNMT family members suggests a potential metabolic–epigenetic axis that may influence immune responses. Further functional investigation of ENO2 and MAT1A may help clarify the mechanisms by which IBDV infection disrupts immune cell homeostasis through the interplay between metabolism and immunity.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that the early dysregulation of innate immune responses and the exacerbation of B cell apoptosis caused by IBDV infection are the main factors leading to the virus-induced immune pathogenesis [27, 28]. Metabolism is recognized as essential for the functionality of immune cells. Aberrant alterations in metabolism can impair immune cell functions, which is considered a molecular mechanism by which tumor cells induce immune suppression [29]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which IBDV induces immune suppression through the reshaping of immune cell metabolism remain to be elucidated. Studies have shown that IBDV infection increases glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotide synthesis, lipid metabolism, and glutathione metabolism in DF-1 cells [14]. Moreover, researchers have employed multi-omics technologies to demonstrate that Nephropathogenic Infectious Bronchitis virus (NIBV) can promote the activation of both pattern recognition receptors and amino acid metabolic pathways [30]. Consistent with this, our results also indicate that IBDV infection in chicken HD11 cells leads to increased glutamine and glucose metabolism. Therefore, we observed that IBDV infection drives a metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, leading to a heightened metabolic state. This increased demand may outpace nutrient availability, effectively resulting in a state of relative energy deficiency. Such a nutrient-depleted microenvironment mirrors the immunosuppressive conditions observed in tumor biology, where excessive metabolic activity contributes to immune dysfunction [31].

To elucidate the key genes associated with nutrient deprivation responses in chicken macrophages during IBDV infection, we subjected HD11 cells to glucose and amino acid deprivation, followed by transcriptome sequencing. The results showed that DEGs related to amino acid deprivation in response to IBDV infection were mainly enriched in immune-related pathways, including the interaction between viral proteins and cytokines and their receptors, Toll-like receptors, TNF, and IL-17 signalling pathways. Numerous studies have reported a direct correlation between amino acid metabolism and immune responses [32]. For instance, L-arginine metabolism plays a crucial regulatory role in macrophage activation mediated by interferon IFN-γ as well as in activation mediated by interleukin IL-4 [33]. In our study, DEGs associated with glucose deprivation during IBDV infection were enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, with the second most enriched pathway being amino acid biosynthesis. Moreover, these top two pathways were also significantly enriched among the DEGs associated with both amino acid and glucose deprivation response during IBDV infection. These findings suggest a complex interplay between amino acid and glucose metabolism and the immune functions of chicken macrophages. Additionally, the metabolic responses to amino acid and glucose deprivation triggered by IBDV infection may lead to aberrant alterations in immune-related signalling pathways [14]. Our results indicate that the immune response induced by IBDV may be closely related to amino acid and glucose metabolism, which is consistent with previous findings on IBDV and other avian viruses [14, 30]. Finally, we identified ENO2 and MAT1A as potential key metabolic genes related to nutrient deprivation responses in IBDV-infected chicken macrophages.

ENO2 is an important metabolic enzyme involved in glycolysis, and studies have shown that viral infections can regulate ENO2 expression to exacerbate the infection process. For example, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) infection significantly induces ENO2 expression, which is particularly important for the remodelling of pyrimidine sugar metabolism, providing the necessary sugar subunits for the accumulation of HCMV viral glycoproteins [34]. Other studies have shown that ENO2 expression is positively correlated with immune suppression markers, promoting the proliferation, invasion, and migration of ccRCC cells [35]. Additionally, ENO2 has been shown to be a biomarker that regulates energy metabolism, affecting cellular glycolysis levels through the “Warburg effect” [36]. Notably, its glycolytic product phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) has been shown to inhibit histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), a mechanism critical for macrophage immune regulation [37]. MAT1A, on the other hand, has been implicated in hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection [38]. Its overexpression increases the production of S-adenosylmethionine, which directly or indirectly promotes ferroptosis in HCC cells by enhancing the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells and the production of IFN-γ [39]. Our findings that IBDV infection upregulates ENO2 and MAT1A are consistent with these observations; however, the impact of their expression on the metabolic pathways and immune function of chicken macrophages requires further investigation.

However, there are three limitations to this study. First, we used an extreme approach by directly depriving HD11 cells of glucose and amino acids. In contrast, IBDV infection typically leads to immune cells being in a state of low nutrient levels rather than complete depletion. Second, we did not establish additional time points for IBDV infection because the expression of many DEGs varies with infection time. Third, we focused on metabolic pathways that were significantly enriched, without considering other potentially relevant pathways. Although ENO2 and MAT1A are promising candidates based on known functions in other species, their precise roles in chicken macrophages during IBDV infection remain to be elucidated.

Conclusions

In summary, our study employs transcriptome sequencing and established screening methods to identify key metabolic genes involved in nutrient deprivation responses in chicken macrophages infected with IBDV. ENO2 and MAT1A, two metabolism-related genes, may be central to the immune damage in macrophages caused by IBDV infection. Further research is needed to understand their impact on chicken HD11 cells and IBDV replication.
