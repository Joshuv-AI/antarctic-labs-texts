PFV Tas protein recruits hnRNPF to promote the splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA
Authors: Shishun Wu, Chenxi Liu, Manman Qiu, Juan Tan, Wentao Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFoamy viruses (FVs) are capable of cross-species transmission to humans and establish latent infections without causing diseases. The transition between latent and lytic FV replication is regulated by the viral proteins Bet and Tas, which are expressed predominantly from the internal promoter (IP). Bet suppresses basal IP activity and initiates latency, whereas Tas activates the IP and the long terminal repeat (LTR) promoter and enables lytic infection. However, the mechanisms regulating the relative levels of Tas and Bet are not completely understood.ResultsWe performed a yeast two-hybrid screen to identify Tas-interacting proteins and discovered an association of the splicing factor heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein F (hnRNPF) with Tas. We found that hnRNPF inhibits prototype foamy virus (PFV) replication by shifting the viral expression profile from Tas to Bet. Importantly, hnRNPF specifically recognizes a G-cluster II cis-acting element (GC-II) within the tas/bet pre-mRNA to promote its splicing into bet mRNA, thereby reducing the levels of the unspliced tas transcript. The viral Tas protein, which interacts with hnRNPF, also possesses this splicing-promoting activity. Tas acts by binding to its DNA target, the Tas responsive element (TRE), thereby recruiting the hnRNPF to nascent viral RNA to enhance splicing efficiency.ConclusionsWe conclude that PFV Tas regulates the expression of itself and the viral Bet protein through splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA by recruiting the cellular splicing factor hnRNPF. We further propose that hnRNPF may play an important role in establishing PFV latency by assisting Tas in decreasing Tas levels and increasing Bet expression to shut down viral gene expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01567-2.

Background

Foamy viruses (FVs) belong to the Retroviridae family and the Spumaretrovirinae subfamily [1]. They are considered the oldest known exogenous retroviruses, endemic in non-human primates, with infectious isolates also identified in many other species [2, 3]. Despite exhibiting broad tropism and causing a severe cytopathic effect in vitro, FVs appear to be apathogenic in vivo and establish latent infection [4]. Unlike typical retroviruses, FVs exhibit a distinct replication strategy. Reverse transcription occurs at a late stage of the viral life cycle, resulting in viral particles that contain both RNA and DNA genomes [5]. Additionally, the Pol protein is translated from a spliced mRNA, rather than from a Gag-Pol fusion protein as seen in other retroviruses [6, 7]. FVs possess two distinct promoters. The first is the 5′ long terminal repeat (LTR) promoter located within the U3 region, which drives the expression of the structural proteins Gag, Pol and Env. The second is the internal promoter (IP), located at the 3′ end of the env gene, which exhibits basal activity and allows the expression of the accessory proteins Tas and Bet [8, 9]. Prototype foamy virus (PFV), the most extensively investigated among FVs, has a genome of approximately 12 to 13 kb, making it the largest retroviral genome identified to date [10]. Due to their non-pathogenic nature and large genome as well as their unique replication strategy, FVs have been engineered as unique vectors for stable gene transfer into various cells and tissues [8, 10].

FV gene expression is regulated by viral Tas and Bet proteins. Initially, the basal activity of the IP enables the low-level expression of Tas protein, which binds with high affinity to the Tas responsive element (TRE) within the IP, forming a positive feedback loop that increases Tas level. Once Tas accumulates above a threshold level, it starts binding to TREs in the long terminal repeat (LTR) promoter to activate structural gene expression. Thus, FV replication is regulated by a Tas-dependent positive feedback mechanism involving both IP and LTR promoters [11, 12]. In cells latently infected with FVs, this feedback mechanism is disrupted, resulting in decreased Tas production and increased expression of Bet, a proposed negative regulator [13–17]. Although the mechanism underlying this shift remains unclear, RNA splicing may play a critical role, because Tas and Bet are produced from the same pre-mRNA through alternative splicing during which a 301-bp intronic sequence is excised from the Tas ORF to generate the bet mRNA [17].

Indeed, our yeast two-hybrid screen revealed the splicing factor hnRNPF as a candidate of Tas-interacting proteins. Human hnRNPF is membre of the hnRNPF/H RNA-binding protein family with predominant localization in the nucleus. It regulates RNA processing by specifically recognizing GGG-rich RNA motifs, and is involved in alternative splicing, polyadenylation, and other RNA-related processes [18–21]. Structurally, hnRNPF contains three quasi-RNA recognition motif (qRRM) domains, with two located at the N terminus and one at the C terminus, as well as two glycine-rich domains (GYR and GY). hnRNPF has been shown to regulate the splicing of Bcl-x, androgen receptor (AR), and TCF3. Moreover, hnRNPF also plays a role in viral replication, including that of porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) [22–28].

Here, we show that the splicing factor hnRNPF interacts with Tas, and enhances tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing via its qRRM2 and qRRM3 domains. Moreover, we uncovered a novel role of Tas as a transactivator in PFV pre-mRNA splicing. Tas binds to the TRE through its DNA binding domain (DNA-BD) and recruits hnRNPF via its activation domain (AD) to the GC-II element on the nascent RNA, enhancing tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing, which increases Bet protein levels and drives PFV toward latent infection.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and viral infections

HEK293T, HT1080, HeLa and PFVL cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For virus production, the PFV infectious clone pcPFV was transfected into HEK293T cells, and the supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm pore-size filter to remove cell debris. The viral titer was determined by infecting PFVL cells, as previously described [29–31], and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) was calculated using the method described by Tai et al. [29]. For viral infection, cells were incubated with PFV stock for 8 h before virus inoculum was removed and replaced with fresh medium. Following an additional 40 h, the culture supernatants or infected cells were harvested and incubated with PFVL cells to determine the viral titres via luciferase activity.

Plasmids and transfection

The following plasmid DNA constructs pcDNA3.1( +)-Tas, pFlag-Env, pFlag-Gag, pFlag-Tas, pFlag-Bet and pCMV-ORF1 + 2 have been described previously [30]. The human hnRNPF cDNA was cloned into the pQCXIP 2.0 vector (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA) with an HA tag, and the ΔqRRM1, ΔqRRM2, ΔqRRM3 and ΔqRRM2 + 3 mutants were generated using this vector. Tas SDmut, Tas 93CKR–LDL95, Tas Δ253–267 and Tas Δ268–282 were generated based on the pcDNA3.1( +)-Tas vector, Flag-tagged versions of these mutants were constructed using the pCMV-Flag-Tas vector. The pCMV-ORF1 + 2 SDmut construct was generated using the pFLAG-ORF1 + 2 vector.

PCR amplification of the tas and orf-2 regions from pcPFV was performed using the primers pGL3-IP + ORF-F Mlu I (5′-TCTACGCGTATCTGTTATGGAAGGAATG-3′) and pGL3-IP + ORF-R Sal I (5′-CTCGTCGACTGTCATGGAATTTTG-3′). The resulting fragment was digested with Mlu I and Sal I, and ligated into the corresponding sites of the pGL3-Basic vector, generating the pIP-ORF1 + 2 plasmid. Based on this, the pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmut and pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmutΔTRE constructs were generated. The pCMV-Bcl-x plasmid was generated based on the report by Wilusz et al. [32], pIP-Bcl-x, pIP-Bcl-xΔTRE and pIP-Bcl-xB2Gmut were derived from this plasmid. The β-globin and EVX1 gene promoters were cloned as reported by Joseph A and Jablonski et al., and the corresponding plasmids were generated using the pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmut backbone [33]. Most of the plasmids mentioned above were constructed using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (C112-01, Vazyme Biotech), following the manufacturer’s instructions (with selected primer sequences listed in Table S1). Plasmid DNA constructs pLTR-Luc, pIP-Luc and pcPFV (PFV full-length infectious clone) were kindly provided by Maxine L. Linial (Division of Basic Sciences, Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, Seattle, WA, USA) [34, 35]. All mutations in this study were verified by DNA sequencing (Tsingke Biotech, Beijing, China).

hnRNPF knockdown

Knockdown of hnRNPF in HT1080 and HEK293T cells was achieved using short hairpin RNA (shRNA). Double-stranded oligonucleotides corresponding to target sequences were inserted into the pSIREN-RetroQ vector (primer sequences are listed in Table S1). Retroviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with pMLV-Gag-Pol, pVSV-G and pSIREN-RetroQ-shhnRNPF. The supernatant was collected and used as the shRNA pseudovirus stock, which was then directly applied to HT1080 or HEK293T cells for hnRNPF knockdown. 48 h post-infection, stably transduced cells were selected in media containing 2 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich). Knockdown efficiency was assessed by RT–qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assay

Cells were harvested 48 h after infection or transfection, and luciferase activity was measured using the Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Alu-PCR

The viral integration level was assessed using Alu-PCR, as previously described [31, 36]. Briefly, HT1080 cells were transiently transfected with hnRNPF expression plasmids or control vectors. 16 h post-transfection, cells were pretreated with AZT (10 μM) and Raltegravir (10 μM) for 2 h and then infected with PFV stocks. Cells were harvested 48 h after infection and total DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Integrated viral DNA was quantified by semi-quantitative and real-time Alu-PCR. The specific Alu-PCR primers used in this study are listed in Supplemental figures and tables.

Reverse transcription PCR and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection and lysed in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with gDNA wiper (R312-01, Vazyme Biotech), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For normalization, gapdh mRNA expression was used as an internal control. The amount of cDNA used in each PCR reaction was adjusted based on total viral RNA content, as determined by RT–qPCR. PCR amplification was carried out for 24 cycles per sample. To ensure result consistency, all assays were performed with at least three independent transfections. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Mix (Vazyme Biotech) on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), with each sample measured in triplicate. Fold change in RNA expression was calculated using the ΔΔCT method.

Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

For co-IP, HEK293T cells were transfected with the appropriate plasmids. After 48 h, cells were harvested and lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer (50 mM Tris–HCL, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 3% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, and a protease inhibitor cocktail without EDTA). Lysates were briefly sonicated and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with specific antibodies at 4 °C for 3 h, followed by rotation with Protein A–agarose beads for an additional 3 h. Immunoprecipitates were washed 7 times with lysis buffer, boiled in 2 × SDS loading buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting. For RNA-IP (RIP), the procedure was performed to detect protein-RNA complexes. Cells were lysed in a modified lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 3% glycerol, and 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with RNase inhibitor. Cell lysates were equally divided and incubated with either anti-HA antibody, anti-hnRNPF antibody, or normal IgG for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Protein A–agarose beads were added and incubated for an additional 1 h. Beads were then washed seven times with the KCl-containing lysis buffer. RNA–protein complexes were digested with Proteinase K (TransGen), and RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed as described above. The resulting cDNA was amplified using PFV-specific primers P1 and P2, and PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. For Western blotting, cells were transfected or infected and lysed in RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble materials. The supernatants were mixed with 6 × SDS loading buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) and boiled at 100 °C for 15 min. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (GE Healthcare). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk (Beyotime Biotechnology) at room temperature for 45 min, incubated with primary antibodies (anti-Flag, anti-HA, anti-GAPDH and anti-hnRNPF; Santa Cruz Biotechnology and Proteintech) for 1.5 h, and then with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 45 min. Signals were detected using HRP substrate (Cell Signaling Technology).

Statistical analysis and data presentation

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Band intensities in Western blots and RT-PCR gels were quantified using Image Lab 6.0.1 software. Values are presented as means ± SEM from at least three independent experiments (n = 3). Specific P values are indicated in the figures; where applicable, “ns” represents P > 0.05. For comparisons between groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA, or Student’s t-test was performed as appropriate.

Results

Tas interacts with hnRNPF

To understand the function of PFV Tas protein, we carried out a yeast two-hybrid screen using a human B cell cDNA library and the Tas domain (amino acids 1–275) as the bait to discover novel Tas-binding cellular proteins. Several candidates were identified (data not shown), including splicing factor hnRNPF. To validate the interaction between Tas and hnRNPF in mammalian cells, we cloned the full-length coding sequence of hnRNPF with an N-terminal HA tag into an expression vector, and co-transfected the DNA construct with either Flag-tagged full-length Tas or an empty vector into HEK293T cells. Results of co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments showed HA-tagged hnRNPF was co-precipitated with Flag-tagged Tas but not with the Flag tag alone or the IgG control (Fig. 1A). GAPDH was used as a loading control for the inputs. Reverse co-IP further confirmed the interaction between Tas and hnRNPF, and this interaction is RNA-independent, because RNase A treatment did not abrogate this interaction (Fig. 1B). The effectiveness of RNase A was confirmed by the degradation of total RNA (Fig. 1B, bottom panel). We further confirmed that in PFV-infected HT1080 cells, endogenous hnRNPF was co-immunoprecipitated with viral Tas following RNase A treatment, while no signal was detected in the IgG control group (Fig. 1C). Additionally, results of immunofluorescence confocal imaging showed that hnRNPF co-localized with Tas in the nucleus, and this co-localization was not affected by RNase A treatment (Fig. 1D and E). hnRNPF negatively regulates PFV replication

To investigate the role of hnRNPF in PFV replication, we transfected HT1080 cells with an HA-hnRNPF expression plasmid followed by PFV infection. Viral titers and replication levels were determined by infecting the PFV indicator cell line PFVL [29]. The results showed that hnRNPF overexpression significantly reduced the levels of both cell-free and cell-associated PFV (Fig. 2A and B). To examine the role of endogenous hnRNPF, we generated stable HT1080 cell lines with hnRNPF knockdown. shhnRNPF-3 produced the most efficient knockdown, was thus selected for downstream studies (Fig. S1A). The results showed that hnRNPF knockdown increased PFV replication and this increase was lost when hnRNPF was re-introduced (Fig. 2C and D). To further confirm these findings, cell-free viruses were passaged in the control and hnRNPF-knockdown cells. Every three days, levels of PFV in cells and supernatants were determined by infecting the PFVL indicator cells. A significant increase in PFV replication was observed in the hnRNPF-knockdown cells (Fig. 2E). Notably, PFV-induced cytopathic effects (CPE) were more pronounced in hnRNPF-knockdown cells, with syncytia formation observed as early as 24 h post-infection (Fig. 2F). hnRNPF suppresses the late stages of PFV replication

To investigate which stage of the PFV replication cycle is affected by hnRNPF, we first assessed viral DNA integration using the Alu-PCR method by performing PCR (Fig. 3A) and quantitative PCR (qPCR) (Fig. 3B). No effect was observed on PFV DNA integration with hnRNPF overexpression. Next, we examined whether hnRNPF affects PFV transcription, which is driven by the viral transactivator Tas. Using LTR-Luc and IP-Luc reporters in HEK293T cells, we found that hnRNPF significantly reduced Tas expression, thus lowering luciferase activity from both LTR and IP promoters (Fig. 3C). Similar inhibitory effects were also observed in HT1080 cells (Fig. S2A). Further studies showed that when co-expressing with individual PFV proteins, hnRNPF specifically decreased Tas levels but did not affect the expression of Env, Gag, or Bet (Fig. S2B to E). We conjectured that as an RNA splicing factor, hnRNPF may suppress Tas expression by modulating the splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA, since it is known that tas mRNA has an intron and excision of this intron gives rise to a truncated bet mRNA. To test this possibility, we constructed a synonymous Tas mutant (Tas SDmut) by altering the splicing donor site (SD10343) without changing the amino acid sequence (Fig. 3D). Transactivation assays showed that hnRNPF no longer affected Tas SDmut expression or its transactivation activity (Fig. 3E). hnRNPF enhances tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing to increase Bet expression

PFV bet and tas share the same viral RNA, where bet mRNA is generated by splicing between the 5′ splice donor (SD10343) and the 3′ splice acceptor (SA10643) sites, whereas Tas is expressed from the unspliced RNA (Fig. 4A). To investigate the regulation of tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing, we constructed two PFV subgenomic reporter plasmids. The wild-type construct pCMV-ORF1 + 2 is capable of expressing both Bet and Tas, and the mutant construct pCMV-ORF1 + 2 SDmut has a synonymous mutation at the splice donor site and expresses Tas only (Fig. 4A). Using these DNA constructs, we examined the impact of hnRNPF on Bet and Tas protein expression. hnRNPF increased Bet and decreased Tas levels from the wild-type construct, but had no effect on Tas expression from the mutant construct (Fig. 4B). We further measured RNA levels by performing Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-PCR) using primers P3 and P4 (Fig. 4A). In the wild-type construct, hnRNPF increased bet mRNA and decreased unspliced tas mRNA, while it had no effect on the total tas mRNA levels expressed from the mutant construct, and bet mRNA was undetectable (Fig. 4C and D). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) further confirmed an approximately 2.5-fold increase in bet mRNA from the wild-type DNA construct upon hnRNPF overexpression (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, hnRNPF increased tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing in a dose-dependent manner, leading to a significantly elevated bet/tas mRNA ratio (Fig. S3A to C). Importantly, the total amount of tas/bet transcripts remained constant across the hnRNPF gradient (Fig. S3D), and hnRNPF failed to increase the levels of bet mRNA or protein when expressed from a Bet expression plasmid (lacking the intron) (Fig. S2D and S3E). hnRNPF directly binds to the GC-II motif to drive tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing

hnRNPF directly binds to the GC-II motif to drive tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing. To identify the specific RNA sequences recognized by hnRNPF, we utilized the MEME Suite (version 5.5.9) for bioinformatic motif discovery. As shown in Fig. S3F, the analysis highlighted two potential G-rich clusters within the tas/bet pre-mRNA: GC-I (located in Exon 1) and GC-II (located in Exon 2). To test their functions, we introduced mutations to disrupt the consecutive G sequences within these clusters in the pCMV-ORF1 + 2 ATGmut reporter (Fig. 5A). RT-PCR analysis showed that mutation of GC-I had little effect, whereas mutation of GC-II significantly reduced the splicing mediated by hnRNPF (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, even with the GC-II mutation, hnRNPF still showed some level of promotion compared to the control, suggesting that additional potential binding sites might also contribute to this regulation (Fig. 5C). To investigate whether the observed regulation is due to the direct recruitment of hnRNPF to the tas/bet pre-mRNA, we performed an RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay. The results demonstrated a significant enrichment of the wild-type tas/bet pre-mRNA in the HA-hnRNPF fraction compared to the IgG control. Critically, the enrichment was substantially decreased when the GC-II motif was mutated, confirming that GC-II is one of the primary binding regions for hnRNPF on the tas/bet pre-mRNA (Fig. 5D). To further evaluate the functional role and sufficiency of this element in a heterologous context, we utilized the Bcl-x minigene reporter system (Fig. 5E). The Bcl-x gene can be selectively spliced into two distinct transcripts: Bcl-xL (the anti-apoptotic variant) and Bcl-xS (the pro-apoptotic variant). In this system, hnRNPF is known to promote the production of Bcl-xS by binding to the endogenous B2G element [18, 22]. We constructed a chimeric reporter, pCMV-Bcl-x-GC-II, by replacing the B2G element with the PFV-derived GC-II sequence (Fig. 5E). RT-PCR analysis showed that overexpression of hnRNPF effectively shifted the splicing pattern toward Bcl-xS in both the wild-type and the GC-II-substituted reporters (Fig. 5F and G). These findings not only demonstrate that GC-II is a functional cis-acting element but also suggest that it can fully substitute for known mammalian hnRNPF-binding motifs. Tas enhances tas/bet pre-mRNA splicing

Given the interaction between Tas and hnRNPF, we speculated that Tas may help to recruit hnRNPF to promote the splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA. To test this, we constructed the IP-driven reporter plasmid pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmut, in which the shared start codon of Tas and Bet was mutated to eliminate the production of endogenous proteins (Fig. 7A and S4A). Western blot and luciferase assays confirmed that the pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmut reporter does not express Tas or Bet proteins, nor does it activate IP transcription (Fig. S4B and C); however, it retains its responsiveness to Tas-mediated transactivation. When exogenous Tas was provided in trans, it substantially increased the overall levels of both tas and bet mRNA (Fig. S5A). While this increase is partly due to Tas-mediated transactivation of the internal promoter, the exceptionally high abundance of these transcripts led to signal saturation and lane overloading, which narrowed the detectable dynamic range and made the ratio difference appear less pronounced by eye. Despite this, a reproducible splicing shift was observed across serial cDNA dilutions (Fig. S5A). Furthermore, we confirmed that hnRNPF failed to increase the levels of bet mRNA or Bet protein when expressed from a bet cDNA expression plasmid lacking the intron (Fig. S2D and S3E). These results demonstrate that Tas-mediated transactivation significantly enhances bet mRNA production by promoting its splicing efficiency rather than by increasing mRNA stability. Since Tas specifically binds and activates the IP, we next asked whether the stimulation of RNA splicing by Tas is IP-specific [37]. We replaced the IP with the β-globin or EVX1 promoter. Intra-group comparisons showed that Tas failed to activate these heterologous promoters (Fig. S5B), and their transcripts did not undergo further splicing in the presence of Tas (Fig. S5C). To determine whether hnRNPF is required for this process, we examined the effect of Tas on RNA splicing in hnRNPF-knockdown HEK293T cells. Notably, while hnRNPF knockdown had little effect on Tas-mediated transcriptional activation (Fig. S5D), it significantly attenuated Tas-stimulated RNA splicing (Fig. 7B and C). We utilized the TRE-deleted reporter pIP-ORF1 + 2 ATGmutΔTRE (Fig. 7A) and found that deletion of the TRE abrogated both Tas-mediated activation (Fig. S5E) and RNA splicing (Fig. 7D and E). By using a Tas-specific antibody to immunoprecipitate Tas-associated complexes from cells transfected with the reporter plasmid, we detected a significant enrichment of the tas/bet pre-mRNA compared to the IgG control (Fig. 8A). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of HA-hnRNPF within these Tas-precipitated complexes (Fig. S6A). Tas-mediated splicing requires both its DNA-binding capacity and direct interaction with hnRNPF

To further examine the role of Tas binding to the IP DNA in promoting RNA splicing, we introduced mutations (93CKR-LDL95) into the DNA-binding domain (DBD) of Tas to abolish its DNA-binding ability [38]. As expected, this DBD mutant lost transactivation activity (Fig. S6C), resulting in significantly reduced transcriptional levels (Fig. S6D). Splicing analysis showed that while wild-type Tas (Tas SDmut) synergized with exogenous hnRNPF to enhance splicing efficiency, the DBD mutant failed to exhibit this cooperative effect (Fig. 9A and B). To determine whether this loss of activity resulted from impaired protein–protein interaction, we performed co-IP assays. Notably, the DBD mutant retained its ability to bind hnRNPF (Fig. 9C), indicating that the DNA-binding capacity of Tas is essential for its splicing-regulatory function even when its interaction with hnRNPF remains intact. Tas activates transcription through its C-terminal activation domain (AD) by recruiting host factors [37]. We hypothesized that this domain might also mediate the interaction with hnRNPF. Discussion

PFV encodes transcription activator Tas and suppressor Bet. Relative levels of Tas and Bet determine the degree of PFV replication and the establishment of latency. Yet, the molecular mechanisms that regulate these levels have not been well characterized. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that PFV Tas regulates the splicing of its own tas/bet pre-mRNA by recruiting the host splicing factor hnRNPF (Fig. 10). The outcome is production of the spliced bet mRNA concurrent with the reduction tas mRNA, the increase of Bet protein and decrease of Tas, together leading to suppression of viral gene expression and the establishment of latency.Fig. 10Schematic model of Tas-mediated splicing regulation. The viral Tas protein binds to the TRE within the internal promoter (IP) via its DNA-binding domain, while concurrently recruiting host hnRNPF through its activation domain (residues 268–282). This recruitment facilitates the loading of hnRNPF onto the GC-II cis-acting element within the nascent tas/bet pre-mRNA, thereby promoting efficient splicing. The resulting shift in the expression profile increases Bet production and reduces Tas levels, which collectively dampens the transcriptional feedback loop to ensure the establishment of PFV latency

Several host factors have been implicated in PFV latency mainly through restricting viral replication. For example, PHF11 has been reported to suppress the basal activity of the IP promoter and thus inhibit Tas expression, while several factors curtail Tas-dependent transcriptional activation, including nuclear factor I (NFI), N-Myc interaction factor (Nmi), p53-induced RING-H2 (PIRH2), promyelocytic leukemia protein (PML), Trim28, TBC1D16, and SGK1 [30, 31, 39–44]. In 2002, Linial et al. identified Tas as the key regulator enabling PFV transition from latency to lytic infection, while the Bet protein negatively regulates PFV replication by inhibiting the basal activity of IP [17]. Since Tas and Bet are expressed from the same IP promoter, it is expected that IP inhibition or diminished Tas transactivation would reduce the levels of both Tas and Bet proteins, as opposed to Tas downregulation and Bet upregulation which occurs during PFV latency. Our study sheds light on the underlying mechanism by showing that Tas recruits the splicing factor hnRNPF to enhance the splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA so as to produce bet mRNA, thereby increasing Bet protein expression while reducing Tas protein levels. This Tas-mediated alternative splicing mechanism should allow PFV to establish latent infection in cells with sufficient levels of hnRNPF to support splicing of tas/bet pre-mRNA.

Modulating the splicing of viral and cellular genes has been one important mechanism at the interface of virus and host interactions. For example, human papillomavirus 16 (HPV16) regulates the splicing of its E2 mRNA using the uridine-less splicing enhancer sequence to recruit cellular hnRNPG [45]. IAV infection stimulates the expression of SRSF5 which in turn binds directly to M mRNA at the conserved sites and promote M mRNA splicing to express protein M2 [46]. Viral proteins have been reported to interact with and recruit cellular splicing factors to modulate the splicing of viral RNA. For example, IAV NS1 inhibits its own NS1 mRNA splicing by interacting with SRSF1 [47]. NS1 also associates with the cellular protein NS1-BP, forms a complex with hnRNPK to promote the splicing of M1 into M2 mRNA [48, 49]. The HPV-5 E2 protein recruits SRSF1 and SC35 proteins to facilitate splicing of viral RNA [50]. HIV-1 Tat protein, when forming the transcriptional complex, recruits the co-transcriptional splicing activators CA150 and Tat-SF which promote splicing of env mRNA by engaging SRSF1 and SRSF2 [33, 51]. Most recently, HTLV-1 Tax was reported to imposes a regulatory constraint on the HBZ gene by inhibiting HBZ splicing through competition with host factors WDR83 and GPATCH1, which is vital for HTLV-1 persistence [52]. In this study, we demonstrate that PFV controls the levels of viral proteins Tas and Bet by regulating the splicing of its own RNA through recruiting the host splicing factor hnRNPF. This mechanism is particularly important for PFV because of its critical role in the establishment of viral latency.

It has been documented that cellular transcription factors contribute to the splicing of the newly synthesized RNA through interacting with and recruiting splicing factors while these factors bind to the promoter DNA and assist the assembly of the transcription complex [53]. For example, transcription factor SMAD3 regulates CD44 mRNA splicing, HIF-1α and HIF-2α enhance ADM expression by promoting its pre-mRNA splicing [54–56]. Some viral transcription factors exhibit similar functions. One example is HPV-5 E2, which recruits splicing factors SRSF1 and SRSF2 to promote the splicing of RNA transactivated by E2 [50]. In this study, we report that the PFV transactivator Tas stimulates the splicing of its own tas/bet pre-mRNA by recruiting the host splicing factor hnRNPF. While we cannot exclude the possibility of Tas binding directly to pre-mRNA, its mechanism of action differs fundamentally from that of the HIV-1 Tat protein, a strictly RNA-binding transcription activator. Specifically, our findings suggest that the TRE within the internal promoter (IP) serves as a critical DNA-based assembly platform for the co-transcriptional splicing complex. By tethering to the TRE, Tas recruits its interacting partner, hnRNPF, and facilitates its loading onto the nascent tas/bet pre-mRNA as it emerges from the transcription machinery (Fig. 10). Consequently, Tas regulates splicing through a DNA-dependent mechanism more akin to that of host transcription factors than to RNA-binding viral proteins.

In this study, we identified a specific cis-acting element (GC-II) within the tas/bet pre-mRNA that serves as a critical cis-acting motif for hnRNPF (Fig. 5). Our RIP and minigene assays demonstrated that hnRNPF binds to this region to facilitate splicing. Importantly, this effect of hnRNPF is highly specific to tas/bet pre-mRNA, as its overexpression or knockdown does not affect the splicing of other PFV transcripts (data not shown). How the recruited hnRNPF specifically interacts with the spliceosomal machinery to catalyze tas/bet splicing remains an intriguing question. It is possible that hnRNPF acts as a bridge, or that Tas recruits additional co-factors to the IP-associated complex to further coordinate this process. Future structural and biochemical studies will be essential to elucidate the precise molecular architecture of this DNA-dependent splicing recruitment.

In summary, we have unraveled the molecular mechanism that allows the expression of PFV Tas and Bet from the same RNA albeit with the opposing trend, i.e. downregulation of Tas concomitant with upregulation of Bet which leads to the establishment of PFV latency. Our findings demonstrate that the viral Tas protein promotes the splicing of its own tas/bet pre-mRNA by recruiting the cellular splicing factor hnRNPF to GC-II on the nascent RNA. This regulation occurs co-transcriptionally, as Tas simultaneously binds to its target TRE within the internal promoter (IP), which serves as a DNA-based assembly platform. This coordinated DNA-dependent and RNA-mediated recruitment shifts the expression profile toward the spliced bet mRNA, leading to a decrease in Tas levels and an increase in Bet production, which collectively ensure the establishment of PFV latency. These results reveal a previously unrecognized role for hnRNPF and its target motif GC-II in viral gene regulation and provide new insights into how PFV balances replication and latency through the precise control of RNA splicing.

Conclusions

In summary, this study defines a novel mechanism by which PFV regulates RNA splicing to control the transition between replication and latency. We demonstrate that the viral transactivator Tas recruits the host splicing factor hnRNPF to GC-II within the nascent tas/bet pre-mRNA. This process is coordinated with Tas binding to its target DNA (TRE) within the internal promoter, effectively creating a co-transcriptional assembly platform. This recruitment shifts the viral expression profile toward the spliced bet mRNA, thereby reducing Tas levels and facilitating the establishment of viral latency. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized role of hnRNPF and its target element GC-II in foamy virus gene regulation and offer new insights into the sophisticated interplay between viral DNA-binding proteins and the host splicing machinery.
