Deceptive Lesion: When Benign Biopsies Mask a Massive Verrucous Carcinoma
Authors: Monica Schouten, Marije M. van Santen, Liesbeth M. Kager
Published: 2026

A 69-year-old man with a 3-year history of progressive dysphagia, previously to avoid medical care, underwent endoscopy revealing a stenosing, ulcerated lesion at the gastroesophageal junction (Figure A). Repeated superficial biopsies demonstrated squamous epithelium with ulcerative inflammation and fungal elements, without evidence of malignancy. Pathology review at a tertiary center was inconclusive. Empiric antifungal therapy failed. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography subsequently showed a fluorodeoxyglucose–avid stenosing mass with regional lymphadenopathy, highly suspicious for esophageal carcinoma (Figure B). Despite these findings, the patient declined both chemoradiotherapy and surgical resection.

Following transient clinical improvement, the patient died of an upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Autopsy revealed an 18 cm hemorrhagic squamous cell carcinoma extending from the esophagus into the proximal stomach, invading adjacent soft tissues, lymph nodes, and spleen, without distant metastases. Macroscopically, the tumor exhibited a verrucous, exophytic growth pattern (Figure C). Histology demonstrated well-differentiated squamous epithelium with hyperkeratosis and acanthosis, consistent with verrucous carcinoma, explaining the repeatedly nondiagnostic superficial biopsies (Figure D).

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge posed by verrucous squamous cell carcinoma of the esophagogastric junction. Such lesions may mimic chronic inflammatory or infectious processes despite persistently benign biopsies. Integration of endoscopic, radiologic, and pathologic findings is crucial for recognizing their malignant nature.
