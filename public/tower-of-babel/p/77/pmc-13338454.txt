Wheat Functional Traits and Photosynthetic Responses to Dynamic Shading in Agrivoltaic Systems
Authors: Clémentine Inghels, Paul‐Emile Noirot‐Cosson, Annie Guiller, Thomas Kichey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe growing demand for food and renewable energy has stimulated interest in agrivoltaic systems, yet their effects on crop physiology remain poorly understood. This study investigates how dynamic shading from high biaxial solar trackers influences wheat photosynthesis and functional traits. Across 2 years and eight field sites in western France, nine leaf‐level traits related to carbon and nitrogen economy, together with plant height and plant density, were measured at anthesis and analysed against 14 environmental and management variables. Received radiation (RR) was the dominant driver of physiological responses, significantly reducing CO2 assimilation (A sat, A max), respiration, and photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE), while changes in air temperature, soil temperature, and relative humidity remained limited under the elevated agrivoltaic system. Contrary to expectations, wheat exhibited no major morphological acclimations and only limited physiological adjustment to shade. Specific leaf area (SLA) remained unchanged, as did the light compensation point (LCP). The only consistent response was an increase in chlorophyll content, associated with higher leaf nitrogen concentration, which was nevertheless insufficient to maintain photosynthetic rates at levels observed under full light. These findings demonstrate that wheat shows limited structural plasticity to intermittent and heterogeneous shading, highlighting the specificity of elevated, mobile agrivoltaic systems compared with low, ground‐mounted photovoltaic installations. We conclude that varietal selection for agrivoltaics should prioritize traits enhancing photosynthetic induction and nitrogen efficiency under fluctuating light conditions.Keywords: agrivoltaics, dynamic shading, leaf traits, photosynthetic plasticity, Triticum aestivum

Introduction

Driven by population growth, improved living standards, and industrialization, the global demand for energy and food continues to increase (Pandey et al. 2016; Widmer et al. 2024). This surge places immense pressure on agricultural systems and natural resources, highlighting the need for innovative solutions that enhance productivity while ensuring sustainability. Agrivoltaic systems, which combine solar energy production with crop cultivation, have emerged as a promising approach. By enabling dual land‐use, agrivoltaics can increase land productivity by 35%–73% (Dupraz et al. 2011) and potentially buffer crops against climate disruptions.

The effects of agrivoltaic systems on crops are, however, largely influenced by macroclimatic conditions and microclimatic modifications induced by the photovoltaic (PV) panels. Beneath the panels, daytime air temperatures are often lower and soil at night may be warmer (Barron‐Gafford et al. 2016), humidity is elevated, and evaporation is reduced (Adeh et al. 2018; Amaducci et al. 2018; Armstrong et al. 2016). These microclimatic changes can enhance soil moisture retention, reduce actual evapotranspiration (ETR), improve water use efficiency (WUE), and support plant growth under suboptimal environmental conditions such as drought (Amaducci et al. 2018; Marrou, Dufour, and Wery 2013; Pataczek et al. 2023). Nonetheless, these potential benefits must be carefully examined as shading can impose constraints and reduce the amount of direct solar radiation reaching crops, particularly photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), which can impact leaf chlorophyll content, light interception, net photosynthesis, and carbon assimilation (Wang et al. 2024). Thus, despite a small compensatory effect of diffuse radiation, shading generally lowers biomass and yield (Dal Prà et al. 2024; Prakash et al. 2023).

Plants respond to low‐light conditions through a variety of morphological and physiological adaptations, including increased plant height, larger specific leaf area (SLA), higher leaf area index (LAI), and lower light compensation point (LCP), which together allow more efficient capture and use of photons (Dal Prà et al. 2024; Mu et al. 2010; Zheng et al. 2011). The extent of these adaptations varies among species, cultivars, and environmental contexts (Elamri et al. 2018). Thus, in winter wheat, shading has been shown to induce physiological and morphological adjustments that mitigate its negative impact on yield (Mu et al. 2010). In hot and dry environments, wheat has shown enhanced photosynthetic efficiency and water use under partial shading, reflecting its capacity to benefit from moderated microclimates (Arenas‐Corraliza et al. 2019; Li et al. 2010; Weselek, Bauerle, Hartung, et al. 2021). However, rapid fluctuations in light intensity, such as those created by PV panels, can challenge photosynthetic regulation. A sudden increase in light after shading may not allow sufficient time for the activation of regulatory mechanisms; conversely, when light intensity drops, photoprotective mechanisms may remain active, leading to unnecessary energy dissipation and transient declines in carbon fixation efficiency (Long et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2023).

While these adaptations have been studied in various shading conditions, most research on agrivoltaic systems has focused on low, ground‐mounted photovoltaic installations, where shading is concentrated near the panels. Fewer studies have examined elevated PV structures, which generate rapidly shifting shadows throughout the day while allowing more diffuse light to reach crops (Inghels et al. 2025). These studies have primarily assessed crop growth and yield, often overlooking detailed physiological responses, which are crucial to understanding plant adaptation and optimizing system design.

Previous work demonstrated that received radiation (RR) is the primary determinant of biomass accumulation and yield responses under elevated agrivoltaic systems, while simulation analyses highlighted the strong dependence of crop performance on panel density per unit ground area (Inghels et al. 2025). Building on this radiation‐driven framework, the present study investigates the physiological mechanisms underlying these responses at the leaf level. We assessed multiple leaf‐level traits across multiple locations beneath the solar tracker to capture spatial variability in responses. Specifically, we aim at testing the following hypotheses:

Dynamic shading induced by elevated, mobile solar trackers primarily affects wheat photosynthesis through reductions in received radiation, while the other associated microclimatic modifications remain limited (H1).

By integrating leaf functional trait measurements with detailed microclimate data, we aim to provide a mechanistic understanding of how dynamic and spatially heterogeneous radiation regimes generated by elevated PV systems shape wheat physiological responses. This approach seeks to clarify the respective roles of microclimatic modifications, with implications for cultivar selection and agrivoltaics system design.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Site, Agrivoltaic System and Crop Management

The study was conducted in western France (Brittany), a region classified as Cfb under the Köppen‐Geiger climate system. At each experimental site, one tracker was selected for analysis among several present in the field. Four distinct wheat fields were investigated over two growing seasons (2023 and 2024), with observations conducted under eight different solar trackers. Environmental characteristics about each site are available in Table S1.

The agrivoltaic system studied consists of the high biaxial solar tracker developed by OKWIND (Torcé, France), as described by Inghels et al. (2025). It consists of a 117 m2 solar panel platform elevated 7 m above the ground. Designed to follow the sun's path throughout the day, this system allows dynamic movement, with the panel tilting from 0° (horizontal) to a maximum of 50° and rotating up to 310° to optimize solar exposure. To avoid self‐shadowing, the trackers are spaced adequately apart, though no back‐tracking control is implemented. The structure is secured to the ground using a 2.5 m × 2.5 m concrete foundation with a depth of 2 m.

Crop Management

Field‐specific information, including tracker age, wheat varieties, sowing and harvest dates, is provided in Table S1. Briefly, the studied varieties included Chevignon, RGT Pacteo, Extase, and a mixed selection of other varieties. All sites were conventionally farmed, with ploughing prior to sowing, mechanical and chemical weed control, and exclusive reliance on rainfall for irrigation. No significant weed infestations or plant diseases were observed throughout the study period. The target sowing density ranged between 250 and 340 seeds m−2 depending on the site. Actual plant density (plant m−2) was assessed at the emergence stage by counting emerged plants within fixed quadrats at each sampling position in order to evaluate potential spatial heterogeneity in crop establishment around the photovoltaic system. No plants were uprooted.

Nitrogen fertilization was managed by the farmers according to local agronomic practices and applied three times during the growth season: once during tillering, again at the stem elongation stage, and a final application at flag leaf emergence. Exact nitrogen application rates were not available for all sites; however, fertilization strategies were comparable among fields and years. Leaf nitrogen content was measured and included in the analyses to account for potential variability in plant nitrogen status.

Sampling Design

To characterize wheat physiological responses along the spatial gradient of radiation generated by elevated solar trackers, we followed the sampling design described by Inghels et al. (2025). Forty sampling points were established at each site × year, along the eight cardinal directions at distances of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 35 m from the center of the solar tracker (Figure 1). This design captures the continuous variation in light availability around each tracker rather than providing independent biological replicates per point.

Sampling plan design. Black circles represent the distance from the tracker center (5, 10, 15, 20 and 35 m). Orange arrows and dashed lines represent the eight cardinal axes. Blue dots represent the sampled point position.

At each sampling point, one representative flag leaf was measured for physiological and functional traits at anthesis. Each point represents a distinct radiation environment, allowing us to characterize spatially distributed physiological responses along the shading gradient. The maximum distance of 35 m was chosen as a reference for unshaded conditions, since radiation received at this distance is reduced by only 1% over the crop cycle.

To minimize the impact of tire tracks, minor positional adjustments were made when sampling points overlapped with tractor pathways. If a sampling point was located within 1 m of a tire imprint, its potential influence was recorded. Additionally, underground wiring locations were mapped using field cartography of the solar tracker installations. Both factors were documented as potential sources of variation using a binary classification (1 = affected, 0 = unaffected).

Collected Data

Microclimatic Data

At each sampling location, soil temperature (°C) and soil moisture (%H) were monitored using TMS4 sensors (TOMST), with data recorded every 15 min. Air temperature (°C) and relative humidity were measured with EM300‐TH devices (Milesight), with a recording interval of 10 min. Sensors were installed after the completion of field mechanical operations: TMS4 units were deployed in early March and EM300‐TH devices in early April, and all sensors were removed at harvest in July. To ensure that air temperature measurements reflected canopy‐level conditions, the sensors were periodically repositioned to follow crop growth. For statistical analyses, time series from each sensor were aggregated by calculating the mean value of each variable at the sampling‐point level over the period of sensor installation to anthesis, resulting in one representative value per microclimatic parameter.

Received radiations (RR) were calculated (Equation 1) based on the approach described in Inghels et al. (2025), following the methodology described by Noirot‐Cosson et al. (2022). Shading effects from the solar trackers were quantified through simulations of the total radiation received (RR) beneath each structure. To better estimate the effect of RR on wheat physiology, RR were calculated from sowing to anthesis for each site. Mean RR values per plot were then computed for the full growing season and used in the statistical analyses. Overlapping shadows from adjacent trackers were also incorporated to account for cumulative shading effects.

Equation (1) Calculation of % RR.

In this framework, RR was assumed to equal diffuse radiation when plots were shaded by a tracker, and to the sum of direct and diffuse radiation when unshaded. These estimates were restricted to crop establishment periods. Potential obstruction of diffuse light by the PV panels themselves was not considered, as the high elevation of the structures (7 m) made this effect negligible. Tracker shadow dynamics were simulated using Python on two‐dimensional maps with a spatial resolution of 1 m2, computed at an hourly time step for every day of 2019. The model combined (i) solar angle data, (ii) tracker geometry and orientation (set to maximize electricity production), and (iii) trigonometric calculations. The total received radiation ratio (%RR) was then derived according to Equation (1), where R dh represents the radiation received by plants (Wh·m−2) at a given hour (h) and day (d), and Rrefdh the corresponding value in an unshaded reference location. Radiation inputs were obtained from the ECMWF‐IFS weather model (ECMWF 2025).

Soil Data

Soil compaction (kPa) was measured after harvest at each sampling point with a Soil Compaction Meter (Spectrum Technologies Inc.). Additional soil samples were collected to a depth of 30 cm and analysed by an external laboratory (Laboratoire d'analyses des sols, LAS, Arras, France). Pebble content (> 5 mm, g kg−1), gravel (2–5 mm, g kg−1), and fine earth (< 2 mm, g kg−1) fractions were quantified following LAS internal procedures. Total nitrogen (g kg−1) was determined according to NF ISO 13878, while total carbon (g kg−1) and total organic carbon (TOC, g kg−1) followed NF ISO 10694. The C/N ratio was then derived, and organic matter (g kg−1) was estimated from TOC multiplied by 1.72. Particle size distribution was characterized using the AFNOR X31‐107 method, distinguishing clay (0–0.002 mm, g kg−1), silt (0.002–0.063 mm, g kg−1), and sand (0.063–2 mm, g kg−1).

Physiological and Morphological Measurement

Photosynthetic gas exchange measurements were conducted on all 40 sampling locations per site during anthesis over two consecutive days, between 9:00 and 13:00. Measurements were performed on the flag leaf, with one leaf per plant and one plant per plot using a LI‐6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI‐Cor Inc.) equipped with a 1 × 3 sample chamber and a red–blue LED light chamber. Leaf temperature was maintained at 25°C under ambient relative humidity throughout measurement with a sample chamber flow rate of 500 μmol·s−1 and a sample chamber CO2 concentration set at 400 μmol mol−1. Leaves were first exposed to a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 200 μmol m−2 s−1, which was subsequently increased stepwise to 800 and 1200 μmol m−2 s−1 until light saturation was reached at 1500 μmol m−2 s−1 (A sat). To determine maximum photosynthetic capacity (A max), CO2 concentration was raised to 1500 μmol mol−1. Respiration in the dark (R d) was then measured after returning the CO2 concentration to 400 μmol mol−1 and switching off the light source. Five logs were recorded after equilibrating for at least 2 min at each PPFD and reaching defined stability parameters based on photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance to water (g sw) to provide technical replicates and improve the reliability of parameter estimates (Figure S1).

Leaf chlorophyll content was then estimated using a SPAD‐502Plus meter (Konika‐Minolta). Three readings were taken on the flag leaf (top, middle, and bottom) and the average SPAD value per leaf was calculated.

Following gas exchange and SPAD measurements, wheat flag leaves were sampled and scanned for leaf area using the ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, USA). They were then oven‐dried at 60°C for 48 h and weighed. Specific leaf area (SLA, cm2 g−1) was calculated as the leaf surface area per unit dry mass. Leaves were subsequently ground to a fine powder and nitrogen and carbon contents (% DW) were measured by elemental analysis (Vario PYRO cube, Elementar), using ca. 8 mg of ground sample. Analyses were performed at the facilities of the University of Rennes (Ecobio, UMR 6553 CNRS—University de Rennes, France). Plant height was measured on the same individuals using a measuring tape, from the soil surface to the base of the flag leaf.

Quantum yield of CO2 assimilation (ΦCO2) and light compensation point (LCP) were derived from the assimilation curves using the “fits” function, from the package photosynthesis (Stinziano et al. 2021, 2023) for the R software (R version 4.3.2.) (R Core Team 2023). Photosynthetic water use efficiency (PWUE) was calculated as the ratio of A sat to g sw, and photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE) was calculated using the ratio of A sat to leaf nitrogen content (%). Photosynthetic efficiency was calculated as the ratio of A sat to R d.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using the R software (R version 4.3.2.) (R Core Team 2023). Linear Mixed Model (LMM) was performed in order to analyse the effects of independent variables (microclimatic variables, soil compaction, soil C:N ratio soil, tires prints, underground wiring, cultivated variety) on dependant variables (SLA, chlorophyll content, leaf C:N ratio, A sat, A max, R d, PWUE, PNUE, LCP, ΦCO2, plant height, plant density), with the site included as a random effect, and identify impacting variables. Prior to LMM, correlation among variables was assessed to remove highly correlated ones and to facilitate model implementation. The model used in LMM is described in Equation (2). The same equation was used on all dependent variables tested. After building the full model, all possible combinations were tested using ‘dredge’ function from MuMIn package (Bartoń 2024). The five best LMMs obtained using the dredge function were compared based on AIC, and the best model was kept for analysis.

Equation (2) LMM statistical model

LMMs were performed using the “lmer” function from the package lme4 (Bates et al. 2015) to fit linear mixed‐effects models. Graphs were generated using the ggplot2 (Wickham 2016) and ggpubr (Kassambara 2023) packages, allowing visualization of the type and strength of relationship between variables, as well as overall trends in the data. In addition, box plots were created to explore the relationships between qualitative explanatory variables and dependent variables.

Between these extremes, leaf chlorophyll content displayed a CV of 11%. A sat and A max vary considerably, with respectively 28.49% and 17.80% CV. Among the microclimatic variables, RR emerged as a key environmental factor, showing a significant effect in all models in which it was included, particularly for leaf chlorophyll content, leaf C:N ratio, R d, A sat, A max, and PNUE. In contrast, air and soil temperatures had no consistent effect on R d, A sat, or A max, likely reflecting the limited gradients created by the elevated tracker and good air circulation at canopy level. Air humidity showed a significant positive effect on PWUE, suggesting that higher relative humidity reduced transpirational demand and influenced water‐use efficiency. Overall, these results indicate that while radiation heterogeneity drives carbon assimilation and nitrogen‐use traits, other microclimatic factors contribute primarily to water‐use efficiency under elevated agrivoltaic systems.

Cultivated variety also explained a significant part of the variance in SLA, leaf chlorophyll content, leaf C:N ratio, PNUE, ΦCO2, and LCP. In addition, SLA was influenced by soil temperature and soil compaction at 24 cm depth, and leaf C:N ratio was likewise affected by soil compaction at the same depth. Underground wiring significantly affected CO2 assimilation models at A sat and A max, while ΦCO2 was additionally controlled by soil compaction at 8 cm depth.

Discussion

This study builds upon the agrivoltaic experimental platform described by Inghels et al. (2025), which primarily focused on yield responses under elevated, mobile solar trackers. Here, we extend this framework to investigate wheat physiological responses to the dynamic shading generated by tracker mobility. By following a spatial gradient approach, we captured variability in received radiation at multiple points within each site. Focusing on key physiological processes such as photosynthetic capacity, together with selected functional traits related to carbon and nitrogen economy, this study provides new insights into wheat acclimation limits under dynamic shading. These findings complement the yield‐focused results of Inghels et al. (2025) and highlight how continuous physiological responses along shading gradients can inform the optimization of crop performance and the selection of varieties suited to fluctuating light environments.

Influence of Environmental and Site‐Specific Factors

In our system, variation in received radiation (RR) quantitatively explained a substantial proportion of the observed variation in leaf‐level photosynthetic traits (A sat, A max, PNUE), whereas other microclimatic variables (air and soil temperature) showed limited or no significant effects, which validates our first hypothesis. For example, sites experiencing a 20% reduction in RR showed around a 6% decrease in A sat. By contrast, microclimatic variables such as soil and air temperature and relative humidity had only limited effects on wheat physiology. Although soil and air temperatures are generally influenced by RR, their response to shading in our system was very small, even when statistically significant, and humidity remained largely unaffected. These variables were instead primarily governed by macroclimatic conditions, with variations of < 0.25°C for air temperature and < 0.1% for relative humidity across sampling points beneath the elevated solar trackers, indicating limited microclimatic heterogeneity.

This contrasts with low, fixed‐panel PV systems, where pronounced temperature and humidity gradients can significantly affect crop physiology (Adeh et al. 2018; Armstrong et al. 2016; Barron‐Gafford et al. 2016; Marrou, Guilioni, et al. 2013). In our study, spatial variations in air temperature and relative humidity beneath the elevated trackers remained very small, indicating weak local microclimatic contrasts. The 7‐m elevation of biaxial trackers, which are mostly isolated in the field, likely enables air circulation and limits the development of temperature and humidity differential. Consequently, in tall agrivoltaic systems, crop responses are less likely to be mediated by temperature or vapor pressure deficit (VPD), in contrast with lower systems where reduced radiation is compounded by significant changes in energy balance (Weselek, Bauerle, Zikeli, et al. 2021).

The limited microclimatic changes observed under the elevated solar trackers explain why wheat responses were primarily driven by radiation rather than by temperature or humidity. This distinction clarifies that, under tall, mobile agrivoltaic systems, radiation heterogeneity dominates wheat's functional responses, while other environmental variables remain largely unaffected. Our findings highlight that extrapolating results from low, fixed‐panel systems to tall, mobile configurations may be misleading, as shading, temperature, and humidity are differently affected by the panels. They also reinforce the need to integrate dynamic radiation patterns into crop models, since these remain the main determinant of physiological responses in elevated agrivoltaic configurations (Amaducci et al. 2018).

A striking and somewhat unexpected finding was the consistent statistical association of underground wiring with measured physiological traits. This does not reflect a biological effect; rather, it arises from the spatial layout of the measurements. Because physiological data were collected in concentric circles around each tracker, the wiring connecting the tracker to the farm intersects several inner circles, resulting in a higher representation of points classified as “with wiring” in the dataset. This illustrates the importance of accounting for technical infrastructure to avoid potential confounding in agrivoltaic experiments, an issue rarely addressed in the literature (Mazzeo et al. 2025; Toledo and Scognamiglio 2021).

Effects of Shading on CO2 Assimilation and Photosynthetic Performance

Our results indicate that CO2 assimilation rates at saturating (A sat) and maximum (A max) light were significantly reduced under shade, with the strongest decreases observed in plants located closest to the tracker. Recent studies have shown that light‐saturated photosynthetic rates can sometimes be higher under PV panels, particularly when shading reduces afternoon heat and vapor pressure deficits (Sturchio et al. 2024). In our elevated, mobile tracker system, however, shading primarily reduces received radiation without substantially altering microclimatic stress, resulting in lower A sat and A max under shade. While our analyses were based on total received radiation and did not explicitly separate morning versus afternoon shading, our results—showing radiation as the main driver—are consistent with previous studies showing that photosynthetic capacity declines sharply under reduced PAR (Li et al. 2010; Mu et al. 2010). Yet, our study adds nuance by showing that in a context of intermittent, mobile shading, these reductions did not extend to all photosynthetic traits.

In particular, photosynthetic efficiency indices such as ΦCO2 and the A:R d ratio remained unaffected by shade. This contrasts with controlled experiments where continuous shading of 20%–60% PAR led to clear reductions in both quantum yield and photosynthetic efficiency (Dong et al. 2014; Zheng et al. 2011). Our findings suggest that the transient and dynamic shading from the biaxial tracker system does not trigger downregulation of photosystem II or reductions in electron transport efficiency. Instead, the main effect is a reduction in total carbon fixation, while intrinsic light‐use efficiency remains largely unaffected. This contrasts with continuous shade, where downregulation of photosynthetic machinery is commonly observed. While intermittent shading is a general feature of agrivoltaic systems, the magnitude and spatial pattern of shading vary between system designs, influencing the absolute carbon fixed without altering the efficiency of light use. These results highlight a key specificity of agrivoltaics: dynamic radiation patterns primarily modulate the amount of light available rather than the functional capacity of photosynthetic processes.

Water‐ and nitrogen‐use efficiencies also displayed distinct patterns. Unlike in dense, fixed‐panel agrivoltaic or agroforestry systems, where reduced evaporative demand often enhances PWUE (Adeh et al. 2018; Arenas‐Corraliza et al. 2019; Marrou, Dufour, and Wery 2013), our results showed no significant change in PWUE. This again reflects the effect of panel elevation: increased air circulation prevents a substantial reduction in evapotranspiration demand. In contrast, PNUE declined under shade, an effect we attribute to increased nitrogen concentration in flag leaves, potentially linked to lower plant density near trackers and reduced competition for soil resources. Such a decline in PNUE under intermittent shading has rarely been documented in agrivoltaics, making this one of the novel contributions of our study.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that high, mobile systems reduce carbon assimilation without conferring the compensatory gains in water‐use efficiency typically reported in fixed‐panel systems. This has strong implications: it suggests that yield penalties near elevated trackers are less likely to be offset by improvements in WUE, meaning that varietal selection should focus on traits related to rapid photosynthetic induction and efficient nitrogen use under fluctuating light.

Limited Physiological and Morphological Adaptation to Shade

Our second hypothesis, which predicted weak morphological and physiological acclimations to shade, was also confirmed. Wheat exhibited limited structural acclimation under the solar trackers. Crop height and crop density showed low marginal R 2 values but high conditional R 2 values, suggesting that radiation or shading position explained only a small fraction of the total variation. Similarly, plant density at emergence, phenology, and tillering index, previously reported in Inghels et al. (2025), showed minimal variation across the shading gradient. Together, these results indicate that structural and developmental traits related to canopy architecture exhibited limited plasticity in response to dynamic shading and are unlikely to compensate for reductions in carbon assimilation at the plot level under elevated agrivoltaic conditions (Marrou, Guilioni, et al. 2013). However, additional architectural traits not assessed here (e.g., tillering, leaf angle dynamics, canopy structure) may further refine our understanding of whole‐plant acclimation under agrivoltaic systems and deserve investigation in future studies.

Wheat showed no significant modifications in SLA or LCP under shade, with the only consistent adaptation being an increase in chlorophyll content. This contrasts sharply with the well‐documented responses to continuous shading, where crops often display increased SLA, thinner leaves, and lower LCP to optimize light capture under persistent low light (Li et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2017; Zheng et al. 2011). The absence of these adaptations in our study suggests that the intermittent shade of high biaxial trackers is insufficient in duration or intensity to trigger structural changes.

Instead, plants relied primarily on biochemical adjustments. The observed increase in chlorophyll content, associated with higher leaf nitrogen and reduced C:N ratio, is consistent with previous findings under shade (Arenas‐Corraliza et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024; Xiong et al. 2015). This adjustment can enhance photon capture efficiency, but our results show it was insufficient to maintain assimilation at unshaded levels. This finding explains why wheat grown near trackers often exhibits reduced biomass and yield (Inghels et al. 2025).

This limited morphological plasticity is particularly significant in the context of agrivoltaics. In agroforestry or greenhouse systems, where shading is stable and predictable, plants frequently develop strong structural acclimations (e.g., higher LAI, altered leaf orientation) that partly mitigate yield losses (Arenas‐Corraliza et al. 2019). By contrast, in our study, dynamic shade fluctuating at daily and intra‐day scales did not trigger such structural adjustments, leaving wheat more exposed to sustained reductions in carbon gain. Our results indicate that carbon assimilation under elevated PV systems is primarily constrained by reduced received radiation, with limited morphological acclimation. Under such conditions, varietal performance is therefore likely influenced more by the capacity to maintain efficient carbon and nitrogen use under intermittently reduced, yet relatively predictable, light availability than by classical shade‐acclimation traits such as SLA plasticity (Long et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2023). Rather than traits associated with very rapid light fluctuations (e.g., sunflecks), breeding efforts for elevated agrivoltaic systems may benefit from targeting improved coordination between leaf nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic capacity, as well as stable photosynthetic functioning under heterogeneous but not highly stochastic light regimes. Importantly, these conclusions are specific to elevated agrivoltaic systems with limited microclimatic effects and should not be generalized to all agrivoltaic configurations.

Finally, the specificity of our results lies in linking these limited adaptive responses to the unique light environment of tall, mobile trackers, where shade is intermittent, heterogeneous, and less thermally constraining than in fixed low‐panel systems. This insight adds a novel dimension to the physiology of crops under agrivoltaics and helps explain why yield losses observed near elevated trackers cannot be compensated by morphological plasticity alone.

Conclusions

This study assessed the effect of high biaxial solar trackers on wheat photosynthesis by examining how dynamic, spatially heterogeneous shading influences morphological and physiological traits. Across eight field sites over 2 years, our results demonstrate that the reduction in received radiation is the dominant driver of photosynthetic responses, whereas microclimatic modifications remain limited due to the elevated design of the system. Importantly, intermittent shading reduced CO2 assimilation and PNUE but did not significantly alter photosynthetic efficiency, SLA, or LCP. The only consistent adjustment observed was an increase in chlorophyll content, reflecting biochemical acclimation to reduced light, yet insufficient to sustain photosynthetic performance at unshaded levels.

These findings provide two major insights. First, wheat exhibits limited capacity to structurally acclimate to the intermittent and dynamic shading of elevated, mobile agrivoltaic systems, contrasting with the stronger morphological plasticity reported under continuous or dense shading conditions. Second, elevated solar trackers impose a unique light environment that primarily reduces carbon assimilation without providing compensatory gains in water‐use efficiency. This specificity underscores the need to adapt breeding targets and management strategies to the fluctuating light regimes characteristic of agrivoltaics, including variation in shading timing throughout the day, which may further influence photosynthetic performance (Sturchio et al. 2024; Marrou, Dufour, and Wery 2013).

By demonstrating that classical shade‐acclimation traits (e.g., SLA plasticity, lower LCP) are not expressed under dynamic shading, our study highlights the importance of selecting varieties with traits that enhance rapid photosynthetic induction, flexible nitrogen allocation, and resilience to fluctuating light. Future research should extend these findings by analyzing pigment composition, canopy‐level light distribution, and full‐cycle yield responses to identify wheat varieties best suited for cultivation under high, mobile agrivoltaic systems.
