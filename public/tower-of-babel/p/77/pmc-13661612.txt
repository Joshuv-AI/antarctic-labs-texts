Rannasangpei and its constituent crocin-1 ameliorate valproic acid–induced autism-like behaviors accompanied by reduced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation
Authors: Rui Qiu, Liguo Li, Tongtong Yao, Quan Tang, Lei Chen, Qiang Fu, Jingkun Yu, Linyu Jiang, Yinqi Xue, Xiaoya Yu, Yong Cheng, Hui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by impaired social interaction and repetitive behaviors, with increasing evidence implicating immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, and altered transcriptional regulation. This study investigated the pharmacological effects of Rannasangpei (RNSP), a classical Tibetan formulation, and its active constituent crocin-1 in a valproic acid (VPA)-induced mouse model of ASD. Behavioral tests demonstrated that RNSP and crocin-1 markedly reduced VPA-induced repetitive behaviors and improved social interaction. Biochemical analyses revealed that these effects were associated with restoration of redox balance, characterized by increased activities of catalase and superoxide dismutase and decreased levels of malondialdehyde, together with improvement of the GSH/GSSG ratio. Both treatments also attenuated neuroinflammation, as indicated by reduced expression of IL-6, IL-17A, TNF-α, and Lepr, together with recovery of IL-10 in the prefrontal cortex and cerebellum. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed that RNSP and crocin-1 were associated with widespread transcriptional reorganization in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and cerebellum. In the PFC, these changes involved pathways related to cell-cell adhesion, morphogenesis, and MAPK signaling. In the cerebellum, the dominant enrichment pattern centered on membrane trafficking and cytoskeleton-related processes, with additional enrichment of oxidative stress response pathways. Integration of pathway analysis and representative gene changes indicated region-specific but functionally related alterations in programs linked to intracellular transport, neuronal structure, and synaptic function. Safety evaluation further showed that neither RNSP nor crocin-1 produced obvious systemic toxicity under the present experimental conditions, as indicated by no consistent treatment-related abnormalities in serum biochemical parameters and histological features of major peripheral organs. These findings indicate that RNSP and crocin-1 were associated with changes in oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and transcriptional regulation across multiple brain regions, supporting their potential therapeutic relevance in ASD. Subject terms: Scientific community, Autism spectrum disorders

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition characterized by impaired social communication, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors [1, 2]. Its prevalence has risen steadily worldwide and now affects approximately 1–2% of children, posing substantial clinical and socioeconomic burdens [3]. Although ASD is influenced by genetic and environmental factors, increasing evidence highlights the contribution of molecular and cellular abnormalities, including immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, and impaired synaptic development [4–11]. Consistent neuropathological findings indicate that the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and cerebellum—two regions essential for social cognition, motor coordination, and executive functioning—are particularly vulnerable to ASD-related alterations [12, 13]. Disruptions in redox balance and inflammatory signaling further exacerbate synaptic dysfunction, contributing to persistent behavioral deficits [14, 15].

Despite advances in understanding the etiology of ASD, no effective pharmacological treatments are available for its core symptoms [16, 17]. Currently approved agents, such as risperidone and aripiprazole, alleviate irritability or hyperactivity but do not improve social communication or repetitive behaviors and frequently cause adverse metabolic effects. Behavioral and educational interventions may offer benefits but require long-term intensive training and yield inconsistent outcomes. These limitations underscore the urgent need for multitarget therapeutic strategies capable of addressing the complex pathophysiology of ASD [18, 19].

Traditional Tibetan medicine provides a valuable pharmacological resource for multifactorial disorders because its formulations contain diverse bioactive compounds with complementary mechanisms [20]. Rannasangpei (RNSP), also known as Qishiwei Zhenzhu Pill, is one of the most representative classical prescriptions in Tibetan medicine [21–24]. It has long been used for neurological, cardiovascular, and gastrointestinal disorders and has been included in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia since 2015. RNSP contains multiple herbal, mineral, and animal-derived components, including Crocus sativus L. (saffron) and several detoxified mineral preparations, which together contribute to its multitarget pharmacological profile [25, 26].

Modern pharmacological studies have shown that RNSP has antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and microcirculatory regulatory effects. RNSP alleviates schizophrenia-like behaviors induced by MK-801, improves cognitive deficits in patients with vascular dementia, and mitigates neurotoxicity and gut dysbiosis in Parkinson’s disease models [24, 25, 27]. Emerging evidence also indicates that RNSP and its major active constituent crocin-1 exert antidepressant effects by modulating oxidative stress, immune responses, and neurotrophic signaling [28]. Because these mechanisms overlap substantially with those implicated in ASD, RNSP represents a promising candidate for addressing ASD-related neurobiological abnormalities [29–33].

On the basis of this rationale, we hypothesized that RNSP could ameliorate autism-like phenotypes, potentially involving modulation of oxidative and immune homeostasis in key brain regions. To test this hypothesis, we used the well-established valproic acid (VPA) mouse model, which reproduces the core behavioral and neuropathological features of ASD [34, 35]. We evaluated whether RNSP and crocin-1 could improve social deficits and repetitive behaviors while evaluating oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and treatment-associated transcriptional changes in the PFC and cerebellum. This study provides the first systematic evaluation of RNSP in a neurodevelopmental disorder and offers new insights into multitarget therapeutic strategies for ASD.

Materials and methods

Animal preparation

C57BL/6 mice were obtained from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed under controlled environmental conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 1% humidity, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, lights on from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.). The mice had ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. All the experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 80-23) and were approved by the Biological and Medical Ethics Committee, Minzu University of China.

VPA model construction

C57BL/6J mice were used to establish a VPA-induced model of ASD. For breeding, one female mouse and one male mouse were housed together in a single cage, and the day of pairing was designated gestational day 1 (E1). The body weight of each female was recorded before mating and remeasured on E7; an increase of >3 g was considered indicative of pregnancy. On E12.5, pregnant mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of VPA (500 mg/kg, 50 mg/mL in physiological saline). Following injection, pregnant mice were housed individually until delivery. Pregnant control mice received an equivalent volume of physiological saline (10 mL/kg, i.p.) at the same gestational time point. The offspring were weaned and maintained under standard conditions. At 5 weeks of age, autism-like behaviors were evaluated to confirm the successful establishment of the VPA and control groups [36].

A total of 2 pregnant dams were included in the prenatal vehicle-treated control (VC) group, and 7 pregnant dams were included in the VPA group. The VC dams produced 11 offspring in total (5 females and 6 males), whereas the VPA-treated dams produced 41 offspring in total (20 females and 21 males). After birth, offspring from the same treatment condition were pooled and randomly assigned to the different experimental groups, with subsequent procedures performed under blinded conditions. To reduce potential litter-specific bias, each group included offspring from different litters as far as possible. For behavioral experiments, 6 offspring per group were randomly selected, including 3 males and 3 females. For oxidative stress analyses, 5 mice per group were randomly selected from the behavioral cohort. For serum biochemical safety analyses, additional serum samples from the remaining mice in each group were used. Both male and female offspring were included in this study. Because VPA-treated offspring were required for subsequent subgrouping and downstream analyses, a larger initial pool was retained, whereas control animals were kept to the minimum necessary for valid comparison in accordance with the 3 R principles.

Source, quality control, and gavage preparation of RNSP

RNSP (Qishiwei Zhenzhu Pills; 1 g/pill; National Medicine Approval No. Z54020062; batch No. 210700122) was obtained from Tibet Ganlu Tibetan Medicine Co., Ltd. (Lhasa, China). RNSP is a commercially manufactured Tibetan patent medicine with a proprietary formulation. Therefore, the exact full prescription composition and industrial manufacturing process are not publicly disclosed by the manufacturer. To ensure traceability and reproducibility, the manufacturer, dosage form, approval number, and batch number are provided, and the product quality was confirmed according to the corresponding finished-product quality standard.

For animal administration, the commercially available RNSP pills were freshly prepared as a homogeneous suspension for gavage rather than re-manufactured or extracted in-house. Briefly, 1.0 g of RNSP was finely ground, suspended in 10 mL of distilled water, and sonicated at 350 W for 30 min. The suspension was thoroughly vortexed immediately before administration and freshly prepared each day.

To provide a measurable chemical reference for comparison with the single-compound treatment, crocin-1 in RNSP was analyzed by HPLC. Briefly, 0.1 g of sample was extracted twice with 80% methanol under ultrasonication at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 7500 rpm for 5 min each time. The combined supernatants were adjusted to 50 mL with 80% methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane before analysis. HPLC was performed on an Agilent 1260 system using a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at 40 °C, with water and methanol (45:55, v/v) as the mobile phase, a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, an injection volume of 10 μL, and detection at 442 nm.

Drug administration

After baseline behavioral testing at Week 7 (D49), offspring were randomly assigned to the following groups: the VC, the VPA model group, VPA + RNSP, VPA + crocin-1 low dose (CRO-L), VPA + crocin-1 medium dose (CRO-M), and VPA + crocin-1 high dose (CRO-H). All treatments were administered to offspring by oral gavage once daily for 28 consecutive days from Week 8 to Week 12 (D56–84) (see Fig. 1A for the experimental timeline).

The mice in the RNSP group were administered RNSP by oral gavage at a dose of 150 mg/kg, which was diluted in physiological saline. Crocin-1 (Chengdu Must Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China; CAS No. 42553-65-1, HPLC ≥ 98%) was used for the treatment groups (its molecular structure is shown in Fig. 1B). Crocin-1 was administered by oral gavage at doses of 1 mg/kg (CRO-L), 5 mg/kg (CRO-M), or 20 mg/kg (CRO-H). The dosages of RNSP and crocin-1 were determined on the basis of previous literature, the Four Medical Tantras (the classical Tibetan medical text), and the modified Chinese Pharmacopoeia. The clinical dosage of RNSP was converted to the corresponding mouse equivalent dose for experimental use [28].

Behavioral tests

Digging behavior

The experimental mice were individually placed in a cage lined with fresh bedding and allowed to explore freely for 20 min. The entire session was continuously video recorded. The first 10 min served as an adaptation period, and the subsequent 10 min were used to quantify the duration of digging behavior. Here, “digging” is defined as the behavior in which a mouse disperses bedding via its forelimbs and head [37].

Self-grooming behavior

For the assessment of self-grooming, the mice were individually placed in an open field without bedding and allowed to explore freely for 20 min. The session was continuously video recorded, with the initial 10 min designated as an adaptation period. The following 10 min were used to record the duration of self-grooming behavior, which is defined as the act of the mouse stroking or scratching its body or face or licking its body parts [38].

Social interaction test

A standardized behavioral paradigm was employed to evaluate the social interaction capabilities of the mice. Initially, each experimental mouse was allowed to acclimate to the behavioral testing room for a minimum of 30 min to mitigate environmental stress. The mice were subsequently individually placed in a clean cage with fresh bedding and allowed to explore freely for 15 min under low-light conditions, which served as an adaptation phase.

Following the adaptation period, an unfamiliar conspecific—matched for age or slightly younger—was introduced into the cage. The dyadic interaction was allowed to proceed freely for 10 min, during which the entire session was video recorded for subsequent analysis. Behavioral analysis focused on quantifying the direct contact behaviors of the test mouse, including body sniffing, following, and climbing [39].

Three-chamber social interaction test

Social behavior was evaluated via a three-chamber apparatus. The apparatus comprised three interconnected rectangular chambers (19 × 45 cm each), with passageways connecting them. In each side chamber, a metal cage—sized to accommodate a single mouse—was centrally positioned.

The experiment consisted of two phases. In Phase 1, the experimental mouse was initially placed in the central chamber and allowed a 5-minute acclimation period. Subsequently, an unfamiliar conspecific (Mouse A) was randomly introduced into one of the side chambers, while the other chamber remained empty. After the partitions were removed, the experimental mouse was permitted to explore all three chambers freely for 10 min. During this period, both the duration of interaction and the number of direct contacts between the experimental mouse and Mouse-A (or the empty cage) were recorded.

In Phase 2, the empty cage was replaced with a second unfamiliar conspecific (Mouse-B). The subsequent 10-minute session involved recording the interaction duration and frequency among the experimental mouse, Mouse-A, and Mouse-B [40].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Transcriptional expression levels of cytokine-related genes were quantified via quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) via standardized procedures optimized in our laboratory [41]. PFC and cerebellum tissues were rapidly dissected on ice following the designated treatments. Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and only samples with A260/280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1 were used for downstream analyses.

To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA via StarScript II RT Mix with gDNA Remover (GenStar, Beijing, China). qPCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 real-time PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with SYBR Green I Master Mix (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Each reaction was run in technical triplicates, and no-template controls (NTCs) were included to monitor nonspecific amplification or primer–dimer formation.

The amplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to confirm amplification specificity. The gene-specific sequences of the primers used are listed in Table 1. The primer efficiencies were verified to be within 95–105% prior to use.

Assessment of oxidative stress markers

Under anesthesia, peripheral blood samples (0.8 mL) were collected from the retro-orbital plexus of mice and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain approximately 300 μL of serum per sample. Oxidative stress markers were evaluated using enzyme-based colorimetric assays according to the manufacturers’ instructions. To comprehensively assess oxidative stress status, serum, PFC, and cerebellum samples were analyzed. The measured parameters included catalase (CAT; A007-1-1), reduced glutathione (GSH; A006-2-1), oxidized glutathione (GSSG; A061-1-2), malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2), and superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2). All assays were performed using commercially available kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, as previously described [42].

Serum biochemical analysis

Serum biochemical parameters, including ALT, TBIL, CK, and UREA, were measured using a Toshiba automatic biochemical analyzer (Japan) with commercial kits obtained from Beijing Beijian Xinchuangyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Fresh serum samples were analyzed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. ALT was determined using the alanine substrate method, TBIL using the vanadate oxidation method, CK using an enzyme-coupled assay, and UREA using the urease-glutamate dehydrogenase method. All measurements were performed at 37 °C, and the results were automatically calculated by the analyzer using the corresponding calibrators.

Histopathological examination and H&E staining

Liver, spleen, and kidney tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard procedures. Briefly, sections were stained with hematoxylin, differentiated, blued, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological changes were examined under a light microscope, and representative images were captured at ×40 magnification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of Q–Q plots. For two-group comparisons, homogeneity of variance was assessed using an F test. When equal variance was met, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used; when unequal variance was detected, Welch’s corrected unpaired two-tailed t test was applied. For baseline sniffing-duration analyses with two factors, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparisons test was used. For comparisons among multiple independent groups, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was used. Homogeneity of variance for multi-group analyses was evaluated using Brown–Forsythe and/or Bartlett’s tests as appropriate. Longitudinal body weight data were analyzed using a mixed-effects model (REML), with time as the repeated factor and group as the between-subject factor; prespecified between-group comparisons at each time point were performed using Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test with the VPA group as the control column. No nonparametric tests were used in the final analyses.

Representative histological images were not subjected to statistical analysis. The specific statistical test used for each experiment, the sample size (n), and exact P values are reported in the corresponding figure legends. Detailed statistical information for each panel, including test statistics, degrees of freedom, variance assessment, and multiple-comparisons corrections, is provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S4. All statistical analyses were pre-specified and conducted in accordance with standard practices in the field.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the PFC and cerebellum of the prenatal vehicle control, VPA model, VPA + RNSP, VPA + low-dose crocin-1, VPA + medium-dose crocin-1, and VPA + high-dose crocin-1 groups, with n = 3 biological replicates per group for each tissue. The RNA samples were then sent to Novogene (Beijing, P. R. China; https://cn.novogene.com/, accessed 10 March 2025) for mRNA library construction and sequencing. Raw reads were quality-filtered according to the provider’s standard pipeline to remove adaptor-contaminated and low-quality reads, and the resulting clean reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm39) using HISAT2 v2.0.5. Gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts v1.5.0-p3. Sample-level reproducibility and intergroup relationships were evaluated by correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) based on FPKM values. No obvious batch effects were observed in PCA clustering; therefore, no additional batch-effect correction was applied.

Differential expression analysis between each treatment group and the VPA model group was performed using DESeq2 (v1.20.0). Raw count data were normalized using the median-of-ratios method implemented in DESeq2, and gene-wise differential expression was modeled using a negative binomial distribution. For each comparison, both nominal P values and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P values (padj) were calculated. Genes with an absolute |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. A heatmap representing gene expression was generated via bioinformatics.com.cn (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/, accessed 23 April 2025), and pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted via Metascape (https://Metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1, accessed 15 May 2025). Raw and normalized gene-level expression values, together with processed differential expression results including log2 fold changes, nominal P values, and adjusted P values, are provided in Supplementary Tables S5 (PFC) and S7 (cerebellum), and pathway-associated gene lists are provided in Supplementary Tables S6 (PFC) and S8 (cerebellum).

Results

RNSP and crocin-1 alleviate stereotyped and social deficits in VPA-induced autism-like mice

To support the comparison between the whole formula and the single-compound treatment, HPLC analysis identified crocin-1 in RNSP, with an estimated content of approximately 4.3 mg/g (Figure S1). We then evaluated the effects of RNSP and crocin-1 on VPA-induced behavioral abnormalities. The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1A, and the chemical structure of crocin-1 is shown in Fig. 1B. Before treatment allocation, baseline behavioral analyses were first performed to verify the successful establishment of the prenatal VPA-induced ASD-like mouse model. Compared with prenatal vehicle control (VC) mice, prenatal VPA-exposed offspring showed marked stereotyped and social abnormalities, including significantly increased digging time (Fig. 1C) and self-grooming duration (Fig. 1D), together with reduced active social interaction time (Fig. 1E). In the three-chamber test, VPA-exposed mice exhibited impaired sociability, as indicated by reduced preference for Stranger 1 over the empty cage (Fig. 1F), and impaired social novelty recognition, as reflected by the absence of a clear preference for Stranger 2 over Stranger 1 (Fig. 1G). These findings confirmed the successful establishment of the VPA-induced autism-like model.

Following baseline behavioral characterization, prenatal VPA-exposed mice were randomized into untreated VPA, RNSP, and crocin-1 treatment groups and then orally treated for 28 days, whereas VC mice received saline. Behavioral assessments focused on stereotypy and social interaction, two core features of ASD [43]. In the digging and self-grooming tests, VPA-exposed mice displayed significantly increased repetitive behaviors compared with VC mice. Both RNSP and crocin-1 reduced digging and self-grooming, with RNSP showing a more pronounced effect (Fig. 1H, I).

Direct social interaction testing revealed that VPA exposure markedly impaired sociability, as evidenced by reduced interaction time. RNSP and crocin-1 treatments restored social interaction, with animals spending significantly more time engaging with conspecifics than VPA-exposed mice did (Fig. 1J). In the three-chamber social test, VPA-exposed mice failed to display normal sociability and preference for social novelty. RNSP and crocin-1 treatments significantly improved both parameters, as shown by increased sniffing by stranger mice, normalized preference indices in the sociability phase (Fig. 1L), and restored preference for novel over familiar mice in the social novelty phase (Fig. 1M). Together, these findings indicate that RNSP and crocin-1 effectively attenuate VPA-induced autism-like behaviors, with RNSP generally showing stronger effects than crocin-1.

RNSP and crocin-1 ameliorate oxidative stress in both the central and peripheral systems

Excessive oxidative stress and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are key pathological features of ASD, contributing to stress responses, neuroinflammation, impaired neurotransmission, disrupted neurogenesis, and synaptic dysfunction [44–46]. To comprehensively evaluate whether RNSP and crocin-1 alleviate oxidative stress, we assessed multiple oxidative stress-related biomarkers, including CAT, GSH, GSSG, the GSH/GSSG ratio, MDA, and SOD, not only in the PFC and cerebellum but also in the serum, thereby covering both the central and peripheral systems.

RNSP and crocin-1 effectively restored antioxidant enzyme activity. CAT, which was significantly reduced in the VPA model, markedly recovered in all the treatment groups, with medium- and high-dose crocin-1 having the strongest effects (Fig. 2A, B). SOD activity also improved, particularly in the PFC and serum, where RNSP and high-dose crocin-1 normalized enzyme function, whereas RNSP had a more prominent effect on the cerebellum (Fig. 2C, D).

The glutathione system, a central regulator of redox homeostasis, was also rebalanced. VPA exposure depleted reduced GSH and increased oxidized GSSG, resulting in a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio. RNSP and crocin-1 restored GSH levels, suppressed GSSG accumulation, and increased the GSH/GSSG ratio in a dose-dependent manner, with high-dose crocin-1 nearly normalizing PFC values and RNSP producing consistent improvements across the brain and serum (Fig. 2E–J). Markers of oxidative damage showed parallel improvements. MDA, a lipid peroxidation product elevated in the VPA model, was significantly reduced by RNSP and crocin-1, with medium- and high-dose crocin-1 exerting the greatest effect across all the tissues examined (Fig. 2K, L).

Taken together, these findings indicate that RNSP and crocin-1 were associated with broad antioxidant effects, as reflected by increased activities of CAT and SOD, improved glutathione-related redox balance, and reduced lipid peroxidation. Consistent changes observed in both brain and serum samples support a coordinated pattern of redox-related improvement under the present experimental conditions. These results are consistent with previous reports of glutathione deficits and oxidative imbalance in ASD and support the potential relevance of RNSP and crocin-1 in modulating oxidative stress–related abnormalities.

RNSP and crocin-1 attenuate neuroinflammation in the PFC and cerebellum

Excessive oxidative stress can trigger inflammatory cascades, while proinflammatory cytokines in turn exacerbate ROS generation, establishing a vicious cycle that accelerates neuronal injury in individuals with ASD [6, 47]. To examine whether RNSP and crocin-1 attenuate this inflammatory imbalance, we quantified the expression of multiple cytokines and receptors in the PFC and cerebellum.

IL-6, a key proinflammatory mediator in ASD, was markedly upregulated in VPA-treated mice, particularly in the PFC (Fig. 3A) [48, 49]. Both RNSP and crocin-1 substantially suppressed IL-6 expression, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the high-dose crocin-1 group. Although the increase in the cerebellum was less dramatic, RNSP and high-dose crocin-1 effectively reduced IL-6 levels toward baseline (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the level of IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, was strongly decreased by VPA exposure in both regions (Fig. 3C, D) [50, 51]. RNSP robustly restored IL-10 expression in the PFC and cerebellum, whereas crocin-1 induced partial recovery, with a trend toward stronger effects at the medium and high doses. The reciprocal regulation of IL-6 and IL-10 highlights the profound disruption of inflammatory homeostasis in VPA-treated mice, and both interventions help to rebalance this homeostasis. IL-17A, another proinflammatory cytokine linked to abnormal neurodevelopment, displays patterns similar to those of IL-6, with striking increases in the PFC and moderate yet significant increases in the cerebellum [52, 53]. Both RNSP and crocin-1 reduced IL-17A expression across regions, reinforcing their anti-inflammatory potential (Fig. 3E, F).

The level of TNF-α, a classical marker of systemic and central inflammation, was elevated in both brain regions of VPA-treated mice (Fig. 3G, H) [54]. RNSP treatment clearly attenuated TNF-α expression in the PFC, whereas both RNSP and crocin-1 had suppressive effects on the cerebellum. Finally, Lepr, a receptor with dual roles in metabolic regulation and neurodevelopment that is increasingly implicated in ASD, was markedly upregulated following VPA exposure (Fig. 3I, J) [55]. Both RNSP and crocin-1 reduced Lepr expression, with stronger effects generally observed in the medium- and high-dose groups.

Overall, these findings indicate that VPA-induced ASD-like pathology was associated with increased levels of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and Lepr) and reduced anti-inflammatory signaling (IL-10). RNSP and crocin-1 were associated with consistent modulation of these molecular changes in both the PFC and cerebellum, indicating that restoration of pro- and anti-inflammatory balance was associated with treatment effects. Notably, RNSP generally exhibited broader and more consistent modulation than crocin-1 across the measured endpoints, suggesting differential regulatory profiles between the multi-component formulation and the single active compound.

Transcriptomic profiling of the prefrontal cortex reveals treatment-related changes in ASD-related pathways

The PFC, a region essential for social cognition and executive control, is frequently implicated in ASD because of abnormalities in cortical development, connectivity, and excitatory–inhibitory balance [36, 56]. To investigate the molecular basis of the protective effects of RNSP and crocin-1, transcriptomic profiling of the PFC was conducted across six groups of mice: the VC group, VPA model, and RNSP group and three crocin-1 treatment groups.

Differential expression analysis revealed marked transcriptional remodeling across the treatment comparisons in the PFC. Heatmap visualization showed clear clustering of DEGs among groups, indicating substantial divergence in transcriptomic profiles relative to the VPA group (Fig. 4A). Volcano plots further demonstrated distinct DEG distributions in each comparison (Fig. 4B). UpSet analysis showed that the VC, RNSP, CRO-L, CRO-M, and CRO-H comparisons contained 506, 244, 68, 3398, and 717 DEGs, respectively, with CRO-M contributing the largest unique DEG subset (Fig. 4C). In addition to these group-specific changes, a subset of DEGs was shared across multiple comparisons, suggesting the presence of both common and treatment-specific transcriptional responses. Detailed differential expression results, including log2 fold changes, p values, and adjusted p values (padj), are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Functional enrichment analysis via Metascape identified 20 significantly enriched pathways in the PFC, with the overall enrichment landscape largely centered on developmental, morphogenetic, adhesion-related, and signaling-associated processes (Fig. 4D, E). Gene lists corresponding to enriched pathways are summarized in Supplementary Table S6. Among these, tissue morphogenesis, cell-cell adhesion, and positive regulation of MAPK cascade were prioritized for downstream analysis because they captured complementary aspects of the PFC response, including tissue organization, cell connectivity, and intracellular signaling (Fig. 4D–F). Cluster analysis further indicated substantial relatedness among enriched terms, supporting the presence of functionally interconnected biological programs.

Further comparison of the three prioritized pathways revealed both pathway-specific and overlapping gene components. Venn analysis identified 71 genes unique to tissue morphogenesis, 60 unique to cell-cell adhesion, and 52 unique to positive regulation of MAPK cascade, together with several pairwise overlaps and 3 genes shared across all three pathways (Fig. 4F). On the basis of pathway membership, expression direction, and biological relevance, eight candidate genes were selected for downstream visualization and integrative analysis: CTNNBIP1, IRX2, FAT2, CTNNB1, RET, GSN, ROR2, and SLITRK2 (Fig. 4G, H).

The heatmap of these candidate genes revealed clear group-dependent expression patterns in the PFC (Fig. 4G). CTNNBIP1 and GSN were relatively increased in the VPA group, whereas IRX2, FAT2, RET, ROR2, and SLITRK2 were relatively reduced. Across treatment groups, these genes exhibited varying degrees of reorganization toward non-VPA expression patterns. CTNNB1 displayed an intermediate pattern and occupied a bridging position across the three prioritized pathways, whereas RET was positioned at the intersection of adhesion-associated and MAPK-associated processes (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these transcriptomic findings indicate treatment-related changes in VPA-disrupted gene expression programs in the PFC, particularly those related to tissue morphogenesis, cell-cell adhesion, and MAPK signaling.

Cerebellar transcriptomic profiling reveals treatment-related changes in membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal organization pathways

The cerebellum, beyond its classical role in motor control, is increasingly recognized as a critical hub in cognitive and social processing, and its dysfunction has been strongly implicated in ASD [57, 58]. To investigate whether RNSP and crocin-1 regulate transcriptional disturbances in this region, cerebellar RNA sequencing was performed across the six experimental groups.

Differential expression analysis revealed substantial transcriptional changes in the cerebellum following RNSP and crocin-1 treatment. Heatmap visualization showed clear group-dependent clustering of cerebellar DEGs, indicating marked transcriptomic divergence among the intervention groups relative to the VPA model (Fig. 5A). Volcano plots further demonstrated distinct DEG distributions across the five comparisons (Fig. 5B). UpSet analysis showed that the cerebellar DEG landscape consisted of both comparison-specific and partially shared gene subsets, consistent with the presence of common as well as treatment-specific transcriptional responses (Fig. 5C). Comprehensive cerebellar differential expression results are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Functional enrichment analysis identified multiple significantly enriched biological processes in the cerebellum, with the dominant enrichment landscape centered on pathways related to membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal organization (Fig. 5D, E). In parallel, response to oxidative stress was recurrently enriched across cerebellar comparisons and was retained as a supportive pathway in the interpretation of cerebellar transcriptomic changes. Based on their functional coherence, membrane trafficking, Golgi to plasma membrane protein transport, and actin cytoskeleton organization were prioritized for downstream analysis.

These three prioritized pathways were functionally related but only partially overlapping at the gene level (Fig. 5F). A focused heatmap of selected rescue-like genes, including APC, ARPC1B, BRF1, IDE, DNMT3A, TET3, GPX5, and AGBL2, demonstrated clear group-dependent expression patterns in the cerebellum following RNSP and crocin-1 treatment (Fig. 5G). A module–gene network further integrated pathway-level and gene-level information, highlighting representative genes associated with intracellular trafficking, membrane delivery, and cytoskeletal remodeling (Fig. 5H). Together, these results indicate that treatment was associated with cerebellar transcriptional changes, particularly in pathways related to membrane trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, and oxidative stress responses.

RNSP and crocin-1 show no obvious systemic toxicity in prenatal VPA-exposed mice

The safety profile of RNSP and crocin-1 was assessed by monitoring body weight, serum biochemical parameters, and histopathological changes in major peripheral organs. As shown in Fig. 6A, body weight increased progressively from 4 to 12 weeks of age in all groups. Mixed-effects analysis revealed significant main effects of time and group, as well as a significant time-by-group interaction, and detailed longitudinal statistical results are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Although significant between-group differences were observed at selected time points, no consistent treatment-related reduction in body weight was detected, suggesting that RNSP and crocin-1 did not produce overt adverse effects on general growth under the present experimental conditions.

Serum biochemical indices related to liver, muscle, and kidney function were then examined. Relative to the VC and VPA groups, treatment with RNSP or crocin-1 did not produce consistent changes in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL), creatine kinase (CK), or urea (UREA) levels (Fig. 6B–E; Supplementary Table S4). These findings suggest that repeated administration of RNSP or crocin-1 did not induce detectable hepatic, muscular, or renal toxicity under the present experimental conditions.

Histological analyses of the liver, spleen, and kidney were further performed using H&E staining. Representative images showed no obvious pathological abnormalities, such as cellular degeneration, structural disruption, inflammatory infiltration, or overt tissue injury, in any treatment group relative to the VC or VPA group (Fig. 6F). Consistently, quantitative analyses revealed no significant differences in hepatocyte count, medullary splenic corpuscles, or intra-nephron cell number among groups (Fig. 6G–I; Supplementary Table S4). Collectively, these results support that RNSP and crocin-1 were well tolerated and did not cause overt systemic toxicity in prenatal VPA-exposed mice under the present experimental conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic potential of RNSP, a traditional Tibetan medicine, and its active component crocin-1 in a VPA-induced mouse model of ASD. Our results demonstrate that both RNSP and crocin-1 significantly ameliorate core ASD-like behaviors, including stereotypy and social deficits. These treatments also restore redox balance and attenuate neuroinflammation in both the central and peripheral systems. At the molecular level, RNSP and crocin-1 were associated with transcriptional changes in the PFC and cerebellum, two brain regions critically implicated in ASD pathology, involving gene networks related to neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal excitability. These changes were accompanied by reductions in oxidative stress markers and pro-inflammatory mediators, together with restoration of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10. In addition, no obvious systemic toxicity was observed under the present experimental conditions, as indicated by the absence of consistent treatment-related abnormalities in serum biochemical parameters and histological features of major peripheral organs. Collectively, these findings suggest that improvements in ASD-related phenotypes were accompanied by changes in oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and transcriptional regulation.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies of saffron- and crocin-based interventions in prenatal VPA models [59, 60]. Seyedinia et al. reported that postnatal treatment with saffron extract or crocin ameliorated autistic-like behaviors and improved brain oxidative stress, as evidenced by reduced MDA and increased GSH and CAT levels in male offspring rats exposed prenatally to VPA [60]. Consistent with this report, we also observed marked restoration of antioxidant defenses in VPA-exposed mice. However, our study further extends these observations by showing that RNSP and crocin-1 not only improved redox homeostasis, but also were associated with improvements in ASD-like behaviors, attenuation of neuroinflammation in the PFC and cerebellum, and coordinated transcriptional reorganization across cortico-cerebellar regions. These results suggest that the effects of crocin-related interventions may extend beyond antioxidant regulation and may also involve changes in neuroinflammation and transcriptional regulation.

More recently, Hosseini et al. showed that crocin dose-dependently reversed behavioral alterations in a prenatal VPA rat model and normalized BDNF and GSK-3beta expression in the medial prefrontal cortex [59]. Although we did not directly assess BDNF or GSK-3beta in the present study, this work provides important mechanistic context, suggesting that crocin may act not only through antioxidative pathways but also through neurotrophic and synaptic plasticity-related signaling. In line with this possibility, our data further demonstrate concurrent alterations in oxidative stress, inflammatory homeostasis, and transcriptional regulation in both the PFC and cerebellum, thereby extending the mechanistic implications of crocin-related intervention in ASD-like conditions.

Notably, crocin-1 did not exhibit a strictly monotonic dose–response relationship across all endpoints in the present study. Although clearer dose-related trends were observed for several oxidative stress indices and selected inflammatory markers, many behavioral and molecular readouts showed non-linear or partially overlapping responses across doses. This pattern may reflect threshold or partial-saturation effects in vivo, such that once an effective biological exposure is reached, further dose escalation does not necessarily yield proportionally greater benefit. In addition, complex behavioral phenotypes and cytokine-related endpoints are shaped by multiple interacting mechanisms, regional sensitivities, and potential ceiling effects, which may obscure a simple linear dose–response relationship. Therefore, the effects of crocin-1 in the present study should be interpreted as endpoint-dependent rather than uniformly dose dependent across all measures.

In addition, Jalili et al. demonstrated that crocin-loaded nanoparticles exerted stronger neuroprotective effects than free crocin-1 in a PTZ-induced seizure model, highlighting the importance of formulation strategy in determining crocin efficacy [61]. Although this study was not performed in an ASD model, it is relevant to our findings because RNSP, as a multicomponent Tibetan formulation, was associated with broader and more consistent effects across behavioral, inflammatory, and transcriptomic outcomes compared with crocin-1 alone. Previous studies have also shown that RNSP exerts neuroprotective effects in other neuropsychiatric models [27, 28]. Extending these observations, our study focused on the PFC and cerebellum and found that RNSP not only attenuated oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, but also was associated with modulation of ASD-related transcriptional pathways in these regions. Together, these findings suggest that the superior efficacy of RNSP may be related to formulation-dependent pharmacological advantages and synergistic interactions among multiple active components.

The multicomponent and multitarget nature of the RNSP distinguishes it from conventional single-compound approaches [25]. Its complex phytochemical composition enables simultaneous modulation of oxidative stress, immune regulation, and neuroplasticity—mechanisms highly relevant to the multifactorial etiology of ASD [62]. These pleiotropic properties may be particularly advantageous in ASD, where diverse biological systems converge to produce heterogeneous clinical outcomes. While crocin-1 showed significant efficacy, it did not fully replicate the effects of RNSP, suggesting that the therapeutic potential of RNSP may arise from synergistic interactions among its multiple active constituents. At the same time, this compositional complexity also makes safety evaluation particularly important. In the present study, no obvious systemic toxicity was detected, supporting the short-term tolerability of RNSP under the current experimental conditions.

Several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, the prenatal VPA exposure model captures many ASD-related features but does not fully reflect the genetic heterogeneity of ASD. Validation of RNSP and crocin-1 in genetic models such as Shank3, CNTNAP2, or Fmr1 knockout mice will be necessary to assess their broader applicability [63]. Second, our behavioral analysis focused primarily on stereotypy and social interaction, while future studies should investigate additional ASD-relevant domains, such as cognition, sensory processing, sleep, and attention [64–68]. Third, although the behavioral improvements were accompanied by changes in oxidative stress, inflammatory markers, and transcriptomic profiles, these data are correlative and do not establish causality. Future studies using functional validation strategies, such as gain-/loss-of-function approaches or pharmacological blocking experiments, will be required to determine whether these pathways are necessary for the therapeutic effects of RNSP and crocin-1. Finally, further pharmacological dissection of the active components of RNSP and their interactions will be essential to better understand their mechanisms of action and advance their therapeutic potential.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that RNSP and crocin-1 ameliorate ASD-like phenotypes and are associated with changes in oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and transcriptional regulation in both the central and peripheral systems. These coordinated molecular and behavioral changes suggest that RNSP represents a potential multitarget intervention associated with improvement of ASD-related abnormalities. These findings not only expand our understanding of the neuroprotective properties of RNSP but also highlight its potential as a candidate intervention for ASD, while warranting further mechanistic validation.

Conclusion

RNSP and crocin-1 alleviated autism-like behaviors in VPA-exposed mice and were associated with multilevel changes in oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and transcriptional networks. These changes were observed across both central (PFC and cerebellum) and peripheral (serum) systems, suggesting a systemic pattern of response. Moreover, no obvious systemic toxicity was observed under the present experimental conditions. Compared with crocin-1, RNSP showed broader effects across the measured endpoints, supporting its potential relevance as a multitarget intervention in ASD-related conditions.
