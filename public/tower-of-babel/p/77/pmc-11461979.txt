Introduction

Rectovaginal fistulas (RVFs) are abnormal epithelial-lined tracts that connect the rectum to the vagina [1]. They can cause social isolation and significant physical and emotional distress [2].

RVFs can occur from a variety of causes, but the most common etiology for RVF of traumatic origin is obstetric injury, especially in resource-limited countries. Other etiologies include pelvic irradiation, Crohn's disease, neoplasms, and infections [3].

Fistulas, once common worldwide, have been eradicated in regions like Europe and North America due to improved obstetric care, whereas studies indicate that in West Africa, the incidence rate remains approximately 1 to 3 per 1000 deliveries [4]. It is noteworthy that over 90 % of rectovaginal fistulas in resource-limited settings are attributable to obstetric trauma [5].

Although uncommon, rectal tuberculosis can result in rectovaginal fistula formation, presenting a diagnostic challenge. Due to its rarity, there are only a few sporadic case reports in the literature [[6], [7], [8]]. A retrospective review of rare genital fistula cases across nine African countries, involving 6787 women, found no instances of fistulas caused by tuberculosis, further underscoring the rarity of this condition [9] There is no established direct link between tuberculosis and rectovaginal fistula. However, it is thought that an inflammatory process, characterized by the development of edema in the affected area, may be involved. This edema can cause the tissue to adhere to adjacent viscera, eventually leading to perforation [10]. This case report presents a unique presentation of a spontaneous RVF in a young woman with undiagnosed extra pulmonary TB (rectal TB). This case highlights the atypical manifestation of tuberculosis and emphasizes the importance of considering it in the causes of spontaneous RVF, especially in seemingly low-risk populations. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [11].

Case presentation

We report the case of a 25-year-old college girl who presented with a 3-month history of nonspecific lower abdominal pain, general body weakness, and passage of stools per vagina for the last month. Two days prior to presentation at the hospital, she noticed a building mass on the vagina as she was straining on the toilet, which subsequently bursted, releasing brownish yellow purulent fluid followed by fecal matter, with no history of instrumentation, radiation or prior anorectal surgical intervention. She denied a history of fever or cough and no change in bowel habits or significant weight loss or close contact with an active TB patient.

Attained menarche at 13 years of age, regular menstrual cycle. At the time of presentation at our hospital, she had undergone a failed repair attempt at another hospital, but the extent of the fistula increased. On examination, she was Alert, afebrile, moderately pale, well-appearing, with no peripheral palpable lymph nodes and normal vital signs. The abdominal examination was unremarkable, and the abdomen had a normal contour and was soft and nontender, with no palpable mass.

On rectovaginal examination, an obvious single rectal vaginal fistula of 2 × 1.5 cm was identified in the mid-vaginal region with surrounding granulation tissue. The margins are rigid and fixed.

Spontaneous discharge of the fecal material was apparent (Fig. 1). The patient had a normal anal sphincter tone, and no other abnormalities in the rectal mucosa were noted.

Appearance of the fistula before surgery.

The rest of the systemic examination was unremarkable. The investigations yielded a haemoglobin level of 8.3 g/dl. The WBC count was 6.2 × 10 9/L, with a differential neutrophil count of 52 %, lymphocyte count of 32 %, and eosinophil count of 16 %. The retroviral screening for HIV-1 and HIV-2 was negative, and the chest X-ray showed no abnormalities.. The patient received parenteral hematenics to optimize her preoperative haemoglobin level She then underwent examination under anaesthesia where a biopsy of the surrounding mucosa was taken.

Considering the patient is nulliparous and has no prior history of pelvic surgery or radiation, we were able to exclude common obstetric causes, as well as trauma or radiation. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) could have been a differential diagnosis; however, the absence of systemic symptoms such as diarrhea, weight loss, or changes in bowel habits lowered the likelihood of this condition This shifted our focus more toward an infectious etiology. Given the endemicity of tuberculosis in the region, and after excluding HIV, tuberculosis became a strong consideration in the diagnostic process.

Histopathological examination of the tissue revealed chronic granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis, consistent with tuberculosis. The lining epithelium showed stratified squamous hyperplasia with prominent granulation tissue and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate composed of neutrophils, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and eosinophils. Necrotizing granulomas and Langhans-type giant cells were also identified. No dysplasia or malignancy was observed. Special stains for alcohol acid-fast bacilli (AFB) demonstrated the presence of these organisms, confirming the diagnosis of tuberculosis.

Antitubercular drugs were used, and she underwent a 6-month course of antituberculosis treatment. The initial intensive phase included the administration of a combination of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for 2 months. This was followed by a 4-month continuation phase using only isoniazid and rifampicin, during which time significant improvement in the size of the fistula was noted.

She was then underwent a surgical repair after bowel preparation, During repair, diluted adrenaline solution was first injected into the rectovaginal septum to facilitate dissection and hemostasis. An incision was then made superficially in the midline of the posterior vaginal epithelium, and the posterior vaginal mucosa was mobilized. The rectovaginal septum was carefully dissected to expose the fistula tract. Fistula edges were trimmed the rectal wall was repaired transversely using interrupted 3–0 polyglactin sutures in a 2 layer techniques. Finally, the vaginal incision was closed in the midline using polyglactin 3–0 sutures, employing a horizontal mattress suture. (Fig. 2).

Appearance of the fistula After surgery.

Post-operatively, sitz baths and analgesics were instituted. On post-operative day 7, she had a normal diet, experienced normal bowel movements, and was discharged home. She was advised to refrain from heavy lifting and sexual intercourse for three months. At the one-month follow-up, she remained well with no signs of gapping.

Discussion

Rectal tuberculosis is a rare clinical entity with various epidemiological characteristics. The first case of rectal tuberculosis was reported in 1957 by Davis et al. [12]. A systematic review in 2022 showed that only 28 cases have been reported since then [13].It is more common in females than in males. The ileocecal junction has the highest incidence of gastrointestinal tuberculosis. This incidence progressively declines as one moves distally toward the rectum, making rectal tuberculosis a relatively uncommon presentation. The incidence of rectal TB among all gastrointestinal TB patients ranges from 2 to 4.5 % [[9], [10], [11]].

In contrast to the index cases that occurred in the 2nd decade of life, the majority of reported cases occurred in the third and fourth decades of life. The major risk factors for developing rectal tuberculosis often result from impaired cellular immunity, usually from immunodeficiency syndrome, or complement deficiency. Patients with leukemia or who are receiving anticancer therapy are also at increased risk [9,12].

It is usually secondary to pulmonary tuberculosis, although it may rarely occur from other primary lesions elsewhere. Primary rectal tuberculosis is characterized by an absence of demonstrable tuberculosis in other locations and is an uncommon presentation of the disease [17].The most common identified route of spread is through hematogenous spread; other potential routes include through perirectal lymphatics or direct spread from a nearby organ such as the bladder. Within the rectum, the lower third demonstrated the highest prevalence of tuberculous involvement, followed by the middle third. The upper third is least affected, and diffuse rectal tuberculosis has not been documented [9,14].

The pathological process resulting in fistula formation is not well understood, but it is thought to be initiated by the edematous process of the affected part. This pathological process results in adherence to the adjacent viscera, causing it to perforate.Another pathological process that may account for fistula formation is the presence of anorectal or perirectal abscess that may rupture, facilitating communication [19].

The clinical presentations of rectal TB vary, but it has been reported that in up to 75 % of cases, constitutional features of TB are observed, hematochezia is the most common presenting symptom when there is ulcer formation, patients may present with fever, changes in bowel habits and lower abdominal pain, and constipation may also be noted when there is stricture formation [9,16]. Pathologically, rectal TB can present as hypertrophic lesions, usually due to primary infection or ulcerative lesions, as in the index case [9,17].

Diagnosing rectal Tb and differentiating TB-induced RVF from other causes of RVF is usually challenging and causes delayed diagnosis because of its varied clinical presentation and because it shares some presentation with other rectal pathologies, such as rectal malignancy, as was the situation in the index case where the patient had a 3-month history of persistent lower abdominal pain and one failed attempt at repair; therefore, clinicians need to have an index of suspicion when encountering such patients without clear risk factors, such as obstetric injury with recto-vaginal fistula.

Definitive diagnosis requires the identification of acid-fast bacilli with characteristic Langhans giant cells in the tubercles associated with central necro-sis. According to histopathological examination [22].

Other imaging modalities that are useful for demonstrating the extent of TB involvement or detecting TB foci include chest X-ray, conventional or endorectal ultrasound, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Investigations can also help to rule out differential diagnoses, such as rectal adenocarcinoma or Crohn's disease [19,20].

Treatment of rectal tuberculosis mainly involves timely initiation of antituberculosis medication, and appropriate surgical intervention whenever necessary. Even without a prior TB history, an isolated tuberculous lesion, such as a rectal TB, qualifies as a new case of extrapulmonary tuberculosis and can be treated with a standard short course antituberculosis regimen for 6 months [25].

The presence of complications such as rectovaginal fistula, as in the index patient, rectal prolapse, or rectal abscess rupture necessitated surgical intervention [26],Generally, two types of surgical repair are performed in relation to the physical location of the fistula and the involvement of the control mechanism. Transverse closure of the rectum +/− disruption of the rectal stricture in the case of proximal fistulas not involving the continence/closing mechanism, as in the index case Longitudinal closure of the anorectum with meticulous reconstruction of the structures involved in distal fistulas involving the continence/closing mechanism [27].

In comparing the current case with two similar cases in the literature, it is noted that, unlike our case, one case involved a 14-year-old female who developed a fistula and was successfully treated with antitubercular therapy without the need for surgery [7]. In contrast, the other case required more invasive surgical interventions, including a colostomy and anorectoplasty, due to extensive strictures [6]. This highlights the varying severity of tuberculosis manifestations.

Post operative care of the patient usually involves a healthy diet typically to prevent constipation and straining to move hard stool past the repair. Laxatives may also be taken into consideration for the first three months. The aim is to have a pliable, molded, and soft stool to prevent repair breakdown, patients are also advised to refrain from heavy lifting and labor for three months, and to resume sexual intercourse after 3–6 months to prevent repair breakdown. Pregnancy should ideally be avoided for a year. Oral antibiotics may be given post operatively [28]. Overall, this case report is a valuable contribution to the medical literature by highlighting a rare cause of rectovaginal fistula, however there might be limitations in the generalizability of the findings considering the endemicity of tuberculosis in the region.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering tuberculosis as a potential cause of non-healing rectovaginal fistulas, especially in patients from TB-endemic regions or those without apparent risk factors. Good public health measures, such as awareness campaigns, should also be given priority to control TB in vulnerable populations Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and consider early diagnostic measures, including biopsy when managing persistent fistulas. Future research should focus on the role of preventative strategies in endemic areas to help reduce the incidence of such complex presentations.

Patient perspective

I was okay and doing well except for the abdominal pain which I didn't consider much, I was shocked the day I was told I had a fistula as a young girl it was hard for me to accept the situation, and my whole life changed, my ability to concentrate in class decreased, I had to change now and then, i had hopes after doing the first surgery only to find that it had failed. I was discouraged, in the search for further care I had to face another challenge that i had TB, I'm young and healthy, and I never thought I'd have TB, especially not somewhere so unusual, I couldn't even figure out where i got it,. And had to endure another 6 months of daily drugs, and it wasn't always pleasant. But I was determined to get better but thanks to the team after the drug i felt great improvement and eventually, they were able to do surgery to repair it completely. It was a relief to finally be free of that constant discomfort. And now after the surgery, i feel confident, l again, my biggest trouble has gone and now I can live like others, looking back to the experience it was scary and frustrating, now i know TB can present itself in unexpected ways, and even young people like me can be at risk people need to look for a proper treatment.
