Investigating the potential causal link between BPA and ovarian carcinogenesis: a network toxicology and mendelian randomization study on the CTRC/PRDX1/SKP1 pathway
Authors: Zunlin Shi, Zhi Li, Fan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study elucidates the association between Bisphenol A (BPA) exposure and ovarian cancer development, along with its underlying mechanisms. We employed a ‘Toxicological Prediction-Causal Inference-Structural Validation’ framework, integrating network toxicology and Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis, enabling insights into BPA’s carcinogenic effects. This approach used network toxicology to predict molecular targets of BPA in the ovary, MR to establish genetic evidence for the causal link, and molecular docking to validate structural basis of interaction with key pathways. Our findings indicate BPA may influence cellular processes, including homeostasis, stress, and cycle regulation, while activating PI3K/Akt signaling. Enrichment analysis revealed pathways associated with amoebic infection, suggesting interactions between infection status and chemical exposure. With evidence that emetine enhances cancer cell sensitivity, we hypothesize BPA may activate inflammation pathways shared with amoebic infection, particularly in predisposed individuals. Consequently, BPA might induce inflammatory microenvironment, thereby influencing progression and treatment response. This study identifies CTRC/PRDX1/SKP1 as crucial and proposes hypothesis of synergistic effects between exposure and infection history. These findings underscore incorporating environmental exposure into risk assessment, offering perspectives on carcinogenesis and avenues for prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01814-z.Keywords: Bisphenol a (BPA), Ovarian cancer, Carcinogenesis, Network toxicology, Mendelian randomization (MR), Mechanism, CTRC/PRDX1/SKP1 pathway, Amoebic infection, Environmental exposure, Risk assessment

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a significant threat to women’s health worldwide, with the highest mortality rate among gynecological malignancies [1–2]. According to statistics, approximately 207,000 women died from ovarian cancer globally in 2022 [3–4]. Due to the subtlety of early symptoms, over 70% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a five-year survival rate of less than 40% and a very poor prognosis [5]. In the face of such a severe public health challenge, there is an urgent need for innovative methodologies to decipher the modifiable risk mechanisms, providing new ideas for prevention, early diagnosis, and treatment.

However, current research on the pathogenesis of ovarian cancer is mired in a “fragmentation dilemma.” On one hand, while women carrying BRCA1/2 gene mutations have a significantly increased lifetime risk of ovarian cancer (BRCA1: 15–45%; BRCA2: 10–40%), existing genetic studies have not effectively integrated the modifying effects of environmental exposure factors on genetic risk [6–7]. On the other hand, numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated a statistical association between BPA exposure and ovarian cancer risk. For instance, a case-control study revealed that women with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) or epithelial borderline ovarian tumors (EBOT) had significantly higher urinary BPA concentrations than healthy controls. After adjusting for age, body mass index, and pregnancy history using multiple linear regression analysis, BPA levels showed a significant positive correlation with urinary creatinine (p = 0.007), suggesting a potential link to renal impairment [8]. Furthermore, the study found that ovarian cancer patients more frequently used plastic containers for food and beverage storage, and certain lifestyle habits—such as reusing plastic bottles—were associated with elevated urinary BPA levels. Nevertheless, while these observational studies provide important clues, the causal chain between exposure and disease has not been fully established, and further research is needed to validate the underlying biological mechanisms and potential causality.

Although cellular studies have elucidated potential mechanisms of BPA action—such as promoting ovarian cancer cell proliferation and migration by mimicking estrogen and activating estrogen receptors α and β, and modulating oncogenic signaling pathways including STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK [9]—these findings are primarily derived from in vitro experiments or animal models. Extrapolating these cellular mechanisms to population-level risk and establishing causality thus require more robust evidence [10–11]. Meanwhile, while infectious disease research has suggested associations between pathogens such as Chlamydia trachomatis and ovarian cancer, the evidence remains inconclusive and lacks consensus. More importantly, these findings have not been linked to host responses triggered by chemical toxic exposures, which remains a major challenge in the field [12]. This fragmentation in research perspectives has led to a critical break in the “environmental exposure–genomic perturbation–pathogen response” cascade, hindering a comprehensive understanding of the complex etiology of ovarian cancer and limiting the development of intervention strategies. Furthermore, the complexity of BPA’s biological effects—shaped by factors such as exposure route, dosage, and duration—adds another layer of difficulty in establishing direct causal links in human populations.

In this context, this study introduces an innovative research paradigm. Notably, our previous work in the field of toxicology established a three-dimensional research framework of “toxicological prediction-causal inference-structural validation,” which has demonstrated its exceptional ability to analyze environmental carcinogens in top toxicology journals [13]. However, this framework has not yet been applied to the field of gynecological tumors. This study pioneeringly “transfers” the framework from toxicology to ovarian cancer research, achieving a triple leap: first, using network toxicology for “toxicological prediction” to screen for molecular switches that BPA may specifically target in the ovaries from vast data; second, employing Mendelian randomization (MR) design for “causal inference” to isolate confounding factors, establish a genetic evidence chain, and validate the causal relationship between BPA and ovarian cancer; third, conducting “structural validation” through molecular docking techniques to lock in the spatial conformation of BPA activating carcinogenic pathways, providing molecular-level anchoring for mechanisms. This interdisciplinary methodological transfer offers a new perspective for exploring the relationship between environmental factors and complex diseases.

This work is the first to practice this full-chain analytical strategy in ovarian cancer: we first utilized network toxicology methods, combined with databases such as ChEMBL/STITCH/CTD, to screen potential ovarian cancer targets of BPA; subsequently, we focused on ovarian cancer-related targets through the CTD/GeneCards/OMIM databases and identified suitable genetic variations using GTEx blood eQTL data; next, we applied MR methods for rigorous causal inference; finally, we validated the interaction between BPA and core targets through molecular docking using software such as AutoDock.

This innovative approach has led to a key breakthrough: we have revealed a “novel inflammatory microenvironment remodeling axis” in the field of gynecological tumors, a unique mechanism not reported in previous toxicological studies. Specifically, we found that BPA may promote the occurrence of ovarian cancer through two key axes: first, the “pathway abnormal activation axis,” where BPA, as an environmental stressor, may directly or indirectly abnormally activate the core signaling pathway PI3K/Akt, simulating or exacerbating the pro-cancer effects caused by genetic mutations (such as PIK3CA mutations), driving cell proliferation and survival; second, the “amoeba-like infection inflammatory axis,” where BPA exposure may simultaneously reshape the local ovarian microenvironment by activating inflammatory pathways shared with amoeba infections (such as TLR4-NF-κB, IL-6/STAT3). This “amoeba-like infection” inflammatory state induced by environmental exposure not only provides favorable conditions for the sustained abnormal activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway but also directly promotes the occurrence and development of carcinogenesis, potentially interfering with the sensitivity of ovarian cancer to treatment. These two axes are interrelated and work together, significantly increasing the risk of ovarian cancer incidence, progression potential, and treatment challenges.

Materials and methods

This study employs an innovative three-phase research design based on the “toxicological prediction-causal inference-structural validation” framework, aiming to elucidate the association between the environmental chemical BPA and ovarian cancer (refer to Fig. 1). First, potential ovarian cancer targets of BPA are screened using network toxicology combined with multiple databases; second, through Mendelian randomization design, the focus is on ovarian cancer-related targets, and strict causal inference is conducted using genetic variations to validate the causal relationship between BPA and ovarian cancer; finally, molecular docking technology is used to verify the interaction between BPA and core targets, anchoring its spatial conformation that activates carcinogenic pathways at the molecular level. This comprehensive analytical strategy achieves interdisciplinary methodological transfer, providing a new perspective for exploring the relationship between environmental factors and complex diseases.

Network toxicology screening of potential targets of BPA in ovarian cancer

We implemented a multi-stage integrative screening strategy to systematically identify potential targets linking BPA to ovarian cancer, forming the basis for subsequent causal inference (Table 1). This approach aimed to maximize the use of public data while ensuring the biological relevance of the final target set. Initially, to build a comprehensive library of potential BPA targets, we employed a union-based strategy that aggregated predictions from multiple databases (ProTox 3.0, ADMETlab 2.0, ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction) [14–21]. This method, which combines data from algorithms with different underlying principles (e.g., structure similarity, literature mining), prevents the information bias associated with using a strict intersection and ensures a broader capture of potential interactions.

In parallel, a comprehensive ovarian cancer disease gene profile was constructed by taking the union of genes from GeneCards, OMIM, and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) [22–28]. The intersection of the BPA target library and the ovarian cancer gene profile was then calculated to identify shared targets, which were regarded as key molecular mediators. To genetically validate these core targets for causal analysis, we filtered them for the availability of genetic instrumental variables by integrating cis-eQTL data from the GTEx v8 and eQTLGen databases [29–30]. This final step provided a high-confidence set of candidate genes for Mendelian randomization.

Mendelian randomization reveals the impact of BPA targets on ovarian cancer

This study employs the Mendelian Randomization (MR) method, combined with network toxicology strategies, to explore the potential causal associations between genetic factors of target genes and the risk of ovarian cancer. We integrated ovarian cancer GWAS data from NHGRI-EBI GWAS, FinnGen R10, and the UK Biobank (accessed through Neale Lab), covering over 700,000 individuals, ensuring the broadness and representativeness of the sample [31–33]. These databases provide rich information on genetic variations, laying a solid foundation for this study. In terms of data processing, we used METAL software for fixed-effect inverse variance weighted meta-analysis to integrate results from different databases and excluded variants with a minimum allele frequency (MAF) of less than 0.5%, ultimately obtaining meta-GWAS ovarian cancer data as the outcome for Mendelian Randomization analysis [34]. Based on preliminary eQTL analysis, we strictly selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with p-values less than 5 × 10^-8, meeting the linkage disequilibrium condition (r^2 < 0.01) and with adequate instrument strength (F-statistic >10) as valid instrumental variables [35–36]. We calculated the overall effect size using a weighted Z-score method and constructed a genetic risk score (GRS) model to quantify the cumulative impact of genetic variations on ovarian cancer risk. Additionally, we conducted linkage disequilibrium analysis, instrument strength tests, and a series of sensitivity analyses (including horizontal pleiotropy, heterogeneity tests, and leave-one-out analysis) to ensure the robustness and reliability of the study results. The main analytical strategy employs the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method for causal effect estimation, supplemented by MR-Egger regression and weighted median methods to verify the robustness of the results [35–36]. To ensure the reliability of the analysis results, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out tests, MR-PRESSO outlier removal, and pleiotropy assessment. Additionally, the causal direction was clarified through the Steiger directional test, effectively overcoming the confounding bias issues in observational studies. The strength assessment of genetic instrumental variables was conducted using the F-statistic, calculated as F = R^2(n − 2)/(1 - R^2), where n represents the effective sample size of the genome-wide association study (GWAS) associated with single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We comprehensively evaluated the results using MR-PRESSO and the inverse variance weighted method, and detected heterogeneity through the Cochran Q test. Pleiotropy assessment was achieved through the observed residual sum of squares from the MR pleiotropy residuals and outliers (MR-PRESSO) method and the intercept in the MR-Egger test. The effective application of this research methodology not only provides new insights into the genetic mechanisms of ovarian cancer but also offers new methodological support for the application of network toxicology in cancer research.

Pathway and functional enrichment analysis of causal target genes

In this study, to further explore the molecular mechanisms of ovarian cancer, we performed KEGG pathway and GO functional enrichment analyses on the causal target genes identified through Mendelian randomization methods [37–38]. First, the target genes were mapped and annotated with known pathways in the KEGG database and biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC) in the GO database. Subsequently, the significance P-values for the enrichment of target genes in each pathway or GO term were calculated using hypergeometric tests or Fisher’s exact tests, and the P-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Finally, using visualization methods such as bubble charts, bar charts, and GO functional classification diagrams, we intuitively presented the relevant information and functional classifications of significantly enriched pathways and GO terms. These analytical results help elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms of causal target genes in the occurrence and development of ovarian cancer, providing important theoretical support for the further development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies.

Molecular Docking validation of key targets

To further investigate the potential interactions between BPA and the key targets identified through Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, we employed molecular docking techniques for structural-level validation. First, we retrieved the official gene names and protein sequences for the eight MR-validated targets from the UniProt database [39]. Subsequently, we screened the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) for suitable three-dimensional (3D) crystal structures of these proteins for molecular docking [40–41]. The selection criteria prioritized: (1) protein structures derived from Homo sapiens; (2) high resolution (< 2.5 Å); and (3) structural completeness, including key functional domains. For targets where human-derived structures were unavailable in the PDB—specifically LAMB2 and LAMC1—we utilized the crystal structures of orthologous proteins from other species as surrogate models. Specifically, the structure for LAMB2 was sourced from Rattus norvegicus (PDB ID: 5LF2), and the structure for LAMC1 was sourced from Mus musculus (PDB ID: 1TLE). To ensure the scientific validity of this surrogate strategy, we performed a homology analysis using the Orthologs tool in the UniProt database. The analysis confirmed that these surrogate proteins exhibit high conservation in their key functional domains compared to their human counterparts, with a perfect domain match of 9/9 for both LAMB2 and LAMC1. This high degree of sequence conservation supports their structural and functional similarity, thereby providing a sound rationale for their use in molecular docking simulations. The PDB IDs, source species, and details of the homology analysis for all target proteins are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Prior to molecular docking, we preprocessed the protein receptors using PyMOL (v2.5), which included the removal of water molecules and original ligands, followed by the addition of polar hydrogen atoms. The 3D structure of the ligand molecule, Bisphenol A (PubChem CID: 6623), was energy-minimized using ChemBio3D Ultra (v14.0). Molecular docking was performed using the CB-Dock2 online platform, a tool that automatically identifies protein binding pockets and generates ligand-protein complex conformations [42–43]. Its docking workflow integrates the template-based FitDock method with the classic AutoDock Vina (v1.2.0) algorithm to ensure the accuracy of the results [44–48]. Upon completion of the docking, we selected the complex model with the lowest binding energy and the most rational conformation for further analysis. Visualization of the complex and analysis of the intermolecular interactions were performed using PyMOL (v2.5) and LigPlot+ (v2.2). The protein and ligand structures were retrieved from the PDB and UniProt databases, respectively, with an access date of April 8, 2025.

Integrated validation using Single-Cell and genomic data

To elucidate the cellular function and expression specificity of our key target gene, we devised a dual-pronged approach integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with cancer genomics. A significant challenge was the scarcity of direct single-cell data for this target (e.g., CTRC) in ovarian cancer, a research area that remains largely unexplored. To overcome this, we initiated an exploratory analysis grounded in biological homology. We leveraged the PanglaoDB database (https://panglaodb.se/) to examine the single-cell expression landscape of the gene in pancreatic tissue [49–51]. The pancreas was selected due to its shared exocrine functions and potential molecular parallels with ovarian cancer. This initial investigation served to map the gene’s expression to specific cellular subtypes, offering the first insights into its potential role within a complex tissue milieu and establishing a foundational hypothesis for our subsequent ovarian cancer-focused studies.

Subsequently, to directly assess the clinical relevance of this target in ovarian cancer, we conducted a comprehensive genomic analysis across large-scale patient cohorts. Utilizing the cBioPortal platform [52–54], we systematically interrogated the genomic alterations of the CTRC gene in several authoritative ovarian cancer datasets, namely TCGA-OV, CPTAC, and MSK (data accessed: August 28, 2025). Our analysis encompassed mutation frequency, copy number variations (CNVs), and structural variations (SVs), yielding a comprehensive genomic alteration profile of CTRC in ovarian cancer patients. This profile furnishes direct, population-level evidence crucial for evaluating the gene’s potential as either a clinical biomarker or a therapeutic target.

Data processing and analysis

Our analytical pipeline began with the acquisition of toxicological and pharmacokinetic profiles for BPA and its derivatives, which were obtained from ProTox 3.0 and ADMETlab 2.0. We then proceeded to identify potential targets by leveraging ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction. In parallel, we compiled a list of ovarian cancer-associated targets from GeneCards, OMIM, and CTD, enriching this dataset with transcriptomic data from GTEx v8 and genetic variants from dbSNP to provide a more comprehensive context for our analysis (Table 1). The core statistical analysis and causal inference were conducted in R (v4.2.3) with the TwoSampleMR package (v0.6.8). All results were visualized using GDP, ensuring clarity and precision in our final figures [55].

Results

PROTox and ADMEtlab - Chemical toxicity prediction

The toxicity prediction of BPA through the PROTox website shows that its organ toxicity prediction results are all inactive, indicating that no significant respiratory toxicity, renal toxicity, neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity were detected under the current model. However, in terms of toxicity endpoints, the prediction result for BPA regarding ecotoxicity is active, suggesting that it may have adverse effects on environmental ecology. Additionally, in terms of toxicity signaling pathways, the prediction results for BPA concerning estrogen receptor α (ER) and estrogen receptor ligand-binding domain (ER-LBD) as well as mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) are active, indicating that BPA may exert effects through these pathways. Furthermore, in terms of metabolism, the prediction results for BPA regarding cytochrome CYP2C19 and CYP2C9 are also active, suggesting that it may be involved in related metabolic processes (see Table 3; Fig. 2). Moreover, the ADMEtlab website indicates that BPA has significant effects on eye irritation, eye corrosion, skin sensitization, and acute oral toxicity in rats. In terms of the tox21 signaling pathways, BPA shows activity for nuclear receptor estrogen receptor (NR-ER), nuclear receptor estrogen receptor ligand-binding domain (NR-ER-LBD), stress response antioxidant response element (SR-ARE), stress response heat shock element (SR-HSE), stress response mitochondrial membrane potential (SR-MMP), and stress response p53 (SR-p53) (Table 4). The retrieval date for the PROTox and ADMEtlab-Prediction databases is January 13, 2025. Overall, although BPA is inactive in terms of organ toxicity, its active effects on specific signaling pathways and metabolic enzymes suggest a potential link between its toxicity analysis and the occurrence of ovarian cancer, warranting further research to clarify the mechanisms and risks. To this end, we have conducted network toxicology analysis to explore whether BPA increases the risk of ovarian cancer.

Network toxicology screening of potential targets of BPA and ovarian cancer

This study systematically predicted the potential targets of BPA and its derivatives using the ChEMBL database, STITCH database, and SwissTargetPrediction database (Fig. 3). Using the GeneCards database, OMIM database, and Comparative Toxicogenomics Database, we conducted a keyword search with the term “ovarian cancer” to collect relevant targets associated with ovarian cancer. By using the Venny website to intersect the predicted targets of BPA and its derivatives with the ovarian cancer-related targets, we identified 277 common targets. Furthermore, by combining the GTEx v8 whole blood transcriptome data (N = 670) and the genetic information provided by the eQTLGen Consortium, we identified that 57 of these targets have genetic variations (Supplementary Table 1). The retrieval date for the above databases was January 13, 2025. The identification of these genetic variation targets provides an important basis for further Mendelian randomization analysis, helping to explore the potential mechanisms of BPA-targeted genes in inducing ovarian cancer.

Pathway and functional enrichment analysis of causal target genes

Using R software to perform KEGG enrichment analysis on causal target genes, these genes are mainly enriched in pathways such as amoebiasis, small cell lung cancer, toxoplasmosis, human papillomavirus infection, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, cell adhesion, focal adhesion, and cobalamin transport and metabolism, among which amoebiasis is the most prominent (Fig. 5). We further categorized the enriched pathways into the following categories: environmental information processing, organismal systems, human diseases, and cellular processes. In the biological systems category, genes are enriched in cell adhesion, focal adhesion, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. In the environmental information processing category, they are enriched in amoebiasis, toxoplasmosis, and human papillomavirus infection. In the human diseases category, the pathways include small cell lung cancer, cell adhesion, focal adhesion, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Integrated analysis and external data exploration: preliminary validation of functional heterogeneity in key targets

This study integrates network toxicology and MR analysis, identifying 8 potential causal genes associated with the risk of ovarian cancer following BPA exposure. This not only provides evidence for environmental-genetic interactions in pathogenesis but also links the two new genes, CTRC and ACOT1, with BPA and ovarian cancer risk for the first time, introducing potential new targets in this field [56–67]. To gain a higher-resolution understanding of the potential cellular functions of these novel targets, we implemented a phased bioinformatic validation approach. Acknowledging the nascent stage of CTRC research in ovarian cancer and the consequent scarcity of directly relevant single-cell data, our initial step was an exploratory analysis. This was performed using the PanglaoDB scRNA-seq database in the pancreas—a tissue chosen for its biological homology—with the objective of uncovering the fundamental cellular functional properties of CTRC.

Focusing on the key target CTRC, our analysis uncovered a striking cell type-specific expression signature (Table 8; Fig. 7). Within the pancreas, CTRC expression was predominantly localized to acinar cells (Cluster IDs 4 and 13; activity scores = 190.114 and 241.617, p < 0.001), with significant expression also detected in diverse endocrine cell types (e.g., Alpha, Beta, Delta, and Gamma cells; adjusted p-value < 0.05). (PanglaoDB database accessed on June 23, 2025). This pronounced enrichment within defined functional cell subpopulations strongly indicates that CTRC’s function is exquisitely dependent on both the cellular context and the surrounding microenvironment. While this observation originates from pancreatic tissue, it offers a pivotal insight into CTRC’s potential role in ovarian cancer. It posits that CTRC’s effect in ovarian cancer is unlikely to be driven by uniform tumor cell expression, but is instead mediated by specific, as-yet-uncharacterized cell subpopulations within the tumor microenvironment (TME), such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) or distinct immune cell subsets. This hypothesis provides a clear and focused direction for future in vitro and in vivo investigations.

Building on this foundational hypothesis of functional heterogeneity, we next sought direct clinical validation by interrogating the genomic landscape of CTRC in ovarian cancer. Leveraging the cBioPortal platform, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of its genomic alterations across multiple authoritative cohorts, including TCGA-OV, CPTAC, and MSK (data accessed on August 28, 2025). Despite this modest mutation frequency, these data robustly confirm that CTRC is not a silent gene in ovarian cancer but is subject to genuine genomic alterations in a patient subset. This discovery provides direct, population-level genetic evidence supporting the potential of CTRC as a biomarker or therapeutic target, thereby closing the logical loop with our initial functional predictions and solidifying its clinical relevance.

Furthermore, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the genomic characteristics of the CTRC gene in ovarian cancer. Based on multi-center ovarian cancer data integrated from the cBioPortal platform (encompassing 3,276 samples / 2,654 patients), we discovered that the CTRC gene exhibits significant genomic alterations in ovarian cancer. Specifically, the mutation frequency of CTRC ranged between 1% and 4% (Fig. 8) and was consistently detected across multiple independent study cohorts (e.g., TCGA-OV, MSK). Although the overall mutation rate is not high, these data clearly indicate that CTRC is not a silent gene in ovarian cancer but rather undergoes driver or passenger alterations in a subset of patients. This finding provides direct genetic evidence for the potential role of CTRC in the pathogenesis of ovarian cancer.

Discussion

Methodological strengths and summary of key findings

This study represents the first integration of network toxicology and MR analysis in the field of ovarian cancer, establishing a comprehensive research strategy that spans from environmental exposure to molecular mechanisms and causal inference. The core strength of this approach lies in its cross-validation through multi-database screening, genetic variant-based causal inference, and molecular docking simulation, effectively overcoming the limitations of conventional bioinformatics methods in terms of data coverage, causal confirmation, and mechanistic elucidation, thereby significantly enhancing the reliability and systematic rigor of the conclusions.

Based on this strategy, we successfully identified and validated eight genes—CTRC, CTRL, LAMB2, SKP1, ACOT1, LAMC1, LAMC3, and PRDX1—as potential causal mediators in the association between BPA exposure and ovarian cancer risk, among which CTRC, PRDX1, and SKP1 were identified as core genes. These genes were significantly enriched in pathways including amoebiasis, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, small cell lung cancer, and HPV infection, supporting a hypothesis that infection-related mechanisms are involved in ovarian carcinogenesis under BPA exposure.

Notably, CTRC and ACOT1 have been rarely reported in ovarian cancer research prior to this study. We are the first to link these genes to BPA exposure and ovarian cancer development, providing novel potential targets for future investigation. Although molecular docking validation could not be completed for some genes due to the lack of protein structure data, the overall findings construct a systematic body of evidence supporting the role of environment-gene interactions in disease pathogenesis, laying a solid foundation for a deeper understanding of the multifactorial mechanisms underlying ovarian cancer.

Biological significance and mechanistic analysis of core genes

CTRC: from a pancreatic gene to a potential pathogenic factor in ovarian cancer

Our computational model identified chymotrypsin C (CTRC) as a key candidate target in ovarian cancer. Although CTRC is predominantly expressed in the pancreas under normal physiological conditions—with minimal expression in ovarian tissue—analysis of pancreatic single-cell data suggested its potential involvement in diverse cellular functions, implying that its biological role in cancer may differ significantly from that in normal tissues [50–51]. To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed ovarian cancer cohorts and found that somatic mutations in CTRC occur in 1% to 4% of patients. Supported by multi-level evidence—including molecular interactions, cellular phenotypes, and genomic alterations—CTRC was elevated from a “candidate gene” to a “potential pathogenic driver.” Beyond its role in protein hydrolysis within the digestive system, CTRC may contribute to tumor progression in the ovarian tumor microenvironment through gain-of-function or ectopic expression. Its aberrant activity may promote tumor invasion and metastasis by activating matrix-remodeling proteases or modulating signaling molecules and immune mediators [68–70]. This mechanism is analogous to that of other proteases, such as matrix metalloproteinases, which are typically lowly expressed in normal tissues but become key drivers under pathological conditions [71].

PRDX1 and SKP1: key regulators of oxidative stress and protein homeostasis

PRDX1 and SKP1 represent two additional core genes identified in this study. PRDX1, a crucial antioxidant enzyme, is responsible for scavenging intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining redox balance. Dysregulation of PRDX1 can lead to the accumulation of oxidative stress, thereby inducing DNA damage and malignant cellular transformation [72]. SKP1, as a core component of the SCF ubiquitin ligase complex, participates in the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of cell cycle regulators and transcription factors, playing an essential role in maintaining normal cell cycle progression and protein homeostasis [73–76]. Aberrations in SKP1 may result in uncontrolled cell cycle progression, enhanced anti-apoptotic signaling, and dysregulated stress responses.

Potential regulatory mechanisms of core gene pathways by BPA exposure

Given the well-documented endocrine-disrupting properties of BPA, we hypothesize that it may influence the function of the aforementioned genes through the following pathways: BPA may indirectly regulate the expression of CTRC, PRDX1, or SKP1 by interfering with estrogen signaling pathways [77–78]. It may also directly or indirectly activate or inhibit oxidative stress pathways or the ubiquitin-proteasome system, thereby affecting the function or activity of these genes [79–81]. For example, oxidative stress potentially linked to BPA exposure may compromise PRDX1’s antioxidant capacity, initiating a vicious cycle; alterations in protein folding or degradation may impair SKP1-mediated protein quality control; and changes in cell adhesion or signal transduction may indirectly affect CTRC activity. Collectively, these perturbations could contribute to the malignant transformation of ovarian cells via pathways such as PI3K/Akt signaling, cell adhesion molecules, or by mimicking infection-induced inflammatory responses.

Complementary insights and challenges to the current etiological Understanding of ovarian cancer

Leveraging the systematic and innovative advantages of the aforementioned methodology, this study not only provides novel molecular clues for understanding the association between BPA and ovarian cancer but also presents significant complementary insights and challenges to the traditional etiological framework of the disease. Conventional research has primarily focused on genetic susceptibility (e.g., BRCA mutations) and hormonal factors. In contrast, through rigorous causal inference and pathway analysis, our study reveals the potential contribution of interactions between environmental exposures (such as BPA) and specific genetic variants (e.g., CTRC, PRDX1, SKP1) to ovarian carcinogenesis, thereby breaking through the singular perspective of genetic determinism [6–7, 82–84].

Furthermore, this study is the first to hypothesize a potential role of “pathogen–host interactions” in ovarian cancer associated with BPA exposure. We unexpectedly found significant enrichment of the amoebiasis pathway among target genes, suggesting that BPA may mimic or activate inflammatory signaling pathways shared with amoebic infection—such as TLR4–NF-κB and IL-6/STAT3—to remodel the local ovarian microenvironment, thereby promoting tumorigenesis or influencing therapeutic sensitivity [85–88]. To support this hypothesis, we cite evidence that emetine, an anti-amoebic drug, significantly enhances the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin. This effect is mediated through the activation of caspase-3, -7, and − 8, and enhanced apoptosis via downregulation of bcl-xL [89]. This finding suggests that ovarian cancer cells with amoeba-related genetic backgrounds may possess specific anti-apoptotic mechanisms, which could explain the suboptimal response to standard therapy in certain ovarian cancers—particularly those arising in the context of BPA exposure.

These insights not only challenge the conventional understanding of BPA’s singular mode of action but also propose new directions for future research: systematic evaluation of the synergistic effects of environmental exposures and history of pathogen infection in ovarian cancer development and treatment response. By incorporating infection status, chronic inflammation, and environmental chemical exposures into ovarian cancer risk assessment frameworks, we aim to provide a more comprehensive theoretical foundation for personalized prevention and therapeutic strategies, thereby advancing the field of ovarian cancer etiology into a new era of multifactorial synergistic analysis.

Research limitations

Although this study constructs a systematic evidence chain—from environmental exposure through molecular pathways to genomic alterations—by integrating multidimensional data including network toxicology, Mendelian randomization, molecular docking, and cancer genomics, thereby significantly enhancing the logical rigor and scientific credibility of the research, several methodological and technical limitations remain and warrant further discussion.

First, as a computational biology study, its core limitation lies in its predictive nature. While network toxicology efficiently identified key genes such as CTRC and SKP1, as well as pathways including PI3K/Akt, oxidative stress, and amoebiasis, these results are subject to potential false positives and require experimental validation to confirm their biological relevance [90]. Notably, the association between the amoebiasis pathway and ovarian cancer remains unclear, presenting a challenging scientific hypothesis worthy of in-depth exploration.

Second, MR analysis is constrained by methodological limitations. Its causal inference relies on the instrumental variable (IV) assumption and cannot entirely rule out the effects of pleiotropy or horizontal pleiotropy [91]. Although multiple sensitivity analyses (e.g., MR-Egger, weighted median method) were employed to enhance robustness, issues such as IV strength, population specificity, and exposure timing windows may still affect the generalizability of the conclusions.

Third, in the molecular docking results, certain targets such as LAMB2 and LAMC1 lack human protein crystal structures, necessitating the use of homologous protein substitutes. These findings should be regarded as preliminary and require further validation through future resolution of human protein structures or more precise homology modeling.

Lastly, while molecular mechanisms suggest that BPA may influence ovarian cancer-related pathways by modulating targets like CTRC, experimental validation is currently lacking [92–93]. The pharmacokinetics, in vivo distribution, and dose-response relationships of BPA remain poorly characterized, limiting a comprehensive understanding of its biological mechanisms.

Future directions

This study proposes a novel hypothesis that BPA may promote ovarian cancer through specific mechanisms, using innovative computational approaches, and constructs an integrated “exposure-gene-pathway-disease” framework. The identified key genes (e.g., CTRC, PRDX1, and SKP1) and associated pathogen infection pathways provide research directions with clinical translational potential for the prevention and treatment of ovarian cancer. Given that these genes and their related signaling pathways (such as PI3K/Akt, oxidative stress, and protein degradation) may be associated with BPA exposure and ovarian cancer risk, they hold promise for development as early diagnostic biomarkers, therapeutic intervention targets, or tools for assessing individual susceptibility to environmental chemicals. For instance, analyzing the expression profiles or mutation status of these genes may help identify high-risk populations or predict disease progression [94].

Future research will build upon the foundation of this study. Our computational approach has identified potential directions for experimental validation. First, the roles of these key genes in mediating BPA-associated ovarian carcinogenesis should be validated through in vitro cellular experiments (e.g., gene knockout/knockdown and overexpression in BPA-exposed ovarian cell lines) and in vivo animal models (e.g., assessment of ovarian tumorigenesis in BPA-exposed animals with specific genetic modifications) [95]. This will facilitate the translation of findings from animal models to human studies. Second, larger-scale, diverse populations and updated genome-wide association study (GWAS) data should be utilized to replicate Mendelian randomization analyses, thereby confirming and consolidating current findings and exploring the joint effects of BPA exposure and genetic variants [96]. Furthermore, given the enrichment of pathogen infection-related pathways, further investigation into the potential interactions between specific pathogens (e.g., Acanthamoeba or related microorganisms) and ovarian cancer risk is warranted, particularly in the context of genetic susceptibility. Finally, attempts could be made to construct an ovarian cancer risk prediction model that integrates BPA exposure assessment with polygenic risk scores, laying the foundation for personalized prevention strategies [94, 97].

Conclusion

This study represents the first integration of network toxicology, Mendelian randomization analysis, and molecular docking to systematically elucidate the potential causal relationship between BPA exposure and ovarian cancer. Through multidimensional analysis, we identified key genes such as CTRC, PRDX1, and SKP1, which may play central roles in BPA-associated ovarian carcinogenesis. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are significantly involved in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, oxidative stress, and amoebiasis-related pathways, suggesting that BPA may promote ovarian cancer through a multi-target, multi-pathway synergistic mechanism.

Based on these findings, we propose the “inflammatory microenvironment remodeling dual-axis” hypothesis: on one hand, BPA activates the PI3K-Akt pathway to promote tumor proliferation; on the other hand, it induces oxidative stress damage through genes such as PRDX1, jointly driving ovarian cancer progression. Although this study provides new insights into the association between environmental exposure and ovarian cancer, further experimental validation is required to confirm gene functions and pathway mechanisms. Future research will focus on BPA dose-response effects, gene regulatory networks, and clinical translation applications, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for early warning and targeted intervention in ovarian cancer.
