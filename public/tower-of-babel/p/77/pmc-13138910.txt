Acupuncture‐Integrated Chinese‐Western Medicine Accelerated Rehabilitation Protocol Following Acute Achilles Tendon Repair: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Gao Si, Chi Zhang, Xiuzhi Li, Yuan Cao, Zengzhen Cui, Jingying Li, Letian Wang, Yang Lv, Dong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePostoperative pain and swelling often impede early rehabilitation after Achilles tendon repair. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of an acupuncture‐integrated Chinese‐Western medicine accelerated rehabilitation protocol following acute Achilles tendon rupture repair.MethodsWe prospectively analyzed 193 patients with acute Achilles tendon rupture treated between May 2022 and November 2023. Participants were stratified into three postoperative rehabilitation groups: accelerated rehabilitation (AR, n = 69), traditional rehabilitation (TR, n = 61), and sham acupuncture (SA, n = 63). All patients underwent identical surgical procedures, with postoperative complications documented. Pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at postoperative days 1 and 4, and weeks 1, 2, and 4. Limb swelling was quantified by measuring calf and midfoot circumference differences between affected and unaffected limbs. Functional outcomes were evaluated at 12 weeks using objective measures (one‐leg heel‐rise height [OHRH] and time [OHRT]) and validated scales (Achilles tendon Total Rupture Score [ATRS], AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot score, and Holden Walking Ability Classification [HWAC]). Functional outcomes were evaluated at 12 weeks. Statistical analyses were performed using one‐way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis H test, χ 2 test, or Fisher's exact test as appropriate, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.ResultsCompared with the TR group and the SA group, the AR group had lower VAS scores on the 4th day (p < 0.001) and 1 week (p < 0.001) postoperatively, and more obvious reduction of limb swelling on the 4th day (p < 0.001) and 1 week (p < 0.001) postoperatively. At 12 weeks postoperatively, the AR group showed higher OHRH (p < 0.001) and longer OHRT (p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in ATRS scores, AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot scores, and HWAC among the three groups at 12 weeks postoperatively.ConclusionThe early adoption of the acupuncture‐integrated accelerated rehabilitation strategy after acute Achilles tendon rupture repair can effectively reduce pain and accelerate the resolution of swelling within 1 week after surgery, which facilitates early functional mobilization for patients.Trial RegistrationNCT05957614 (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/, registered 15 July 2023)

Introduction

The Achilles tendon, formed by the confluence of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, constitutes the strongest and thickest tendinous structure in the human body [1]. Despite its biomechanical significance, this tendon demonstrates particular vulnerability to traumatic injuries, with acute ruptures predominantly affecting physically active middle‐aged populations [2]. While conservative management demonstrates reduced complication rates across multiple studies, surgical intervention remains the preferred therapeutic approach for acute ruptures due to its reliability in restoring functional capacity for patients with high physical demands [3].

Postoperative rehabilitation critically influences recovery trajectories, yet substantial equipoise persists regarding optimal early rehabilitation protocols following Achilles tendon repair [4]. Conventional strategies typically mandate 2–8 weeks of immobilization followed by graded ankle mobilization and protected weight‐bearing [5], approaches frequently complicated by arthrofibrosis, muscular atrophy, and protracted functional recovery [6]. These limitations underscore the imperative for developing evidence‐based accelerated rehabilitation paradigms.

The integration of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) with Western rehabilitation modalities has garnered increasing clinical attention in recent years. Contemporary research highlights acupuncture's advantages in pain management, demonstrating comparable efficacy to pharmacological interventions while minimizing adverse effects and treatment costs [7, 8]. Notably, the World Health Organization endorses acupuncture as a first‐line non‐pharmacological analgesic modality [9]. Building upon these foundations, we developed an integrative accelerated rehabilitation protocol incorporating acupuncture as a distinguishing feature. This prospective cohort study aimed to: (i) evaluate the early postoperative analgesic and anti‐edema effects of an acupuncture‐integrated accelerated rehabilitation protocol; (ii) compare its impact on functional recovery at 12 weeks post‐surgery; and (iii) assess the safety profile of this integrative approach relative to traditional and sham acupuncture controls.

Methods

This investigation received institutional review board approval from our institution (IRB00006761‐M2020315) and was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05957614) in accordance with International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines.

Design and Population

This prospective cohort study enrolled 334 consecutive patients with acute Achilles tendon rupture (ATR) treated at our institution between May 2022 and November 2023. Sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0 software. Based on preliminary data (VAS scores at week 1 postoperatively: 1.4 ± 1.3 vs. 2.5 ± 1.5), the minimal clinically important difference was 1.1. With α = 0.05, power = 80%, 27 patients per group were required. Accounting for an anticipated 50% dropout rate, the minimum enrollment was set at 108 participants. Following written informed consent acquisition, 141 patients failing inclusion criteria or declining participation were excluded, yielding 193 analyzable cases (57.8% retention). Preoperative ultrasonographic confirmation by musculoskeletal radiologists documented rupture location and severity.

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) Age 18–60 years, (2) Closed traumatic injury, (3) Acute ATR diagnosis (< 7 days post‐injury), (4) Rupture > 3.5 cm proximal to calcaneal insertion (ultrasound‐verified), (5) Absence of acupuncture contraindications.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Partial thickness tears, (2) Insertional ruptures (< 3.5 cm from calcaneus), (3) Comorbidities affecting functional assessments (diabetes mellitus, neuropathies, autoimmune disorders).

Participants were stratified into three intervention arms using computer‐generated randomization, with allocation concealed via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent research assistant not involved in patient enrollment or intervention delivery: Accelerated Rehabilitation (AR), Traditional Rehabilitation (TR), and Sham Acupuncture (SA) cohorts.

Surgical Procedure

All procedures were performed prone under spinal anesthesia with tourniquet control. Preoperative Achilles tendon resting angle (ATRA) was measured bilaterally [10]. A posteromedial incision exposed the rupture site. Tendon repair employed combined Krackow locking loop and modified Kessler techniques using nonabsorbable sutures (Figure 1) [11], reinforced with six figure‐eight sutures (4–0 absorbable). Post‐repair ATRA was verified against the contralateral limb. Closure included paratenon approximation, subcutaneous sutures, and skin staples. Standardized postoperative immobilization used a below‐knee brace in 15°–25° plantar flexion.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

AR Group

Phase 1 (Weeks 0–2 Postoperative): During the initial postoperative phase, patients received comprehensive inpatient rehabilitation in the Department of Traditional Chinese Medicine, which included daily 30‐min acupuncture sessions administered using sterile, disposable stainless steel needles (25 mm and 40 mm length, 0.3 mm diameter; Huacheng, Beijing Keyuanda Medical Device Manufactory) according to WHO Standard Acupuncture Locations [12]. All acupuncture procedures were performed by the same TCM medical team (licensed acupuncturists with a minimum of 5 years' clinical experience), who completed identical protocol training including standardized needle insertion techniques and depth verification. The treatment protocol targeted specific acupoints: ST36, GB34, SP9, ST34, and SP10 on the affected limb, and ST36, GB34, SP9, SP6, BL60, KI3, LR2, and PC7 on the contralateral limb (Figure 2 and the Supporting Information), with technical specifications including 25 mm needles (10–25 mm depth) for PC7 and 40 mm needles (15–30 mm depth) for all other points. These points were selected based on TCM principles for promoting Qi (vital energy) and blood circulation, resolving edema, alleviating pain, and strengthening tendons, and are commonly used in musculoskeletal rehabilitation protocols [12, 13]. Rehabilitation progression involved initiating toe flexion/extension and quadriceps isometric exercises on postoperative days 1–3, followed by daytime splint removal for elevation and non‐weight‐bearing ambulation with assistive devices on days 2–3. By day 4, patients commenced ankle dorsiflexion exercises (3 × 20 min daily), while wound care consisted of dressing changes every 3–5 days with suture removal at 2 weeks.

Locations of the selected acupoints.

Phase 2 (Weeks 2–10+ Postoperative): The outpatient rehabilitation protocol progressed systematically, beginning with splint discontinuation, continued ankle range of motion (ROM) exercises, and initiation of partial weight‐bearing using a walking boot (DJO Aircast Walker) during weeks 2–4, alongside progressive standing tolerance starting at 1 h daily. From weeks 4–6, patients advanced to full weight‐bearing in the boot and incorporated squat exercises with boot protection. Weeks 6–8 marked the transition to boot removal, initiation of bilateral heel raises, and ambulation on level surfaces (< 1000 steps/day). During weeks 8–10, rehabilitation intensified with single‐leg heel raises on the affected side and increased walking distance, followed by slow jogging initiation after 2 weeks of successful single‐leg raises. Finally, after 4 weeks of progressive jogging (≥ 10 weeks postoperative), patients were permitted to engage in higher‐intensity non‐contact activities [14].

TR Group

During the initial 2‐week postoperative period, patients were immobilized using a splint maintaining 15°‐25° plantar flexion. Standard wound care included dressing changes every 3–5 days with suture removal at 2 weeks. Subsequent rehabilitation followed the identical protocol as the AR group.

SA Group

Patients in the SA group received standardized sham acupuncture interventions at our Traditional Chinese Medicine outpatient clinic, maintaining identical treatment parameters to the AR group in terms of acupoint selection (though actual needle placement avoided standard meridians), needle specifications (same manufacturer and dimensions), treatment duration (30 min daily), and needle insertion depth. The key methodological distinction involved non‐acupoint stimulation (NAS), a validated sham acupuncture approach [15], implemented by placing needles (1) ≥ 1 cm lateral to standard acupoint locations, (2) avoiding all classical meridian pathways, (3) steering clear of known neurovascular bundles, and (4) specifically targeting anatomically neutral areas without documented physiological effects. All remaining rehabilitation components, including exercise protocols and wound care, precisely mirrored the AR group's established protocol.

Data Collection

Patients underwent standardized follow‐up assessments on postoperative days 1 and 4, and at weeks 1, 2, 4, and 12, with all evaluations performed by the same attending surgeon who was blinded to group allocation to minimize assessment bias, particularly for clinician‐administered scales. The comprehensive surveillance protocol encompassed: (1) complication monitoring including superficial wound infection, tendon re‐rupture, and venous thromboembolism (VTE); (2) early postoperative metrics (days 1–4, weeks 1–4) featuring Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for pain assessment along with bilateral limb measurements of calf circumference and foot arch circumference differentials to quantify postoperative limb swelling; and (3) functional outcome measures at week 12 incorporating objective heel‐rise parameters (one‐leg heel‐rise height [OHRH] and time [OHRT]) and validated scoring systems including the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) [16], AOFAS Ankle‐Hindfoot Scale [17], and Holden Walking Ability Classification (HWAC) [18].

The VAS utilized a 10‐cm linear scale ranging from “no pain” (0 cm) to “worst imaginable pain” (10 cm) for patient self‐reported pain levels. The ATRS, a patient‐reported outcome measure, comprised 10 items evaluating symptoms and physical activity limitations using an 11‐point Likert scale (0–10) per item, yielding total scores from 0 to 100 (higher scores indicating better function) [16]. The clinician‐administered AOFAS Scale provided a 100‐point assessment with categorical outcomes (90–100: excellent; 75–89: good; 50–74: fair; < 50: poor) [17], while the HWAC classified walking ability across five tiers (Grade 0‐I: non‐ambulatory/extensive assistance; Grade II‐III: assisted ambulation with limited safety; Grade IV‐V: independent level‐surface ambulation) [18]. Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were first assessed for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data with homogeneous variance were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x¯±s) and analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using the Bonferroni test. Non‐normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)] and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test.

Ordinal data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables (including gender and complication rates) were presented as counts (percentages) and compared among the three groups using either the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate, and the Bonferroni test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For all statistical tests, a two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Group Characteristics

The study cohort comprised 193 patients (189 males, 4 females) with acute closed Achilles tendon ruptures, ranging in age from 27 to 48 years (mean 37.07 ± 7.41 years). The injuries involved 92 left‐sided and 101 right‐sided cases. The cohort demonstrated a mean body mass index of 25.87 ± 2.49 kg/m2, with surgical procedures averaging 45.15 ± 11.78 min in duration and 7.15 ± 2.48 mL in estimated blood loss.

Participants were randomly allocated to three intervention groups: accelerated rehabilitation (AR, n = 69), traditional rehabilitation (TR, n = 61), and sham acupuncture (SA, n = 63). Baseline characteristics including age, BMI, affected side, gender distribution, operative time, and blood loss showed no statistically significant differences among the three groups (all p > 0.05), confirming balanced group allocation (Table 1).

Postoperative Complications

The study documented several postoperative complications across treatment groups: VTE occurred in 2 cases (3.3%) in the TR group and 1 case (1.6%) in the SA group, while superficial wound infections were observed in 3 cases each in the AR (4.3%) and TR (4.9%) groups and 2 cases (3.2%) in the SA group, with all infected wounds resolving completely following standard wound care and antibiotic therapy. Regarding tendon re‐ruptures, the AR group had 1 case (1.4%) at 2 weeks postoperatively, the TR group reported 2 cases (3.3%) at 2 and 3 weeks, and the SA group experienced 2 cases (3.2%) at 3 weeks postoperatively—all resulting from accidental falls with direct limb trauma confirmed by MRI and successfully managed conservatively with satisfactory outcomes. Importantly, statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in complication rates among the three treatment groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Functional Status

Postoperative VAS scores peaked on postoperative day 1 and progressively declined, reaching the lowest levels at 4 weeks (Figure 4). The AR group demonstrated a significantly faster reduction in pain scores compared to both the TR and SA groups (p < 0.001). At postoperative day 4, the AR group exhibited significantly lower VAS scores (1.39 ± 1.24) than the TR (2.54 ± 1.21, p < 0.001) and SA (2.10 ± 1.01, p = 0.002) groups, with no significant difference between TR and SA (p > 0.05). By postoperative week 1, the AR group (0.39 ± 0.71) maintained superior pain relief compared to both TR (1.74 ± 1.03, p < 0.001) and SA (1.21 ± 0.77, p < 0.001), while TR showed higher pain scores than SA (p = 0.002).

Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for Pain.

Limb swelling severity followed a similar temporal pattern, peaking on postoperative day 1 and gradually subsiding by week 4 (Figure 5). The AR group exhibited significantly faster swelling resolution than both control groups (p < 0.001). At day 4, calf circumference differences (mean ± SD) were 1.24 ± 0.04 (AR), 1.45 ± 0.06 (TR), and 1.35 ± 0.08 (SA), with all intergroup comparisons showing statistical significance (Bonferroni‐adjusted p < 0.001). By week 1, the AR group (0.92 ± 0.07) maintained significantly smaller swelling measurements than both TR (1.14 ± 0.06) and SA (1.17 ± 0.07) groups (both p < 0.001), with no significant TR vs. SA difference (p > 0.05).

Mean differences of lower leg circumference at each time point.

Midfoot swelling measurements showed similar patterns, with significant intergroup differences at several early timepoints (Figure 6). At day 4, midfoot circumference differences measured 0.89 ± 0.06 (AR), 1.45 ± 0.10 (TR), and 1.38 ± 0.13 (SA) (all pairwise p < 0.001). These differences persisted at week 1 (AR: 0.68 ± 0.06 vs. TR: 1.13 ± 0.08 vs. SA: 1.05 ± 0.11; all p < 0.001) but became nonsignificant between AR and control groups by week 2 (AR: 0.44 ± 0.07 vs. TR: 0.45 ± 0.06 vs. SA: 0.41 ± 0.07; all p > 0.05), though TR and SA remained significantly different (p = 0.007).

There was no statistically significant difference in the ATRS scores (Table 3), AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot scores (Table 4), and HWAC (Table 5) among the three groups before surgery and at 3 months postoperatively. However, at 3 months postoperatively, the AR Group showed a higher OHRH and a longer OHRT than the TR Group and the SA Group, and the differences were statistically significant (all p < 0.001, Table 6).

Discussion

Summary of Main Findings

The management of acute Achilles tendon rupture presents a significant rehabilitation dilemma. While surgical repair effectively restores tendon continuity, prolonged immobilization protocols (typically 2–8 weeks) remain commonplace despite robust evidence highlighting their detrimental effects [19, 20]. Immobilization‐induced arthrofibrosis, disuse atrophy of the gastrocnemius‐soleus complex, and impaired venous return collectively contribute to delayed functional recovery and increased complication risks such as deep vein thrombosis and tendon adhesions [21, 22]. The resultant muscle atrophy can cause calf circumference reduction within weeks, profoundly impacting plantarflexion strength [21]. Furthermore, the intense postoperative pain and significant limb swelling characteristic of the early recovery phase act as major barriers to initiating timely mobilization [19, 22]. Traditional approaches often rely heavily on pharmacologic analgesia, which carries risks of side effects and dependency [23], while swelling management strategies like limb elevation and diuretics offer limited efficacy and potential systemic complications [24, 25]. This therapeutic impasse underscores the critical need for innovative strategies that safely mitigate early postoperative symptoms, thereby enabling accelerated functional rehabilitation without compromising repair integrity.

Effects on Postoperative Pain and Swelling

Postoperative pain and swelling following ATR repair arise from complex inflammatory cascades, where surgical trauma triggers the release of pro‐inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, leukotrienes, TNF‐α), sensitizing peripheral nociceptors (“inflammatory pain”) and increasing vascular permeability [20, 26, 27]. Concomitant immobilization exacerbates limb swelling by impairing the muscle‐pump mechanism essential for venous and lymphatic drainage [27, 28]. Traditional Chinese Medicine conceptualizes this condition as “Qi stagnation and blood stasis”, where trauma disrupts meridian flow [29, 30], a framework supported by contemporary research on acupuncture's mechanisms. Neurophysiological studies demonstrate that acupuncture modulates inflammation by suppressing pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6) while enhancing anti‐inflammatory signals [31]; regulates pain pathways by downregulating pain‐transducing channels (TRPV1) in sensory neurons and activating endogenous opioid systems [29]; and improves microcirculation by enhancing local blood flow and lymphatic drainage, thereby facilitating limb swelling resolution [27, 28, 30]. Accordingly, our protocol strategically incorporated acupoints (ST36, GB34, SP6, BL60), selected based on TCM principles for resolving dampness, promoting Qi and blood circulation, alleviating pain, and strengthening sinews, while also aligning with contemporary neuroanatomical mapping [12, 32, 33].

The clinical outcomes of this study substantiate the efficacy of the Acupuncture‐Integrated Chinese‐Western Medicine Accelerated Rehabilitation protocol in overcoming three fundamental obstacles to early postoperative mobilization following ATR repair. Regarding pain management, the AR group demonstrated a statistically and clinically significant reduction in VAS scores compared to both TR and SA groups, with mean differences of 1.15 points (day 4) and 1.35 points (week 1) versus TR, and 0.71 points (day 4) and 0.82 points (week 1) versus SA (all p < 0.001). This analgesic effect, which surpassed the placebo response observed in the SA group, enabled the AR cohort to initiate active ankle dorsiflexion exercises significantly earlier than conventional protocols—thereby mitigating the risks of adhesion formation and disuse atrophy that commonly plague immobilized patients [34, 35]. The protocol's impact on postoperative limb swelling was equally noteworthy, with serial circumference measurements revealing significantly faster resolution of swelling in both the calf (mean difference reduction of 0.21–0.22 cm vs. TR and 0.12–0.25 cm vs. SA by week 1) and midfoot (0.56–0.45 cm vs. TR and 0.49–0.37 cm vs. SA by week 1) regions (all p < 0.001). These findings correlate with the earlier attainment of protected weight‐bearing status and may contribute to the observed reduction in DVT incidence (0% in AR vs. 3.3% in TR). Most compelling were the objective functional outcomes at 12 weeks, where the AR group exhibited superior performance in both One‐Leg Heel‐Rise Height (44.6% greater than TR, 32.6% greater than SA) and Heel‐Rise Time (30.2% longer than TR, 23.7% longer than SA) (all p < 0.001). These biomechanical advantages, not captured by subjective rating scales, likely reflect the protocol's unique capacity to facilitate earlier neuromuscular retraining through its dual modulation of pain and swelling pathways. Importantly, these benefits were achieved without compromising surgical safety, as evidenced by comparable re‐rupture rates across groups (AR: 1.4% vs. TR: 3.3% vs. SA: 3.2%, p = 0.598). The integrative value of this protocol lies in its strategic combination of acupuncture's anti‐inflammatory and analgesic properties with evidence‐based rehabilitation principles, creating an optimized environment for accelerated functional recovery while maintaining stringent safety parameters.

Impact on Functional Recovery

The absence of statistically significant differences in patient‐reported (ATRS) and clinician‐rated (AOFAS, HWAC) scores at 12 weeks, despite superior objective functional performance (OHRH, OHRT) in the AR group, warrants consideration. This discrepancy likely stems from several factors inherent to subjective assessment tools. First, instruments like ATRS and AOFAS often exhibit ceiling effects in mid‐term recovery phases, potentially masking subtle but clinically relevant differences in functional capacity captured by biomechanical testing [36]. Second, these scales primarily assess pain, activities of daily living, and perceived limitations, which may have normalized across groups by the 12‐week endpoint as acute symptoms resolved, whereas OHRH/OHRT directly quantify specific muscle performance parameters (strength, endurance) crucial for higher‐demand activities [19]. Finally, patient expectations and adaptation to disability might influence self‐reported scores differently than objective performance measures, particularly in cohorts motivated for recovery like the SA group. This highlights the critical importance of incorporating objective functional metrics alongside traditional subjective scores to fully evaluate rehabilitation efficacy, especially in the early post‐surgical period.

Sham Acupuncture Versus Traditional Rehabilitation

In this study, the SA group exhibited better outcomes than the TR group in several early postoperative indicators, including pain reduction and swelling resolution. Several factors may account for this observation. First, the placebo effect associated with acupuncture procedures is well‐documented; even non‐insertive or non‐meridian needling can elicit analgesic responses through expectation‐driven neurobiological mechanisms [37, 38]. Second, patients in the SA group received daily face‐to‐face interactions with acupuncture practitioners, which may have enhanced therapeutic alliance and improved adherence to rehabilitation protocols compared with the TR group, who had no scheduled acupuncture visits [39]. Third, the structured nature of the SA intervention—requiring regular attendance at the outpatient clinic—may have reinforced patients' engagement with the overall rehabilitation program, leading to more consistent execution of prescribed exercises [40]. These non‐specific effects, while not directly related to acupoint‐specific physiological modulation, likely contributed to the superior performance of the SA group over the TR group in the early postoperative phase.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study demonstrates promising clinical outcomes, several limitations must be acknowledged to properly contextualize the findings and guide future research directions. The 12‐week follow‐up period, while adequate for assessing early rehabilitation progress, precludes evaluation of long‐term outcomes critical for comprehensive protocol validation, including tendon healing quality (as assessed by ultrasound or MRI), sustained return‐to‐sport rates, and late complications such as tendinopathy or chronic calf atrophy [6]. Methodological constraints include the inability to blind patients and acupuncturists to the intervention, which may introduce performance bias; as well as the reliance on subjective outcome measures which may lack sensitivity to detect nuanced functional improvements, suggesting the need for more objective assessment tools like instrumented gait analysis, isokinetic dynamometry, and wearable activity monitors in subsequent studies. The single‐center design and predominantly male cohort (97.9%)—while reflective of ATR epidemiology—limit generalizability and preclude gender‐specific analyses. Furthermore, while the sham acupuncture control addressed needle insertion effects, inherent challenges in acupuncture blinding remain, and the study did not investigate potential mechanistic pathways through which acupuncture may influence tendon healing at the cellular or molecular level.

These limitations delineate clear priorities for future investigation: (1) multicenter prospective RCTs with extended follow‐up to validate long‐term efficacy and safety; (2) mechanistic studies employing animal models and human tissue biopsies to elucidate acupuncture's effects on tendon repair biology; and (3) optimization studies to determine ideal acupuncture parameters (treatment frequency, point combinations) and potential synergies with adjunctive modalities like electroacupuncture. Addressing these knowledge gaps will be essential for establishing evidence‐based guidelines for integrating acupuncture into postoperative rehabilitation protocols.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that acupuncture‐enhanced accelerated rehabilitation after Achilles tendon repair significantly improves early postoperative recovery by reducing pain and swelling, facilitating mobilization. The approach safely enhanced 12‐week functional outcomes (OHRH, OHRT), synergizing traditional Chinese medicine with modern rehabilitation. Further studies should validate long‐term benefits and mechanistic insights.
