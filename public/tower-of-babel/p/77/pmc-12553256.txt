Localization of the mental foramen in relation to the mandibular bone using ultrasonography and orthopantomography: a comparative study in the Turkish population
Authors: Ahmet Tohumcu, Esin Akol Gorgun, Serhat Efeoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mental foramen (MF) is a critical anatomical landmark in dental and maxillofacial procedures. Accurate localization is essential to avoid nerve injury. While orthopantomography (OPG) is commonly used, it has limitations due to its two-dimensional nature and susceptibility to artifacts. Ultrasonography (USG), which offers real-time imaging without ionizing radiation, has emerged as a promising alternative. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of OPG and USG in identifying the MF and measuring its distance to the mandibular bone.MethodsA total of 120 patients (60 males, 60 females), aged 18 and older, were included in the study. A total of 240 mental foramina were evaluated. Vertical measurements were taken from the superior border of the MF to the alveolar crest and from the inferior border to the inferior cortical border of the mandible. Ultrasonographic imaging was performed using an extraoral probe in a longitudinal position. OPGs were acquired using standard protocols, and calibration was achieved by attaching orthodontic wires to the second premolars. All measurements were taken on calibrated digital images using dedicated measurement software. Statistical analysis included paired samples t-test, one-way ANOVA, independent samples t-test, and Pearson correlation.ResultsSignificant differences were found between the two methods in all regions (p < 0.05), with OPG yielding higher distance values than USG. MF–mandibular bone distances were significantly greater in males than in females (p < 0.01). OPG showed significantly greater inferior distances in participants aged 40 years and older, while USG revealed no significant age-related differences. A strong positive correlation (r = 0.801–0.911) and high intra-observer reliability (r = 0.982–0.996) were observed between the methods.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that USG is a reliable, non-invasive alternative to OPG for locating the mental foramen. OPG yielded higher distance values (14.42 mm right, 14.27 mm left) compared to USG (14.12 mm right, 14.03 mm left) (p < 0.05). Background

The mental foramen (MF) is an anatomically vital structure located at the level of the premolar teeth in the mandible, through which the mental nerve and accompanying vascular structures pass. The position of the MF can show significant variation among patients depending on demographic factors such as ethnicity, age, and gender. Although it is commonly reported to be located at the level of the apex of the second premolar tooth, such generalizations may not fully reflect patients variability. In some patients, the MF may be located at any point between the canine and the first molar. Therefore, patient-specific anatomical assessment has become a critical necessity for enhancing clinical safety [1] Accurate localization of the MF is of great importance in preventing iatrogenic nerve injuries during many invasive dental and maxillofacial procedures such as dental implant placement, endodontic treatment, orthognathic surgery, trauma repair, and local anesthesia administration [2].

Currently, one of the most commonly used methods for imaging the MF is orthopantomography (OPG). However, due to its two-dimensional nature, it cannot fully reflect the depth and three-dimensional relationships of anatomical structures. Moreover, OPG has technical limitations such as superimposition, distortion, metal artifacts, low soft tissue contrast, and variability in image quality due to patient positioning. These factors can make it difficult to clearly distinguish small and critical anatomical structures [3].

At this point, ultrasonography (USG) is considered a suitable method for clinical situations where radiation exposure is contraindicated or needs to be minimized, thanks to its advantages such as the absence of ionizing radiation, portability, and real-time imaging capability [4]. Such clinical scenarios include pregnant women, children in growth and development stages, patients with a history of radiotherapy or undergoing active oncological treatment, and patients requiring repeated radiological imaging. Additionally, in hospitalized patients with limited mobility, USG can provide a practical and accessible solution for pre-implant planning or pre- and postoperative nerve region evaluation [5].

In this study, the use of OPG and USG in determining the localization of the mental foramen and its distance from the mandibular bone was compared. The applicability of ultrasonography as both a complementary and, in certain situations, a standalone diagnostic tool was evaluated. Comparing these two methods may contribute to determining more appropriate and safer imaging options during clinical decision-making. Moreover, the findings can guide clinical practice in personalized treatment planning and prevention of potential complications.

The aim of this study is to compare the effectiveness of OPG and ultrasonography in determining the localization of the mental foramen and its distance to the mandibular bone. It also aims to assess the potential of ultrasonography as a complementary or standalone diagnostic tool, particularly in clinical situations where radiation exposure is not preferred, where the MF cannot be clearly visualized on OPGs, or where imaging capabilities are limited.

Methods

This study was conducted in 2024 at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Faculty of Dentistry, Adiyaman University, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Adiyaman University Faculty of Dentistry with the decision dated 02.02.2024 and numbered 2024/2 − 1. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 120 patients (60 female, 60 male), aged 18 years and older, who visited Adiyaman University Faculty of Dentistry for various reasons and had available OPG images and volunteered to participate, were included in the study. The participants were divided into three age groups: 18–29, 30–39, and 40 years and older [6].

Ultrasonographic imaging

Ultrasonographic examinations were performed using a GE Versana Premier (GE HealthCare, USA) device at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Faculty of Dentistry, Adiyaman University. A 12 L-RS linear probe (4.2–13.0 MHz) was used for the imaging. Prior to the study, a methodological calibration was performed by two observers with at least 5 years of experience in OPG and at least 3 years of experience in ultrasonography. They jointly evaluated previously obtained OPG and USG images and reached a consensus on measurement techniques. All final measurements in the study were carried out by a single observer following calibration.

A total of 240 mental foramina from 120 patients included in the study were measured using both USG and OPG, and ultrasonographic examinations were performed while the patients were in a resting position [7]. The ultrasound probe was placed extraorally and longitudinally, perpendicular to the mandibular corpus at the premolar level (Fig. 1). The MF was identified as an interruption in the continuity of the mandibular cortex.

Placement position of the USG extraoral probe

During the ultrasonographic evaluation, the examiner positioned themselves on the patient’s right side while examining the right MF, and on the left side while examining the left MF. This approach was adopted to optimize probe positioning and enhance methodological consistency by minimizing examiner-related measurement variability.

Each patient’s right and left MF regions were evaluated separately. Once the optimal image was obtained, it was frozen, and the measurement was taken using the digital caliper tool on the device’s measurement panel. The same procedure was then repeated on the contralateral side. Measurement parameters included the perpendicular distance from the superior border of the MF to the superior border of the alveolar crest and the perpendicular distance from the inferior border of the MF to the inferior cortical border of the mandible (Fig. 2). To increase measurement reliability, two separate images were taken from each patient approximately 5–6 min apart, and the average of the distances from both images was recorded as the final measurement.

Imaging with orthopantomography

OPG were obtained using a Planmeca ProMax device (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland) located in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology at the Faculty of Dentistry, Adiyaman University, with settings of 64 kVp, 3 mA, and 16 s. The images were obtained with the patients’ heads aligned so that the Frankfurt horizontal plane was parallel to the floor, and with the teeth in maximum intercuspal position (MIP). To minimize the limitations arising from the two-dimensional nature of OPG and potential magnification effects, orthodontic wires of 15 mm in length were placed on the buccal surfaces of the right and left second premolars, and radiographic images were acquired accordingly (Fig. 3). The wires were positioned parallel to the long axis of the second premolars without any anomalies, adapted to the buccal contour curvature of the teeth, fixed with composite resin material, and applied in such a way that they did not contact the opposing arch teeth. The procedure was performed in patients with a stable MIP relationship, without pronounced buccal cortical bone prominences, and without markedly different mandibular angles that could affect the measurement results. The obtained images were exported in DICOM format, and calibration measurements were carried out using the known wire length as a reference with the MicroDicom Viewer software (MicroDicom Ltd., Bulgaria) (Fig. 4). During the measurement process, as in ultrasonography, the vertical distances from the superior border of the mental foramen to the most superior radiographically visible point of the alveolar crest, and from the inferior border of the mental foramen to the inferior cortical border of the mandible, were evaluated (Fig. 5). To improve visual clarity and make important anatomical structures more prominent, contrast and brightness adjustments were made using MicroDicom Viewer software, enhancing sharpness and reducing noise in the images. These enhancements were applied to ensure transparency and reproducibility of the images.

OPG measurements

The OPG measurements were performed on a different day after the preparation of appropriate panoramic images for the study and the blinding of the USG measurement results.

All evaluations were carried out by the same researcher, and to assess intra-observer reliability, 30 randomly selected images were remeasured two weeks apart.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software, based on a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a test power of 99.9%, and a significance level (α) of 5%. It was determined that a minimum of 104 participants would be required for the study.

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated by examining skewness and kurtosis values. To determine the differences between USG and OPG measurements, the Paired Samples t-test was used. For comparisons between gender and age groups, the Independent Samples t-test and One-Way ANOVA were applied, respectively.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess intra-observer consistency. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

Results

In this study, the vertical distances from the MF to the mandibular bone measured using USG and OPG were compared, and differences based on age and gender groups were analyzed.

Comparison of MF-mandible distances measured by USG and OPG

Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were found in all regions between the mean vertical distances from the MF to the mandibular bone measured by USG and OPG. The values measured by OPG were consistently higher than those measured by USG in all regions.

The mean values measured by OPG were 14.42 mm (distance between the superior border of the right MF and the alveolar crest), 11.07 mm (distance between the inferior border of the right MF and the mandibular base), 14.27 mm (distance between the superior border of the left MF and the alveolar crest), and 11.01 mm (distance between the inferior border of the left MF and the mandibular base), while the corresponding values measured by USG were 14.12 mm, 10.82 mm, 14.03 mm, and 10.75 mm, respectively (Table 1).

Intra-Observer agreement

To compare repeated measurements performed by the same observer at different times, a Pearson correlation analysis was applied. The results revealed a very high intra-observer reliability for both imaging methods (r = 0.982–0.996; p < 0.001).

Discussion

Accurate identification of the anatomical location of the MF is critically important in many clinical fields, including dental surgery, implantology, and local anesthesia applications. Therefore, morphometric evaluation of the MF plays a vital role both in treatment planning and in preventing complications [8]. Although various imaging techniques are used for this purpose today, USG stands out due to its non-invasive nature and the quality of the images it provides [9]. In the present study, measurements regarding the location of the mental foramen were compared using OPG and USG techniques, aiming to contribute to the literature by evaluating the concordance between these two commonly used clinical methods.

In the present study, the MF–mandibular bone distances obtained by OPG were found to be higher than those obtained by USG in all measured regions. This discrepancy is likely due to the technical differences between the imaging methods. As a two-dimensional technique, OPG is prone to magnification, distortion, and superimposition effects [10], which can cause small anatomical structures to appear higher or more distant than they actually are.

In contrast, USG is a real-time, radiation-free imaging modality that allows evaluation of soft tissues and the buccal surface of the cortical bone [11]. However, since USG can only assess the buccal cortical surface, the distance measured is not from the MF to the crest tip itself, but rather to the most visible bone contour that the probe can detect. This limitation has been reported by Mehdizadeh et al. (2021) [12]. This discrepancy in reference points may partially explain the systematically higher values obtained via OPG.

In both OPG and USG measurements, male participants showed significantly greater MF–mandibular bone distances compared to females. Similar results have been reported by Kumar et al. (2023) and Laher et al. (2016), who found that males tend to have higher vertical positions of the MF [13, 14]. This difference is attributed to generally thicker cortical bone and larger mandibular anatomy in males [15].

However, in the present study, the distance from the superior border of the left MF to the alveolar crest did not significantly differ between genders in USG measurements. This may be due to more variable imaging conditions in this region or morphological differences that masked inter-gender variability. Moreover, since USG only assesses the buccal cortical surface, establishing a consistent anatomical reference point becomes more challenging, potentially leading to variations in measurement.

A study by Petrovski et al. (2020) using OPGs reported no significant gender-based differences in MF position [16]. Similarly, Gungor et al. (2017), using CBCT in patients aged 10–70 years, found no gender-related differences in MF location [17]. These inconsistencies suggest that methodological differences and demographic variability may influence outcomes.

Although OPG measurements in the present study showed significantly greater inferior MF–mandibular base distances in patients aged 40 and older, USG did not reveal significant age-related differences. This may indicate that USG’s capacity to detect age-related bone morphology changes is limited due to its reliance on soft tissue imaging. These findings align with previous literature. Aguilar La Barrera and García Linares (2024) emphasized that with aging, bone resorption may alter MF positioning [6]. Similarly, Phillips et al. (1992) noted that alveolar bone resorption could influence the relative location of the MF [18]. Kieser et al. (2008) also reported that with age, the MF tends to move further from the inferior mandibular border, with a possible association with sex [19]. On the other hand, Kqiku and Weiglein (2013) reported no age-related changes in MF position [20].

The present study identified a high positive correlation between USG and OPG measurements of MF–mandibular distances (r = 0.801–0.911; p < 0.001), supporting the reliability of ultrasonography as an alternative method for MF localization. Several studies have evaluated the accuracy of USG in MF localization. Artas and Yalçın (2023) compared USG with Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) and found USG to be less reliable in measuring MF size but useful for visualization and detecting blood flow [21]. Similarly, Laher et al. (2016) reported that USG provided satisfactory results in terms of both accuracy and repeatability [14].

Numerous studies in the literature have evaluated the MF morphometrically using CBCT [22–25], OPG [25, 26], and USG [7, 14, 27, 28]. However, the present study differs from previous work by directly comparing OPG and USG measurements.

In a comparative study conducted by Pauly et al., which aimed to determine the localization of the MF using CBCT and OPG, significant differences were found between MF measurements made using OPG and CBCT. However, it was noted that both methods have high internal consistency. This study demonstrated that both methods are reliable for MF localization, although they may show differences in some anatomical regions [25].

In another study conducted by Beshtawi et al. (2020), significant differences were found between OPG and CBCT in the localization of the MF, and these differences were emphasized to stem from the technical characteristics of both methods. It was particularly noted that the two-dimensional nature of OPG could lead to limitations in the accurate evaluation of anatomical structures [29].

These findings suggest that the consistency between CBCT and OPG may vary depending on the characteristics of the techniques used and the anatomical regions being measured.

In this study, to minimize this limitation, OPG images were calibrated using the orthodontic reference wire method in order to achieve standardization of the measurements. Calibration was applied only in patients with a stable occlusal relationship, without pronounced buccal cortical bone prominences, and without markedly different mandibular angles that could affect the measurement results; thereby, measurement deviations related to anatomical variations were minimized. However, the absence of a comparison with CBCT in our study may be considered a methodological limitation. Future studies comparing OPG measurements with CBCT will provide clearer evidence regarding the validity and reliability of the orthodontic reference wire method. Therefore, it is important to select the appropriate method for MF localization in clinical applications, considering the advantages and limitations of both methods.

Although CBCT provides the advantage of three-dimensional imaging, it was not used in the present study due to its higher radiation dose, cost, limited accessibility, and infrequent routine clinical use. Instead, OPG was preferred. OPG remains a key imaging modality in daily dental practice because of its low radiation dose, wide availability, and ease of use [30].

In a similar CBCT-based study, Caglayan et al. (2018) reported that the highest point of the buccal cortical bone rather than the alveolar crest tip was selected as the reference point for alignment between USG and CBCT, since USG can only visualize the buccal cortical surface [21]. Furthermore, as USG images only depict the buccal cortical bone and cannot differentiate whether the visible alveolar crest is buccal or lingual, OPG were used in the present study as the basis for measurement, and anatomical contours visible in OPG were taken as reference.

However, it should be noted that USG has certain limitations, such as its inability to assess beyond the buccal cortical surface, limited representation of anatomical detail, and inadequacy in demonstrating three-dimensional anatomical relationships. Additionally, USG is highly examiner-dependent, and inter-observer variability may affect the reliability of measurements. Therefore, future studies should include larger sample sizes and cover varied age and ethnic subgroups to comprehensively evaluate the method’s validity. Moreover, systematic comparisons between USG and advanced imaging methods such as CBCT and magnetic resonance imaging would contribute to a more objective assessment of its diagnostic reliability.

While intra-observer reliability was assessed in this study, inter-observer reliability was not evaluated as measurements were performed by a single operator. Future studies could benefit from assessing inter-observer reliability, particularly for USG measurements, which may enhance the accuracy and consistency of the findings. This assessment could further validate the clinical applicability and reliability of both methods.

Conclusion

Due to its portability and real-time imaging capabilities, ultrasonography presents a safe and effective imaging alternative, particularly in clinical situations where radiation exposure is contraindicated or must be minimized.

Our findings suggest that USG shows potential not only as a complementary imaging technique but also, in specific clinical situations, as an alternative to traditional imaging methods, such as OPG. However, we emphasize that further research is required to validate USG as a standalone diagnostic tool. More comprehensive studies involving CBCT are necessary to assess the accuracy of USG’s independent diagnostic capabilities.

To our knowledge, no previous study has comparatively evaluated the morphometric features of the mental foramen using both OPG and USG. This highlights the novelty of the present study and suggests that the findings could make a meaningful contribution to the literature.
