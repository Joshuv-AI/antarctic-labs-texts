Multi‐Responsive PAA/PNIPAM Semi‐IPN Hydrogel Properties Controlled by PAA Topology
Authors: Suraj Aswale, Gyeong Min Choi, Hyerin Kang, Aruna Kumar Mohanty, Hanyoung Kim, Hong Y. Cho, Heon Sang Lee, Hyun‐jong Paik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolymer topology has emerged as a key strategy for advancing hydrogel design, offering the ability to impart tunable and enhanced properties through structural control beyond chemical composition. In this work, we specifically focus on the impact of polymer topologies such as linear and cyclic forms of poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) incorporated in poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) based semi‐interpenetrating polymer networks. Topologically distinctive PAAs (linear and cyclic) were synthesized and characterized through 1H NMR, SEC, and DSC. Both linear and cyclic PAA with comparable molecular weights were incorporated during the PNIPAM hydrogel preparation enabling a direct evaluation of topological effects. Swelling experiments revealed that hydrogels containing cyclic PAA demonstrated significantly lower and controlled swelling at varying pH conditions compared to their linear counterparts due to the structural constraints imposed by cyclic architecture. Rheological analyses showed that introduction of PAA topologies decreased the mechanical strength of pure PNIPAM hydrogel. Cyclic PAA/PNIPAM in the hydrated (swelling) state showed better network stability than linear PAA analogue due to additional mechanical crosslinking effect, while under thermal responsive phase transition (de‐swelling), linear PAA/PNIPAM showed the highest mechanical strength. This study confirms the complex role of topological PAA in tuning the property of PNIPAM hydrogels under various conditions.

Introduction

Stimuli‐responsive, or “smart,” hydrogels are polymeric networks capable of reversibly altering their physicochemical properties in response to external or internal stimuli such as temperature, pH, light, magnetic fields, and redox environments. Owing to their tunable responsiveness and biocompatibility, they have attracted extensive attention for applications in drug delivery, biosensing, soft actuation, and tissue engineering. Among various responsive materials, poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) represent a model combination for constructing multi‐stimuli‐responsive hydrogels [1, 2]. These multi‐stimuli‐responsive hydrogels are highly sought after, as their physicochemical versatility allows fine‐tuning of swelling, drug release, and mechanical properties to meet specific biomedical needs [3, 4]. PNIPAM and PAA represent an excellent combination for engineering multi‐responsive hydrogels, primarily due to their complementary stimulus sensitivities and intrinsic biocompatibility [5]. PNIPAM is renowned for its sharp thermal response, exhibiting a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) near 32°C, which enables rapid and reversible volume changes in response to physiological temperature variations [6, 7]. PAA, on the other hand, is highly sensitive to pH changes due to its ionizable carboxylic groups, allowing swelling or deswelling depending on acidic or basic environment [8]. To integrate these two distinct modes of responsiveness into a single material, researchers have developed composite hydrogel architectures in which PNIPAM and PAA networks coexist and interact. Among these approaches, interpenetrating polymer networks (IPNs) and semi‐interpenetrating polymer networks (semi‐IPNs) have proven particularly effective, as they combine independent yet interlocked polymer networks that retain the individual responsiveness of each component while providing mechanical reinforcement [9]. In these systems, the interpenetration of linear and cross‐linked chains facilitates efficient stress dissipation and reinforces network integrity, resulting in improved stability and adaptability. Such hybrid architecture preserves the distinct thermal and pH responses while improving network stability, making them attractive platforms for biomedical and soft‐material applications [10]. Previous studies on PNIPAM‐based semi‐IPNs incorporating linear PAA chains demonstrated dual responsiveness; although PAA had little influence on the LCST or overall volume change, it significantly modulated injectability, transparency, and phase‐transition behavior [11, 12]. Similar behavior has been observed in PNIPAM/PAA semi‐IPN systems, where interpolymer interactions govern phase transition, viscoelastic properties, and temperature pH responsive swelling suitable for drug delivery applications [13, 14]. Despite these advances, most studies have focused primarily on chemical composition or copolymer design, while the influence of polymer topology on hydrogel behavior remains poorly understood.

While polymer topology has received limited attention, extensive studies have sought to enhance the dual responsiveness of PNIPAM/PAA hydrogels through compositional and structural design [14, 15]. Unlike copolymerization, which can disrupt stimuli‐responsive transitions due to the unequal distribution of monomer units along the chain, semi‐IPNs maintain independent network structures while enabling synergistic hydrogen‐bonding interactions [13, 16]. Incorporating linear PAA chains into PNIPAM networks modulates injectability, transparency, and phase transition behavior through hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions, while maintaining distinct network domains. Recent strategies employing movable cross‐linkers or macrocyclic moieties, such as vinyl‐modified cyclodextrins, have demonstrated that cyclic structural elements can improve elasticity and energy dissipation [17, 18]. Cyclic cross‐linkers improve mechanical strength, but cyclic macromolecular networks offer a more promising approach to couple enhanced robustness with preserved multi‐stimuli responsiveness [19, 20]. Motivated by this, we developed a pH‐responsiveness system when optimized for improved mechanical properties. However, most studies have focused primarily on linear polymer architectures, with limited exploration of diverse macromolecular topologies as secondary networks [9, 21]. However, cyclic cross‐linker‐based approaches often sacrifice pH sensitivity and rely on small molecular structures rather than achieving macromolecular level topological control. Consequently, the influence of polymer topology, particularly the distinction between linear and cyclic architectures, on the coupled thermal, pH, and mechanical responses of PNIPAM‐based hydrogels has remained largely unexplored. To address this gap, we designed a topology driven system based on PNIPAM semi‐IPN hydrogels incorporating either linear or cyclic PAA of comparable molecular weight.

Here, we introduce a topology‐driven design of PNIPAM‐based semi‐IPN hydrogels that incorporate either linear or cyclic PAA of comparable molecular weight, thereby enabling a direct comparison of topological effects on network behavior. The linear and cyclic PAA polymers with comparable molecular weights were synthesized through activators regenerated by electron transfer atom transfer radical polymerization (ARGET ATRP) and Cu(I)‐catalyzed azide alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click reactions followed by azidation (Scheme 1) and thoroughly characterized using analytical techniques including proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), size exclusion chromatography (SEC), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) to confirm their molecular architecture and thermal properties. By eliminating chain‐end effects and imposing conformational constraints, the cyclic PAA component serves as a structural modulator that alters network connectivity, elasticity, and swelling dynamics [22]. These distinct PAA topologies were then incorporated into PNIPAM based semi‐IPNs to explore their influence on hydrogel mechanical performance and multi‐responsive behavior. Comparative analyses of the hydrogels’ swelling behavior and rheological responses reveal that cyclic PAA induces more controlled pH‐dependent volume transitions, elevated thermal stability, and enhanced mechanical resilience relative to its linear counterpart. These findings establish polymer topology as an independent design parameter, distinct from chemical composition or molecular weight, for developing multifunctional hydrogels with predictable responsiveness and enhanced mechanical performance, offering versatile potential for applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, and soft actuators.

Result and Discussion

Linear and CYCLIC Polymer Synthesis

In this study, linear PAA was synthesized via deprotection of tert‐butyl group in poly(t‐butyl acrylate) (PtBA) using trifluoroacetic acid. The PtBA precursor was prepared by ARGET ATRP using initiator EBiB. The polymerization was conducted in a mixture of initiator, monomer, reducing agent, catalyst, ligand in acetone. When the monomer conversion measured through 1H NMR reached 95%, the reaction stopped by the addition of excess THF. The isolated polymer was characterized through SEC and 1H NMR spectroscopy. A number‐average molar mass (M n,SEC) and polydispersity (Đ) were determined to be 5.3 kg mol−1 and 1.06, respectively (Figure 1). For comparison purposes, cyclic PtBA of similar molar mass was prepared. Alkyne‐functionalized PtBA‐N3 was cyclized under dilute conditions via a CuAAc click reaction, and the resulting cyclic PtBA was isolated by preparative SEC to remove linear by‐products, as described in our previous work [23]. The molar mass of the cyclic PtBA was measured by SEC to be 4.3 kg mol−1. Due to the compact nature of cyclic topology, the apparent molar mass of cyclic PtBA was observed to be lower range shown in Figure S1. A comparison of the molar mass of linear PtBA and precursors of cyclic PtBA is shown in Figure 1. The purified cyclic PtBA was then deprotected with trifluoroacetic acid to afford cyclic PAA. The cyclic PAA was confirmed through the 1H NMR and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR). Following deprotection, t‐butyl group signal ‘g’ disappears, instead a broad peak around 12.2 ppm appears. Which is characteristic of the carboxylic acid proton. The triazole proton peak ’a’ remains unchanged at 8.2 ppm in (Figure 2). The methylene protons at positions ‘b’ and ‘f’ show similar chemical shifts in both compounds (∼5.0 ppm), and the resonances for protons ‘c’, ‘d’, and ‘e’ are observed in the same regions. Figure S2 presents the FT‐IR spectra of cyclic PtBA and its deprotected product, cyclic PAA, confirming the successful removal of t‐butyl groups and formation of carboxylic acid functionalities. In the FT‐IR spectrum of cyclic PtBA (magenta line), a strong absorption band at 1726 cm− 1 corresponds to the ester C═O stretching, accompanied by a C─O stretch at 1366 cm− 1. After deprotection, cyclic PAA (blue line) exhibits a broad ─OH stretch at 3370 cm− 1 and a shifted C═O band at 1700 cm− 1, characteristic of carboxylic acids. Additional C─O and ─OH bands at 1239 and 1366 cm− 1 further support successful conversion to PAA. Figure 3A presents the DSC thermogram of linear PAA (black line) and cyclic PAA (blue line), highlighting their respective glass transition temperatures (T g). The linear PAA exhibits a T g at 121°C, while the cyclic PAA shows a slightly higher T g at 125°C. This upward shift in T g for the cyclic polymer is attributed to its topological constraint, which restricts segmental motion compared to the linear counterpart [24]. The observed difference supports the concept that cyclization enhances thermal rigidity by eliminating chain ends and reducing configurational freedom. The results obtained from SEC, 1H NMR, FT‐IR, and DSC analyses confirm the successful synthesis of linear and cyclic PAAs.

Preparation and Confirmation of Linear and Cyclic PAA in PNIPAM Hydrogels

The schematic in Scheme 2 illustrates the preparation of a linear or cyclic PAA/PNIPAM semi‐IPN hydrogel. Linear/cyclic PAA and NIPAM were separately dissolved in water, then mixed. Upon addition of redox initiator ammonium persulfate (APS) and N,N,N′,N′‐ tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), and the N,N′‐methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA) crosslinker, NIPAM undergoes polymerization in the presence of linear/cyclic PAA, separately. The resulting mixture was transferred to a petri dish, where gelation occurs, yielding a homogeneous PAA/PNIPAM semi‐IPN hydrogel (Figure S3). The schematic also highlights the network structure, where linear/cyclic PAA is embedded within the PNIPAM gel matrix, and includes a photograph of the final hydrogel, demonstrating successful gel formation. As summarized in Table S1, all hydrogel formulations were prepared using identical monomer and crosslinker ratios, with the only variable being the topology of the incorporated PAA. Both linear and cyclic PAA architectures were introduced under consistent polymerization conditions. The resulting crosslinking efficiencies for hydrogels containing linear PAA and cyclic PAA were similar (82%); comparable to gels formed without PAA polymer. Crosslinking efficiency was determined from the gel fraction i.e. insoluble polymer fraction after purification by immersing in water to remove unreacted species, followed by vacuum dried to constant weight. The gel fraction was calculated by comparing the insoluble residual mass with the solid content of reaction. To confirm the incorporation of cyclic PAA within the semi‐IPN hydrogel network, the solution 13C NMR spectrum of cyclic PAA was compared with the solid‐state 13C NMR spectra of the corresponding semi‐IPN hydrogels (Figures S4 and S5). Solid‐state 13C NMR spectra after purification, for the linear and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM (red and blue colored in the Figure S5, respectively) hydrogels revealed characteristic signals of both PNIPAM and PAA chains. In particular, the cyclic PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel exhibits additional peaks (f, g) in the 100–150 ppm region, which are assigned to the cyclic topology and the triazole ring formed during cyclization. The similar chemical shifts for the main PNIPAM peaks (1, 5) in both spectra confirm the successful incorporation of both topologies into the hydrogel network. The 1H NMR spectra of the washing solution (Figure S6) confirm the incorporation of both topologies in PNIPAM network. While both linear PAA/PNIPAM (red line) and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM (blue line) hydrogels show characteristic PNIPAM signals, the linear and cyclic PAA signals are not distinctly observed due to restricted mobility and entrapment within the PNIPAM network. These results conclusively confirm the incorporation of both linear and cyclic PAA topologies within the IPN hydrogels.

Swelling Behavior

The swelling behavior of semi‐IPN hydrogels containing either linear or cyclic PAA topologies was systematically investigated as a function of pH (Figure 4A). Swelling ratios were determined using 100 mg dry hydrogel samples, which were immersed in 0.1 m sodium‐based buffer solutions of defined compositions (sodium acetate/acetic acid for pH 4.4 and 5.5, and sodium carbonate/sodium bicarbonate for pH 7.4 and 10.0); after reaching equilibrium swelling, the swollen sample weight was measured and the swelling ratio was calculated as: Ws−Wd/Wd×100% where W s and W d are the swollen and dry weights, respectively.

Semi‐IPN hydrogel swelling ratio with increasing pH (A), normalized volume (V/V0) change of PNIPAM, linear PAA/PNIPAM, and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM according to time at 40°C (B).

Both hydrogel systems exhibited a pronounced decrease in swelling ratio with increasing pH. At pH 4.4, linear PAA/PNIPAM displayed the highest swelling ratio (∼331%), while cyclic PAA/PNIPAM showed a comparatively lower swelling ratio (∼290%), despite both containing the same amount of PAA. At higher pH values (pH 5.5, 7.4, and 10), this trend persisted: swelling ratios declined steadily, with cyclic PAA/PNIPAM consistently maintaining a lower swelling ratio than linear PAA/PNIPAM at pH values examined. This behavior can be attributed to two main factors. First, as reported in prior studies on PAA‐based micellar systems, protonation of carboxylic acid groups at low pH promotes hydrogen bonding and results in larger, more tightly aggregated polymer structures that enhance swelling due to increased hydrophilicity. Above the pK a (∼4.0), deprotonation suppressing hydrogen bonding and leading to more stable, less aggregated network with reduced swelling [25, 26]. Second, in semi‐IPNs, the associated rise in counterions (e.g., Na+) induced charge screening and formation of ionic crosslinks, further tightening the network and restricting expansion [27, 28]. Across all pH values, cyclic PAA/PNIPAM hydrogels consistently exhibit lower swelling compared to linear PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel (Figure 4A). Cyclic PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel shows ∼12%–25% lower swelling ratios at each pH: linear (331, 310, 276, 224) vs cyclic (290, 250, 208, 188) from pH 4.4 to 10. This is attributed to the cyclic topology of PAA, which introduces structural constraints and eliminates chain ends, resulting in a more stable and homogenous mixing with in the network. The incorporation of PAA introduces additional charged groups within the microgel network, leading to an increased concentration of counterions. This enhances the osmotic pressure difference across the microgel boundary, which in turn induces deswelling [29]. Cyclic PAA structure further limits chain mobility and network expansion, especially at higher pH. Consequently, cyclic topology provides consistently lower swelling levels across entire pH range, enabling more controlled volume changes, an advantage for applications requiring precise swelling behavior.

Thermo‐Responsive Volume and Rheological Behavior

Figure 3B shows the DSC thermograms for PNIPAM, linear PAA/PNIPAM, and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM. Pure PNIPAM exhibits a sharp endothermic transition near 30°C, corresponding to its well‐known LCST. Upon addition of either cyclic PAA or linear PAA, the endothermic transition is delayed, leading to both a shifting of LCST to higher temperatures and a broadening of the endothermic peak. The broadening of phase transition demonstrates the heterogeneity of the polymeric system [30, 31]. This behavior is attributed to the increased entropy of mixing in the PAA/PNIPAM/water ternary system compared to the PNIPAM/water binary system. The higher entropy of mixing lowers the chemical potential of water in the hydrogels at T > LCST [32, 33]. Consequently, the osmotic compressibility decreases upon incorporation of PAAs into PNIPAM.

The normalized volume (V/V0) is shown in Figure 4B. At the same solute concentration, V/V0 is the highest for linear PAA/PNIPAM, followed by cyclic PAA/PNIPAM and pure PNIPAM. This trend provides direct evidence for reduced osmotic compressibility. Since viscosity and elasticity describe the response of a system to deformation rate or deformation under external stress, the decrease in osmotic compressibility results in an increases in both the loss modulus (G′′) and the storage modulus (G′). The moduli were measured using a rotational rheometer under oscillatory shear flow at 25°C and 40°C (Figure 5A,B) respectively. The temperatures were considered to observe the viscoelastic properties of hydrogels with swelling (below LCST) and deswelling (above LCST) transition. The viscosity and elasticity response of the hydrogels remained almost stable or slightly increased with shear sweeping. PNIPAM chains exist in extended, hydrated coil conformation with extensive hydrogen bonding between the ‐amide groups and water molecules. In the hydrated state, PNIPAM gel forms a strong polymer network with hydrogen‐bonded junctions and inter and intra‐chain amide‐amide interactions resulting in the highest G′ and G″ among the studied systems. In principle, the moduli of a hydrogel increases with increasing crosslink density (i.e., the number of crosslinking points per unit volume) as well as with reduced osmotic compressibility. As linear PAA is introduced, the original strong interactions among PNIPAM were disrupted resulting in lower G′ and G″ even though a certain amide carboxyl interaction formed. The cyclic PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel exhibits the lowest G′ due to a limited chain interactions since cyclic PAA has a compact topology in comparison to extended linear PAA. Interestingly, the cyclic topology also provides mechanical crosslinking effect in the system and allows stress relaxation by easier retraction due to limited entanglement and through sliding of chains. Due to mechanical crosslinking, cyclic PAA showed higher G′ at higher strain, as the mobility of polymer chains was relatively restricted, indicating higher elastic strength. G″ at high strain provides information about the energy dissipation nature. The increasing trend of G″ in the cyclic PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel at high frequency suggests higher energy dissipation, indicating a stable gel system. In other words, the incorporation of cyclic PAA in PNIPAM hydrogel improved the viscous and energy dissipation behavior showing resistance to thermal deformation. On the other hand, the linear PAA/PNIPAM hydrogel, in the absence of any crosslinking effect, showed a rapid decline in G′′ at higher strain, indicating network breakdown and a weaker gel system. Upon heating above LCST (at 40°C), the increase in moduli relative to that below the LCST (25°C) primarily reflects the change in osmotic compressibility (Figure 5B). Cyclic PAA, due to its compact and closed topology, provides mechanical crosslinking and forms fewer bridging interactions with PNIPAM clusters, while allowing local chain mobility and slip at the polymer–polymer interface. This topology‐driven relaxation results in a moderate G″ while G′ remains comparatively low. The results clearly demonstrate the topology‐driven mechanical responses associated with the entropic osmotic compressibility of hydrogels.

G′ and G′′ measurement of PNIPAM, linear PAA/PNIPAM, and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM with respect to angular frequency (ω) at 25°C (A) and 40°C (B), ratios of G′(40°C)/G′(25°C) (C) and G″(40°C)/G″(25°C) (D) as a function of ω for PNIPAM, linear PAA/PNIPAM, and cyclic PAA/PNIPAM.

In Figure 5C,D, the ratios of G′(40°C)/G′(25°C) and G′′(40°C)/G′′(25°C) are plotted. As expected, both G′ and G′ are higher at 40°C than at 25°C, due to partial water expulsion at T > LCST (Figure 5A,B). The shrinking of the chains above the transition resulted in an increase of one to three orders of magnitude of both G′ and G″ indicating a transition from soft to harder hydrogel. Thus, the increase in the complex modulus, , follows the same order as the inverse of osmotic compressibility, linear PAA/PNIPAM > cyclic PAA/PNIPAM > pure PNIPAM. Especially, the ratio G′′(40°C)/G′′(25°C) (Figure 5D) follows the same order as the inverse of osmotic compressibility (Figure 4B). The trend in G′(40°C)/G′(25°C) is similar, although cyclic PAA/PNIPAM and pure PNIPAM exhibit comparable values. Since the complex viscosity depends on both G′ and G′′, the viscosity of the hydrogels is likewise highest for linear PAA/PNIPAM, followed by cyclic PAA/PNIPAM and pure PNIPAM, again in agreement with the osmotic compressibility behavior. These observations underscore that the viscoelastic response of semi‐IPN hydrogels is a complex interplay between polymer topology, hydrogen bonding, and temperature‐induced hydrophobic association, providing a molecular‐level rationale for the distinct mechanical behavior observed across the LCST transition. Previous studies by Y. Takeoka et al. primarily investigated the network properties of PNIPAM and ring‐containing PNIPAM gels under static solvent conditions, where cyclodextrin served as a mechanical crosslinker [34]. This approach is extended here by incorporating a pH‐responsive polymer to introduce tunable intermolecular interactions within the PNIPAM‐based network. This work demonstrates control of hydrogel osmotic compressibility through topological modification and their responses to thermal and mechanical stimuli, providing systematic insight into structure‐property relationships.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully prepared semi‐IPN hydrogels incorporating linear and cyclic PAA architectures of similar molecular weight within a PNIPAM matrix. By isolating topological effects from molecular weight influences, we demonstrated that cyclic PAA imparts more controlled swelling behavior, enhances deformability, and improved thermal responsiveness compared to its linear counterpart. Rheological analysis demonstrated that cyclic topology facilitates efficient stress relaxation and constrains network expansion, while linear chains promote interdomain bridging, resulting in greater energy dissipation and enhanced responsiveness to environmental stimuli such as temperature. Cyclic PAA hydrogels exhibit reduced swelling across the studied pH range compared to their linear counterparts, indicating topology‐driven control over network homogeneity and environmental sensitivity. These structural features provide an effective approach to design hydrogels with tunable properties for targeted applications such as drug delivery, tissue engineering, soft actuators, and other advanced soft materials.

Experimental Section

Materials

Ethyl α‐bromoisobutyrate (EBiB, Sigma‐Aldrich), CuIIBr2 (Sigma–Aldrich, 98.0%), tert‐butyl acrylate (tBA, Sigma‐Aldrich, 98%), tin(II) 2‐ethylhexanoate (Sn(EH)2, Sigma–Aldrich, 98.5%–100.0%), tris(2‐(dimethylamino)ethyl)amine (Me6TREN, Aldrich, 97.0%), N‐isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM, TCI, >98%), N,N′‐methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA, Sigma–Aldrich, 99%), Ammonium persulfate (APS, Sigma–Aldrich, >98%), N,N,N′,N′‐ tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED, TCI, 98%), trifluoracetic acid (Sigma–Aldrich, 99%), and other chemicals were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich and used as received.

Characterization

1H NMR spectra were recorded on 500 MHz Agilent and 400 MHz JEOL superconducting FT‐NMR spectrometers using CDCl3 as the solvent. SEC was performed with an Agilent 1100 pump equipped with refractive index RI and UV detectors, employing a PSS SDV precolumn (5 µm, 50 × 8.0 mm) and PSS SDV columns (5 µm; 105, 103, and 102 Å; 300 × 8.0 mm). Calibration was carried out using polystyrene standards, with THF as the eluent at 40°C and a flow rate of 1.0 mL min− 1. Infrared spectra were collected on a Nicolet 6700 FT‐IR spectrophotometer. Differential scanning calorimetry analyses were conducted on a TA Instruments Q100 under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating/cooling rate of 10°C min− 1 within a temperature range of −10°C to 210°C. Two complete heating‐cooling cycles were performed: the first cycle involved heating to 210°C and subsequent cooling to −10°C, followed by a second identical cycle. The glass transition temperature was determined as the midpoint of the heat‐flow change observed during the second heating run. For volume change measurements, the samples were immersed in distilled water at 25°C in a glass dish for 1 h, after which the temperature was raised to 40°C and the changes in sample diameter and thickness were recorded to monitor the volume variation over time. The rheological measurements were performed using a controlled stress rheometer (model AR‐G2 from TA Instruments). For rheological measurements, each sample was immersed in distilled water maintained at 25°C and 40°C in a glass dish for 1 h prior to testing. A parallel plate geometry was used with a gap size of 1.0 mm and a plate diameter of 20 mm. A dynamic frequency sweep test was conducted at a strain amplitude γ = 1% over an oscillating frequency range of 100 to 0.1 rad/s at 25 and 40°C. A solvent trap was installed on the rheometer to prevent the evaporation of water during the measurement time.

Synthesis of Linear Poly(t‐butyl acrylate)

The polymerization of linear PtBA was carried out in acetone using a mixture of initiator, monomer, reducing agent, catalyst, and ligand, maintaining a molar ratio of [I]0:[M]0:[Sn(EH)2]0:[CuIIBr2]0 = 1:40:0.6:0.18:0.05. A dry 25 mL Schlenk flask was purged with nitrogen and charged with initiator 1 (200 mg, 0.98 mmol) and CuIIBr2 (11 mg, 0.05 mmol). A degassed solution of purified tBA (5.0 g, 39 mmol) and Me6TREN (40 mg, 0.17 mmol) was introduced into the flask via a nitrogen‐purged syringe. A pre‐degassed mixture of Sn(EH)2 (238 mg, 0.58 mmol) and acetone (5.6 mL) was then transferred into the reaction vessel. The reaction mixture was stirred under nitrogen for 10 min, and an initial aliquot was withdrawn for analysis. The flask was sealed and immersed in a thermostat‐controlled oil bath at 60°C, allowing polymerization to proceed for 3 h. Upon completion, the catalyst was removed by passing the reaction mixture through neutral alumina. The polymer was then precipitated in a cold methanol: water (80: 20 v/v) solution and dried under vacuum. The obtained PtBA was characterized by SEC, yielding a M n,SEC of 5.3 kg mol− 1 with a Đ of 1.06.

Synthesis of Linear Polyacrylic Acid

A solution of PtBA (2 g) in dichloromethane (50 mL) was cooled to 0°C under vigorous stirring. Trifluoroacetic acid (5 mL) was then added dropwise to the solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. After completion, the solvent was carefully evaporated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was precipitated in diethyl ether. After filtration, the solid was washed thoroughly with diethyl ether and subsequently dried in vacuo overnight to obtain the final product.

Synthesis of Cyclic Polyacrylic Acid

The cyclic PtBA used in this study was synthesized following our previously reported procedure [23]. A solution of cyclic PtBA (2 g) in dichloromethane (50 mL) was cooled to 0°C under vigorous stirring. Trifluoroacetic acid (5 mL) was then added dropwise to the solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 48 h. After completion, the solvent was carefully evaporated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was washed thoroughly with diethyl ether and subsequently dried in vacuo overnight to obtain the final product.

Synthesis of Interpenetrating PNIPAM Network with Linear or Cyclic PAA

Semi‐IPN hydrogels were synthesized by dissolving 0.85 g of NIPAM in 5 mL of deionized water along with 0.015 g of APS. Linear or cyclic PAA (55 mg) and MBAA (55 mg) were then added to the solution and stirred until completely dissolved. All polymerizations were carried out under ambient conditions. To initiate polymerization, 65 µL of TEMED was added, and the resulting solution was immediately poured into a petri dish prior to gel formation and left undisturbed for 12 h to ensure complete polymerization. The crosslinked hydrogels were washed by immersing them in deionized water with occasional stirring for one week. The water was refreshed daily to remove unreacted monomers, linear oligomers, and residual initiator fragments. The washing solution was concentrated under reduced pressure to remove water and further dried under vacuum. The dried sample was then dissolved in D2O, and 1H NMR measurements were performed.
