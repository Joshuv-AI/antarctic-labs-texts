Satellite observations reveal widespread alteration of river thermal regimes by US dams
Authors: Emily A. Ellis, George H. Allen, Christian E. Torgersen, Katie A. McQuillan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dams are well known to alter river thermal regimes, but assessments of downstream temperature changes have been constrained to single dams, single basins, or specific seasonal windows, thus limiting knowledge of their widespread impacts. We used satellite-based thermal infrared observations to quantify river surface temperature differences up- and downstream of 287 large dams in the United States from 2013 to 2024 across all seasons. We found downstream river temperature differences for the majority (71%) of longitudinal river profiles. These downstream changes were typically warmer (60%) and, on average, were sustained or continued to increase within 20 km downstream of the dam. We also found that the magnitude and frequency of downstream alterations varied by dam type, with 91% of extreme (≥±4°C) differences occurring at dams with reservoirs. This work documents the thermal effects of large dams on downstream ecosystems across all seasons on a national scale.

INTRODUCTION

Dams are a primary driver of anthropogenic alteration to river thermal regimes (1, 2). A river’s thermal regime refers to the timing, variability, and magnitude of water temperature changes at various temporal and spatial scales (3). Dams alter downstream thermal regimes by impeding the natural flow of water and altering mixing within the water column, which can lead to thermal heterogeneity, stratification, and the release of warm or cool water downstream. These dam-induced changes to downstream thermal regimes have wide-ranging consequences for aquatic ecology and biogeochemical processes (4, 5). Altering thermal regimes can prevent species from completing their life cycles (1, 3, 6), create favorable conditions for harmful algal blooms (7, 8), affect the success of fish spawning (9, 10), and affect a river ecosystem’s metabolism contributing to processes such as deoxygenation and the emission of CO2 and CH4 (11). Despite these known effects, on a broad scale, a baseline understanding of thermal conditions and their longitudinal variations within obstructed rivers remains relatively limited (12). Thus, the overall impact of large dams on the thermal regimes of US rivers is not well understood.

To identify the impact of dams on river temperatures, past studies have typically used numerical modeling approaches, site-specific thermal observations, or a combination of both. Several studies have developed or applied process-based or statistical models, which can be used to estimate alterations to downstream temperature regimes (13–16). While models are an effective means for providing spatially contiguous and temporally continuous water temperature estimates, they require site-specific calibration to simulate the complex thermal dynamics of rivers near dams. In situ water temperature observations, commonly taken at gages, can provide accurate, high-frequency measurements (17–19), but their collection is limited to discrete locations over a small fraction of the dam sites.

Alternatively, thermal infrared (TIR) satellite remote sensing offers a publicly available and spatially continuous option for empirically estimating river surface temperatures across larger areas and longer time periods (20–23). To date, the application of satellite-based methods for studying the thermal impact of dams has only focused on single dams or dams within single river basins (24–27). Although satellite remote sensing enables large-scale investigations of river surface temperature, its application is confined to wide rivers due to the coarse resolution of TIR sensors aboard civilian satellites (e.g., Landsat 7 ETM+ produced the finest scale imagery at 60 m) (20, 21). Regardless of this limitation, satellite TIR can offer previously unknown insights into broad-scale river thermal regimes.

Recently, several studies have investigated the thermal impact of dams by focusing on a few dams (≤10 dams) at a variety of locations, dam sizes, and dam types. Some studies have specifically highlighted downstream cooling (24) or downstream warming effects (17, 28). Other studies have found more complex effects of dams on downstream temperatures, including seasonality in downstream warming versus cooling (15, 29–32). These studies have attributed the downstream thermal changes to a variety of factors such as impoundment size, residence time, groundwater interactions, dam operations, discharge, climate, and local topography. While these studies have demonstrated that dams often play a role in shaping river thermal regimes, they have not characterized the overall impact of dams on downstream river temperatures beyond a case-by-case basis.

By focusing only on single dams, small regions, or constrained seasonal time frames, the current understanding of dam thermal impacts, at large, remains unknown. Given the importance of river temperature alterations for downstream ecosystems, the goal of this study is to track and understand the impact of large dams on thermal regimes in rivers by leveraging satellite observations throughout the United States. Using Landsat 8 TIR imagery, we seek to describe the differences in river temperatures downstream from dams, the extent of their alterations, and how those downstream effects vary seasonally.

RESULTS

Quantifying thermal impacts downstream from dams

To assess the broad-scale thermal impact of large dams, we identified dams in the United States located on rivers greater than or equal to 100 m in width (Fig. 1A). Across these 287 dams, predominantly located in the eastern half of the United States and Pacific Northwest, we extracted 11,868 longitudinal river surface temperature profiles from Landsat 8 TIR imagery from 2013 to 2024. The profiles capture a spatially continuous snapshot of river surface temperatures extending both upstream and downstream from the dams (Fig. 1B). We divided each profile into three main waterbody categories—upstream river, reservoir, and downstream river—to detect spatial variability in river surface temperatures and quantify downstream impacts (Fig. 1C).

Validating satellite-based river temperature estimates

To validate the satellite-based river surface temperature estimates, we compared Landsat derived values to in situ water temperature observations at United States Geological Survey (USGS) streamflow gage sites within individual Landsat scenes. Satellite-based observations only measure the temperature of the water’s surface, which can greatly differ from in situ measurements taken at depth if thermal stratification is present (21). The primary objective of this study is to track and understand the impact of dams on downstream river surface temperatures using the temperatures of upstream rivers as the preimpoundment benchmark; thus, we took a validation approach that best quantifies this relative relationship (i.e., do the comparisons between Landsat values match the direction and magnitude of temperature differences between USGS gages). We selected all in situ temperature sensors at gages on large rivers that fall within the same Landsat scene as at least one other gage (N = 87; Fig. 1A). For each unique gage pairing, we calculated the difference between the Landsat temperature estimates and the difference between the gages’ submerged temperature measurement taken within an hour of the corresponding satellite overpass (e.g., Fig. 2A).

In general, Landsat estimates correctly quantified the river temperature relationships between in situ measurements at the gages (Fig. 2B). The variability in this relationship is expected given the differences in scale of observation (100-m pixel versus in situ point measurement) and the potential differences between in situ gage depths, especially if the water column is stratified. Landsat observations accurately reflect intraimage temperature variability [root mean square error (RMSE) = 1.8°C, mean absolute error (MAE) = 1.3°C, bias = −0.3°C]. We found some degree of seasonality in accuracy; however, variations were minor, and RMSE remained <2.1°C across all four seasons (see section S1 for additional validation information). Landsat observations had a median width of 219 m, which is similar to the median width of the in situ validation sites at gages (228 m; Fig. 2C). We also found that the river temperature retrieval accuracy does not vary substantially by river width (table S1).

River surface temperatures are altered downstream from dams

We assessed the river temperature profiles both seasonally and by basic dam type (i.e., with or without a reservoir) to investigate the timing and potential influences of downstream river temperature differences. Seasonally, fall months had the greatest number of observable cloud- and ice-free profiles, while winter months had the fewest observable profiles (Fig. 3A). More than half of the profiles (55%) contained dams without a reservoir. Profile average temperatures followed a predictable seasonal cycle with the largest ranges in mean temperature occurring during the spring and fall months (Fig. 3A). Both dam types exhibited very similar average profile seasonality. However, comparing up- and downstream river temperature changes, notable differences are apparent between dam types (Fig. 3B). For example, we found that profiles with reservoirs exhibited greater downstream differences (means > ±1°C) and had wider ranges in magnitude than those without reservoirs (Fig. 3B and table S3). A total of 75% of moderate (± 2–4°C) and 91% of extreme (≥ ±4°C) downstream differences occurred on profiles with reservoirs (table S4).

We found that 71% of profiles (n = 8368) had a difference in temperature between the upstream and downstream river reaches. While downstream temperatures were typically warmer, 40% of profiles experienced cooling downstream of the dam. On average, the magnitude of downstream temperature changes varied with distance from the dam. Within the first 2-km downstream, the mean temperature differences range from 0.5° and 0.8°C warmer or 0.4° and 0.9°C cooler than their corresponding mean upstream river temperature. Downstream trends demonstrate that, across all seasons, mean downstream river temperatures either sustained their differences or continued to increase downstream from the dam (Fig. 3C). By 18- to 20-km downstream, the mean temperature differences range from 0.6° to 1.3°C warmer or 0.7° to 1.5°C cooler. The trends of increasing mean differences downstream were significant (significance level P < 0.05 hereinafter) for all seasons in cooler profiles and during the summer in warmer profiles.

River surface temperature deviations display thermal seasonality

To assess the spatiotemporal variation in river surface temperatures, we calculated temperature deviations (i.e., the differences between the Landsat temperature measurements versus the annual, profile, or upstream river mean) for each point along a longitudinal profile (see Materials and Methods). We then used these values to determine the mean temperature deviation for each waterbody category (i.e., upstream river, reservoir, and downstream river) within a longitudinal profile. For all waterbody categories, river surface temperature deviations exhibited some degree of thermal seasonality. Seasonal effects were dominant across all dam types and waterbody categories (Fig. 4, A and B).

Dam type influenced the magnitude and timing of temperature deviations from the profile and upstream river means. Deviations relative to the profile’s mean temperature revealed considerable differences between waterbody types (Fig. 4, C and D). Greater differences occurred along profiles containing dams with reservoirs. In the spring, reservoir deviations were considerably cooler than both the up- and downstream river reaches. These cooler reservoir deviations persisted through the summer months and early fall. The timing of significant downstream deviations differed by dam type. Profiles with reservoirs exhibited downstream deviations that were significantly warmer than their upstream rivers for all months except June, July, and August (Fig. 4C). Displaying muted and slightly shifted mean deviation patterns, profiles without a reservoir were also significantly warmer than their upstream rivers for all months except March, May, and June (Fig. 4D). The magnitude of downstream river deviations was notably larger when compared to the mean upstream river temperature (Fig. 4, E and F). These downstream deviations were more variable during the spring and summer months for all dam types. However, these mean downstream deviations had a much wider range of values for profiles with reservoirs (Fig. 4E).

DISCUSSION

Implications of altered river thermal regimes

By assessing the mean river surface temperature deviations of each waterbody category, we provide the first broad-scale, spatiotemporal assessment of dam impacts on river thermal regimes across the United States. We find that river surface temperatures, on average, change by ±0.7° to 1.4°C from upstream to downstream of dams and that the change tends to be warmer downstream (table S2). Previous studies have noted specific situations where downstream river temperature increases occur, such as during winter months or at diversion dams (33, 34). Our study is the first to identify widespread warming of river surface temperatures downstream from US dams, with broad consequences for cold-water organisms. For example, we find that the largest observed river temperature differences typically occur during spring and summer months (Fig. 4, E and F, and table S3), which can be critical periods for fish reproduction, growth, and survival (10, 35). In addition, water temperature is one of the primary factors controlling riverine water quality, and in cases with more extreme downstream temperature differences, biogeochemical processes and water quality are likely to be affected (1, 36–38). For instance, warming water temperatures have been shown to decrease dissolved oxygen (39, 40) and increase denitrification (41). Moreover, as climate change continues to compound the pressures placed on riverine ecosystems, it will become increasingly important to find ways to mitigate temperature changes within rivers (3, 4). Isaak et al. (42) noted the importance of protecting regions of cold-water refuge within river systems, which can act as a “climate shield” for cold-water species. Using remote sensing, we can identify instances of cooling downstream from large dams and provide a potential means of locating areas where important cold-water refuges can be created through dam operations (1, 3, 4).

Downstream differences and trends

Our assessment of downstream temperature differences falls within the range of values reported in smaller-scale case studies. While downstream temperature changes differ by location and reservoir type, continued downstream thermal impacts are typically reported between 20 and 50 km, with longer cases extending up to 100 km (18, 32, 43). Similarly, we find that mean temperature differences increase or remain stable for 20 km downstream and do not return to upstream river temperatures (Fig. 3C). The magnitude of the mean change that we observe (±0.5° to 1.5°C; Fig. 3C and tables S2 and S3) is also within the range of values in other studies assessing the thermal impacts of dam construction (31–33). Some studies have noted more extreme downstream differences (e.g., 4° to 6°C) (44) during summer months, which we also observe for 2% of our downstream differences (table S4) and can be seen in our example profiles (Fig. 1C) and temperature deviations (Fig. 4, E and F). While downstream temperature differences may be attributed to the seasonality of energy fluxes at the air-water interface and thermal inertia, the intense shifts we observed are most likely related to the presence and timing of thermal stratification and the temperature of the water being released from the dam (3, 15, 45). For example, cold-water releases from the bottom of the reservoir would cool downstream river temperatures, whereas water released from the spillway would warm downstream temperatures.

River temperature differences continue to increase farther downstream from the dam without returning to their upstream temperature (Fig. 3C). This finding has not been previously observed on a broad scale. While gradual downstream warming in rivers can be common due to atmospheric heat exchange (45), some studies have noted that downstream thermal patterns can be highly variable (29). Where major disturbances occur along a river, the most notable impacts are thought to be present directly downstream, and then these effects gradually dissipate with increasing distance downstream (46). This phenomenon has been observed for other water quality parameters, such as suspended sediment and dissolved oxygen (47, 48). Large rivers have thermal regimes that are predominantly governed by heat exchange at the air-water interface and tend to equilibrate with air temperature in a downstream direction (1, 45). However, our results indicate that river surface temperatures downstream of US dams may not necessarily reach this equilibrium within 20 km post-dam but instead display a more complex set of longitudinal temperature patterns [Fig. 3C; (29)].

Most of the warmer profiles either maintain their temperature or continue warming. However, 40% of the profiles are cooler downstream and continue to cool. In these cases, the dam’s cold-water releases (likely being drawn from deeper within the water column) may be exacerbated and compounded downstream. The continued downstream cooling effects may be related to multiple factors including atmospheric heat exchange, advection from incoming tributaries, topographic shading, and the timing of Landsat’s midday overpass in relation to a thermal regime’s diel cycle (29, 45,49–52). Some studies have shown that groundwater inputs can also play a critical role in influencing downstream temperatures (49, 50). Even small dams have been shown to modify downstream groundwater table conditions (17), and dams located in watersheds with groundwater contributions may be less likely to warm downstream (19). Further research may help explain why these widespread downstream patterns occur. Our observations highlight immediate downstream impacts of dams, and these changes may have an irreversible effect on the thermal regimes of rivers, with consequences for water quality and riverine ecosystems tens of kilometers downstream. However, these effects on water temperature may be mitigated by dam type and strategic management practices (3).

Potential drivers of magnitude and frequency

Dam type and reservoir dynamics are likely drivers of the observed variability and magnitude of downstream thermal impacts. River temperature changes of the greatest magnitude occur downstream from dams with reservoirs (Fig. 3B and table S2). While we do not have explicit information about each dam’s intake depth, we do observe patterns consistent with hypolimnetic (i.e., bottom layer) releases on a broad scale, evidenced by the largest absolute temperature differences and variations during the summer when stratification is most likely (Fig. 4B) (53). Hypolimnetic releases may affect the relationship between water and air temperatures by altering interactions at the air-water interface of the downstream river (54, 55). Reservoirs can also introduce thermal variability by buffering heat exchange in upstream impoundments, which have higher thermal inertia than rivers (15); this is demonstrated in our profile temperature deviation results (Fig. 4C). During spring months, upstream river temperatures begin to warm, but the bulk temperature of the reservoir is still cool and has not completely mixed. Meanwhile, downstream river temperatures are warmer from the dam release and the influence of increasing solar radiation. This distinct up- and downstream difference becomes less pronounced in the transition from spring to summer. We also note instances where there is a transitional period or lag between winter and spring (e.g., February) and fall to winter (e.g., November).

All dam types have the potential to alter downstream river temperatures, but dams without reservoirs tend to have less dramatic downstream effects (Fig. 4, D and F). Dams without a reservoir may have thermal regimes similar to unregulated rivers; however, they still cause downstream river temperature changes (56). Although dams without reservoirs store less water than conventional storage dams, they still raise upstream water levels, regulate flow velocity, and modify residence times (4, 34, 57). These effects alter the mixing regime within the water column (45). Our results show the varying degrees of seasonal river stratification and mixing among dam types (table S2) that can be inferred from observing variation in satellite-based river surface temperatures. Moderate and extreme temperature alterations generally occur downstream of reservoirs (table S4) and have potentially detrimental ecological implications. These results underscore the importance of dam type in optimizing dam design, operations, and river management strategies.

A broader lens for river temperatures

Using satellite-based TIR remote sensing, we can identify the impacts of dams on river thermal regimes at a scale which would not be feasible using traditional methods. We found, on a broad scale, that river surface temperatures are frequently altered downstream of large U.S. dams. Overall, downstream temperature differences increased with distance from the dam. Longitudinal temperature profiles also exhibited distinct seasonality, with the largest downstream differences and variability occurring during the summer. These downstream differences, sustained over tens of kilometers, suggest a widespread alteration of important biogeochemical processes with implications for downstream riverine ecosystems. Our findings highlight the spatial and seasonal heterogeneity of downstream thermal impacts of dams and illustrate the utility of satellites to quantify this variability, even at locations without in situ water temperature sensors. Using remotely sensed data, we can investigate and understand the timing and drivers of downstream alterations. Although satellite TIR observations are temporally less frequent than other in situ monitoring methods, these estimates provide an unprecedented opportunity to improve the synthesis of broad-scale river temperature trends. Future applications of satellite-based monitoring of river surface temperatures may be used to guide management practices and estimate the potential impacts of climate change on managed river systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Extracting river surface temperature profiles from Landsat

To extract surface temperatures of river reaches adjacent to large dams, we first identified impounded rivers in the United States from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission River Database (SWORD) version 16 (58), derived from Landsat data (59), in ArcGIS Pro. We only considered rivers with a mean width of ≥100 m, corresponding to the smallest observable rivers by Landsat 8 TIR imagery (100-m resolution; see section S2) (Fig. 1A). We identified dams and locks, which span the entire channel width, located along these large rivers using the Global River Obstruction Database (60) and spatially joined the locations with the Hydropower Infrastructure – Lakes, Reservoirs, and RIvers (61) and National Inventory of Dams databases to identify dam types (i.e., with or without a reservoir). To estimate complete longitudinal river surface temperature profiles at each dam, we selected SWORD centerlines extending at least 50-km up- and downstream of the obstruction in R (62). If the dam’s reservoir extended more than 50 km upstream, then additional lengths of 20 km were added as needed to estimate river temperatures upstream of the uppermost point where the river flows into the reservoir. Up- and downstream centerlines were terminated where they intersect with another reservoir or obstruction. Because of upstream tributaries and the often dendritic shape of reservoirs, we used ArcGIS Pro to manually remove some spurious SWORD centerlines to create the shortest single, continuous centerline upstream of the dam.

Although SWORD generally provides accurate centerline locations and widths of rivers, these attributes can change over time, causing some Landsat-derived river surface temperature pixels to be contaminated by land surface temperatures. Therefore, to minimize the effect of mixed water and land pixels, we used the Google Earth Engine (GEE) Python API (63, 64) and a modified version of the RivWidthCloud algorithm (65) to extract dynamic river centerlines and measure river widths along SWORD rivers for each Landsat scene. For all cloud-free (<10% scene coverage) and ice-free images between 2013 and 2024, we extracted river surface temperatures from the dynamic river centerlines using the USGS Landsat 8 Level 2 Surface Temperature product (band 10) in GEE. We selected Landsat 8 for this analysis because of its ability to provide high spatial resolution imagery from a single sensor with more than a decade of continuous data. After extracting river surface temperatures from each centerline pixel, we aggregated the river temperature data back to their respective SWORD centerline nodes (spaced ~200 m apart, Figs. 1B and 5) using the mean of the five nearest RivWidthCloud observations. This step was necessary to retain the topology and node characteristics (e.g., river/reservoir classifications) from the SWORD dataset. For consistent comparisons, we only analyzed profiles containing at least 1 km of cloud-free upstream river and 1 km of downstream river available within a single satellite scene (e.g., Fig. 1C). The output resulted in a longitudinal river surface temperature profile collected at midday for each dam on a given date. We completed all processing and analysis of extracted temperature data using Python (see Fig. 5 for a conceptual workflow diagram).

River surface temperature analysis

To understand how dams influence downstream river surface temperature, we quantified the differences between up- and downstream river temperatures for each profile. We compared each profile’s temperature nodes in the upstream river reaches to those within 20-km downstream of the dam. We chose the distance of 20 km as a compromise between (i) ensuring adequate data availability and (ii) limiting external downstream influences on temperature (e.g., tributaries and environmental changes). We identified profiles with differences in upstream versus downstream river temperatures using a bootstrapped Kruskal-Wallis significance test (66, 67) and a threshold filter of P < 0.05 (see section S3). For each profile exhibiting altered downstream temperatures, we calculated the difference between the spatially averaged up- and downstream river surface temperatures to determine whether profiles were warmer or cooler downstream from the dam. To assess how downstream temperatures vary with distance, we also calculated the difference between the mean upstream river temperature and the temperature for each kilometer downstream (Fig. 5). We used the difference between the mean upstream temperature and each downstream kilometer to create seasonal averages across all warming and cooling profiles for investigating seasonal variability.

We analyzed spatiotemporal variation in river surface temperature by calculating temperature deviations (i.e., the differences between the Landsat temperature measurements versus the mean) along every profile from 2013 to 2024. We investigated three types of temperature deviations (i.e., annual, profile, and upstream river) and determined a mean deviation value for each waterbody category (i.e., upstream river, reservoir, and downstream river) within each profile (Fig. 5). Calculating these different deviation values allowed us to explore how profile temperatures vary seasonally and spatially within a single profile and in relation to upstream river temperatures. Last, to assess if deviations varied by waterbody type, we tested the waterbody categories’ mean deviations for significant differences by month using the Kruskal-Wallis significance test (66, 67).
