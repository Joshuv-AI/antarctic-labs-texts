Integrative transcriptomics and machine learning reveal key regulatory genes for meat quality traits in pigs
Authors: Shuya Ma, Xiezong Hu, Jianwei Yang, Kunpeng Shi, Xiaodong Zhang, Yueyun Ding, Xudong Wu, Mengting Zhu, Zongjun Yin, Xianrui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeat quality traits are typically regulated by multiple genes, each contributing a small effect. In this study, to pinpoint candidate genes involved in meat quality traits, we performed transcriptome profiles of porcine longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle and applied machine learning (ML) models to analyze RNA-seq data. We also carried out Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA), Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and functional validation of putative target genes to better support the biological relevance of our findings.ResultsIn this study, LD muscle samples were collected from 142 Huoshou Black (HSH) pigs and 191 Anqing Six-end-white (AQLB) pigs. Based on results of the estimated breeding values (EBV) analysis, of meat quality traits, we selected 101 HSH pigs and 99 AQLB pigs for transcriptomic analysis. Using an integrative analytical framework that combined ssGSEA and WGCNA, we identified 197 candidate genes 197 candidate genes. These genes were significantly associated with various metabolic pathways, including fatty-acid elongation and metabolism, amino-acid catabolism, protein turnover, and biosynthetic processes. To further refine the identification of key regulatory genes, we systematically evaluated ten ML models, ultimately selecting XGBoost, Random Forest, and Lasso Regression for subsequent analysis. This approach pinpointed CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 as the key regulatory genes. To investigate the functional roles of CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 in intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition, we established a porcine intramuscular adipocyte model via siRNA-mediated knockdown of either CYSLTR1 or LPCAT2. RNA-Seq analysis identified 339 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the siLPCAT2 group and 2,376 DEGs in the siCYSLTR1 group relative to the control. Heatmap analysis indicated that genes involved in triacylglycerol (TAG) biosynthesis were upregulated in the siCYSLTR1 group, but downregulated in the siLPCAT2 group. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that LPCAT2-associated DEGs were predominantly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and glycerolipid metabolism—pathways closely linked to cell proliferation, nutrient sensing, and lipid remodeling. In contrast, CYSLTR1-associated DEGs were significantly enriched in lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis, and adipocytokine signaling pathways—processes directly implicated in adipocyte differentiation, lipid storage, and inflammatory crosstalk within adipose tissue. Collectively, these findings elucidate distinct yet complementary regulatory roles for CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 in intramuscular adipogenesis and provide mechanistic support for targeting these genes to modulate IMF content and improve pork quality traits.ConclusionsIn conclusion, CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 were identified as pivotal regulatory genes governing IMF deposition. Functional enrichment and pathway analyses revealed that both genes exert their effects on IMF accumulation through the coordinated regulation of lipid metabolism–associated pathways—including fatty acid synthesis, triglyceride assembly, and phospholipid remodeling. These findings offer mechanistically grounded evidence and actionable biological insights for improving pork quality traits, particularly marbling and tenderness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12734-7.Keywords: Pigs, Meat quality traits, Transcriptome, Machine learning

Introduction

In recent years, ustained global economic growth has led to a continuous rise in the demand for high-quality pork [1, 2]. As a result, the focus of pork production has shifted from prioritizing “quantity” to emphasizing “quality“ [3]. Modern consumers have increasing demands for the nutritional value and flavor profile of meat products [4, 5]. Meat quality traits are influenced by the complex interplay of genetic, nutritional, environmental, and molecular factors [6–8]. Meat quality evaluation system primarily encompasses pH, meat color parameters (L*, a*, b* values), drip loss, and intramuscular fat (IMF) content [9]. These traits are regulated by a combination of biological and genetic factors that collectively determine the sensory characteristics and nutritional value of pork [10, 11].

As one of the world’s major centers of pig domestication, China possesses abundant genetic resources in indigenous pig breeds. The HSH and AQLB pigs are indigenous high-quality breeds native to Anhui Province, China. Shaped by both long-term natural selection and purposeful artificial breeding, these breeds have evolved distinctive meat-quality traits-particularly high reproductive efficiency, exceptional meat flavor, well-developed type I and IIa muscle fibers, and elevated IMF content-relative to imported lean-type commercial pigs [12, 13]. Systematic characterization of the molecular regulatory mechanisms and gene regulatory networks governing these meat-quality traits in HSH and AQLB pigs is therefore essential to uncover the genetic architecture underlying key phenotypes such as IMF deposition, fiber-type composition, and sensory quality.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has demonstrated significant potential for applications in genetic improvement programs for livestock and poultry [14–16]. However, conventional transcriptomics approaches harbor inherent limitations: they predominantly focus on genes exhibiting statistically significant differential expression, thereby risking the omission of genes with modest yet biologically consequential expression changes. This analytical bias impedes a holistic understanding of complex gene regulatory networks underlying quantitative meat-quality traits [17, 18]. In recent years, machine learning (ML) approaches have been widely employed to identify candidate genes associated with economically important traits in livestock and poultry. Growing evidence further indicates that these methods have considerable utility for gene prioritization and prediction [19]. For instance, Liu et al. conducted transcriptomic analyses of Songliao Black and Landrace pigs, by integrating multiple machine-learning models, identified FASN, APOD, and APOA1 as candidate biomarkers related to fat deposition [20]. In a related study, machine-learning analyses based on RNA-seq datasets implicated PLA2G6, MPV17, NUDT2, and ND4L as key regulators of IMF accumulation in pigs [21]. Beyond biomarker discovery, these approaches have also been widely widely adopted to evaluate economically relevant traits in livestock. For example, Wang et al. [22] integrated gradient boosting decision trees (GBDT) with Random Forest and achieved a marked improvement in the genomic prediction accuracy of reproductive traits in Rongchang pigs. In addition, a recent study developed four ML models based on environmental data to predict piglets’ physiological temperature responses, aiming to mitigate the adverse effects of heat stress and thereby improve animal health and productive performance [23].

To overcome the limitations of single-algorithm approaches, we developed an integrative analytical framework that synergistically combines ssGSEA scoring with multiple complementary ML algorithms-specifically Random Forest, Lasso regression, and XGBoost. This multi-layered strategy enables sensitive detection of subtle, coordinated expression shifts across functionally related gene sets, while cross-algorithm validation markedly enhances result robustness, reproducibility, and biological interpretability. Collectively, this framework provides a rigorous, data-driven foundation for deciphering the molecular architecture of pork quality traits and offers actionable insights to guide precision breeding and targeted improvement of meat quality.

Materials and methods

Animal and sample preparation

The animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Agricultural University (No. KJLL2025074). A total of 191 AQLB and 142 HSH pigs were reared at Anhui Agricultural University under standardised conditions, including controlled temperature of 15–23 °C, humidity of 75–85% and good ventilation. All animals were clinically healthy and had ad libitum access to feed and water. They were humanely sacrificed and sampled at seven months of age. LD muscle samples were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to prevent protein degradation, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis, including assessments of meat quality traits, RNA extraction, and IMF content measurement. The average pre-sacrifice live weight of the AQLB pig was 95 kg, while the HSH pig had an average pre-sacrifice live weight of 115 kg.

The IMF cells of pigs were obtained by the collagenase digestion method used in our previous experiments [24].

RNA extraction and RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from LD muscle samples using the Trizol regent. RNA integrity, purity, and concentration were assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA samples that met the quality control criteria underwent ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion and messenger RNA (mRNA) enrichment, followed by PCR amplification to construct complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries. High-throughput sequencing was performed on an MGIseq2000 platform using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) strategy. The sequencing process was conducted by Wuhan Frasergen Gene Technology Co., Ltd. Raw sequencing reads (RawReads) were subjected to quality control filtering to remove low-quality reads bearing adapters or exhibiting an N proportion exceeding 10%. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Sus scrofa v11.1) using the Hisat2 software.

ssGSEA

ssGSEA was performed using the GSVA package (version 1.46.0) in R. Fat deposition-related gene sets were downloaded and used to calculate enrichment scores for HSH pig samples. Based on the median score, the samples were categorized into high and low scoring groups.

Wilcoxon rank-sum test

DEGs between the scoring groups were assessed in R version 4.2.3 using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes with a log2 fold change ≥ 0.5, FDR < 0.05, and a P-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed and selected for analysis.

WGCNA

WGCNA was performed on the HSH pig transcriptome data using the WGCNA 1.72-1 R package. Prior to network construction, outlier samples were removed and low-expression genes were filtered. Combined with meat quality phenotype data, the soft-threshold parameter β was selected to ensure a balance between high scale-free topology fit (R2) and mean network connectivity. The topological overlap matrix (TOM) was calculated to construct a scale-free network, and co-expression network analysis was performed to identify functional modules and hub genes significantly associated with the traits.

Gene enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.2.3) in R. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) functions were employed to map DEGs and hub genes to KEGG/GO pathways. Pathways with a corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was subsequently performed using GO as the reference gene set, with the GO database as the reference for pathway enrichment. Pathways with a P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Construction of ML models

ML analysis was performed on the HSH dataset containing key variables using R version 4.2.3. Data preprocessing was conducted using the tidyverse package, where scoring groups were used as classification phenotypes and features were batch converted to floating-point types. The mlr3 package was utilized to establish ten ML models, including XGBoost, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Lasso, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Glmnet, and Classification Tree. An internal and external cross-validation strategy was implemented to assess various model performance metrics, including area under the curve (AUC), classification error (CE), accuracy (ACC), recall, sensitivity, and specificity. Based on these evaluation results, the XGBoost, Lasso, and Random Forest models, which exhibited superior predictive performance, were selected for subsequent analysis. The ggplot2 package was employed to visualize the model evaluation results.

In this study, a stratified random partitioning strategy was employed, with a nested 10-fold cross-validation approach. The outer 10-fold cross-validation was used to assess model generalization performance, while the inner 10-fold cross-validation was utilized for hyperparameter tuning. A systematic comparison was conducted on ten different ML models. The dataset was divided into 10 folds, with each iteration using 9 folds for training and 1 fold for validation.

Model interpretation using SHapley additive explanations (SHAP)

In this study, SHAP values were computed for the XGBoost model’s output to assess the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions. The shap.score.rank function was utilized to compute SHAP values for each feature using a pre-trained XGBoost model and the training dataset. This approach quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction outcomes. To ensure consistency in comparison across features, the std1 method was adopted to standardize the feature values, converting them to a common range.

Correlation analysis

The expression levels of key genes in the HSH and AQLB pig datasets were calculated using the ggpubr 0.6.0 R package. The correlation coefficients and significance values for the key genes were subsequently determined for each dataset.

Validation of key genes

IMF content in the HSB and AQLB pig populations was measured using the SOX406 fat analyzer. Subsequently, median-based grouping was employed to select individuals without outliers for statistical analysis. The expression levels of key genes within each group were then assessed, and the results were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

siRNA transfection

The cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured until reaching 60%-80% confluency. siRNA and Lipofectamine 2000 were then diluted in Opti-MEM medium at the specified ratio, thoroughly mixed, and left at room temperature for 20 min to form transfection complexes. The resulting transfection mixture was gently added to the cell culture wells, ensuring uniform distribution, and incubated under standard conditions for 24–48 h. After 24 h of transfection, the gene silencing efficiency was assessed using qRT-PCR, while the protein expression levels of the target gene were analyzed 48 h post-transfection using western blot and other appropriate methods.

qRT-qPCR assays

qRT-PCR was carried out using a CFX96 real-time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad) and iQ SYBR Green Supermix (1708880, Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. A melting curve was created for each amplicon to verify its accuracy. The levels of target mRNAs were normalized to those of β-actin. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates for 48 h, washed with PBS, and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010; Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor mixture (04693132001; Roche Diagnostics Ltd., Mannheim, Germany). Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010; Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (04693132001; Roche Diagnostics Ltd., Mannheim, Germany). (R011470, Epizyme), LPCAT2 (15082-1-AP, Proteintech), CYSLTR1 (ER63188, HUABIO) β-actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (CW0098, CW Biotech, Beijing, China, 1:1,000), and goat anti-rabbit antibody (CW0103, CW Biotech, 1:2,000). Relative protein abundance, normalized to β-tubulin, was analyzed using ImageJ soft ware.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (v.8.4.3, La Jolla, CA, USA). The values were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05 for all groups), and homogeneity of variances by the Brown–Forsythe test (p > 0.05). Differences between the two groups were assessed by an unpaired Student’s t-test. For experiments involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test were applied. Data were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05 (***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05).

Identification of DEGs

Following data normalization and the filtration of low-expression genes in HSH pigs, differential expression analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with thresholds of |Fold-Change| > 1.4 and P-value < 0.05. As illustrated in Fig, 1D, a total of 1,075 DEGs were identified. Among which 271 genes (25.21%) were upregulated and 804 genes (74.79%) were downregulated in the high-score group.

Identification of candidate genes

In traditional single-dimensional studies, identifying major effect genes that regulate meat quality traits is often challenging. This study employed WGCNA to screen key genes associated with meat quality traits. Initially, raw count data were normalized and batch effects were removed. Subsequently, we performed WGCNA using 15,054 genes from 50 HSH pigs, incorporating meat quality parameters including meat color, drip loss, pH value, and IMF content. This analysis resulted in the identification of five co-expression modules. Notably, the ME turquoise module genes were found to be significantly positively correlated with the IMF and meat color L values in HSH pigs (Fig. 1E). AD cross-analysis was performed between the 2,258 genes in the ME turquoise module and the 1,075 DEGs, yielding a total of 197 candidate genes (Fig. 1F).

Enrichment analysis of candidate genes

Functional enrichment analysis of the 197 candidate genes revealed several critical pathways associated with meat quality regulation. KEGG pathway analysis identified 20 significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05). Among these, the fatty acid elongation pathway (ssc00062) and glutathione metabolism pathway (ssc00480) likely play pivotal roles in lipid metabolism and antioxidant regulation (Fig. 2A).

GO enrichment analysis further substantiated these findings. At a significance threshold of P < 0.05, the metabolic process of unsaturated fatty acids (GO:0033559) and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (GO:0006636) were significantly enriched, suggesting a central role for these candidate genes in IMF deposition (Fig. 2B).

Notably, GSEA revealed a pronounced upregulation of protein metabolism pathways, which was corroborated by the enrichment of cyclin-dependent protein kinase activity regulation (GO:1904029) and the transforming growth factor-β receptor signaling pathway (GO:0017015) (Fig. 2C).

Construction of ML models and identification of key genes

The mlr3 package in R 4.2.3 was employed to evaluate the performance of ten ML models using candidate genes as predictive variables. The evaluation included the following models: XGBoost, SVM, Random Forest, Naïve Bayes, Logistic Regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), KNN, Glmnet, and Classification Tree. Key performance metrics were computed for each model, including ACC, AUC, CE, precision, recall, sensitivity, and specificity. Based on their superior performance, three models—XGBoost, Random Forest, and Lasso Regression—were selected for further investigation (Fig. 2D). The results indicate that XGBoost demonstrated moderate and balanced performance across various metrics. The Random Forest model performed slightly better, exhibiting higher predictive performance. Lasso displayed strong balancing capabilities, making it suitable for handling predictions of both positive and negative classes.

The Random Forest model yielded an AUC of 0.844, with the model stabilizing when the out-of-bag (OOB) and CE reached 0.16 (Fig. 3A). This model identified 20 key genes, including CYSLTR1, LPCAT2, and STK17B (Fig. 3B). The Lasso Regression model achieved an AUC of 0.831 and selected 28 key genes, including CYSLTR1, LPCAT2, CIP2A, and SLAMF (Fig. 3C and D). The XGBoost model exhibited the most accurate predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.902 (Fig. 3E), identifying 30 key variables, including LPCAT2, NMI, IFI44L, PGAP6, ECM2, among others (Fig. 3F).

To further elucidate the importance of features within the XGBoost model, SHAP was employed. Among the 30 variables selected by XGBoost, 15 genes including LPCAT2, NMI, IFI44L, PGAP6, and ECM2 exhibited high SHAP values (Fig. 3G). The expression levels of LPCAT2, NMI, IFI44L, ECM2, CYSLTR1, and XAF1 were negatively correlated with undesirable meat quality traits, whereas PGAP6, ADORA1, and GPX1 exhibited a positive correlation with favorable trait outcomes. A comparative analysis of the three models (Fig. 3H) revealed that several genes, (such as LPCAT2, PGAP6, and CYSLTR1) were consistently identified as key regulators across all models. This consistency suggests that these genes may play a crucial role in the molecular mechanisms governing meat quality regulation.

Functional validation of key genes

LPCAT2 and CYSLTR1 were selected as key genes for subsequent analysis and validation. Using HSH pigs as the training set and AQLB pigs as the validation set, we investigated the expression differences of these key genes across different scoring groups. The results demonstrated that the expression levels of both LPCAT2 and CYSLTR1 were significantly higher in the low-scoring group compared to the high-scoring group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, strong positive correlations were observed for these key genes in both the training(R = 0.83, P＜0.001) and validation sets (R = 0.83, P＜0.001) (Fig. 4A & B).

In the analysis of tissue-specific gene expression, the expression levels of LPCAT2 and CYSLTR1 were significantly higher in adipose tissue compared to muscle tissue (Fig. 4D & E). Further stratification of the samples based on IMF content revealed that, regardless of whether the training set or the validation set was used, the expression levels of LPCAT2 and CYSLTR1 were significantly lower in the high IMF content group compared to the low fat content group (Fig. 4F).

A heatmap of triglyceride-metabolism gene expression indicated reciprocal regulation of triacylglycerol (TAG) biosynthetic transcripts: they were upregulated in the siCYSLTR1 group and downregulated in the siLPCAT2 group (Fig. 5H). To delineate the transcriptional consequences for lipid anabolism, we quantified mRNA levels of LEP, MBOAT7, SCD5, FASN, PLIN3, FABP5, ACSL4, and PRKAA1. qRT-PCR showed that LPCAT2 knockdown significantly decreased LEP, MBOAT7, SCD5, and FASN transcripts (Fig. 5I), while CYSLTR1 knockdown significantly reduced PLIN3, FABP5, ACSL4, and PRKAA1 (Fig. 5J), consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results. Discussion

In recent years, ML has been increasingly integrated into bioinformatics, driving substantial advances in gene expression modeling, systems-level interpretation, and regulatory network inference [25, 26]. Within animal science, ML methodologies have demonstrated robust utility in genomic selection and the prediction of economically relevant traits-including growth rate, feed efficiency, and reproductive performance [27]. Notably, ML-based predictive models consistently outperform traditional statistical methods in accuracy when forecasting porcine growth and reproductive phenotypes [28].

In the present study, we integrated transcriptomic profiling with multiple ML approaches to systematically identify functional genes associated with meat quality traits. By combining ssGSEA and WGCNA within an integrative analytical framework, we identified 197 candidate genes that were significantly associated with key biological pathways, including fatty acid elongation, amino acid degradation, and protein metabolism. These pathways are closely related to lipid deposition, muscle fiber characteristics, and postmortem metabolic processes, all of which are critical determinants of porcine meat quality. The enrichment of genes in these pathways underscores the complex molecular networks underlying variation in meat quality traits. To further prioritize key regulatory genes with predictive potential, we performed a comprehensive evaluation of ten ML algorithms. Using multidimensional performance metrics—such as accuracy, precision, and specificity—we identified three robust models (XGBoost, Random Forest, and Lasso) for categorical prediction. The convergence of these models led to the identification of two key gene features, CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2. Notably, both genes have been implicated in lipid metabolism and inflammatory or immune-related processes, suggesting potential mechanistic links between metabolic regulation and meat quality phenotypes. These findings provide new insights into the molecular basis of meat quality and offer promising candidate biomarkers for genetic improvement and precision breeding strategies.

We employed developed an integrative analytical framework to overcome the limitations of traditional methods, which often fail to detect genes exhibiting moderate expression changes yet exerting substantial biological effects. Identifying an optimal model that is suitable for variable selection and can accurately estimate variable importance is a critical prerequisite for the successful implementation of this study [29]. In this study, after a systematic comparison of ten ML algorithms, we first excluded models with inadequate stability (e.g., linear discriminant analysis, LDA) as well as models that were not subjected to the prescribed training and validation procedures (e.g., k-nearest neighbors, KNN). Next, using elevated classification CE as the exclusion criterion, we further removed Logistic Regression, SVM, and Naive Bayes. Among the remaining five models, two similar algorithms, glmnet and Lasso, both utilized lambda as the optimal parameter for variable selection; however, due to glmnet exhibited lower computational efficiency in the R environment and functional redundancy relative to Lasso, it was excluded. Ultimately, based on a comparison of AUC values (which represent training accuracy), three models—XGBoost, Lasso, and Random Forest—with higher AUC values of 0.79, 0.88, and 0.88, respectively, were selected.

The three ML models selected in this study—XGBoost, Lasso, and Random Forest—have been extensively and robustly applied to binary classification tasks. For instance, XGBoost has been employed to construct predictive models for non-small cell lung cancer, in which clinical characteristics and biomarkers were incorporated as explanatory variables to quantitatively assess their effects on patient prognostic outcomes [30]. Lasso, by imposing regularization, can effectively reduce model complexity and mitigate overfitting, thereby providing strong support for the selection of key predictive features and the identification of disease-associated core genes [31]. Similarly, Random Forest has demonstrated substantial utility in epidemiological surveillance; by comparing recent case reports with actual confirmed cases across 190 countries and regions, it contributes to the early detection and prevention of epidemic outbreaks [32].

LPCAT2, a member of the long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase family, plays a pivotal role in the biosynthesis of phospholipids enriched with multiple unsaturated fatty acids, thereby providing essential substrates for lipid peroxidation reactions [33]. By modulating fatty-acid metabolic pathways, LPCAT2 positively regulates lysophospholipid metabolism and ultimately influences IMF deposition [34]. Additionally, LPCAT2 and its associated metabolites are predominantly enriched in pathways related to steroid hormone biosynthesis, unsaturated fatty-acid biosynthesis, and sphingolipid metabolism [35]. These findings provide valuable insights for elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying unsaturated fatty-acid synthesis in AQLB and HSH pigs, and they establish a solid theoretical basis for the genetic improvement of meat-quality traits. In the present study, GSEA further idicated that LPCAT2 may contribute to the regulation of IMF content by modulating steroid metabolic processes.

CYSLTR1 is a key gene encoding member of the G protein-coupled receptor 1 family. In mouse models, mutations in CYSLTR1 have been reported to significantly increase body length, tail length, grip strength, and locomotor activity [36]. Similarly, CYSLTR1 as a potential candidate gene associated with body size traits in the Yangyuan donkey [37]. Collectively, these findings suggest that CYSLTR1 may represent a potential target involved in improving growth performance and promoting muscle development in animals. Moreover, as a central mediator of inflammatory lipid-signaling pathways [38], CYSLTR1 has been clearly implicated in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis [39]. In this study, CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 were found to be strongly correlated across multiple datasets, with enrichment analyses indicating involvement in steroid hormone regulation. This may provide insight into the potential role of CYSLTR1 in modulating IMF content.

Triglycerides constitute the primary component of IMF, the deposition of which is regulated by a multitude of physiological and molecular factors. The MAPK signaling pathway includes p38, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (Erk), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (Jnk) pathways, which play a crucial role in the development of adipocytes and muscle intramuscular adipocytes [40, 41]. Additionally, the mTOR pathway governs the balance between lipid synthesis and degradation in muscle tissue and is closely interconnected with the MAPK pathway and other signaling pathways. mTORC1 regulates lipid generation and metabolism through mediators such as SREBP1 [42, 43]. Unsaturated fatty acids are critical for maintaining membrane fluidity and facilitating metabolic processes in adipocytes. Their biosynthesis involves key enzymes, such as fatty acid desaturase (FADS), which influence the fatty acid composition of meat. The regulation of unsaturated fatty acids intersects with the MAPK and mTOR pathways, as these molecules modulate signaling processes that control lipid synthesis and storage [44, 45]. This study identified DEGs, including CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2, which regulate the expression of genes involved in TAG synthesis and subsequently impact IMF content. Mechanistic analyses revealed that DEGs are primarily involved in fat metabolism-related pathways, such as the MAPK signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycerolipid metabolism, lipid and atherosclerosis pathways, and adipocytokine signaling pathways. These pathways collectively influence IMF accumulation.

Although the machine-learning models employed in this study demonstrated strong predictive performance, several limitations should be acknowledged. These primarily include the relatively small sample size and the narrow range of pig breeds examined, which may reduce the generalizability and extrapolative value of the findings across diverse pig populations. To address these constraints, future studies should prioritize expanding the sample size and increasing breed diversity, while integrating more comprehensive phenotypic and genomic datasets. Such efforts are expected to further improve model accuracy and robustness, thereby providing a stronger foundation for subsequent research.

In summary, this study employed three ML algorithms-XGBoost, Lasso, and Random Forest-to identify and predict candidate genes associated with IMF content in indigenous Anhui pig breeds. Among the identified genes, CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 emerged as key co-regulatory molecules. These findings provide a scientific foundation for genetic improvement of pork quality traits and offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying meat quality regulation.

Conclusion

This study utilized the ssGSEA algorithm to group local pig populations and perform a Wilcoxon test identifying 1075 DEGs associated with fat deposition. Based on WGCNA, five co-expression modules were obtained, along with the identification of 2257 key genes, from which 197 candidate genes were selected. Various ML models were employed for evaluation, with three models-XGBoost, Lasso, and Random Forest-demonstrating the highest accuracy. Combined analysis led to the identification of 12 major functional genes associated with IMF content in local pigs, which include OGN, C1QTNF3, GHSR, SNX10, FADS3, PGAM1, B3GAT1, ADORA1, NOD2, ECM2, ASAH1, and LDLR. Most of these genes are implicated in lipid metabolism, lipid peroxidation, fatty acid metabolism, and adipocyte differentiation. Among them, CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 were identified as genes with significantly different mRNA expression levels between the high IMF and low IMF groups. In the porcine intramuscular adipocyte model with CYSLTR1 and LPCAT2 gene knockdown, heatmap analysis of DEGs related to triglyceride metabolism revealed that the expression of TAG synthesis-related genes was upregulated in the siCYSLTR1 group, whereas it was downregulated in the siLPCAT2 group. Mechanistically, LPCAT2 and CYSLTR1 were primarily involved in several lipid metabolism-related pathways, including the MAPK signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycerolipid metabolism, lipid and atherosclerosis, and the adipocytokine signaling pathway, thereby influencing IMF content.
