Trajectory Analysis of Pediatric Longitudinal Allergen Sensitization Patterns From Real World Data
Authors: Sungyun Kim, Minjeong Lee, Ji Soo Park, Jaehee Gung, Siyeon Yi, Dong In Suh, Kwangsoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the relationship between allergen sensitization patterns and allergic disease diagnosis, with a particular focus on longitudinal sensitization trajectories. Using a large pediatric and young adult cohort from a common data model (CDM)-formatted clinical database, we applied unsupervised learning techniques to identify both static and dynamic sensitization clusters. We developed a predictive model to assess how early sensitization profiles contribute to developing allergic disease later in life.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study using electronic health records from the Seoul National University Hospital CDM. Patients who underwent a skin prick test, multiple allergen simultaneous test, or ImmunoCAP between 2002 and 2021 were included if they were tested in 2 or more age groups. Seventeen allergen categories were analyzed. Latent class analysis identified cross-sectional sensitization clusters, and Gaussian mixture models were used to derive longitudinal trajectories. A Categorical Boosting multilabel classification model was used to predict allergic diagnoses with interpretability enhanced by SHapley Additive exPlanations.ResultsA total of 1,656 patients were analyzed. Four cross-sectional clusters were identified (house dust mite [HDM]-dominant, HDM + food, polysensitization, and multiple inhalant clusters). Nine longitudinal trajectories showed a developmental shift from food sensitization to aeroallergen sensitization. Early HDM sensitization was strongly associated with subsequent asthma, whereas eczema and food allergies are linked to food or polysensitization trajectories. Rhinitis was common across aeroallergen clusters. The prediction performance was robust for asthma (F1 score, 0.73; area under the curve, 0.80), moderate for eczema and food allergies, and limited for rhinitis.ConclusionsLongitudinal sensitization trajectories, particularly early HDM dominance, were predictive of asthma development, whereas the predictive values for eczema, food allergies, and rhinitis were weaker. Modeling sensitization history using machine learning may enable the early identification of high-risk children and inform personalized strategies for allergy prevention.Keywords: Hypersensitivity, allergens, food allergy, asthma, dermatitis, rhinitis, machine learning

INTRODUCTION

Allergic diseases, such as asthma, eczema, rhinitis, and food allergies, are among the most common chronic conditions in children and young adults, with an increasing prevalence worldwide.1,2,3,4 A key feature of these disorders is allergen sensitization, which often precedes the clinical manifestation of allergic diseases and may evolve with age and environmental exposure. Several studies have investigated the cross-sectional associations between allergen sensitization and allergic disorders; however, the impact of evolving sensitization patterns—especially in early life—on subsequent disease risk remains poorly understood.5,6

The advent of machine learning methods and access to longitudinal clinical data offer new opportunities to model and predict these complex relationships.7,8,9 Although previous studies have identified age-specific prevalence and cluster-based sensitization profiles, only a few have systematically evaluated how the temporal trajectories of allergen sensitization influence the development of allergic conditions.10 Understanding these longitudinal patterns could enable earlier identification of at-risk individuals and support more personalized approaches to allergy prevention and management.11,12

This proof-of-concept study aimed to explore the relationship between allergen sensitization patterns and allergic disease diagnosis, with a particular focus on longitudinal sensitization trajectories. Using a large pediatric and young adult cohort from a common data model (CDM)-formatted clinical database, we applied unsupervised learning techniques to identify both static and dynamic sensitization clusters. We developed a predictive model to assess how early sensitization profiles contribute to the risk of developing allergic disease later in life.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical considerations

This single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study used electronic health records from Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH). This study used data formatted under the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM), which enables standardized and interoperable healthcare research. All data were pseudonymized before analysis in accordance with the Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidelines, and no personally identifiable information was included. Ethical approval was obtained from the SNUH IRB, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the deidentified nature of the data.

Study population and inclusion criteria

The study population comprised pediatric and young adult patients who underwent allergen sensitization testing, including the skin prick test (SPT), multiple allergen simultaneous test (MAST), and specific immunoglobulin E (sIgE; ImmunoCAP) testing, at SNUH between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2021. Patients who underwent their first allergen sensitization test before the age of 30 years and their second test before the age of 40 years were included. Patients who tested positive to at least one antigen were included. Age at testing was categorized into 6 groups: group 1 (0–2 years), group 2 (3–5 years), group 3 (6–11 years), group 4 (12–19 years), group 5 (20–29 years), and group 6 (30–39 years). Patients in at least 2 age groups were included if they had undergone allergen testing (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Allergen sensitization testing

The patients underwent at least one of the following 3 allergen tests: SPT, MAST, or ImmunoCAP. The SPT included either inhalant allergens or a combination of food and inhalant allergens, depending on the order of testing. The SPT was considered positive if the mean wheal diameter was ≥ 3 mm and exceeded that of the histamine control. Allergens that were not tested were considered negative. MAST (Advansure; LG Life Sciences, Seoul, Korea) is a semi-quantitative assay for multiple allergens that detects allergen-sIgE antibodies in human serum. Results of MAST were reported in 7 classes (0–6), with a class ≥ 1 defined as positive. Allergen panels varied slightly by testing year; untested allergens were regarded as negative. Because allergen testing in routine clinical practice is guided by clinical history and pre-test probability rather than performed exhaustively, untested allergens were assumed to reflect low expected positivity, corresponding to a missing not at random (MNAR) mechanism. The ImmunoCAP system (Phadia; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden) was used for the quantitative measurement of allergen-sIgE to single allergens. The results were reported in standardized units (kU/L) with a dynamic range of 0.1–100 kU/L. A value of ≥ 0.35 kU/L was considered positive. Allergens not tested were considered negative. For the analysis, the individual allergens included in the SPT, MAST, and ImmunoCAP were consolidated into 17 allergen categories (Supplementary Table S1). Allergy tests were grouped according to age intervals, and all tests performed within the same age group were treated as single testing episodes. If a patient underwent multiple tests within the same age group, sensitization to an allergen was defined as positive if any test within that age group yielded positive results.

Diagnostic classification

Clinical diagnoses of asthma, eczema, food allergies, rhinitis, and other conditions were extracted from the CDM using concept sets defined in OMOP and cross-mapped to the corresponding Korean Standard Classification of Diseases codes (Supplementary Table S2). If a patient received one or more relevant diagnostic codes at any point within a given age group, all corresponding diagnoses were considered present for that age group. In the absence of any diagnostic codes in an age group, the diagnosis was classified as missing for that age group.

Clustering of cross-sectional allergen sensitization pattern

A cluster analysis of allergen sensitization was conducted using data from 17 allergen categories across all test modalities. Latent class analysis (LCA) was used to objectively identify latent subgroups without prior assumptions. LCA is well-suited for analyzing both categorical and continuous variables and estimating the probability of cluster membership for each individual, thereby accounting for uncertainty in the classification. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), using the elbow point of the model-fit curve as an additional reference. Based on these criteria, a 4-cluster solution was selected for this study. To evaluate the robustness of the MNAR assumption for untested allergens, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which untested allergens were treated as missing values and the clustering analysis was repeated using allergens with acceptable missingness (< 70%).

Allergen sensitization longitudinal trajectory analysis

For longitudinal trajectory analysis, 4 clusters of allergen sensitization were identified among patients in groups 1–3. In age group 4, 72.7% of the data from the 1,656 patients were missing, rendering a meaningful statistical analysis for this group impossible. A total of 937 patients (1,918 test cases) with at least 2 allergen test results were included in this analysis. Missing values were imputed using probability distributions derived from the overall dataset. Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) were used to classify allergen sensitization trajectories based on changes across age groups.13 Unlike hard clustering methods such as K-means, GMM provides soft clustering by assigning probabilities to each data point belonging to multiple clusters. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the AIC and BIC, with the elbow point of the graph used as a reference. Based on these criteria, 9 longitudinal sensitization trajectories were selected for this study.

The Monte Carlo Fisher’s exact test was performed to determine differences in the incidence of allergic disorders in age group 3 across trajectory groups, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted.

Allergic disorder prediction model

To assess the association with allergic disorders in a given age group, a χ2 test was conducted to determine whether the changes in allergen sensitization pattern clusters across age groups were independent of disease status. The results showed that changes in allergen clusters were not independent, supporting the validity of using the results from earlier age groups to predict outcomes in subsequent groups.

To predict the diagnoses of age groups 2, 3, and 4, the diagnoses of prior age groups and allergen sensitization cluster information from the prior and current age groups were used. A Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) algorithm was used for diagnostic prediction because of its effectiveness in handling categorical data.14 The model was trained using data from each age group to simultaneously predict multiple diagnosis classes. Hyperparameters, such as the learning rate (0.001), depth (8), and number of iterations (500), were optimized for performance. As a single patient can be diagnosed with multiple allergic disorders simultaneously, multiple diagnostic categories can be assigned to each patient. To accommodate this characteristic, the problem was approached as a multilabel classification task. The model performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. The average metrics across the folds were reported as the results. Accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), precision, recall, and F1 score were used as evaluation metrics. To enhance interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated to analyze the top 5 features influencing diagnosis predictions.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as numbers (percentages) or medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]). The glca package in R version 4.3.3 was used for LCA of allergen sensitization pattern clustering. The scikit-learn package in Python version 3.10.16 was used for GMM analysis in the allergen sensitization trajectory analysis. To train the allergic disorder prediction model, the scikit-learn and CatBoost packages in Python version 3.10.16 were used.

RESULTS

Study population

The study included 1,656 patients with a median age of 5 years (IQR, 2–11 years) at the first test. Among these patients, 65.9% were male. A total of 3,402 allergy tests were conducted at a median age of 7 years (IQR, 4–14 years). The types of allergy tests administered included SPT (16.2% of patients, 21.0% of total tests), MAST (70% of patients, 56.6% of total tests), and sIgE testing (13.8% of patients, 22.4% of total tests). The most frequently diagnosed allergic disease was rhinitis (47.2% of patients, 54.4% of total tests), followed by respiratory diseases (41.2% of patients, 39.4% of total tests). Eczema (24.2% of patients, 24.0% of total tests) and asthma (20.0% of patients, 23.5% of total tests) were the most frequently diagnosed diseases (Supplementary Table S3).

Allergen sensitization pattern cluster

Allergy test results were analyzed to classify patients into 4 sensitization clusters (Fig. 1). Cluster 1 was mainly highly sensitized to house dust mites (HDMs) (69.3%), with other minor sensitizations to aeroallergens, including animal dander (12.2%), tree pollen (11.6%), and weed pollen (10.5%). Cluster 2 was sensitized to common food allergens, including eggs (19.8%), grains (13.1%), legumes (16.5%), meat (27.1%), milk (15.9%), and shellfish (21.6%) while also being sensitized to HDM (34.8%). Cluster 3 was polysensitized to all 17 allergen categories, with a sensitization rate of over 50% for all categories except nuts (29.0%). Finally, cluster 4 had a high rate of sensitization to multiple aeroallergens, including HDM (44.0%), mold (11.2%), animal dander (12.2%), tree pollen (13.1%), weed pollen (12.6%), grass pollen (8.5%), and insects (7.5%) (Fig. 1).

Cross-sectional analysis of allergic disease prevalence by clusters

The prevalence rates of allergic diseases, including asthma, eczema, food allergies, and rhinitis, were compared among the allergen sensitization clusters in age groups 1–5. In all age groups, asthma was most prevalent in cluster 1, which was mainly sensitized to HDM. The prevalence of asthma in cluster 1 was 32.4%, 44.6%, 46.7%, 28.8%, and 22.9% in age groups 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. Eczema showed the highest prevalence in cluster 2 in age groups 1, 2, 3, and 5 (56.7%, 52.0%, 41.7%, and 60.7%, respectively), and in cluster 3 in age group 4, with a prevalence of 48.6%. Food allergies showed the highest prevalence in cluster 3 for age groups 1, 2, and 3, with prevalence rates of 29.7%, 27.0%, and 31.0%, respectively. In age group 5, cluster 2 had a higher prevalence of food allergies than the other clusters. Clusters 1 and 4 demonstrated a higher prevalence of rhinitis than the other clusters (Fig. 3).

Allergen sensitization longitudinal trajectories

Cluster analysis of longitudinal allergen sensitization trajectories in age groups 1–4 yielded 9 trajectories (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S5). Differences were noted in the prevalence of allergic diseases in age group 3 according to allergen sensitization trajectories. Asthma in age group 3 was highly prevalent in trajectory 7 (78/131 patients), followed by trajectory 4 (33/66 patients). Trajectories 7 and 4 mainly had cluster 1 allergen sensitization patterns in age groups 2 and 3. Eczema in age group 3 was highest in trajectory 2 (42/103 patients), which mainly showed cluster 2 in age groups 1 and 2. Food allergies were highest in Trajectory 8 (42/148 patients), which mainly showed cluster 3 in age groups 1 and 2. Rhinitis was lowest in Trajectory 2 (36/103 patients), followed by Trajectory 8 (56/148 patients), and was highest in Trajectory 5 (121/162 patients), which shifted from variable clusters in age groups 1 to cluster 4 in age group 2, and finally to cluster 1 in age group 3 (Table 1). Differences in allergic disease prevalence according to allergen sensitization trajectories were observed in age groups 1 and 2 (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7).

Allergic diagnosis prediction model

A multilabel classification model using CatBoost was used to predict asthma, eczema, food allergies, and rhinitis. The model demonstrated robust overall performance across various metrics within the disease categories (Table 2). Asthma prediction achieved the highest F1 score (0.7262), with an accuracy of 0.8775 and an AUC of 0.8032, indicating strong discriminative power and a balanced performance between precision (0.8189) and recall (0.6545). Eczema prediction yielded moderate performance (F1 score 0.5955), with lower precision (0.5961) and recall (0.5989), suggesting greater difficulty in identifying true positives. Food allergy prediction achieved the highest accuracy (0.9550), but with moderate recall (0.5389) and F1 score (0.6347). Rhinitis prediction showed lower overall accuracy (0.6775) and AUC (0.6761), but relatively balanced precision (0.6449) and recall (0.6588). The prediction models for age groups 2 and 4 showed modest results, mainly due to insufficient data (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9).

DISCUSSION

This study identified distinct patterns and developmental trajectories of allergen sensitization in a large cohort of pediatric and young adult patients, demonstrating their association with various allergic disease diagnoses. Using LCA and GMM, we classified patients into cross-sectional sensitization clusters and longitudinal trajectories. Our findings revealed dynamic age-related transitions from food allergen to aeroallergen sensitization. These sensitization patterns, particularly the early dominance of HDM sensitization, were significantly associated with asthma diagnosis in a later stage. A predictive model using CatBoost further confirmed that prior sensitization profiles contributed to the risk of future allergic disorders, highlighting the clinical value of longitudinal sensitization tracking.

Previous studies have consistently shown that sensitization patterns change with developmental stage. Food sensitization predominates in infancy and early childhood, whereas aeroallergen sensitization increases during school age and adolescence.15 In a Korean population–based survey, HDM was the most frequent allergens across age groups, and sensitization was strongly associated with rhinitis and eczema.16 Longitudinal component-resolved studies further demonstrated that early life trajectories of IgE responses are critically important, with grass/cat or HDM sensitization patterns strongly predicting asthma and rhinitis at a later stage.15 Our findings align with these observations but extend them using hospital-based real-world CDM data and unsupervised machine learning to model sensitization trajectories at scale.

The clinical implications are particularly evident in asthma. HDM sensitization was a key driver of asthma in our cohort, which is consistent with the findings of previous pediatric studies in both the general and severe asthma populations.17,18 Multiple sensitizations, particularly polysensitization to aeroallergens and food components, contribute to severe asthma. Studies have reported that sensitization patterns alone do not distinguish between disease severities. These conflicting findings suggest that while sensitization is a necessary component of disease pathogenesis, non-IgE-mediated mechanisms and environmental exposure may modulate disease severity. Our results suggest that early sensitization trajectories may be more predictive of risk than static sensitization profiles at a single time point.

Our model showed lower predictive performance for eczema and food allergies. Previous studies have indicated that food-dominant or mixed sensitization clusters in early childhood are associated with eczema and food allergies.19,20,21 Longitudinal analyses of population-based cohorts have demonstrated heterogeneous trajectories, with some food-sensitized children remaining asymptomatic or outgrowing their allergies.5,10 These findings highlight that sensitization is an associated factor but not a sufficient predictor of persistent eczema or food allergy, which may explain the limited predictive performance of our model for these conditions.

Rhinitis showed limited predictive accuracy in our model, despite being the most common allergic diagnosis. Rhinitis is a heterogeneous disease with multiple endotypes, in which allergen sensitization interacts with type 2 inflammation, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and other host factors.22 Such complexity likely reduced the predictive contribution of sensitization trajectories in our hospital-based dataset. These findings suggest that while asthma risk is closely linked to sensitization trajectories, especially early HDM sensitization, other allergic diseases, such as eczema, food allergy, and rhinitis, may require the integration of additional clinical, immunological, or environmental variables to improve prediction.

This study has several strengths. First, it leveraged a large cohort comprising over 3,400 allergy tests conducted over 2 decades, allowing for a robust longitudinal analysis. Second, by structuring the data within the OMOP-CDM framework, our study demonstrates a scalable approach that facilitates future reproducibility and generalizability in multi-center or international contexts.23 Third, the study incorporated multi-modal allergen testing data, including SPT, MAST, and ImmunoCAP assays, providing a comprehensive view of sensitization profiles. Finally, the integration of advanced computational methods—combining LCA and GMM for clustering with CatBoost predictive modeling and SHAP-based explainable AI—facilitated clinical interpretability, generating actionable insights into how specific clusters contribute to allergic disease.

However, this study has certain limitations. The single-center retrospective design may limit the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Because this study was conducted in a tertiary care setting, clinical selection bias is likely to occur, resulting in an overrepresentation of patients with more severe or persistent symptoms and complex sensitization trajectories. The diagnoses were derived from administrative codes, raising the possibility of underreporting or misclassification. Important factors such as family history, socioeconomic status, and environmental exposure were not available and could not be included in the analysis. Variability in allergen panels over the study period introduced heterogeneity, and missing data in older age groups reduced the robustness of longitudinal analyses beyond adolescence. These limitations indicate that the findings must be interpreted cautiously and validated in prospective multicenter cohort studies.

This study demonstrated that allergen sensitization trajectories, particularly those involving the early dominance of HDM, were strongly associated with the development of subsequent asthma. In contrast, their predictive values were lower for eczema, food allergies, and rhinitis. By combining longitudinal data with machine-learning approaches, we demonstrated the feasibility of early risk prediction models based on sensitization patterns. These results underscore the importance of early-life monitoring of sensitization trajectories and suggest that integrating this information with additional clinical and environmental data could further enhance personalized allergy prevention and management.
