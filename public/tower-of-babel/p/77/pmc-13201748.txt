A proteome optimal allocation model for elucidating effects of temperature on bacterial growth
Authors: Deyu Wang, Qing Zhang, Hualin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough considerable research has been conducted on how bacteria respond to temperature, there are still few studies that use simple protein allocation models to characterize these responses. To bridge this gap, we utilize a protein allocation framework to delineate quantitative correlations among temperature, growth rate, and the fraction of functional protein sectors. By calibrating the model with experimental temperature-dependent growth curves of Escherichia coli, we predict how proteomic resources are reallocated across various temperatures. This approach enables us to elucidate, from a proteomic perspective, the temperature dependency of constitutive β-galactosidase expression and the variations in E. coli cell size. Our research deepens the understanding of bacterial physiological adaptation to temperature changes.Subject terms: Biophysics, Cell biology, Systems biology

Introduction

Bacteria in natural environments encounter diverse stressors, among which temperature is a particularly critical factor. Even slight variations in temperature can significantly impact ecosystems1. The effects of temperature on bacterial growth have been extensively studied for several decades2–8. A consistent observation is that the temperature-dependent growth curves exhibit strikingly similar shapes2 (Fig. 1) across nearly all bacterial species, irrespective of their optimal growth temperature. Within a moderate temperature range, commonly referred to as the normal range or Arrhenius region9, bacterial growth curves generally follow the Arrhenius equation:

This relationship suggests that growth within this range can be described as governed by a single rate-limiting reaction, while intrinsic physiological properties remain relatively stable. However, when temperatures exceed this normal range—known as the heat region—or fall below it (the cold region), bacteria activate distinct stress response mechanisms3–8, accompanied by a sharp decline in growth rate compared to the normal range (Fig. 1).

After half a century of dedicated research, the heat shock and cold shock responses in E. coli have been extensively characterized3–8,10. These findings provide a crucial foundation for temperature regulation in bioengineering11,12 and food safety applications13,14. Given the intrinsic complexity of bacterial temperature response mechanisms, it is both valuable and necessary to develop a simplified temperature-dependent model based on general principles, which will enhance our understanding of these processes10.

Previous models15–20 developed to explain temperature-dependent growth curves have primarily focused on traits within the normal range, frequently assuming that a single rate-limiting enzymatic reaction governs bacterial growth. Within the heat region, high temperatures might destabilize enzymes, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth. Alternative approaches have been limited to fitting empirical formulas without providing deeper explanations21–26. In this study, we apply a coarse-grained protein allocation model to quantitatively characterize the temperature-dependent growth curves of E. coli.

The process of cell growth can be viewed as a process of “self-replication,” and the cell is a “self-replicator.” A simple self-replicator is that ribosomes translate themselves as well as other proteins27. In the model of protein allocation, proteins are categorized into classes based on their functions, and bacteria adjust the composition of functionally diverse protein sectors in response to environmental changes28–35. Given the constraint of mass conservation, this model correlates the allocation ratios of different protein sectors with growth rates and environmental conditions, thereby offering a clear framework for delineating bacterial physiology. Such models have been further corroborated by quantitative proteomic data36,37. Moreover, from the protein allocation perspective, researchers have proposed several quantitative growth laws that describe bacterial physiology under steady-state conditions28–35,37.

It is reasonable to expect that in E. coli, the allocation of functionally distinct protein sectors depends on temperature8,38. In our previous work29, we developed a protein allocation model that explicitly incorporated a chaperone sector to account for protein allocation under nonlethal protein maturation stress. In the present study, we extend this framework to gain a deeper understanding of how temperature variations affect bacterial physiology. While there is conceptually analogous research39, our model offers a distinct perspective and helps elucidate the temperature dependence of more physiological variables—such as β-galactosidase expression and cell size—directly from the principles of protein allocation.

We will first extend our previous model29 by incorporating temperature dependence into its parameters, thereby quantitatively associating chaperone levels with cellular growth in E. coli. The extended model will then be calibrated against experimental data to account for variations in growth rate across a temperature range. Employing the temperature-dependent parameters obtained from this calibration, we will predict protein allocation across varying temperatures. Finally, by integrating documented correlations between protein allocation and specific physiological traits in E. coli from previous research29,33,40, we will derive predictions for the variation of these physiological parameters with temperature. Our predictions can offer interpretative insights into the experimental observations, thereby advancing our understanding of how E. coli responds physiologically to changes in temperature.

Results

Flux and protein allocation in bacterial growth

We begin by describing the previous coarse-grained protein allocation model29, which focuses on protein synthesis and quality control.

The first step in protein synthesis is the transformation of extracellular substrates (S) into intracellular amino acids (AA). This conversion is carried out by a set of proteins—comprising metabolic enzymes, amino acid synthesis enzymes, and transporters—collectively referred to as P1-class proteins. The rate at which amino acids are produced by this class is denoted as vS↦AA. Analogous notation will be used for corresponding parameters in the subsequent processes. It should be noted that even in bacteria cultured in nutrient-rich media, such as LB, amino acid synthesis and degradation continue to play a significant physiological role41. The reason is due to the preferential uptake of specific amino acids and the discrepancy between the amino acid composition of the medium and the physiological requirements of E. coli41.

Subsequently, ribosomes and associated auxiliary proteins, collectively referred to as R-class proteins, utilize genetic information to convert AAs into unfolded proteins (UP). This conversion occurs at a flux rate denoted as vAA↦UP. To acquire biological function, these unfolded proteins must achieve the correct conformation. Molecular chaperones, categorized as P2-class proteins, facilitate the folding process, transforming unfolded proteins into normal proteins (NP) at a flux rate vUP↦NP. However, a fraction of proteins fails to achieve their native conformation, resulting in the production of aberrant proteins (AP) at a flux rate vUP↦AP. These aberrant proteins can either be degraded into AAs by proteases, which are classified as P3-class proteins with a flux rate vAP↦AA, or they may form protein aggregates (PA) at a flux rate vAP↦PA. Such aggregates can have cytotoxic effects on bacterial cells42.

In addition to the previously described functional protein classes, a distinct category known as Q-class proteins exists. The total cellular abundance of Q-class remains invariant across different growth conditions28, as this class primarily comprises essential housekeeping proteins.

Within this framework, the proteome is divided into five functional categories: R, P1, P2, P3, and Q (see Fig. 2a). The roles of these sectors in the protein synthesis and quality control network are summarized in the accompanying schematic diagram (see Fig. 2b).

To facilitate proper protein folding, bacteria express molecular chaperones as auxiliary factors. Given the relatively low abundance of chaperones within E. coli (e.g., ϕ2 ≈ 0.05 in glucose, ϕ2 ≈ 0.08 in LB at 37 °C36), this chaperone-assisted folding mechanism must be highly efficient, with the rate-limiting bottleneck residing in the quantity of unfolded proteins. We can assume that the flux rates for proper folding (vUP↦NP) and misfolding (vUP↦AP) are both proportional to the amount of unfolded proteins, i.e., vUP↦NP ∝ MUP, vUP↦AP ∝ MUP. The partitioning between these two competing pathways is determined by the availability of chaperones that facilitate proper folding.

From the perspective of Eq. (16), a reduction in translational capacity kr—for instance, due to translation-inhibiting antibiotic treatment—should lead to a reduction in folding pressure ψf. This prediction appears consistent with the effects of chloramphenicol, which has been shown to inhibit the expression of heat shock proteins62 (primarily composed of chaperones). This is equivalent to treating folding pressure as constant at a given temperature. In fact, a non-zero ψf,1 is also capable of capturing the variations observed in growth rate across different temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 1).

Temperature dependence of key parameters

To model temperature effects on E. coli physiology, we extend the aforementioned framework by incorporating the temperature dependence of critical kinetic parameters. The translational capacity kr is directly associated with the peptide chain elongation rate (ER)28. Assuming a stable translational system, where the rRNA fraction in total RNA, the mass ratio of R-class proteins to rRNA, and the composition of the R-class proteome remain constant, kr is directly proportional to ER28. Therefore, all fitting reported in the main text was performed under this constraint, reducing the model to four free parameters.

The model captures effects of temperature on protein allocation

In our previous fitting of growth rates, we did not incorporate proteomic information. Based on the analytical solution derived from our model (Eq. (16)), we can utilize the parameters obtained from growth rate fitting to predict variations in protein allocation across different temperatures (Fig. 5b, c; see Method for classification about R and P2).

As shown in Fig. 5b, c, the model predictions closely align with the observed variations68 in both R-class and P2-class proteins within the heat region. With increasing temperature, the folding pressure kf increases sharply, prompting E. coli to upregulate chaperone expression to ensure proper protein folding and reduce metabolic waste. This adaptive response occurs at the expense of allocations to both amino acid supply and translational machinery. As a result, the chaperone fraction increases in the heat region, while the proportion of translation-associated proteins decreases. This trend is evident in the recent independent proteomic dataset64, which shows substantial agreement with our model predictions within the normal and heat ranges, with only a nearly constant offset in ϕr. Such discrepancy potentially stems from variations in bacterial strains or differences in experimental conditions, as supported by the reported ϕ0 > 0.1 in this studied proteomic data—approximately double the value of ϕ0 = 0.066 adopted in our analysis.

Within the normal temperature range, folding pressure becomes negligible, resulting in a nearly constant chaperone allocation. Under the assumption that E1 = Er, the ratio krk1 is temperature-independent, resulting in approximately stable proportions of both R-class and P1-class proteins. Consequently, the model predicts largely invariant protein allocation across this range, which is consistent with the widely reported experimental results66.

However, within the cold region, the model predictions exhibit some discrepancies from the experimental trends. The observed discrepancy arises from our assumption that ψf increases as the folding rate decreases, resulting in predicted reductions in ϕr and increases in ϕ2 (Eqs. ((16), (17), (18))). Although existing proteomic data64,68 somewhat reflect this directional trend, the observed changes are less pronounced than the model predicts. The available dataset encompasses a limited temperature range in the cold region, which limits its ability to characterize proteomic shifts at lower temperatures comprehensively. Nonetheless, the discrepancies within the data range are relatively modest.

The model quantifies the temperature-dependent expression of β-galactosidase from a constitutive promoter

P1-class proteins comprise constitutively expressed proteins28 whose expression is regulated indirectly through competition for transcriptional and translational resources. A canonical example is the constitutively expressed β-galactosidase, which is widely employed as a reporter gene due to its easily measurable enzymatic activity. Prior research28,53 indicates that under carbon or nitrogen limitation, or in the presence of translation inhibitors, the activity of constitutively expressed β-galactosidase closely aligns with the allocation trend of P1-class proteins. In this study, we use the activity of constitutively expressed β-galactosidase (which is proportional to its amount per unit biomass) across different temperatures as a proxy to infer temperature-dependent changes in the relative abundance of P1-class proteins.

The model captures effects of temperature on cell size

At a fixed temperature, bacterial cell size exhibits an exponential dependence on growth rate76. Based on the threshold accumulation hypothesis for bacterial cell size control, protein allocation models have been developed to explain the relationship between cell size and nutrient quality33,40 at a specific temperature. The analysis of correlations between cell size and the proportions of various protein sectors reveals that the division-related sector remains minimal across different nutrient conditions and is co-regulated with P1-class proteins33,77. This conclusion is further supported by dynamic single-cell experiments78. Temperature affects bacterial cell size, causing morphological changes at both low and high temperatures64,79,80. We hypothesize that the established relationship in Eq. (24) is valid across different temperatures and that the proportional coefficient is independent of temperature.

As shown in Fig. 6b, the model effectively captures the size characteristics of E. coli within a suitable temperature range79. Cell volume increases when temperatures deviate from the normal range, and changes in folding pressure drive this response. At elevated temperatures (e.g., T ≥ 42 °C), our model predicts a rapid increase in bacterial cell volume, which may correspond to filamentation observed under heat stress80. One possible explanation is that we consolidate all growth-rate-independent components of the P1-class protein into the Q sector.

Recent studies81 indicate that high expression of the chaperone HtpG can interfere with the assembly of the cell division machinery and the formation of the FtsZ-ring, resulting in filamentation. Furthermore, the polymerization of FtsZ is additionally inhibited by chaperone ClpX82. These findings may provide a possible explanation for the validity of Eq. (24) across different temperatures: in our model with constant nutrient conditions, the reduction in ϕ1 is predominantly driven by increased chaperone expression.

Discussion

By incorporating temperature dependence into the established protein allocation model and calibrating it with experimental growth data, we quantitatively characterize the relationship between growth rate of E. coli and temperature. This calibrated model enables us to predict temperature-dependent shifts in protein allocation. Furthermore, under the assumption that certain physiological parameters—such as ρ—are stable across temperature, we can explain the temperature dependence of cell volume and the constitutive expression of β-galactosidase.

Our model indicates that within the normal temperature range, folding pressure is low, implying a correspondingly low cellular demand for chaperones. The model fitting further supports the relationship Er = E1, suggesting that variations in growth rate within this regime are governed by the activation energies associated with protein translation and amino acid supply. Thus, the apparent “activation energy” for growth (Eμ) within the normal range is given by Eμ = Er = E1.

However, ER data (Fig. 3b) show a slight discrepancy between the activation energy of translation and the “activation energy” of the growth within the normal range66. This discrepancy could stem from experimental methodology or may indicate a genuine difference between the activation energy for amino acid supply E1 and that for translation Er. If the latter holds, Eμ would represent a combined effect of E1, Er, and the nutrient quality of the culture medium, as formulated in Eq. (22). This interpretation is consistent with a recent study64 reporting that Eμ varies with medium composition.

When temperature deviates from the normal range, our model suggests that increased folding pressure requires higher chaperone expression in E. coli. This reallocation of cellular resources reduces the proportion of R-class proteins, ultimately leading to a slower overall growth rate. While this mechanism provides a plausible explanation for growth limitation in the heat region, its relevance to the cold region remains less clear. At low temperatures, protein synthesis in E. coli is primarily constrained by the accumulation of inactive ribosomes66 (Supplementary Note 1) and the stabilization of mRNA secondary structures6. Substantial evidence66,83 further indicates an increased activation energy for protein synthesis at low temperature, which would lead to a steeper decline in translational capacity at lower temperatures. In contrast, although folding pressure could be a potential contributing factor84, its role in the low-temperature physiology remains underexplored in the literature.

It is generally accepted that the physiological state of E. coli remains relatively stable across the normal temperature range, as demonstrated by several experimental studies73,85. However, proteomic data64,68 reveal subtle yet consistent variations in the abundance of translation-associated proteins even within this temperature range. This suggests that E. coli may continuously fine-tune its translational machinery in response to temperature changes, rather than maintaining a fixed state.

The role of chaperone proteins in the cold region remains poorly characterized. On one hand, many psychrophilic bacteria exhibit upregulation of chaperone genes86, and the cold-induced inactivation of chaperones is known to impair E. coli growth at low temperatures87,88. The heterologous expression of psychrophilic chaperones has even been shown to enhance E. coli growth at low temperatures86. On the other hand, overexpression of heat shock proteins (primarily chaperones) in E. coli inhibits cellular growth89. This apparent contradiction suggests that E. coli, as a mesophile, may lack efficient chaperone-mediated adaptation to cold stress. Potential missing adaptive mechanisms or other intrinsic bacterial limitations may result in the cells growing in a suboptimal state under certain environmental conditions55. This may partially explain the discrepancies between our model predictions and experimental data.

The temperature dependence of E. coli cell volume remains incompletely characterized. Data from multiple studies indicate that the nutrient quality strongly influences volumetric changes in response to temperature elevation. When the cultivation temperature is increased from 37°C to 42°C, E. coli cell volume decreases in glucose-supplemented minimal medium90, but increases in nutrient-rich medium79 or in MR medium supplemented with glucose80. Notably, the increase in cell volume observed in LB medium is primarily due to changes in cell width64, whereas in MR medium, it is associated with an increase in cell length80. Conversely, when the temperature decreases, cell volume increases in both minimal and complex media79. These context-dependent results highlight the need for further experimental work to disentangle the interplay among bacterial cell size, temperature, and nutrient conditions.

A comprehensive protein allocation model should be capable of elucidating the physiological impacts of various orthogonal stresses on bacterial systems. As validated in our earlier work29, the model demonstrates consistent applicability across various stress conditions. However, a more refined partitioning of the proteome may introduce correlations among parameters characterizing distinct functional sectors (Supplementary Fig. 1). For instance, while various translational inhibitors effectively validate a coarse-grained model consisting solely of the R, P1, and Q sectors28, they exert differential effects on chaperone expression levels56. This underscores the necessity of rigorous experimental design for validating refined protein allocation models.

The previous model39 also effectively captured temperature-dependent growth and protein allocation by explicitly incorporating chaperones. In contrast to our experimentally motivated assumption—that flux rates scale linearly with the mass of corresponding protein classes—their approach conceptualizes amino acid synthesis and translation as enzymatic kinetic processes. It is noteworthy that when the translation half-saturation constant is very low, and the amino acid synthesis efficiency is at most marginally higher than translation efficiency, the effective translation efficiency in their model approaches a constant. This result is consistent with our assumed formula (Eq. (8)). Additionally, incorporating the effect of substrate concentration on amino acid synthesis efficiency, the enzymatic kinetics related to amino acid synthesis become consistent with our assumption (Eq. (9)). As a result of the conceptual differences in defining protein availability, we employ a distinct functional form to characterize the temperature dependence of folding pressure. Consequently, while both models exhibit strong explanatory power for variations in growth rate, discrepancies emerge in their predictions regarding protein allocation, such as the variations in ϕ2 and ϕr within the normal temperature range. Beyond this, our model provides additional explanatory insights into the temperature dependence of bacterial cell volume and constitutive β-galactosidase expression. We believe that this model and our model are complementary in nature.

In the previous analysis of bacterial physiology across temperatures, we assumed that protease activity was inefficient. Thus, the model predicts minimal expression of proteases across temperatures, and ϕ3 = 0. This is consistent with quantitative proteomic data36,64, which show that protease levels remain mainly at basal expression (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although protease upregulation is a documented part of the E. coli heat-shock response4, genome-wide data consistently show that ϕ3 remains quantitatively small64. In Supplementary Note 4, we explore scenarios in which this assumption is relaxed. The results show that as folding pressure increases, the fraction of expressed proteases rises in parallel with the fraction of chaperones. This pattern provides a plausible explanation for the observed upregulation of protease expression in minimal media at elevated temperatures36,56,59,64.

Although a five-component protein allocation model was insufficient to fully capture E. coli’s temperature response, incorporating a chaperone sector significantly improves its ability to explain certain thermal adaptation phenomena. A more comprehensive model91 could provide a more complete representation. Moreover, the physiological interpretations provided by our model are based on the assumption that existing parameter values remain valid over an extended temperature range, even if some could be temperature-dependent. Consequently, predictions at extreme temperatures may be unreliable if these assumptions are not met. Given the scarcity of quantitative physiological parameters and the presence of conflicting experimental observations under these conditions, we anticipate that future work will elucidate the quantitative physiology of bacteria in such regimes. Such advances will substantially deepen our understanding of the fundamental principles governing bacterial growth in relation to temperature.

Methods

Optimization problem solving

Here we provide a concise description of solving the optimization problem (Eq. (14)). Although the flux rate of aberrant protein degradation by proteases, JAP↦AA, is constrained by other flux rates according to JAP↦PA = JUP↦AP − JAP↦AA≥0, we initially disregard this constraint because, under our model assumptions, the amount of protein aggregates does not affect the growth rate. To maximize growth, the two processes supplying amino acids, JS↦AA = k1ϕ1 and JAP↦AA = k3ϕ3, must collectively minimize the total proteomic cost required to deliver a given amino acid flux rate.

Assuming that the cost of acquiring amino acids from the external environment is always lower than that of obtaining them through aberrant protein degradation, i.e., k1 > k3, the optimal solution allocates a mass fraction of zero to the protease sector under all conditions, ϕ3 = 0. This allocation automatically satisfies the constraint JAP↦PA = JUP↦AP − JAP↦AA = JUP↦AP≥0.

Classification for proteins class R, P2

We adopt the protein classification scheme established by Proteomap92. Consistent with previous studies28,93, the R-class proteins are defined as those encompassing functional modules involved in gene expression, specifically including “tRNA loading,” “translation factors,” “RNA polymerase,” and “ribosome.” The P2-class proteins are defined as the cluster annotated “chaperones and folding catalysts.”

The quantitative proteomic dataset from prior studies68 includes measurements for only 133 proteins. To enable direct comparison with model predictions, these data are linearly transformed based on a correlation derived from the proteomic datasets36,64 (Supplementary Note 2).

Model fitting and protein allocation predicting

The model was fitted to temperature-dependent growth data66,68—obtained under the same medium conditions—by the nonlinear least-squares regression using the scipy.optimize.curve_fit function in Python (see Supplementary Note 5 for the complete formula about μ). With the value of kr and k1 at 37 °C predetermined, the fitting procedure needs to determine the three remaining parameters, Er, E2, and N. The initial values were set as follows: Er = 60 kJ/mol68, N = 325 (the average protein length in E. coli46), and E2 = 0kJ/mol. In practice, the fitting results are robust to a wide range of initial values. When the value of k1(Tn) for a given medium71,72 is unknown, an initial value of k1(Tn) = 20h−1 is assigned for the fitting process.

We obtained the values of k1, kr, and ψf across temperature by fitting the model solely to growth rate data. Using the optimal solution of the model (Eq. (16)), we then predicted protein allocation across different temperatures.

Inferring k1(Tn) for basic salts + succinate

The phenomenological parameter k1 is not available for the medium “basic salts + succinate” used in ref. 75 or other succinate-supplemented minimal media. Assuming that folding pressure and translational capacity remain constant at a given temperature, k1 can be estimated using Eq. (21). If we further assume that the salt composition of the minimal medium has a negligible influence on k1, then the reported k1(Tn) value for M9 + glucose29, together with the measured growth rate of E. coli in M9 + succinate and M9 + glucose36, can be used to estimate k1(Tn) ≈ 1.40 h−1 for basic salts + succinate medium.
