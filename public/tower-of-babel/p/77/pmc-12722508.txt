Chemerin Exacerbates Pulmonary Inflammation in Type 2 Diabetes and Mycobacterium Tuberculosis Infection Comorbidity
Authors: Giseli Furlan Corrêa, Núbia Sabrina Martins, Ualter Guilherme Cipriano, Ana Flávia Gembre, Daniel Rodrigues, Vinícius Bottura Apolloni, Leandra Naira Zambelli Ramalho, Thais Fernanda de Campos Fraga-Silva, Rita Tostes, Vânia Luiza Deperon Bonato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The adipokine chemerin is increased in the serum of individuals with obesity and type 2 diabetes. Patients with type 2 diabetes exhibit a threefold increased risk of developing tuberculosis, are more refractory to tuberculosis treatment and display more severe forms of the disease. Patients with type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis exhibit a dysfunctional immunological response characterized by a higher frequency of peripheral Th1 and Th17 cells, increased concentrations of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, and a reduced microbicidal capacity compared to subjects affected exclusively by tuberculosis. In the present study, we investigated whether chemerin exerts a pro- or anti-inflammatory effect on macrophages in vitro and its role in the lungs of normoglycemic or hyperglycemic (obese plus type 2 diabetes) mice infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) cultured with hyperglycemic medium and infected with M. tuberculosis secreted increased IL-6 and reduced IL-10 concentrations following chemerin treatment. BMDM from obese (fed with high-fat diet, HFD), non-diabetic mice were also pro-inflammatory, while BMDM from obese and diabetic mice (db/db) showed no significant difference compared to BMDM from normoglycemic mice (db/+). In vivo, db/db mice exhibited an increase of bacterial load and an exacerbated pulmonary immunopathology. Treatment of infected db/db mice with CCX832 chemerin receptor (ChemR23) antagonist significantly reduced pulmonary inflammation with no effect on bacterial load. Our findings show that blocking chemerin receptors may represent an adjuvant therapeutic strategy to mitigate pulmonary immunological response-mediated pathology accentuated by type 2 diabetes in active tuberculosis.Graphical AbstractType 2 diabetes causes more severe forms of tuberculosis and is associated with high levels of adipokine chemerin. Introduction

Chemerin is an adipokine and signaling protein that may exert effects locally or systemically. Chemerin is associated with body mass index and serum glucose levels, and its concentration is increased in the serum of individuals with obesity and type 2 diabetes [1–4]. Higher levels of serum chemerin are associated with type 2 diabetes complications, such as retinopathy and nephropathy [5, 6].

Although chemerin is primarily produced by white adipose tissue, it is also expressed in brown adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, kidney, ovary, heart, and lungs [7]. The effects of chemerin are multiple in different cells and tissues. Chemerin reduces gluconeogenesis in the liver, contributes to insulin resistance in skeletal muscle, increases angiogenesis and increases macrophage migration [8, 9]. The ChemR23 chemerin receptor, also referred as CMKLR1 (G protein-coupled receptor chemokine-like receptor 1), is expressed by various cell types, including epithelial and endothelial cells, keratinocytes, hepatocytes, fibroblasts, natural killer cells, dendritic cells and macrophages, where it exhibits signaling activity. CMKLR1 expression is lower in white adipose tissue and higher in skeletal muscle of obese and diabetic (db/db) mice compared to wild type (WT) mice [2]. Also, higher expression of CMKLR1 is associated with proliferative diabetic retinopathy [6]. In contrast, the chemokine receptor-like 2 (CCRL2), which is also a G protein-coupled receptor, does not mediate any signaling [10, 11]. Due to its structural homology with cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide, the potential role of chemerin in protecting against infection was also investigated. Skin keratinocyte-derived chemerin was shown to inhibit bacterial growth [12]. Full length and truncated chemerin significantly inhibited the growth of Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae compared to vehicle-treated E. coli or K. pneumoniae, respectively, and to the positive control human cathelicidin LL-37 [13]. Using a model of mouse pneumonia virus (PVM), Bondue et al. showed that ChemR23 deficient mice (ChemR23−/−) exhibited increased mortality, enhanced lung neutrophil inflammation and delayed viral clearance. The immunological protection against viral pneumonia was dependent on type I interferon and plasmacytoid dendritic cell migration, but it was not dependent on chemerin. However, the higher susceptibility of ChemR23−/− mice to viral infection compared to wild type (WT) mice was attributed to an unbalanced inflammatory response, suggesting that chemerin may act as an anti-inflammatory target [14]. Serum chemerin concentrations were significantly lower in COVID-19 patients compared to healthy volunteers, although no correlation was found between chemerin levels and COVID-19 severity [15].

Dependent on chemerin fragments or peptides generated by proteases in the microenvironment, chemerin may function as either a pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory mediator. It may contribute to the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases or play a role in the resolution of the inflammatory process [16]. The anti-inflammatory role of chemerin was reported in non-pathogenic diseases, such as COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease) [17] and asthma [18].

Tuberculosis is a pulmonary chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. In 2023, tuberculosis again became the leading cause of death from a single pathogen, 3 years after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Although only 5–10% of infected individuals develop the disease, more than 10 million people fall ill with tuberculosis annually [19]. The heterogeneity of tuberculosis is multifactorial, with type 2 diabetes increasing the risk of developing tuberculosis threefold [20, 21]. Patients with type 2 diabetes are more refractory to tuberculosis treatment and infection control, and exhibit more severe forms of tuberculosis [22]. Control of M. tuberculosis infection requires activation of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, i.e. macrophages expressing the inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) enzyme, Th1 and Th17 cells, and secretion of mediators such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)−6, IL-12, IL-23, IL-17, among others [23, 24]. Although protective, the magnitude of type 1 inflammation, characterized by increased frequency of M1 macrophages, Th1 and Th17 cells, can lead to pulmonary damage and immune-mediated disease [24, 25]. Patients with type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis exhibit a higher frequency of peripheral Th1 and Th17 cells and a lower frequency of regulatory T cells compared to subjects affected exclusively by tuberculosis [26]. Monocyte-derived macrophages differentiated from peripheral blood from patients with type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis and subsequently infected in vitro with BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guérin) displayed a lower microbicidal capacity than macrophages derived from peripheral blood of tuberculosis patients [27], showing that under hyperglycemic conditions, altered macrophage functions are positively correlated with increasing disease severity [27]. Patients with pulmonary tuberculosis and type 2 diabetes, or latent tuberculosis and type 2 diabetes, exhibited decreased plasma concentrations of the adipokines adiponectin and adipsin, along with increased concentrations of circulating leptin, visfatin and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, compared to patients with tuberculosis alone [28].

Given that adipokines are imbalanced in obesity and type 2 diabetes, potentially contributing to inflammation, pathogenesis and tuberculosis-mediated immunological response, the aim of the present study was to investigate whether chemerin exerts a pro- or anti-inflammatory effect on macrophages in vitro, and its role in the lungs of M. tuberculosis-infected mice in a normoglycemic or hyperglycemic (type 2 diabetes) microenvironment.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Female wild-type (WT), 6 to 8 weeks old C57BL/6 mice, and mice deficient for the leptin receptor (db/db) and their respective control (db/+), were obtained from the breeding facility of the Ribeirao Preto Medical School, University of São Paulo (Ribeirao Preto, SP, Brazil). Although type 2 diabetes is associated with tuberculosis recurrence in men (1.23, 95% confidence interval 1.15–1.32) compared to women (0.96, 95% confidence interval 0.85–1.09) [29], we used female animals because the experimental design demands more than 10 weeks and male animals frequently hurt each other. All animals were maintained in sterile environmental cages and received sterile food and water ad libitum. All animals were euthanized with ketamine (300 mg/kg) (Agener, Embu-Guaçu, São Paulo, Brazil) and xylazine (30 mg/kg) (Syntec, Santana de Parnaiba, Bazil). All animal procedures were carried out in accordance with the Ribeirao Preto Medical School Ethic Committee in Animal Use (protocol numbers 116/2018, 226/2019, 8/2019, 72/2019).

Diet-induced Obesity

C57BL/6 mice were fed a high fat diet (HFD: 60% of lipid, 20% of protein and 20% of carbohydrate) or low-fat diet (LFD: 10% of lipid, 70% of carbohydrate and 20% of protein) for 12 weeks (Pragsoluções Biociências, Jaú, SP, Brazil). Body weight was measured weekly until the end of the experiment.

Obese and Diabetic Mice

db/db (homozygous) and db/+ (heterozygous) mice were fed a regular diet for 21 weeks. At 21 weeks, db/db mice developed obesity and type 2 diabetes, while db/+ mice - normoglycemic and lean - were used as controls.

Blood Glucose Measurement

Fasting blood glucose was measured in LFD and HFD groups at 18 weeks of age. Fasting blood glucose was determined in db/db and db/+ groups at 21 weeks (150 days) of age.

Bone Marrow-derived Macrophages (BMDM) Differentiation

Femurs and tibias harvested from WT on LFD or HFD, db/db, and db/+ mice were flushed with RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) medium. Precursor cells were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 20% L929 cell-conditioned medium and 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Seven days after culture, differentiated BMDM were harvested. Adherent cells were plated on 24-well culture plate (Costar®, Corning #3524) in RPMI medium with 10% FBS and mannitol (25 mM, normoglycemic condition) or glucose (25 mM, high glucose or hyperglycemic condition). Cells were cultured for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

In vitro M. tuberculosisInfection

BMDM (1 × 106 cells) were infected with M. tuberculosis strain H37Rv (American Type Culture Collection 27294, Rockville, MD, USA) in a multiplicity of infection (MOI) 1. After 4 h, BMDM cultures were washed once with phosphate buffered saline (PBS 1x) to remove extracellular, non-phagocytized bacteria. Infected BMDM were cultured for 20 h in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and mannitol or glucose.

Invitro Treatment

BMDM were infected or non-infected with M. tuberculosis (MOI 1) and 4 h post infection, washed once and treated with CCX832 (1 µM, a gift from ChemoCentryx, Mountain View, CA). Chemerin (100 ng/mL, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was added to cultures with normoglycemic or hyperglycemic medium 1 h after CCX832 treatment. The concentrations of CCX832 and chemerin were defined as described [30].

Invivo Infection

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation using isoflurane in an atmosphere containing 5% isoflurane, with a flow of 1 L per minute (LPM) by the infusion inhalation anesthesia equipment (Bonther, Ribeirao Preto, Sao Paulo, Brazil). After anesthetizing, db/db or db/+ were infected by the oropharyngeal route with 1 × 103 or 1 × 104 Colony Forming Unit (CFU) M. tuberculosis (Mtb). Mice were followed up until the final effect of anesthesia. Mice were euthanized 20 days post-infection.

Treatment

Ten days post-infection, M. tuberculosis-infected db/db or db/+ mice were treated with CCX832 (55 mg/kg), by oral gavage for 7 consecutive days.

Colony-Forming Unit (CFU) Assay

In the in vitro CFU assay, which quantifies bacterial load or CFU recovery, BMDM were lysed 24 h post-infection and plated on supplemented 7H11 agar medium (Sigma-Aldrich). In the in vivo CFU assay, the lower and middle right lobes of the lungs were digested with collagenase (2.2 mg/mL) and DNase (0.055 mg/mL) and lung cell suspensions were serially diluted and plated in supplemented 7H11 agar medium. The plates were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 28 days.

ELISA

IL-6, TNF, IL-10, IFN-γ and chemerin concentrations were determined in lung homogenates and culture supernatants by ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The limit of detection was 31.2 pg/mL for TNF, IFN-γ and IL-10 and 15.6 pg/mL for IL-6.

Western Blot Analysis

BMDM infected with M. tuberculosis and/or stimulated with chemerin were homogenized in a lysis buffer (50 mM Tris/HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P40, 1 mM EDTA, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 µg/mL pepstatin, 1 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 mM sodium fluoride) and centrifuged at 16,000 x g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected for protein quantification by the Bradford method. Proteins (15 µg) were separated by electrophoresis on 12% polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with rabbit anti-mouse IgG anti-ChemR23/CMKLR1 (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, EUA) or rabbit anti-mouse IgG anti-β-actin (1:5000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, EUA) overnight at 4 °C. The antibodies were diluted in TBS-T. The membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. The signals were obtained by chemiluminescence and quantified by densitometric analysis using the software ImageJ (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, Maryland).

Histopathology Analysis and Inflammation Score

The upper right lobes of the lungs were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin blocks and cut into 5 μm thick sections. Lungs were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E). Pulmonary inflammation score was determined as previously described [31]. Briefly, score 0: without inflammation; 1: mild inflammation; 2: moderate inflammation; 3: severe inflammation.

Flow Cytometry

The phenotype of BMDM and lung cells populations was determined by flow cytometry as described in Table 1. Cultured BMDM were removed with PBS 1x plus EDTA 0.05% and stained with viability stain (Live/Dead, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Anti-arginase 1 (Arg-1) and anti-inducible nitric oxide synthase enzyme (iNOS) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were used to characterize M1 and M2 macrophages subsets, respectively. Arg-1 and iNOS expression was evaluated on gated Live/Dead−CD11b+CD64+ cells. For vivo experiments, lung cell suspensions (1 × 106 cells) were obtained as previously described (Palma Albornoz et al. 2023; Palma Albornoz et al. 2021), stained with viability stain (FVS780, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and mAbs to characterize alveolar (FVS−CD64+CD11b−SiglecF+ cells) or interstitial macrophages (FVS−CD64+CD11b+SiglecF− cells), M1 (iNOS+ cells) or M2 macrophages (Arg1+ cells) and Th1 cells (CD45+CD3+CD4+IFN-γ+ cells). Samples were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde. Acquisition was performed in BD FACSCanto cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Total events per sample were collected and analyzed according to size, granularity and fluorescence intensity using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego CA, USA) was used for data analysis and preparation of all graphs. Data normality was evaluated by Shapiro-Wilk normality test. An unpaired Student’s t-test was used to detect significant differences between two groups for parametric data. Mann-Whitney U test was used to detect significant differences between two groups for non-parametric data. For inflammation score analysis, relative numbers of mice with different histological scores were calculated and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare overall scores between different groups. Data were shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Chemerin Impairs the Control of M. tuberculosis Infection and Induces an Increase of IL-6 and a Reduction of IL-10 Production by Macrophages Under Hyperglycemic Conditions

First, we evaluated the effect of hyperglycemia in M. tuberculosis (Mtb) infection because type 2 diabetes increasing the risk of developing tuberculosis threefold [20, 21]. For that, BMDM were cultivated in normoglycemic medium (mannitol) or in hyperglycemic medium (glucose) and subsequently infected with Mtb (Fig. 1A). Mtb infection did not affect the expression of CMKLR1, the canonical chemerin receptor, in BMDM (Fig. 1B and C). No significant difference was observed in the bacterial load (CFU numbers) recovered from BMDM cultured either in mannitol or glucose at 4 h post-infection (hpi), indicating that hyperglycemia did not affect the phagocytosis of Mtb (Fig. 1D). A reduction in CFU numbers/bacterial recovery was observed in BMDM cultures with high-glucose medium compared with mannitol medium at 24 hpi and with high glucose medium at 4 hpi (Fig. 1D). Because chemerin concentration is increased in the serum of individuals with obesity and type 2 diabetes [1–4], higher levels of serum chemerin are associated with type 2 diabetes complications [5, 6], and the role of chemerin was not investigated in the type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis comorbidity, next, BMDM cultured in hyperglycemic condition and infected with Mtb were treated with chemerin. The treatment of BMDM with chemerin, in high-glucose medium, but not in mannitol medium, significantly increased the bacterial load at 24 hpi compared to the respective untreated controls. This suggests that chemerin impairs the ability of macrophages to control Mtb infection under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 1D).

Next, we assessed cytokine secretion by M. tuberculosis-infected BMDM under hyperglycemic and normoglycemic conditions. BMDM cultured in high glucose medium secreted significantly higher concentrations of TNF compared to those cultured in mannitol medium. Chemerin treatment did not affect the secretion of TNF by BMDM cultures under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 1E). Glucose did not affect IL-6 secretion by BMDM; however, chemerin enhanced the secretion of this cytokine irrespective of the normoglycemic or hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 1F). Glucose also increased the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 by BMDM, while chemerin treatment significantly reduced IL-10 concentrations in hyperglycemic cultures (Fig. 1G).

Collectively, our results show that chemerin enhances the inflammatory response of infected macrophages while impairing bacterial load control.

Blockade of the Chemerin Receptor has no Effect on IL-6 or IL-10 Secretion in Hyperglycemic Conditions

Considering the pro-inflammatory and non-protective role of chemerin in M. tuberculosis-infected BMDM cultured in hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 1), we sought to determine whether blockade of CMKLR1 chemerin receptor could enhance bacterial clearance. M. tuberculosis-infected BMDM cultivated in hyperglycemic medium were treated with CCX832, a CMKLR1 antagonist, as outlined in Fig. 2A. Treatment with the CMKLR1 antagonist had no effect on bacterial load (Fig. 2B). As anticipated, M. tuberculosis infection significantly increased the secretion of TNF, IL-6 and IL-10 compared to non-infected cells, and the treatment with the CMKLR1 antagonist did not reduce IL-6 secretion, which was positively regulated by chemerin (Fig. 2C, D). The treatment with the CMKLR1 antagonist showed no significant difference in the IL-10 production compared to chemerin treatment (Fig. 2E).

These results suggest that the regulation of IL-6 or IL-10 secretion by infected macrophages under hyperglycemic conditions is independent of CMKLR1.

Macrophages from Obese and Non-diabetic Mice Secrete Elevated TNF and IL-6 Concentrations While Limiting Bacterial Load

High serum concentrations of chemerin are a hallmark of obesity and diabetes [2, 32]. We have previously shown that obesity exacerbates the immunopathology of M. tuberculosis infection [31, 33]. Next, we asked whether BMDM from mice fed with HFD would show the same phenotype we found for BMDM cultured in a hyperglycemic condition. Mice fed a High Fat Diet (HFD) for 12 weeks exhibited increased body weight (Fig. 3A) without changes in fasting blood glucose (Fig. 3B) and lung chemerin concentrations (Fig. 3C) compared to mice fed a Low-Fat Diet (LFD). To investigate the interaction of obesity with macrophage activation, BMDM from the LFD and HFD groups were infected with M. tuberculosis for 24 h (Fig. 3D). Phagocytic capacity showed no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 3E). However, macrophages from the HFD group exhibited a reduced bacterial load compared to macrophages from the LFD group at 24 hpi (Fig. 3E). The secretion of TNF and IL-6, and the anti-inflammatory IL-10 cytokine was significantly higher in BMDM from the HFD group versus the LFD group (Figs. 3F-H). Although the HFD group displayed normal glucose levels, the findings with BMDM from the HFD group were similar to those observed for BMDM cultured under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 1E and G), except for the secretion of IL-6 (Fig. 1F). The expression of iNOS and Arg-1 was assessed to confirm the inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes. While M. tuberculosis infection induced a significant reduction in anti-inflammatory macrophages (CD64+Arg1+ cells) and a significant increase in inflammatory macrophages (CD64+iNOS+ cells) compared to non-infected BMDM, HFD had no impact on the inflammatory profile, as indicated by iNOS expression (Fig. 3I and J).

Our data suggest that TNF and IL-6 secreted by infected BMDM from obese mice may contribute to attenuating bacterial growth in vitro, independent of iNOS expression.

Macrophages from Obese and Diabetic Mice Show Similar Inflammatory Response and Microbicidal Capacity Compared to those from Non-obese and Non-diabetic Mice

Leptin receptor-deficient mice (db/db) are used as a model for studying type 2 diabetes [34]. Next, we addressed whether BMDM from obese and diabetic mice would exhibit similar phenotypes from that found for BMDM cultured in hyperglycemic condition. Obese (db/db) mice exhibited increased body weight (Fig. 4A and B), fasting blood glucose levels (Fig. 4C) and total cholesterol levels in the serum (Fig. 4D) compared to heterozygous (db/+) lean mice. No changes were observed in serum triglycerides levels (Fig. 4E). Given that hyperglycemic medium induces the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and restricts bacterial load by BMDM (Fig. 1), we investigated BMDM from diabetic db/db animals as outlined in Fig. 4F. We found no differences in CFU counts between BMDM from diabetic (db/db) and non-diabetic (db/+ mice) groups (Fig. 4G). Likewise, the secretion of TNF, IL-6, and IL-10 was comparable between BMDM from db/db and db/+ groups (Fig. 4H-J). The frequency of macrophages expressing Arg1 was also similar between db/db and db/+ groups (Fig. 4K). Although no difference was found in the frequency of iNOS-expressing macrophages between db/db and db/+ groups, infected macrophages from the db/db group exhibited higher iNOS expression than their non-infected counterparts (Fig. 4L).

Obese and Diabetic Mice Display Increased Pulmonary Inflammation

High glucose concentrations positively regulated the inflammatory response and restricted M. tuberculosis load in vitro by BMDM (Fig. 1). In contrast, BMDM from obese and diabetic mice (db/db mice) did not affect these outcomes in vitro (Fig. 4). To investigate the impact of obesity and type 2 diabetes in vivo, db/db and db/+ mice were infected with M. tuberculosis by oropharyngeal route and euthanized 20 days post-infection (dpi) (Fig. 5A). We did not observe any differences in lung chemerin concentrations (Fig. 5B). Contrary to our in vitro findings with macrophages from db/db mice (Fig. 4F), we found an increase, albeit not significant, in lung bacterial load from the db/db group compared to db/+ group (Fig. 5C). Histopathological analysis revealed a significant increase in pulmonary inflammation in the db/db group compared to db/+ group (Fig. 5D and E). The frequency of lung IFN-γ-producing CD4+ cells was reduced, although not in a significant way, in the db/db group (Fig. 5F). IFN-γ, TNF, IL-6 and IL-10 concentrations showed no difference between both groups (Figs. 5G-J).

We also evaluated lung macrophage populations. Obese and diabetic mice exhibited an increase in alveolar macrophages (CD64+CD11b−SiglecF+ cells) (Fig. 5K) and a reduction in interstitial macrophages (CD64+CD11b+SiglecF− cells) (Fig. 5L). There were no differences between the groups in the frequency of Arg1- or iNOS-expressing interstitial macrophages (Figs. 5M, N).

These results show that diabetes exacerbates pulmonary inflammation during tuberculosis.

Blockade of Chemerin Receptor Attenuates Pulmonary Inflammation in Obese and Diabetic Mice

Although the chemerin concentrations were similar between diabetic and infected mice compared with non-diabetic and infected mice, lungs of the diabetic and infected mice showed an exacerbated inflammation. Considering the pro-inflammatory effect of chemerin in BMDM cultured in hyperglycemic condition and infected in vitro with M. tuberculosis, we investigated the effect of chemerin in vivo. Obese and diabetic mice (db/db) were infected with M. tuberculosis and treated with CCX832, an antagonist of CMKLR1 chemerin receptor, by oral gavage, 7 consecutive days as outlined in Fig. 6A. Treatment with CCX832 had no effect in the body weight (B) and in bacterial load, as CFU numbers were similar between treated db/db and non-treated (vehicle group) db/db mice (Fig. 6C). Diabetic and infected animals treated with CCX832 exhibited a significant reduction in pulmonary inflammation (Figs. 6D, E). An increase of lung CD4+IFNγ+ lymphocytes was observed in treated diabetic and infected mice compared to their non-treated counterparts (Fig. 6F). IFN-γ concentrations in the lungs of CCX832-treated mice were significantly higher than those in the non-treated group (Fig. 6G). No differences were found in the concentrations of TNF, IL-6 or IL-10 (Figs. 6H-J). Additionally, CCX832 treatment increased alveolar macrophages (Fig. 6K) and reduced interstitial macrophages (Fig. 6L), although not significantly, compared to the non-treated group. The frequency of Arg1- and iNOS-expressing interstitial macrophages was similar between the two groups (Figs. 6M, N).

These findings suggest that although chemerin-CMKLR1 signaling is proinflammatory, its blockade does not impact the progression of M. tuberculosis infection in diabetic mice in vivo.

Discussion

Type 2 diabetes increases the development of infectious diseases [35, 36]. Chronic type 2 diabetes affects innate and adaptive immune responses against tuberculosis [37]. In general, type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis comorbidity decreases the number of circulating monocytes and dendritic cells, and alveolar macrophages [28, 38, 39], increases the circulating levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [37], heightens circulating Th1 and Th17 cells and reduces regulatory T cells [26, 37]. Monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) from type 2 diabetic individuals infected in vitro with M. tuberculosis exhibit lower clearance of infection compared with MDMs from healthy subjects [40]. In a mouse model of streptozotocin-induced diabetes (type 1 diabetes), a delay of M. tuberculosis antigen presentation in the lung draining lymph nodes also delays the activation of adaptive immune response [41]. Alveolar macrophages harvested from mice with streptozotocin-induced diabetes showed an impaired phagocytosis of M. tuberculosis in vitro [42]. The impairment of M. tuberculosis phagocytosis was also reported with MDMs from type 2 diabetes subjects [27]. However, the lack of studies in vivo makes it difficult to understand the mechanisms of pulmonary immunopathology and susceptibility to M. tuberculosis infection associated with type 2 diabetes.

Metabolic dysfunctions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity are followed by increased concentrations of adipokines in circulation [43, 44]. Leukocytes express adipokine receptors and that axis affects the immune response in tuberculosis as mice deficient for leptin receptors were more susceptible and secreted lower IFN-γ concentrations than WT mice after M. tuberculosis infection [45].

Here we show that high glucose conditions restricted bacterial replication and significantly increased TNF and IL-10 in vitro. Different from our findings, Terán et al. showed that high glucose medium aggravates M. tuberculosis replication in BMDM by a mechanism dependent on inhibition of the transcription factor HIF-1α [46]. The impairment in M. tuberculosis phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages harvested from streptozotocin-induced type 1 diabetic mice negatively affected the activation of adaptive immune response represented by a significant reduction in the frequency of TNF-producing CD4+ cells [42]. In type 1 diabetes, which is also characterized by hyperglycemia, alveolar macrophages but not BMDM macrophages exhibited an impairment in phagocytosis. We found that BMDM under hyperglycemic conditions had no difference in phagocytosis compared to BMDM from normoglycemic conditions. BMDM from db/db mice did not show a difference in internalization of M. tuberculosis compared to BMDM from db + mice. These collecting findings, ours and others, show that it is still a matter of discussion how high glucose condition affects bacterial internalization and growth.

Considering that the role of chemerin has not been investigated in tuberculosis, here we showed that the addition of chemerin in the hyperglycemic medium significantly increased IL-6 secretion and reduced IL-10, unveiling a pro-inflammatory effect of chemerin in vitro. In vivo, mice with type 2 diabetes (db/db mice) and tuberculosis comorbidity were susceptible to M. tuberculosis infection and showed significantly increased pulmonary immunopathology as previously described in a different type 2 diabetic mouse model with male mice fed with HFD for 8 weeks and receiving streptozotocin following M. tuberculosis infection [47]. In this study, Martinez and coworkers described higher mortality, exacerbated lung pathology, and higher bacterial load compared to mice treated with streptozotocin or HFD alone [47]. Therefore, independent of the experimental model of type 2 diabetes, pulmonary immunopathology is aggravated in M. tuberculosis infection.

In the present study, we show that lung pathology in comorbidity type 2 diabetes (db/db mice) and tuberculosis was dependent on chemerin, given that the treatment of infected type 2 diabetic mice with CCX832 chemerin receptor (CMKLR1 or ChemR23) antagonist significantly ameliorated pulmonary inflammation with no effect in bacterial load. Other studies reported the pro-inflammatory role of chemerin receptors. In type 2 diabetes, CCX832 treatment of db/db mice improved vascular function, partially restored vascular insulin responses, decreased glucose levels and vascular oxidative stress [48]. Blockade of CMKLR1 with CCX832 also inhibited the progression of diabetic nephropathy [49]. However, in hepatocellular carcinoma, it was associated with inhibition of nuclear factor-κB activation and reduction in expression of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and IL-6, followed by reduced tumor angiogenesis and restoration of IFN-γ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ cells [50].

Despite the reduction of pulmonary inflammation, here we showed that the treatment with CCX832 chemerin receptor antagonist increased IFN-γ-producing CD4+ cells and IFN-γ secretion. It is well established that type 2 diabetes induces dysfunctional activation of innate and adaptive response. An increase of circulating CD4+IFN-γ+ cells was demonstrated in patients with type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis [26, 37], while the deficiency leptin receptors was associated with reduced lung IFN-γ concentrations [45]. Our findings suggest that although CMKLR1 (ChemR23) is pro-inflammatory, it can negatively affect the recruitment or activation of Th1 cells. Chemerin contributes to inflammatory responses in different ways. It induces macrophage chemotaxis dependent on CMKLR1. Moreover, chemerin induces adhesion through the VLA-4-VCAM-1 interaction and retention of macrophages at inflammatory sites through VLA-5-fibronectin interaction [51]. Here, the blockade of the chemerin-CMKLR1 axis neither affected the influx of alveolar and interstitial macrophages nor the populations of inflammatory (M1/iNOS) and anti-inflammatory (M2/Arg-1) macrophages, but increased Th1 cells. It remains to be investigated whether CMKLR1 might act directly in the recruitment or indirectly in the differentiation or activation of Th1 cells. The limitation of our study is to compare the expression of chemerin receptors in lung endothelial cells, macrophages and Th1 cells, and to correlate the expression of chemerin receptors with VLA-4 on lung leukocytes and VCAM-1 on lung endothelial cells, and to evaluate whether chemerin increases IFN-γ secretion by CD4+ cells. An issue that remains to be investigated is to evaluate the expression of chemerin receptors in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with type 2 diabetes and tuberculosis compared to tuberculosis patients or type 2 diabetes patients, and to evaluate the role of chemerin in MDMs cultured in high glucose medium and infected with M. tuberculosis or MDMs differentiated from the peripheral blood of type 2 diabetic patients and infected with M. tuberculosis. Therefore, the molecular pathways involved in the attenuation of pulmonary immunopathology as well as in increase of IFN-γ associated with blocking of chemerin receptors remain to be investigated in the tuberculosis that courses with type 2 diabetes.

Although similar levels of lung chemerin were found in the lungs of db/db and db + groups, the blocking of CMKLR1 attenuated lung inflammation during M. tuberculosis infection. This finding suggests that the concentrations of serum chemerin might be increased in diabetic group (db/db) compared to non-diabetic (db+) group and that the blocking of adipokine chemerin was beneficial to mitigate lung inflammation.

In conclusion, we show that the blockade of chemerin receptors would represent a target to negatively regulate pulmonary immunological response-mediated pathology accentuated by type 2 diabetes in active tuberculosis.
