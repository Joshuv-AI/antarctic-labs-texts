Kidney dysfunction and its associated factors among people living with HIV on antiretroviral therapy in rural Rwanda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jean Baptiste Niyibizi, Anne Niyigena, Zena Salim, Robert Ojiambo, Arsene Hobabagabo, Robert Tumusime, Grace Umugiraneza, Grace Ganza Songa, Symaque Dusabeyezu, Phoebe Mwiseneza, Jean d’Amour Ndahimana, Innocent Kamali, Chester Kalinda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople living with HIV (PLHIV) have a higher risk of developing stage 3–5 of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) and this risk is further impacted by the use of antiretrovirals including Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), a commonly used antiretroviral throughout SSA. Although, routine renal function testing amongst PLHIV is recommended and integrated into HIV and Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD) national guidelines, the implementation of these guidelines among PLHIV in Rwanda remains a challenge. This study assesses the prevalence of proteinuria and stage 3–5 CKD and its associated risk factors among PLHIV on long-term Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) antiretroviral therapy (ART) in Kayonza district, Rwanda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among PLHIV in Kayonza district, rural Rwanda from April 2023 to October 2023. Urine and blood were collected to test for proteinuria and creatinine. Estimated Glomerular Filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated from serum creatinine using the CKD-Epi formula. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages. A chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the associations between sociodemographic and clinical characteristics with proteinuria and stage 3–5 CKD. A logistic regression model was performed to determine the predictors of proteinuria and CKD at a significance level of < 0.05.ResultsThis study included 1,302 participants, of whom 79.5% (n = 1,034) were aged between 35 and 65 years and 71.2% (n = 928) were female. The majority (84.4%, n = 1,036) were on TDF-based therapy. Proteinuria was observed in 46.8% (n = 610) of participants, while the overall prevalence of stage 3–5 CKD was 2.6% (n = 34). TDF-based therapy (aOR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.1–2.2) and random blood glucose > 200 mg/dl (aOR = 4.60, 95% CI: 1.2–16.5) were significant predictors of renal dysfunction. For CKD, being aged 50 and above (aOR = 8.7, 95% CI: 3.0–25.0) and female gender (aOR = 7.1, 95% CI: 2.1–23.8, p = 0.001) were significant predictors of CKD.ConclusionWe found a low prevalence of stage 3–5 CKD among people living with HIV in Kayonza district, but with a high prevalence of proteinuria. TDF-based therapy and high blood glucose levels were significantly associated with proteinuria. Proteinuria is an early marker for renal dysfunction in HIV patients on TDF-based regimens. Our findings highlight the need for implementing guidelines that enhance routine proteinuria testing and eGFR estimation along with blood glucose monitoring at the early stages of treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13169-x.

Introduction

People Living with HIV [PLHIV] are at increased risk of renal disease including CKD when compared to the general population [1–3]. Proteinuria can indicate early renal glomerular disease and its severity correlates with disease progression and thus is used widely for monitoring of renal impairment [4, 5]. CKD is a condition characterized by a gradual, irreversible loss of kidney function [1]. The estimated global burden average of CKD ranges from 5.9% to 11.7% [6] compared to 12.2–16.5% in SSA [7]. There is not enough data on the prevalence of CKD in SSA among PLHIV but the most recent studies suggest that it ranges anywhere from 11 to 90% [8, 9]. Though, there are 5 stages of CKD, the chronic renal insufficiency is observed in stage 3 of CKD [eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73m2] [10].

Renal dysfunction in PLHIV has a very complex pathophysiology and is often attributed to drug toxicity, late disease diagnosis, and inadequate follow-up [1]. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) therapy remains the standard first line treatment of HIV in many SSA countries [1, 11–14]. Yet, its long-term use in the management of HIV has been shown to damage the proximal tubules of the kidneys which can lead to CKD [15]. Although risk may continue with prolonged exposure, TDF-associated renal damage, including proximal tubular dysfunction and decreased eGFR, has been found to occur more frequently in the first few years following treatment commencement. We employed a 2-year duration criterion to facilitate stable comparisons in our dataset and to differentiate between shorter and longer exposure in a clinically interpretable manner, as early toxicity is frequently seen within this window [9, 16–18].

Rwanda has made progressive gains in attaining the UNAIDS 95-95-95 target where by 95% of Rwandans know their status, 95% receive antiretroviral treatment and 95% attain viral load suppression before 2030 [19]. In this regard, as of 2019, 83.8% adult living with HIV knew their HIV status, and amongst those 97.5% were enrolled into care and treatment programs receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). Of the patients actively on ART, 90.1% had viral suppression [20]. Though there is a great progress towards achieving UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets across countries in East Africa, Rwanda continues to take the lead [20–23]. As of 2022, the prevalence of HIV in the general population in Rwanda was at 3% in 2022 [13].

Efforts to enhance disease prevention, management and survival of PLHIV in Rwanda have been facilitated by increasing the frequency of disease screening, enhanced health promotion activities, and increased home visits by caregivers and community health workers [24]. Although these activities have led to a reduction in HIV-associated mortalities, there has been a rise in the prevalence of age and drug toxicity leading to the increased risks of NCDs such as CKD [14, 25]. This was incorporated following the guidelines of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) for routine evaluation of renal function using proteinuria and serum creatinine tests in patients receiving treatment for HIV [26]. Unfortunately, studies have shown that shortage of laboratory supplies such as urine dipsticks and reagents is a prevalent barrier to the timely detection and management of kidney diseases in Rwanda’s health facilities [27]. As such the magnitude of renal dysfunction among PLHIV in Rwanda is not well characterized. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of proteinuria, stage 3–5 CKD, and their associated risk factors among PLHIV on ART regimen in rural Rwanda.

Methods

Study setting

Our study was conducted in southern region of Kayonza district, a low-income setting located in the Eastern province of Rwanda. Southern Kayonza is serviced by one district hospital (Rwinkwavu District Hospital) and nine health centers. These health facilities are supported by Partners in Health/Inshuti Mu Buzima (PIH/IMB). PIH/IMB is an international non-government organization (NGO) which has supported the Rwandan Ministry of Health (MOH) in health systems strengthening since 2005. PLHIV are managed in HIV clinics at health centers or at district hospitals. HIV care and treatment in Rwanda is provided by nurses, and each health facility has basic laboratory units, which are capacitated to support routine diagnostic tests including urine dipstick to measure proteinuria and serum creatinine testing [28].

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was conducted during a mass screening campaign for kidney function among PLHIV enrolled into health facilities in southern Kayonza by a joint effort of PIH and MOH. The target population in this study was PLHIV, who were enrolled in the HIV treatment program at nine health centers in the catchment areas of Rwinkwavu hospital in Kayonza district.

Initial proteinuria and serum creatinine tests were done during the mass screening campaign, and a 3-month follow up for retesting for proteinuria and serum creatinine were done for participants who had an abnormal eGFR at the initial testing. The initial screening was conducted from April to June 2023 and retesting follow up was conducted from September to October 2023.

Sampling and inclusion criteria

Study participants included PLHIV enrolled in HIV treatment at health centers or the district hospital within Rwinkwavu catchment area during the study period. They were informed of the CKD screening program and invited to voluntarily participate. Convenient sampling was used to include all PLHIV who attended the screening activities at each health facility. The study included PLHIV who had been on ART for at least 2 years, voluntarily attended the mass screening campaign between April 28th and May 30th, 2023, and who provided informed written consent form to participate in the study. For participants aged less than 18, the consent was provided by their parents or guardians. We excluded PLHIV who declined to consent to participate in the screening activities.

Data collection and variable

Routine clinical data were collected during the CKD mass screening campaign by trained clinicians and lab technicians along with self-reported demographic and clinical data. We collected sociodemographic variables that included age, gender, weight, height, marital status, occupation, and ubudehe categories. Ubudehe is a system of socio-economic ranking with 4 tiers, where ubudehe 1 represents the poorest while ubudehe 4 represents the most economically stable [29]. Lifestyle characteristics included the history of the use of traditional medicine, diet restriction due to other diseases and use of alcohol for the last 12 months. Clinical characteristics included random blood glucose levels, current ART regimen as documented in patients electronic health records, BMI, proteinuria, and serum creatinine levels.

Urine samples were tested for total proteinuria using dipstick reagent strips, and blood samples were analyzed for serum creatinine. Random capillary blood glucose levels were measured using point of care SD Codefree glucometer. Serum creatinine levels were determined using kinetic Jaffe method with a Humalyzer 3000 clinical chemistry analyzer calibrated against a standard concentration of creatinine [30]. Data were recorded by trained data collectors using mUzima, an offline mobile-based app that is interoperable with OpenMRS, the electronic medical record (EMR) system. The offline data was then synced with electronic medical records in Open MRS once connected to the internet.

Diabetes was suspected for random blood glucose levels > 200 mg/dl. Body Mass Index (BMI) cutoff was considered to classify participants as being underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal (BMI = 18.50-24.99), overweight (25-29.99) and obese (≥ 30). We indicated proteinuria in participants who had trace up to ≥ 1000 mg/dl (4+) protein in urine based on color change on chemical urine reagent strips. The Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) was calculated from serum creatinine using the 2021 CKD-EPI eGFR Eq. (32) computed using STATA software version 18, a new more equitable equation that do not consider race. There are five stages of CKD according to eGFR results for 3 months or more: Stage 1 [eGFR ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73m2, Normal kidney function], stage 2 [eGFR 60-89 ml/min/1.73m2, Early stage kidney disease], stage 3 [eGFR 30-59 ml/min/1.73m2, Mild to moderate loss of kidney function], stage 4 [eGFR15-29 ml/min/1.73m2, Severe loss of kidney function] and stage 5 [eGFR<15 ml/min/1.73m2, Kidney failure] [31, 32]. In our study, stage 3 CKD was diagnosed if the calculated eGFR was < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 and stage 3 CKD was ruled out if eGFR was > 60 ml/min/1.73m2 [32–34]. Serum creatinine was measured at baseline, and participants with eGFR<60 ml/min/1.73m2 were retested 3 months after the initial measurement. Stage 3–5 CKD was confirmed only when a patient had eGFR<60 ml/min/1.73m2 at both baseline and the 3 month- follow-up.

Data analysis

We analyzed socio-demographic characteristics of the participants who tested positive for stage 3-5 CKD including sex, age, level of education, and marital status, categorized as single, married or cohabitating and widow or divorced/separated. Additionally, we described lifestyle characteristics such as restricted diet (participants who are on low salt, low carbohydrate or low-fat diet regimen), alcohol use history, and use of traditional medicine. We recorded self-reported comorbidities at the time of screening, including hypertension, proteinuria, CKD and ART regimen with a particular focus on TDF or non-TDF-based regimens.

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data using expressed as means and standard deviations. Association between socio-demographic and clinical characteristics with eGFR were assessed using Chi-Square tests, or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to determine predictors of proteinuria and CKD (eGFR), including socio-demo and clinical characteristics with a p-value of less than 0.1 from the bivariate analysis.

All analysis was conducted using STATA statistical Software version18 (STATASE. Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Participants considered in the research project and renal function test results

In this study, it is worth noting that there has been a variation in sample size from initial selection throughout follow up. The initial total number of participants was 1302 who were tested for anthropometric screening measurements and of these only1287 participants were tested for serum creatinine and urine protein. Initial results revealed that 1147 had normal eGFR and thus were excluded from re-testing after 3months. A total of 140 participants had abnormal eGFR and were followed-up for re-testing after 3months, but 43 were lost to follow-up. From the initial testing, a total of 610 had proteinuria. Among the 97 who were retested, 34 were confirmed to have developed stage 3–5 CKD and among these, 16 had proteinuria (Fig. 1).

Social-demographic, and clinical characteristics of study participants results

Of the 1302 participants included, 10.1% (n = 132) were under 35 years of age 79.5%(n = 1,034] were between 35 and 65 years, 71.2% (n = 928) were female. Additionally, 39.4%(n = 513) had no formal education (Table 1). A total of 12.7% (n = 163) reported using traditional medicine, while 12.6% (n = 164) were on a restricted diet. Furthermore, 40.7% (n = 531) had consumed at least one glass of alcohol in the last 12 months. The majority of participants, 84.3% (n = 1,036) were on TDF-based therapy. Of the participants, 1.4%(n = 19) screened positive for diabetes; 25.1%(n = 327) for hypertension; 46.8% (n = 610) had proteinuria; and 2.6% (n = 34) were confirmed to have stage 3-5 CKD.

Bivariate analysis of social demographic and clinical characteristics and proteinuria

In the bivariate analysis, high random blood glucose levels and employment status were significantly associated with proteinuria (p = 0.005 and p = 0.013 respectively). Among those who were unemployed, 48.8% (n = 42) had proteinuria, while 35.4% (n = 45) of those employed did not. Furthermore 78.9% of those with a random glucose reading of > 200 mg/dl had proteinuria. Age, sex, education, marital status, BMI, ART regimen, hypertension, use of traditional medicine, restriction from some foods, history of taking alcohol in the past 12 months, receiving advice for salt diet control, frequency of eating fresh vegetables and Ubudehe categories were not associated with proteinuria (Table 2).

Bivariate analysis of social demographic and clinical characteristics and stage 3-5 CKD

Age, sex, marital status, receiving advice for diet salt control, and diet restriction were associated with stage 3-5 CKD (p < 0.001, p = 0.008, p = 0.002, p = 0.001, p = 0.006, respectively). 3.5% (n = 31) of females compared to 0.8% (n = 3) of males had stage 3-5 CKD; 5% (n = 19) of divorced participants compared to 1.5% (n = 5) of married participants had CKD; 6.1% (n = 9) of those who were not on diet restriction compared to 2.2% (n = 24) of those who were on diet restriction had stage 3-5 CKD; Additionally, 6.2% (n = 12) of those who received advice for salt control compared to 2.0% (n = 22) of those who did not receive salt control advice had stage 3-5 CKD (Table 3). Education, BMI, ART regimen, levels of random blood glucose level, blood pressure, proteinuria status, use of traditional medicine, receiving advice for salt control, diet restriction, and alcohol use were not statistically associated with stage 3-5 CKD.

Multivariable analysis independently associated variables with proteinuria

In Table 4, education, current ART regimen and history of diabetes were associated with proteinuria. The odds of having proteinuria were 23% (aOR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.6–0.9) lower in participants with primary school education compared to those with no formal education. In addition, the odds of having proteinuria among participants on TDF-based therapy were 61% (aOR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.1–2.2) higher compared to those on abacavir-based therapy. Furthermore, for those on other non-TDF and non-Abacavir combinations, the odds of having proteinuria were 2.1 (95% CI:1.0–4.4, p-value = 0.047) fold higher than those on Abacavir therapy. Similarly, the odds of having proteinuria were 4.6 times among diabetics compared to non-diabetic participants (aOR = 4.6, 95% CI: 1.2–16.5). Employment, Ubudehe category, hypertension, and frequency of eating vegetables were not significant predictors of having proteinuria (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study reports the prevalence of proteinuria and stage 3–5 CKD among PLHIV in rural setting of Rwanda. We found that 46.8% (n = 610) of PLHIV had proteinuria, while the prevalence of stage 3–5 CKD was 2.6%. The prevalence of proteinuria in our study aligns with findings from a study done in Tanzania which reported proteinuria of 50% [35], however the prevalence of proteinuria in the current study was higher than those reported in Ghana (30.9%) [36] and DRC(20.5%) [37]. The differences in proteinuria levels between our study and others may be due to the inclusion of ART- naïve participants in other studies, whereas our study included only those who had been on ART for at least two years. An earlier study in Rwanda reported a 9% prevalence of proteinuria among ART naïve participants, except for some participants who were exposed to single-dose nevirapine prior to the study [38].

This study shows that the prevalence of proteinuria was higher among individuals with elevated random blood sugar levels and those on a TDF-based regimen. These findings align with other studies conducted in other countries across the globe [37, 39–43]. The growing burden of type 2 Diabetes Mellitus has been previously shown to contribute to the increasing prevalence of renal disease, proteinuria and end-stage kidney disease [44]. Albuminuria, which progresses from microalbuminuria to nephrotic-range proteinuria is a clinical marker of diabetic nephropathy [45]. It affects approximately one-third of diabetic patients and is regarded as an independent risk factor for the advancement of diabetic nephropathy and all-cause mortality [46, 47]. Proteinuria develops due to the destruction of any of the Glomerular Filtration Barrier (GFB) layers in the diabetic kidney [45]. TDF regimens have been linked to damage in kidney glomerulus or nephrotic tubules [14, 48]. Peyriere et al. (2015) suggested that switching from TDF to non-TDF based therapy can reverse proteinuria levels, emphasizing the need for continuous and routine testing for proteinuria and blood glucose to detect renal dysfunction early in PLHIV [45].

The results from this study showed that the odds of having proteinuria among participants on TDF-based therapy were 61% (aOR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.1–2.2) higher compared to those on Abacavir-based therapy. In Rwanda, though both TDF based therapy (TDF/3TC/DTG or TDF/3TC/EFV600) and Abacavir are considered to be the first line regimen for HIV treatment in adolescent and adults, the preferred first line is TDF based therapy. Abacavir is used when TDF therapy is contraindicated such as when the eGFR is < 60, according to the national guidelines [49]. The results of this study confirm the possibility of TDF toxicity or adverse effects to the kidney as higher proteinuria levels were detected in PLHIV who were on TDF compared to those who were on Abacavir. These findings underline the importance of knowing a patient’s eGFR prior to initiating therapy. Thus, we recommend that eGFR be tested for all new PLHIV prior to starting treatment in order to guide which ART therapy of choice to start with.

This study observed lower prevalence of stage 3-5 CKD compared to other reports from sub-Saharan Africa from which CKD prevalence averaged from 12.2 to 16.5% [50–52]. In this study, obesity was not statistically associated with CKD development. Therefore, low prevalence of CKD from the current study could be a result of the low incidence of obesity in the study population (2.1%) which is known to be a major independent risk factor for CKD development [53–57]. Another reason could be Rwanda’s national policy for early NCD screening for diseases including high blood pressure, diabetes and obesity that might reduce the comorbidity with other chronic diseases including HIV [58]. Several factors influence creatinine clearance, including diet, muscle mass, medication, and concurrent sickness [59, 60], in this study, due to some interventions including advice for diet, physical exercise and therapy may reduce the risk of developing CKD [61–65].

TDF has a demonstrable effect on tubular function, although this is usually not clinically apparent unless aggravated by risk factors such as pre-existing renal illness, age, and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus [38]. In this study, among the comorbidities assessed such as hypertension and diabetes also had a low incidence and thus were not statistically associated with development of CKD. However, our findings are consistent with earlier studies in SSA that reported a higher prevalence of CKD among individuals aged 50 years and above [1, 36, 38, 66, 67]. As people age, their glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal blood flow gradually decline suggesting the need for more and regular screening among aging HIV positive patients [68].

In addition, the findings from this study showed that women are at higher risk of developing stage 3-5 CKD compared to males, an observation also made by Obri-Yeboah et al., in Ghana [36]. Women may have CKD at higher rates because they get urinary tract infections more frequently than males, as well as pregnancy-related issues including high blood pressure, gestational diabetes, or eclampsia [69]. Despite this, men are at higher risk of progressing to end stage kidney failure than women because of changes and differences in hormone levels [70]. For instance, men who have higher testosterone levels may experience decreased renal function [71] while estrogen, which serves as a protective renal hormone, is lower in men [72].

In this study, TDF containing regimens were not statistically associated with developing CKD, an outcome that contradicts what several studies have found [1, 11, 12, 14]. This finding could mean that participants who experienced TDF side effects may have switched to alternative ART regimens during the study potentially mitigating the early development of CKD. In addition to this, since the study did not capture information regarding the duration on a specific ART, or if switched to other ARTs, participants who had only been on TDF therapy for a short period of time would be less likely to have developed CKD. Furthermore, in this study, there was a low proportion of participants with high-risk features for developing CKD, such as aged 65 and above (10.3%), obesity (2.1%), and diabetes (1.4%). In fact, among participants who developed stage 3–5 CKD, only one was obese while none of them were diabetic.

Strength and Limitations

Our study was unique in that it was one of few studies that have been conducted in a rural area of the SSA country. The study involved a large number of study participants and collected a wide range of information such as socioeconomic status, nutrition status, alcohol use and other related lifestyle that contribute to normal renal function risks of developing the disease. Moreover, our study repeated the measurement of both creatinine and proteinuria at 3 months.

Our study had several limitations. First, it focused on one rural district of Rwanda, which may limit, the generalizability of the findings. Second, we did a semi-quantitative for the total protein detection in urine and we did not measure quantitative urine albumin or 24-hour urine creatinine levels to determine the albumin-creatinine ratio, which is more specific for renal glomerular function. Third, the specific duration of ART therapy was not captured (apart from being on 2 or more years on ART), preventing assessment of the impact of treatment duration on stage 3–5 CKD and proteinuria development. Furthermore, we did not evaluate other co-infections (including hepatitis C or B), immunological markers (such as CD4, CD8), or viral load. Future studies should expand nationwide with a focus on comprehensive lab testing, duration of drug-based therapy, and other co-infections. The authors acknowledge the effect of the sample size limitation and loss of some participants to follow up towards findings of the current study. The study employed convenience sampling methods and as such participants who had previous renal dysfunction caused by something other than TDF regimen therapy, could have attended the screening which subsequently would have led to selection bias. Since this was a cross-sectional study, a cohort research study is needed to infer causality and determine temporal relationships between exposure and outcome with closed monitoring of the drug therapy regimen and the shifting pattern.

Conclusion

We found a high prevalence of proteinuria with 2.6% of participants having stage 3–5 chronic kidney disease. Proteinuria serves as an early marker for renal dysfunction in HIV patients on TDF based therapy. We recommend frequent testing of proteinuria for PLHIV who are on TDF-based therapy, as well as routine estimation of eGFR alongside serum creatinine levels for the care of PLHIV in rural HIV clinics in Rwanda and similar settings across Sub-Saharan Africa. Prior to starting ART regimen, it is advisable to have eGFR determined to decide on which first line to use. High random blood sugars were found to be predictors for developing proteinuria. PLHIV should have their BMI, blood sugar and blood pressure checked regularly as to prevent risks of developing stage 3–5 chronic kidney disease.
