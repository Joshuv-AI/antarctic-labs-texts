Challenging the Tradition of Fasting: Early Oral Refeeding After Post-Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography Pancreatitis Shows Safety and Efficiency
Authors: Yoon Suk Lee, Jai Hoon Yoon
Published: 2025

Despite decades of clinical practice, the decision on when to resume feeding after pancreatitis often relies more on tradition than evidence. Traditionally, the amount of caloric and fat intake of initial meal for pancreatitis patients is step-wisely increased from clear liquid to full caloric solid diet.1 This conventional sequence is mainly based on anecdotal experience rather than scientific evidences on refeeding progress, believing that early oral intake could stimulate exocrine activities, thereby potentially delaying clinical improvement of the inflamed pancreas.2 However, this traditional treatment strategy for acute pancreatitis has been challenged, and emerging evidence suggests that early feeding with initial soft diet is safe and well tolerated, particularly in mild severity grade of acute pancreatitis.3,4 However, acute pancreatitis caused by iatrogenic injury during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) were excluded in the abovementioned studies as the pathophysiology of post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) may be differ by potential coexistence of procedure-related injuries. Moreover, the risk of procedure-related complications could be up to 14.7% in high-risk patients or technical difficulty cases.5,6 Therefore, for the patients with PEP, clinical evidence of early refeeding cannot be fully supported from the clinical evidences in patients with acute pancreatitis so far, thereby limiting the extension into the patient with PEP.

In the current issue of Gut and Liver, Jo et al.7 present a multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) that directly challenges this conventional management in the specific context of PEP, providing the first prospective evidence that early oral refeeding (ERF) is both safe and beneficial in patients with mild PEP. This open-label, prospective, multicenter RCT enrolled 80 patients diagnosed with mild PEP across nine academic hospitals in South Korea between February 2021 and September 2022. Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either ERF or delayed refeeding (DRF). In the ERF group, oral intake began 24 hours after PEP diagnosis, whereas in the DRF group, refeeding commenced once bowel sounds normalized and abdominal pain decreased to a visual analog scale score below 2. The primary endpoint was PEP-related hospitalization duration, and secondary endpoints included the development of severe pancreatitis, 30-day re-admission, and PEP-related mortality. Baseline characteristics, procedural parameters, and PEP severity were well balanced between two groups. No significant differences were found in procedure time, or cannulation difficulty with common bile duct stones being the most frequent indication for ERCP. The key finding was a significantly shorter hospital stay in the ERF group compared with the DRF group (2.93±1.59 days vs 3.78±1.97 days; p=0.026) with the relative risk of prolonged hospitalization was 0.75 (95% confidence interval, 0.59 to 0.97) Importantly, there were no statistical differences in the rates of refeeding interruption, re-admission within 30 days, or PEP-related mortality with no patient who developed severe or necrotizing pancreatitis between two groups. Therefore, this RCT provides the first clinical evidence supporting the early refeeding strategy can be applicable even in PEP patients without increasing the risk of complications and delaying recovery. Although this study only included the PEP patients with mild severity grade, the approximately 1-day reduction in hospital stay implies economically meaningful benefits given that the mild severity grade comprised about 80% of all PEP patients.

As a clinical consideration and caution, while ERF proved safe overall in this randomized trial, clinicians should remain cautious when applying this strategy to real-world settings. Notably, one patient in the ERF group developed hematemesis, whereas another in the DRF group experienced prerenal acute kidney injury. Given that post-ERCP bleeding occurs in approximately 1% to 2% of cases and may be delayed up to 2 weeks after the procedure,8 early refeeding should be individualized, particularly in patients with intraprocedural bleeding at the endoscopic sphincterotomy site, underlying coagulopathy, or recent antithrombotic use.9 Conversely, DRF may predispose patients to dehydration and renal hypoperfusion; therefore, adequate intravenous hydration is essential during fasting.

In conclusion, this RCT represents a meaningful step toward evidence-based management of PEP patients, demonstrating that ERF is both safe and effective in mild PEP. However, when it comes to the application into real clinical practice, the early refeeding strategy should be balanced with the severity grade of PEP and risk of endoscopic sphincterotomy site bleeding, thereby it should be applied into clinical practice by patient-centered approaches. Therefore, future trials including moderate or severe PEP are warranted to delineate whether the benefit of early feeding extends beyond mild cases.
