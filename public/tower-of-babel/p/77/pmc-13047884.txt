Pancreas Transplantation in the Modern Era: Observations and Implications of Donor Pancreas Extraction Time
Authors: Michael A. Deal, Christof Kaltenmeier, Perwaiz Nawabi, Steven I. Hanish, Prabhakar Baliga, Santosh Nagaraju, Kannan Samy, Dirk J. van der Windt, Diego DiSabato, John W. McGillicuddy, David J. Taber, Joseph R. Scalea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere has been a worldwide reduction in pancreas transplant volume over the last decade. Understanding factors, such as procurement extraction time (ET), that play a key role in organ recovery can increase confidence in organ acceptance and improve organ utilization. All patients who underwent deceased donor pancreas transplantation at a single US center between January 2019 and August 2024 were stratified by ET and retrospectively reviewed. A total of 137 transplants were included in the analysis with 72 patients in the ET ≤ 40 min group and 65 patients in the ET > 40 min group. We found no significant difference in all‐cause pancreas graft loss between the ET ≤ 40 min and ET > 40 min group (6.9% vs 12%; p = 0.482), but there was a higher readmission rate at 90 days in the ET ≤ 40 min group (60% vs 39%; p = 0.015) regardless of adjustments in multivariate analysis. We demonstrate for the first time at a US center that while ET is not associated with all‐cause graft loss, it may impact postoperative outcomes such as readmission rates and could have important implications for the future of pancreas utilization.

Introduction

The management of diabetes mellitus and its associated complications remains a challenge amongst physicians and healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2, 3]. Pancreas transplantation is a definitive management strategy to reduce the long‐term complications associated with diabetes while improving quality of life through durable glycemic control and long‐term insulin independence [4, 5]. Despite multiple surgical advances in pancreas transplantation and dramatic improvements in pancreas graft survival in the last two decades, there has been a steady decline in the number of pancreas transplants nationwide [5, 6]. Described as a “crisis in confidence” among leaders in the field, this decline has become particularly precipitous since 2005 with similar trends in South America, Africa, and Europe [7, 8]. Declines have been attributed to the lack of primary referrals for transplant, improvements in diabetes care, and aversion to the well‐documented risks of pancreas transplant [9, 10]. Integral to the conceptualization of this study, reductions in pancreas utilization may also be related to the recovery of the pancreas. This may be for several reasons, including its technical challenges and rates of reported organ injury in an estimated 25%–50% of cases [11, 12]. Injuries incurred during recovery and longer preservation times can contribute to duodenal leaks, wound infections, graft thrombosis, and rejection after pancreas transplantation [13, 14].

Given the decreasing number of US pancreas transplant centers, American Society of Transplant Surgeons (ASTS)‐certified pancreas transplantation fellowship training programs, and number of abdominal organ recovery surgeons who are experienced in pancreas recovery, understanding the nuances of recovery and effects of factors related to recovery are becoming increasingly paramount [8]. Additionally, and less described, are the changing trends in organ recovery. With a decrease in pancreas utilization, there are also fewer opportunities to learn the recovery procedure. Historically, the recovery surgeon was also the surgeon performing the transplant. Though today, there are multiple organ recovery services that contract with hospitals to recover organs, changing the dynamic of recovery, including the practice of recovering the pancreas.

One aspect of organ recovery includes extraction time (ET), the period between aortic cold flush and the static cold storage of the organ. Assessing the relationships between ET in the recovery of abdominal organs and posttransplant outcomes has been most described in kidney transplant and to a lesser degree in liver transplant [15, 16, 17, 18]. To our knowledge, there have only been 2 prior studies examining these relationships in pancreas transplantation, neither of which were performed in the United States thus demonstrating a gap in our knowledge [6, 10].

The primary aim of our study was to assess the relationship between ET and all‐cause pancreas graft loss. The secondary aim was to assess the relationship between ET and (1) glycemic control in the intra‐ and postoperative period and (2) postoperative outcomes, including length of stay, re‐laparotomy, percutaneous drain placement, delayed renal allograft function, readmission at 30 and 90 days, and serum lipase. We hypothesized that a longer ET would be associated with increased all‐cause graft loss and negatively impact recipient outcomes.

Methods

This study was reviewed by the Medical University of South Carolina Institutional Review Board (Pro00142549) and determined to be exempt from further review.

Data Collection

All patients who underwent deceased donor pancreas transplantation at a single US center between January 2019 and August 2024 were retrospectively reviewed.

Operative technique for pancreas transplant was simultaneous pancreas and kidney (SPK) transplant. Patient demographics, including dialysis duration and indication for transplant, were reviewed. Donor information, including donor demographics, cold ischemic time (CIT), kidney donor profile index (KDPI), and ET were examined. There was no standardization of procurement surgical teams or approach. Intraoperative data, such as intraoperative glucoses and use of drains, as well as postoperative data, such as length of stay, blood glucose, A1c, serum lipase, initiation of hypoglycemic agents, reoperation, percutaneous drain placement, delayed renal allograft function, and readmission at 30 and 90 days were reviewed. Patients were then stratified into two groups: ET ≤ 40 min and ET > 40 min based on cohort median ET and distribution of available data.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the relationship between ET and all‐cause pancreas graft loss during the study period. Secondary outcomes included (1) blood sugar control in the intraoperative time frame as well as the postoperative time frame assessed by blood glucoses during the index hospitalization, A1c at 1 year, and initiation of hypoglycemic agents; (2) length of stay; (3) re‐laparotomy; (4) postoperative percutaneous drain placement; (5) delayed renal allograft function; (6) readmission at 30 and 90 days; and (7) postoperative serum lipases.

Recipient Operative Course

All pancreas transplants in this study were from deceased donors and performed by two surgical teams—one team performing the backbench preparation while the second team performed the implantation. These teams worked simultaneously to minimize operative time. The back bench preparation included a splenectomy, shortening and oversewing of both duodenal ends, oversewing of the small bowel mesentery, and creation of the Y‐graft. The implantation began with a midline laparotomy and mobilization of the right colon and small bowel mesentery to expose the inferior vena cava and aortic bifurcation. For portally drained allografts, the recipient's superior mesenteric vein was used as an outflow. A handsewn, 2‐layered, side‐to‐side duodenojejunostomy approximately 40 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz was used for exocrine drainage. The kidney was placed intraperitoneally on the left side. Prophylactic transabdominal drains in the retroperitoneum around the pancreas. After pancreas reperfusion, the mean arterial pressure was maintained over 75 mm Hg, and point‐of‐care glucose checks were performed every 10 min.

Posttransplant Care and Immunosuppression

Postoperatively, mean arterial pressure was maintained at > 75 mm Hg. Frequent lab monitoring, including immediate amylase and lipase, as well as hourly glucose checks were performed for the first 24 h then transitioned to every 4 h for the remainder of the hospitalization to closely monitor for both hyper‐ and hypoglycemia. Nasogastric decompression was maintained for 24 h posttransplant followed by NGT removal and progression to ice chips on postoperative day (POD) 2, clear liquids on POD 3, and a regular diet on POD 4. A doppler ultrasound of the renal allograft was performed on POD 0 and of the pancreas allograft on POD 2. Drain amylase was checked at the discretion of the surgeon based on clinical suspicion for pancreas leak. After discharge, all patients were followed closely in the outpatient setting. Drains were usually removed at the first outpatient follow‐up visit if there was no suspicion of a pancreas leak.

For induction immunosuppression, patients either received thymoglobulin or basiliximab. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolic acid, and prednisone.

Statistical Analysis

Chi‐square tests were used to compare all categorical variables between the two groups. Two‐tailed T‐tests with equal variances assumed were used to compare all continuous variables between the two groups with the mean and standard deviations reported as appropriate. A multivariate logistic regression was performed for selected subgroup analyses. Statistical significance was determined at the α = 0.05 level (p ≤ 0.05) for all tests. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, MS Excel, and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Exclusion Criteria

A total of 156 pancreas transplants were performed between January 2019 and August 2024. Of these, 15 transplants did not have ET available and 1 transplant had ET > 300 min, so these were excluded from analysis. Patients who underwent pancreas transplant alone [3] were also excluded from analysis. With the remaining 137 transplants included in analysis, 72 patients were in the ET ≤ 40 min group (median ET: 30 min, range: 8–39 min) and 65 patients were in the ET > 40 min group (median ET: 51 min, range: 40–103 min) (Figure 1). The total study population included 87 males (64%) and 50 females (36%) with a total median ET of 39 min (IQR: 30–49 min). The most common indication for transplant was T1DM (64%) (Table 1).

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

There was no significant difference in all‐cause pancreas graft loss between the ET ≤ 40 min and ET > 40 min group (6.9% vs 12%; p = 0.316), (Table 4) up to 5 years posttransplant for our cohort. Additionally, there was no difference in mean ET between those that experienced all‐cause graft loss and the rest of the cohort (42.2 min vs 40.8 min; p = 0.771). Of the patients that had pancreas graft loss, 54% (7/13) required reoperation and 46% (6/13) required readmission. Causes of death‐censored graft loss included thrombosis (2/3, 66%) and rejection (1/3, 33%) in the ET ≤ 40 min group and similarly thrombosis (2/3, 66%) and rejection (1/3, 33%) in the ET > 40 min group. Of those that experienced death‐censored graft loss, only 3 total patients experienced delayed renal allograft function requiring dialysis. Regarding the secondary outcomes, there was a significantly higher readmission rate at 90 days, but not 30 days, in the ET ≤ 40 min group compared to the ET > 40 min group (60% vs 40%; p = 0.032). A thematic analysis identified some common themes for all‐cause readmissions in both groups, including infectious causes (such as, bacteremia, UTI, Clostridium difficile, norovirus, toxoplasmosis), laboratory abnormalities (such as, elevated creatinine, hyperglycemia, hyperkalemia, anemia), and fever of unknown origin. There was no difference between the 2 groups in terms of length of stay, intraoperative glucoses during transplant, A1c at 1‐year posttransplant, postoperative serum lipase, average postoperative glucose during index hospitalization, postoperative initiation of injectable hypoglycemic agents, reoperation, delayed renal allograft function, or percutaneous drain placement (Tables 5, 6). The most common indications for reoperation among the study population included bleeding (10/29, 34%), small bowel obstruction (6/29, 21%), and graft thrombosis (6/29, 21%). The incidence of graft thrombosis was similar between the two groups (ET ≤ 40 min: 5.6%, 4/72; ET > 40 min: 3.8%, 2/65). All percutaneous drain placements were for large peri‐transplant fluid collections (8/10, 80%) or abscess (2/10, 20%).

Multivariate Analysis—ET as Continuous Variable

To minimize confounding, ET was then used as a continuous variable, scaled in 10‐minute increments as part of a multivariate regression model. First, a multivariable linear regression was completed with adjustments for recipient age, A1c, BMI, and dialysis duration. In this model, each additional 10‐minute increase in ET was associated with a 0.53‐day longer postoperative LOS, although this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063). There also was no statistical significance in postoperative lipase at day 0, 1 month, 3‐month, and 6‐month time points or intraoperative glucose average (Table 7). For our binary outcomes, a multivariable logistic regression was then completed again with adjustments for recipient age, A1c, BMI, and dialysis duration. There was no significant difference in re‐laparotomy (p = 0.61), percutaneous drain placement (p = 0.56), delayed renal allograft function (p = 0.650) or 30‐day readmission (p = 0.080). There was, however, a persistent inverse relationship with 90‐day readmission rate (p = 0.019) in which each additional increase in 10‐minute increments of ET decreased the adjusted odds of 90‐day readmission by 24% (Table 8). Lastly, within this multivariate model, there was no extraction‐time threshold that was associated with increased odds of re‐laparotomy, drain placement, or readmission within the limitations of a low sample of patients with longer ETs (ET > 60 min: 15, ET > 70 min: 7).

Subgroup Analysis—ET Over Study Period

Given our study population spanned 5 years of data collection, we were also interested in understanding the trend of ET for accepted organs and if this significantly differed over time. Prior to 2022, the mean ET for accepted pancreata (n = 65) at our institution was 43.1 min (SD 18.7) compared to 38.4 min (SD 14.7) during and after 2022 (n = 73). This was an absolute difference of 4.6 min (SEM = ± 2.8) and though it trended towards significance, it was not statistically different (p = 0.10).

Discussion

Here we describe a thorough retrospective assessment of the relationships between ET and posttransplant outcomes in 137 pancreas transplant recipients over a 5‐year period at a single center in the United States.

The median ET for our study population was 39 min with the selected extracted time stratification of ≤ 40 min versus > 40 min allowing for a near equal number of well‐matched patients in each study group (72 vs 65). These groups had no significant difference in sex, race, indication for transplant, operative technique, induction agent, age, BMI, A1c, kidney preservation time, or dialysis duration. As expected, pancreas preservation time was significantly different between the two groups given that ET is a component of cold time and total preservation time. While there was inherent variability in donors, our group did not differ in age or BMI but did differ in KDPI with a higher KDPI in the ET > 40‐minute group. Only 5 patients in our study population had both an ET > 40 min and KDPI > 50 indicative of the selective nature for pancreas donation. A1c was not included in our donor dataset or results as donor A1c has previously been shown to not be associated with various short‐term outcomes in pancreas transplant patients [19].

Regarding our primary outcome, we did not demonstrate a significant difference in all‐cause pancreas graft loss between ET groups with an overall graft and patient survival rate of 90.5%, which is comparable to other similar volume US pancreas transplant centers [20] and should instill confidence in surgeons accepting a pancreas for transplantation. These results are also consistent with the two European‐based prior studies that have assessed the associations of ET and various pancreas transplantation outcomes [6, 10]. Interestingly, in those prior studies the median ETs were 64 min [6] and 65 min [10]. While we did not have a sufficient study population to create ET ≤ 60 min and ET > 60 min groups, we can assume that an upper limit exists. We anticipate that within a well‐powered analysis including patients above a theoretical upper limit (i.e., ET > 60–70 min), there would be increased rates of graft dysfunction and all‐cause graft loss.

The purpose of our secondary outcomes was to identify any associations between ET and the various complications of pancreas transplantation. Given there are well‐documented rates of surgical complications following pancreas transplant [21], we reasoned that there would be a significant difference in various complications when comparing the ET ≤ 40‐minute group to the ET > 40‐minute group or when assessing ET as a continuous variable. There were no significant differences in length of stay, intraoperative or postoperative glucose averages, A1c at 1 year postoperatively, serum lipases at various postoperative time points, initiation of insulin postoperatively, re‐laparotomy, percutaneous drain placement, or delayed renal allograft function. There was, however, a significant difference in the rate of readmission at 90 days with a trend toward significance of readmission at 30 days with higher readmission rates in the ET ≤ 40‐minute group. These findings persisted in multivariate analysis, decreasing the potential for confounding. These results are of unknown clinical significance but perhaps suggest possible occult injury to the pancreas during an accelerated recovery related to a less meticulous technique that contributes to downstream effects prompting readmission. Alternatively, longer ETs may occur in a healthier donor in whom there was an increased number of procured organs. This could serve as an additional explanation as to why increased ETs were associated with lower readmission rates. Despite theoretical concerns that longer ETs would affect graft perfusion and therefore rates of graft pancreatitis and thrombosis, there were no significant associations with postoperative glucose, postoperative serum lipase, percutaneous drain placement, delayed renal allograft function, or re‐laparotomy in our multivariate analysis. In our literature review, we noted a paucity of investigation into damage incurred to the donor pancreas during procurement [22]. Therefore, a study designed to further delineate pancreas anatomy at the time of recovery to detect possible occult injuries is a current focus at our center.

There are several possible interpretations of our findings. Although supporting data are limited, our results suggest that longer ETs did not negatively impact recipient outcomes. Second, longer ETs may reflect healthier donors and an increased number of procured organs without significant effect on pancreas transplant outcomes. Finally, as the number of surgeons trained in pancreas recovery declines and surgical recovery services expand, it is possible that the highest‐quality organs (e.g., from highly selected donors with low KDPI) are increasingly concentrated among a small group of experienced surgeons, further diminishing the apparent influence of traditionally important risk factors such as ET.

This work contributes to the further optimization of pancreas recovery and utilization. With new allocation strategies, such as the Kidney Allocation System (KAS) and Share 15/35 systems, and technological advances in machine perfusion impacting kidney and liver utilization [23, 24, 25, 26, 27], we speculate that there have already been unanticipated effects on pancreas recovery. In this study, ET did not significantly differ over the course of the study period but had less variability and a lower absolute difference during and after 2022 compared to the preceding years possibly owing to continued advances in organ recovery. Previous work has already demonstrated that with machine perfusion and organ recovery becoming increasingly specialized over the last decade, there have been shifts in abdominal recovery practices, to include: (1) time‐shifting of donor and recipient surgery for kidney and liver transplantation, and (2) time, cost, and resources required for liver recovery [28]. The relationship between changes in national abdominal recovery practices and pancreas ET/CIT has not been well characterized and would be a worthy consideration for continued investigation. Machine perfusion for pancreas is another growing field of investigation [29, 30] and could offer a solution for CIT challenges with the potential to increase confidence in organ acceptance despite longer ET. One consideration could be repurposing liver machine perfusion systems for pancreas preservation given the similarity in portal and arterial inflow rates. The management of on‐pump levels of insulin production and pancreatic duodenal effluent could also be accomplished using available technology, and the refinement of these systems is an additional focus of interest at our center.

Our study is not without limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the study contributed to lack of data for some patients, such as A1c at 1‐year posttransplant, and additionally prevents us from drawing causal relationships between ET and various transplant outcomes. Second, this is a single‐center study, and our results may not be generalizable to other domestic and international centers. There was also an inherent sampling bias in our database given the inherent selectivity of pancreas donation and our center's ET limit of 70 min since 2022. Lastly, while we standardized the recipient's operative and postoperative course at our center, there was no standardization of the procurement surgical team or approaches which would contribute unknown variability to the results based on the experience of the procuring surgeon and procurement technique. Relatedly, an additional future area of investigation could be creating specialized pancreas recovery teams to standardize procurement technique and preservation method, minimize pancreas injury at time of recovery, and improve pancreas utilization nationwide.

In summary, improved organ recovery practices have likely contributed to less variable ETs. Additionally, we demonstrate for the first time at a US pancreas transplant center that while ET may not be associated with all‐cause graft loss, it may impact postoperative outcomes such as readmission rates and could have important implications for the future of pancreas utilization.
