Age Is a Key Factor Influencing the Choice of Treatment for Primary Patellar Dislocation: A Systematic Review and Meta‐analysis
Authors: Yi Chen, Yaobin Wang, Hefang Xiao, Ao Yang, Fei Teng, Zhi Yi, Xiaoyun Sheng, Shifeng Zhang, Bin Geng, Yayi Xia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary patellar dislocation has a certain recurrence rate after either conservative or surgical treatment, and the optimal treatment for patients with primary patellar dislocation of different ages remains unclear. This study aims to compare the clinical efficacy of surgical and conservative treatments for primary patellar dislocation across different age groups. According to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) checklist, we conducted a systematic search for randomized controlled trials. We searched the PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for randomized controlled trials of primary patellar dislocation treated surgically or conservatively up to January 2023. Data searching, extraction, analysis, and quality assessment were carried out in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration guidelines. A total of nine studies with 433 patients were included in our study. There was no statistically significant difference between the two treatment modalities in terms of Kujala score, pain score, patient satisfaction, reoperation, and Tegner score. The rate of re‐dislocation after surgical treatment is lower than that after conservative treatment. Subgroup analysis based on mean age showed that when the age was >20 years, Kujala scores were higher after surgical treatment than after conservative treatment (p < 0.0001, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 10.41–21.30). When the age was ≤20 years, the difference in Kujala scores between the two treatment modalities was not statistically significant. When the age was >20 years, the recurrence rate of patellar dislocation was lower after surgical treatment than after conservative treatment (p = 0.009, 95% CI = 0.08–0.70). When the age was ≤20 years, the difference in the recurrence rate of patellar dislocation between the two treatment modalities was not statistically significant. When the age of patients with primary patellar dislocation is ≤20 years, both surgical and conservative treatments result in similar clinical outcomes. When the age is >20 years, better clinical outcomes can be achieved by opting for surgical treatment. Therefore, surgery may be a better option for patients with primary patellar dislocation whose age is >20 years.Keywords: Conservative, Patellofemoral Instability, Primary Patellar Dislocation, Surgical

Introduction

Primary (first‐time) patellar dislocation refers to a clinical phenomenon characterized by the initial occurrence of patellar displacement, predominantly attributed to a traumatic event leading to the disruption of the previously unaffected medial peripatellar anatomical components.1, 2, 3 Its prevalence ranges from 5.8 to 77.8 per 100,000, with a particularly high occurrence among young individuals.4, 5 Appropriate therapeutic intervention is crucial to mitigate adverse outcomes, such as recurrence of dislocation, distressing subluxation, and the onset of osteoarthrosis.1, 5, 6 Some opinions suggest that when patients with primary patellar dislocation do not have chondral lesions, osteochondral fractures, or severe tears in the medial support band, they should receive non‐operative treatment.5, 7 In the past, conservative therapeutic regimens were regarded as the preferred modality; however, non‐operative interventions were associated with a documented recurrence incidence of 44%.8, 9 In light of these results, surgical intervention gained prominence. Nevertheless, the surgical approach also exhibited a notable recurrence rate, ranging from 10% to 35%.10, 11 Thus, the question of which method should be prioritized remains controversial.

The optimal treatment for primary patellar dislocation has been the subject of debate, and the optimal treatment for different age groups has not been clearly defined. The severity of symptoms following primary patellar dislocation and the likelihood of recurrent instability may depend on factors such as activity level, predisposing conditions, and age at the time of the initial dislocation.12, 13, 14, 15 A systematic review and meta‐analysis of overall populations concluded that conservative treatment may be more effective than surgery for primary patellar dislocation, taking into account the Tegner activity score. 16 However, in another study, surgical treatment of primary patellar dislocation in children and adolescents was found to have a lower risk of recurrent dislocation and higher health‐related quality of life and motor function compared with conservative treatment. 17 Additionally, the results of many previous studies on acute patellar dislocation are difficult to interpret, because the study context included both first‐time dislocations and recurrent dislocations.2, 15, 18 Further, several studies included patients of widely varying ages, and most studies utilized retrospective and non‐randomized designs.1, 2, 18 Therefore, it is essential to study the effect of age on primary patellar dislocation.

To date, no meta‐analysis of studies comparing conservative and surgical treatments of primary patellar dislocation in different age groups has been seen. The primary research purpose of this study is twofold: first, to compare the clinical efficacy of surgical versus non‐surgical treatment of primary patellar dislocation by including high‐quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and, second, to clarify the optimal treatment of primary patellar dislocation at different ages in order to guide clinical practice. Our study was analyzed in several directions and we expect to provide a medical basis for the choice of clinical treatment. Based on the fact that the incidence of primary patellar dislocation is highest under the age of 20 years, we chose the age of 20 years as the cutoff.1, 19 We hypothesize that surgery may be a better option for patients with primary patellar dislocation whose age is >20 years.

Methods

Literature Search

The implementation process of our research was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases from inception to January 2023 were searched using the following terms: (“patella* dislocation”) AND (primary OR acute OR first‐time OR traumatic) AND (operat* OR surg*) AND (conservative OR non‐operat* OR non‐surg*). Additional manual inspections were performed on the reference of all obtained articles to identify studies with potential relevance.

Eligibility Criteria

All studies found through the literature search were examined and selected according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) patients with primary patellar dislocation, (2) RCTs comparing a surgery group with a conservative treatment group, (3) patients with complete clinical outcomes, (4) patients treated surgically or conservatively within the last 30 years, and (5) original articles published in peer‐reviewed English journals. Surgical techniques used in the included studies included arthroscopic and open medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction or repair, lateral support band release, and distal repositioning. Conservative treatment included patient education, immobilization with braces or splints, physical therapy, and exercise rehabilitation. There are no restrictions on surgical or conservative treatment modalities.

Exclusion criteria included (1) retrospective studies, cohort studies, and trials without an RCT design and (2) animal or cadaveric testing, case reports, reviews, editorials, or expert opinion studies. We considered only studies that reported quantitative data under findings. Disagreements between authors were discussed and mutually resolved.

Data Extraction

The data were extracted independently by two researchers. Information extracted included (1) the name of the first author, journal title, publication date, type of study, and study period; (2) general characteristics of the study and the included patients; (3) surgical and non‐surgical interventions undertaken and duration of treatment; and (4) treatment outcomes. The primary outcomes included the following: patellar re‐dislocation rate, pain scores (a subjective scale used to assess the pain level of patients), Kujala scores (a scoring system specifically designed to assess knee function in patients with patella‐related lesions, with a maximum score of 100, where higher scores indicate better knee function), and Tegner scores (a scale used to assess a patient's level of motor activity, a scoring system that ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating a higher level of motor activity). Secondary outcomes included reoperation rate and patient satisfaction. Two evaluators were asked to agree on the selected articles, and the information was excerpted. If agreement could not be reached, a third reviewer would be invited to assist in the evaluation. If data were incomplete or lacking in mean or standard deviation (SD) and year of surgery, the appropriate authors were contacted to obtain these data.

Assessment of Methodologic Quality

Two investigators (YC and Y‐B, W) independently assessed the methodological quality of each study using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Controlled Trials (rct). Agreement between reviewers of RCTs was measured (Cohen κ). Any disagreements between authors were resolved through discussion or through review by third‐party investigators (Y‐Y, X). Potential publication bias could not be assessed because statistical robustness was compromised by the limited number of studies included in each of the predefined study areas, particularly fewer than 10.

Statistical Analysis

The ReviewManager software (version 5.3; The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, England) was employed for the computation of the overall aggregated effect size for each outcome. A meta‐analysis was conducted exclusively encompassing randomized trials and employing a random‐effects model. In the case of continuous outcomes, the mean difference (MD) accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated using the inverse‐variance method. Binary outcomes were assessed by computing the risk ratio (RR) between groups through the Mantel–Haenszel method. The degree of statistical heterogeneity among the studies was appraised utilizing the I 2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% denoting low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, supplemented by the Cochrane Q statistic (c‐square test). A significance level of p < 0.10 was stipulated to identify a substantial degree of heterogeneity.

The Primary Outcomes

Comparison of the Recurrence Rates of Patellar Dislocation

The recurrence rate of patellar dislocation was documented in all nine studies. A total of 433 patients with primary patellofemoral dislocation were evaluated, with 227 in the surgical group and 206 in the conservative group. Heterogeneity tests between the mean age ≤20 and mean age >20 subgroups showed high heterogeneity. Therefore, a random‐effects model was used for analysis. When the mean age was >20, the recurrence rate of patellar dislocation was better in the surgical group than in the conservative group, and the difference was statistically significant (RR = 0.24, 95% CI 0.08–0.70, p = 0.009); when the mean age was ≤20, the difference in the subgroup results was not statistically significant (RR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.39–1.05, p = 0.07). Overall, the surgical group was superior to the conservative group, and the difference was statistically significant (RR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.29–0.86, p = 0.01) (Figure 2).

Comparison of the re‐dislocation rates of primary patellar dislocation based on mean age.

Comparison of Kujala Scores

Eight studies evaluated the Kujala score of patients. A total of 392 patients were analyzed, with 201 in the surgical group and 191 in the non‐surgical group. We categorized the patients into mean age ≤20 years and mean age >20 years. The heterogeneity test between subgroups showed high heterogeneity. Therefore, a random‐effects model was used for analysis. After surgical or conservative treatment, in the mean age >20 years subgroup, the Kujala score was higher in the surgical group than in the conservative group, and the difference was statistically significant (weighted mean difference [WMD] = 15.85, 95% CI = 10.41–21.30, p < 0.00001). In the mean age ≤20 years subgroup, the difference was not statistically significant (WMD = −2.79, 95% CI −6.47 to 0.90, p = 0.14). After subunion, the difference was not statistically significant (WMD = 6.21, 95% CI = −1.42 to 13.83, p = 0.11) (Figure 3).

Comparison of Kujala scores of primary patellar dislocation based on mean age.

Comparison of Tegner Scores

The results of Tegner score were mentioned in three articles. A total of 167 patients were analyzed, with 89 in the surgical group and 78 in the conservative group. Using a random‐effects model, the combined data showed no significant difference between the surgical and conservative groups (RR = −0.75, 95% CI −1.67 to 0.16, p = 0.11) (Figure 4).

Comparison of Tegner scores of primary patellar dislocation.

Comparison of Pain Scores

The results of pain scores were mentioned in three articles. A total of 179 patients were analyzed, with 95 in the surgical group and 84 in the conservative group. Using a random‐effects model, the combined data showed no significant difference between the surgical and conservative groups (RR = 0.15, 95% CI −7.20 to 7.50, p = 0.97) (Figure 5).

Comparison of pain scores of primary patellar dislocation.

The Secondary Outcomes

Comparison of Reoperation

The results of reoperations were mentioned in three articles. A total of 132 patients were analyzed, with 68 in the surgical group and 64 in the conservative group. Using a random‐effects model, the combined data showed no significant difference between the surgical and conservative groups (RR = 0.43, 95% CI 0.07–2.57, p = 0.35) (Figure 6).

Comparison of the reoperation rate of primary patellar dislocation.

Comparison of Patient Satisfaction

Five articles reported the results of patient satisfaction. A total of 204 patients were analyzed, 106 in the surgical group and 98 in the conservative group. Using a fixed‐effects model, the combined data showed no significant difference between the surgical and conservative groups (RR = 1.18, 95% CI 0.97–1.44, p = 0.09) (Figure 7).

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that when the age of patients with primary patellar dislocation is ≤20 years, the clinical outcomes of surgical and conservative treatment are similar. When the age is >20 years, the choice of surgery for primary patellar dislocation resulted in better clinical outcomes, lower rates of patellar re‐dislocation, and higher Kujala scores.

Factors to Consider in the Treatment of Primary Patellar Dislocation

Currently, conservative treatment is recommended by some scholars as the treatment of choice for primary patellar dislocation, except when primary patellar dislocation is combined with articular surface pathology (e.g., osteochondral fracture), in which case surgical treatment is necessary. 25 However, conservative treatment of primary patellar dislocation may result in a relatively high rate of re‐dislocation or failure to return to athletic activity or even cause patellofemoral arthritis. For these reasons, a subset of scholars advocates for surgical treatment of primary patellar dislocation, and their studies have found that surgical treatment is superior to conservative treatment in terms of improved clinical outcomes.23, 24, 27 Conversely, other studies have found that surgical treatment did not improve clinical outcomes.21, 22, 25 Furthermore, although there have been meta‐analyses comparing surgical and conservative treatments for patellar dislocation, some limitations exist29, 30: (1) Confusing recurrent patellar dislocation with primary patellar dislocation may lead to bias and influence conclusions. Patellar dislocations are categorized differently and correspond to different treatments. Most patients with recurrent patellar dislocation have anatomical abnormalities such as increased TT–TG distances, elevated Q angles, abnormal carriage development, and abnormal lines of force. 30 Due to the ineffectiveness of conservative treatments, these patients usually require surgical treatment to restore normal anatomy. 31 (2) Because primary patellar dislocation often occurs in younger patients, a study that directly addresses the overall population or directly uses a combined effect size may be biased. 4 Therefore, when comparing surgical versus conservative treatment of primary patellar dislocation, these factors be taken into account.

The Effect of Different Ages on Primary Outcomes in Patients with Primary Patellar Dislocation

The results of this study showed that after subgroup analysis based on mean age, the recurrence rate of patellar dislocation was lower with surgical treatment than with conservative treatment when the age was >20 years, and the Kujala score was higher with surgical treatment. When the age was ≤20, patellar dislocation recurrence rates and Kujala scores were similar between the two groups. This may be due to the fact that older patients may have experienced more knee trauma or chronic instability and have less natural healing ability, factors that may make it difficult for conservative treatments to effectively restore patellar function and stability. Since the Kujala score is primarily used to assess knee function and pain, surgical treatment reduces the risk of patellar re‐dislocation by directly repairing or reconstructing the structure, thus demonstrating better results on the Kujala score. The results of this study showed that the pain scores and Tegner scores, similar efficacy was achieved with either surgical or conservative treatment. In terms of pain scores, there was no significant difference between the two groups. This may be due to the fact that both surgical and conservative treatments include measures for pain management, leading to similar results in these aspects. In addition, since the Tegner scores are used to assess the patient's overall activity level, unlike the Kujala scores' assessment of specific joint function, the lack of a significant difference in Tegner scores between the two groups may indicate that neither treatment significantly changed the patient's overall activity level. This could be related to the patient's lifestyle (e.g., whether or not they exercise regularly) and rehabilitation program. A study of primary patellar dislocation by Atkin et al. 1 found that age at the time of the initial dislocation may be a factor influencing the severity of symptoms and recurrent instability after primary patellar dislocation.

A study found similar findings between conservative and surgical treatments for re‐dislocations and knee function in adolescents and children.21, 28 In addition, Zhang et al. 32 found that in adolescents with primary patellar dislocation, surgery was superior to conservative treatment in the short term for reducing the rate of re‐dislocation. However, knee function outcomes based on the Kujala and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) scores were poorer. Moreover, the advantages of either surgical or conservative treatment did not persist in adolescents in the long term. 32 Although a recent study found better pain outcomes in the conservative treatment group, there was no significant difference in clinical outcomes between conservative and surgical treatments of acute patellar dislocation in children and adolescents. 33 The authors do not advocate routine surgical treatment of acute patellar dislocation in children and adolescents. 32 Conversely, another study found that surgery was associated with fewer re‐dislocations and better knee function compared to conservative treatment. 17 This result may reflect excessive tension or lack of tissue integrity and weaker muscle function in adolescents, which may not match their accelerated growth. 28 Our findings may also help to explain why previous correlational studies involving meta‐analyses of adults and adolescents have produced contradictory results.34, 35, 36 The results of our meta‐analysis remind us that surgery is not always effective in the overall population, especially in patients with primary patellar dislocation aged ≤20. Before proposing a treatment plan, we need to take into account the patient's age and the presence or absence of coexisting osteochondral lesions, which can minimize the invasiveness and cost of the procedure and maximize the benefit to the patient.

The Effect of Different Ages on Secondary Outcomes in Patients with Primary Patellar Dislocation

The results of this study showed that the efficacy of surgical or conservative treatment was similar in terms of patient satisfaction and reoperation rates. There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of patient satisfaction. This may be due to the fact that both surgical and conservative treatments included pain control and patient psychological support measures and therefore achieved similar results in these areas. The lack of a significant difference in reoperation rates between the two groups suggests that surgical treatment, although more effective in some respects, did not significantly increase the probability of reoperation. This may be related to advances in modern surgical techniques and postoperative management. By comparing conservative and surgical treatments of primary patellar dislocation, Xing et al. 16 found that there was no statistical difference between the two groups in terms of patient satisfaction and number of reoperations. The results of our meta‐analysis remind us that surgery is not always effective for all populations. Based on our study and considering the invasiveness and cost of surgery, we should carefully weigh the pros and cons of surgery in terms of maximizing the benefits from making patients with primary patellar dislocation.

Optimizing Treatment Approaches: Future Directions for Research on Primary Patellar Dislocation

Although many of the available studies have compared surgical versus conservative treatment of primary patellar dislocation, most have not adequately described the specific procedures utilized, resulting in a limited ability to replicate these clinical trials. Surgical treatment consists of many different interventions, and we are not equipped to use evidence to determine the efficacy of each specific procedure. Although different surgeons perform various surgical approaches, the literature does not clearly indicate the rationale for their adoption of each procedure. Several recent studies have suggested that this should be emphasized, and we therefore recommend that these improvements continue to be advanced so that in the future it will be possible to assess whether a particular patient is best suited for surgical or conservative treatment based on their family history, anatomical bone morphology, soft‐tissue integrity, and activity level.21, 22, 37

Patients with primary patellar dislocation aged ≤20 years treated conservatively had a favorable prognosis in terms of joint function compared with those aged >20 years. The study by Palmu et al. 21 suggests that future research with adults and adolescents should focus on assessing function‐based outcomes. There are relatively few descriptions in the literature regarding conservative treatment strategies for primary patellar dislocation. Therefore, it is difficult to assess whether considerable methodologic heterogeneity exists in the conservative management of these patients. Our study demonstrates that the use of conservative treatment for patients with primary patellar dislocation aged ≤20 results in similar re‐dislocation rates and good knee function as surgical treatment. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal conservative treatment strategy for patients after primary patellar dislocation.

Strengths and Limitations

The main finding of this study is that the clinical outcomes of surgical and conservative treatment of primary patellar dislocation are similar when the patient's age is ≤20 years. When the age is >20 years, surgical treatment of primary patellar dislocation resulted in lower rates of patellar re‐dislocation and higher Kujala scores. The quality of meta‐analysis can be assured by following the steps generated by the Cochrane Systematic Reviews and the principles of the PRISMA guidelines. Inclusion of only randomized controlled studies can effectively reduce selection bias. In this study, the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the literature were clearly defined, and the included studies were rigorously evaluated for quality. Statistically, the RevMan 5.3 software was applied according to Cochrane standards to ensure the quality of the study.

This study exhibited several limitations. First, regardless of the type of treatment, every registered study was categorized as either surgical or conservative, increasing the risk of selective bias. Second, surgical and conservative treatments varied in these studies, which may have affected the results. Third, the risk of bias assessment indicates that certain studies within the analyzed RCTs exhibited a propensity toward high risk, despite the entirety of data being sourced from RCTs. Additionally, although these studies compared surgical and conservative treatments, most did not describe the specific treatment procedures in detail. In particular, conservative treatment options were poorly described, impeding the reproducibility of these clinical trials. Finally, combining studies of different racial populations may bias results. Therefore, future studies should cover a wider sample of the population, standardize interventions for these patients, and conduct larger and higher‐quality randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

Surgery should be actively considered for patients >20 years of age with primary patellar dislocations due to the significant improvement in clinical outcomes, including improved Kujala scores and reduced recurrence rates of patellar dislocations, when compared with conservative treatment. In contrast, for patients ≤20 years of age, the clinical outcomes of surgical and conservative treatment were similar. Therefore, both treatments may be appropriate for treating patients aged ≤20 years with primary patellar dislocations, with the flexibility to choose on a patient‐by‐patient basis. Future studies should continue to explore the effects of age and other factors on treatment outcomes to further refine the clinical decision‐making process for the treatment of primary patellar dislocation.
