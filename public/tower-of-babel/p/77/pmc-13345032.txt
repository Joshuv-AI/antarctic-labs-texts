Clinical Implementation and Accuracy Evaluation of a Novel Intrafraction Cone-beam Computed Tomography Technique for Stereotactic Radiosurgery and Radiotherapy
Authors: Deeksha Jaiswal, Monika Goyal, C. P. Bhatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the clinical implementation and setup accuracy of real-time intrafraction cone-beam computed tomography imaging during stereotactic radiosurgery and stereotactic radiotherapy.Materials and Methods:Ten patients treated with intracranial SRS/SRT were retrospectively analyzed. All patients were immobilized using the Fraxion stereotactic head system and treated with volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) on a linear accelerator (Elekta Versa HD). A custom intrafraction cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) preset was developed in X-ray volume imaging to acquire kV projections during each VMAT arc. Translational (X, Y, and Z) and rotational (pitch, roll, and yaw) errors were calculated by registering intrafraction CBCT to the planning CT using a 6D match and mean values were compared with SRS tolerance thresholds (0.5 mm and 1°).Results:The mean intrafraction translational errors were 0.4 mm (lateral), 0.3 mm (longitudinal), and 0.4 mm (vertical), while rotational errors were 0.3° in (pitch), 0.3° in (roll), and 0.4° in (yaw), all within SRS tolerances. Real-time intrafraction CBCT provided clinically acceptable volumetric images with minimal scatter, enabled continuous motion monitoring during VMAT delivery, eliminated additional CBCT scans and couch adjustments, and reduced treatment time by approximately 3–5 min per arc.Conclusion:Real-time intrafraction CBCT imaging enables highly accurate, efficient, and reproducible setup verification during SRS/SRT. The workflow maintains sub-millimeter precision, reduces treatment duration, and enhances clinical confidence in dose delivery. This approach represents a significant advancement in stereotactic image-guided radiation therapy and warrants the evaluation for extracranial SBRT applications.

INTRODUCTION

Image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT) has significantly reduced uncertainties in the delivery of radiation to the target volume.[12] Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) are highly precise treatment modalities used for targeting small tumors or abnormalities.[34] SRS is a noninvasive technique that delivers highly focused radiation in a single fraction, commonly for intracranial lesions,[56] whereas SRT delivers radiation in multiple smaller fractions to achieve similar precision with improved normal tissue tolerance.[7]

In modern radiotherapy, accurate patient positioning, or patient setup, is crucial to ensure precise dose delivery to the target while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues.[89] Inaccurate setup may result in geographical miss, compromising tumor control and increasing the risk of toxicity.[1011] This becomes particularly critical in SRS/SRT, where very high doses are delivered in a limited number of fractions with steep dose gradients. Even minimal setup deviations can lead to significant dosimetric uncertainties, affecting both target coverage and organ-at-risk (OAR) sparing.[1213]

Intrafraction cone-beam computed tomography imaging providing information on setup errors during treatment delivery. The “real-time” refers specifically to the acquisition of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images during MV beam-on, i.e., intrafraction imaging performed concurrently with treatment delivery. The intent is to highlight that the imaging captures the actual patient position during irradiation, thereby providing information on setup deviations occurring in real treatment conditions, which is not available with conventional pre- or post-treatment CBCT.

In the current clinical practice, CBCT is routinely used for pretreatment, intrafraction (intermediate), and posttreatment imaging, providing information on setup errors before, during, and after treatment delivery. While these imaging time points allow the detection and correction of interfraction and limited intrafraction variations, they do not provide continuous or actual setup verification of patient position during beam delivery.[1415]

Surface-guided radiotherapy has emerged as a modern technique for intrafraction monitoring; however, it relies on external surface tracking, which may not accurately represent internal target setup position, particularly for intracranial lesions.[16] Similarly, magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy systems (MR-LINAC) provide real-time imaging capabilities but are limited by high cost and limited clinical availability, restricting their routine use in SRS/SRT.[17]

Alternative intrafraction verification methods, such as 2D planar imaging systems (e.g., ExacTrac), utilize X-ray-based tracking with digitally reconstructed radiographs for six-degree-of-freedom (6D) corrections.[18] However, these approaches rely on 2D–3D image matching, which is complex and may not reliably localize the target relative to surrounding bony and soft-tissue anatomy.

Currently, CBCT remains the most widely available and clinically practical imaging modality for setup verification in radiotherapy.[192021] It provides three-dimensional volumetric imaging, enabling accurate visualization of patient anatomy and facilitating precise alignment with planning CT images. The use of CBCT allows improved detection and correction of setup errors and is therefore well suited for 6D positional verification in high-precision treatments such as SRS/SRT.[22232425]

Given these advantages, the implementation of real-time with MV beam on intrafraction CBCT imaging has the potential to enhance setup accuracy during treatment delivery. This study aims to clinical implementation and evaluate the setup accuracy of real-time intrafraction CBCT imaging in SRS/SRT, thereby addressing a critical gap in current image-guided radiotherapy workflows for SRS/SRT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection and simulation

This retrospective study included 10 patients with intracranial single lesions treated using SRS or SRT with single isocenter. Patients were selected based on the following criteria: (i) diagnosis of primary brain tumors or brain metastases, (ii) treatment delivered on Elekta Versa HD equipped with kilovoltage (kV) CBCT, (iii) immobilization using the Fraxion stereotactic head fixation system, (iv) treatment planning performed using Monaco Treatment Planning System (v6.1), and (v) availability of planning CT images acquired with 1-mm slice thickness.

All patients underwent CT simulation in the supine position using a standard stereotactic immobilization system consisting of a Fraxion head frame, carbon fiber SRS baseplate, and secure head fixation to minimize intrafraction motion. CT images were acquired with a slice thickness of ≤1 mm. Planning CT datasets were co-registered with contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for accurate target delineation. Patients with incomplete imaging datasets or inability to maintain immobilization were excluded.

The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated based on MRI-CT fusion. The clinical target volume was considered equal to the GTV. A planning target volume (PTV) margin of 1–2 mm was applied to account for residual setup uncertainties. OARs were contoured according to the institutional guidelines.

Treatment planning

Treatment planning was performed using the Monaco Treatment Planning System for delivery on Elekta Versa HD.[15]

All treatment plans were generated using volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with 6 MV flattening filter-free (FFF) photon beams. A total of 3–5 arcs were used for each plan, including both coplanar and noncoplanar configurations during optimization.

Both coplanar and noncoplanar plans were evaluated based on target coverage, conformity, dose gradient, and OAR sparing. If comparable dosimetric parameters were achieved, coplanar VMAT plans were preferred to facilitate the implementation of real-time intrafraction CBCT imaging, reduce treatment complexity, and improve workflow efficiency. A minimum of two coplanar arcs was used in all accepted treatment plans, while noncoplanar arcs were included only when clinically justified.

Dose calculation was performed using a Monte Carlo algorithm with a grid size of ≤2 mm. The prescribed dose was normalized to ensure ≥95% PTV coverage. Plan evaluation was performed using standard dosimetric indices, including conformity index, gradient index, and OAR dose constraints. All plans underwent patient-specific quality assurance before treatment delivery.

Intrafraction cone-beam computed tomography preset development

A custom intrafraction CBCT preset was created in Elekta X-ray volume imaging (XVI) to enable kV imaging during MV beam delivery, as shown in Figure 1.[2627] The intrafraction CBCT preset used in this study was developed within the XVI system using an iterative optimization approach to ensure synchronization with VMAT delivery. Preset parameters were systematically adjusted based on VMAT arc characteristics, including arc direction, gantry start and stop angles, frame rate (frames per degree), and maximum number of projection frames, to achieve optimal alignment between imaging acquisition and treatment delivery. The key imaging parameters used to create preset are as follows: Mode: Clinical, kV: 120, Gantry rotation: Full 360° arc, kV collimator: S20 small field of view, Maximum frames per rotation: 320 frames were collected for the analysis during each full rotation of the gantry, ensuring comprehensive imaging data for evaluation, which provides clinically acceptable CBCT images for matching with planning CT to localized the setup error during treatment.

Data analysis

All positional corrections and intrafraction shifts were tabulated for translational and rotational errors and graphed to analyze the patient motion patterns. A translation error in radiation therapy is defined as shift or displacement of the patient or tumor in each of the cardinal directions (X, Y, or Z axis) between planning and actual treatment and a rotation error is an angular misalignment, compared to the planned position. Mean ± errors values were compared with established SRS tolerances to determine the accuracy and reliability of the intrafraction CBCT workflow.

DISCUSSION

This study shows that intrafraction CBCT imaging during MV beam on; using a custom CBCT preset, provides highly accurate and reliable monitoring of patient positioning during SRS and radiotherapy.

The custom preset produced clinically acceptable 3D images during each arc with minimal MV scatter, allowing to know about the patients setup error during treatment that conventional intermediate CBCT cannot capture. Prior to clinical implementation, the finalized preset was validated through dummy (dry-run) executions using nonpatient setups. This validation ensured accurate synchronization, consistent image acquisition, and seamless workflow integration. These steps confirmed that the intrafraction CBCT process was reliable, reproducible, and clinically implementable before use in patient treatments.

Across ten intracranial patients, mean translational and rotational errors remained within 0.3–0.4 mm and 0.3°–0.4°, respectively, well below stereotactic thresholds. For few fraction, some intrafraction CBCT setup errors were out of departmental tolerance limit, for which we performed the setup corrections and took a CBCT image before starting another VMAT arc.[31]

Compared with prior reports using CyberKnife 6D skull tracking (Murphy et al.,[32] Hoogeman et al.,[33] and Inoue et al.[34]) or ExacTrac systems (Ma et al.,[35] Rossi et al., and Wang et al.), our approach achieves comparable or superior sub-millimeter stability while providing 3D CBCT images for better setup verification for target and bonny structure matching and getting 6D setup errors.

Unlike intermittent 2D verification, the intrafraction CBCT is acquired during MV beam-on; however, image registration is performed postacquisition using automatic matching algorithms provided by the software, followed by visual verification and manual adjustment if required.

In a conventional workflow without intrafraction CBCT, setup verification during treatment typically requires interruption between VMAT arcs, where a separate CBCT acquisition is performed. This process generally includes CBCT acquisition (~1–2 min), Image verification and correction (~1–3 min), Couch adjustment, and preparation for the next arc. This results in an additional ~3–5 min per arc. In contrast, the proposed intrafraction CBCT approach acquires imaging during beam delivery, thereby eliminating the need for separate imaging interruptions between arcs. Hence, the overall treatment time is reduced. These findings are consistent with CBCT-based studies in head-and-neck radiotherapy (Wang et al., Velec et al., and Hurkmans et al.), extending them by demonstrating sustained sub-millimeter accuracy for intracranial SRS/SRT on a conventional LINAC.[36]

The clinical advantages of real-time intrafraction CBCT are substantial. Continuous comparison with planning CT allows the instant detection and correction of setup errors, supporting patient-specific motion assessment, optimal margin selection, and enhanced SRS/SRT strategies.[373839] The observed low translational and rotational deviations confirm that the combined workflow creates a highly stable setup, ensuring safe delivery of steep dose gradients inherent to stereotactic therapy.

Although the results are encouraging, the study has limitations. The small number of patients limits how widely the findings can be applied and larger studies are needed. Furthermore, while scatter from small SRS fields had little effect on image quality, larger SBRT fields may cause more difficulties.[28] Since this study focused only on intracranial cases, further research is needed to evaluate real-time intrafraction CBCT in extracranial sites where setup errors are more due to physiological motion.

Overall, this study confirms that real-time intrafraction CBCT imaging is a powerful tool for improving treatment setup, efficiency, and confidence in SRT.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that intrafraction CBCT provides precise, reliable, and efficient verification of patient setup during SRS/SRT. The technique consistently maintained sub-millimeter translational accuracy and sub-degree rotational stability, confirming accurate patient positioning during beam delivery. The acquisition of 3D CBCT imaging immediately after a coplanar VMAT arc enabled detection of intrafraction motion that may not be captured by conventional verification systems.

Furthermore, the proposed workflow was found to be clinically feasible and time-efficient, without disrupting routine SRS/SRT practice. Although this study is limited by a small cohort of intracranial cases, the findings suggest that real-time intrafraction CBCT enhances the confidence in dose delivery and setup verification in stereotactic treatments.
