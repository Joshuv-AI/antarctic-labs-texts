Latent class profiles of psychiatric comorbidity among substance-affected Nigerian adolescents: a population-based analysis
Authors: Abiodun O Adewuya, Olushola Olibamoyo, Azizat Lebimoyo, Olabisi E Oladipo, Arit Esangbedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Substance use disorders (SUD) during adolescence rarely occur in isolation. A substantial proportion of affected youth meet criteria for additional psychiatric conditions, and the co-occurrence of multiple disorders (what has been termed psychiatric multimorbidity) is associated with greater functional impairment, more complex clinical trajectories, and elevated suicidal behaviour [1]. Although such patterns have been examined extensively in high-income settings, considerably less is known about how psychiatric conditions cluster among substance-affected adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa, where sociocultural contexts, family structures, and mental health resources differ markedly from Western settings [1, 2].

Traditional epidemiological approaches quantify comorbidity through prevalence estimates or counts of co-occurring diagnoses. While informative, these methods assume relative homogeneity within substance-using populations and do not clarify whether qualitatively distinct configurations of psychiatric burden exist. Person-centred analytic strategies, particularly latent class analysis (LCA), offer an alternative framework by identifying probabilistic groupings of individuals based on shared patterns across multiple indicators [3]. Rather than treating psychiatric multimorbidity as a single severity dimension, LCA can delineate statistical strata characterised by differing constellations of disorders. Such strata represent data-driven groupings derived from observed indicators and should not be interpreted as discrete clinical entities; their utility lies in characterising heterogeneity in burden and clarifying the distribution of associated risk correlates [4].

The need to characterise psychiatric burden among substance-affected adolescents is particularly salient in Nigeria. Mental health service capacity is severely limited, with fewer than 0.1 psychiatrists per 100,000 population [5], and the majority of adolescents with psychiatric disorders receive no formal diagnosis or treatment [6]. Understanding how psychiatric morbidity clusters among those already meeting criteria for SUD or AUD may have implications for conceptual models of risk stratification and the design of more targeted approaches to assessment in resource-constrained systems. Schools represent one of the few structured platforms through which large numbers of Nigerian adolescents can be systematically reached. At the same time, school-based samples necessarily exclude out-of-school youth, who may have substantially higher rates of both substance involvement and psychiatric morbidity; estimates derived from in-school populations should therefore be understood as conservative lower bounds of the broader adolescent burden [7].

Previous Nigerian studies have reported non-trivial prevalence of depressive symptoms, psychotic-like experiences, and substance use among secondary school students [8, 9]. However, few investigations have focused specifically on adolescents already meeting diagnostic criteria for SUD or AUD and examined the structure of additional psychiatric comorbidity within this subgroup. International evidence suggests that substance-affected adolescents frequently exhibit clustering of internalising disorders, behavioural dysregulation, and trauma-related exposures, with cumulative adversity exposure associated with more complex multimorbidity profiles [10, 11]. Whether comparable patterns are observable in West African contexts (where normative disciplinary practices, extended family systems, and polygamous household structures may modulate the expression and clustering of psychopathology) remains uncertain.

In addition to clarifying comorbidity structure, there is growing interest in whether readily measurable psychosocial indicators might discriminate adolescents at highest psychiatric burden within substance-affected samples. In resource-constrained systems, even modest discrimination could inform conceptual approaches to stepped or tiered care. However, analyses examining predictive performance must be interpreted cautiously: within-sample analyses are subject to circularity when predictors and outcomes derive from overlapping assessments, and implementation-ready screening claims require replication in independent cohorts [12].

Using data from the Lagos Schools Emotional and Behavioural Health Survey (LSEBHS) [8, 9], the present study addresses these gaps. We restrict analysis to adolescents meeting DSM-IV criteria for SUD and/or AUD and apply latent class analysis using five psychiatric comorbidities as indicators. Our objectives are: (1) to identify latent profiles of psychiatric comorbidity within this substance-affected population; (2) to examine associations between class membership, suicidality, and cumulative adversity, using regression methods that adjust for classification uncertainty; (3) to describe functional impairment across classes; and (4) to conduct a proof-of-concept discrimination analysis, tested against an independent external criterion. Given the cross-sectional design, all findings are interpreted as associational; temporal ordering cannot be inferred.

Methods

Study design and sampling

This study draws on data from the LSEBHS, a cross-sectional epidemiological investigation of public secondary school adolescents in Lagos State, Nigeria. A stratified multistage probability sampling design was employed. The five administrative divisions of Lagos State served as primary sampling strata. Within each division, public mixed-gender senior secondary schools were selected using probability-proportional-to-size sampling weighted by official school enrolment figures. Schools were further stratified by geographic classification as rural, semi-urban, or urban. Within each of 47 selected schools, two intact classes were randomly sampled from each of the three Senior Secondary School levels (SSS1–SSS3), and all students in selected classes were invited to participate.

Of 10,202 eligible students, 9,441 provided both parental consent and adolescent assent (participation rate: 92.5%). Non-participation arose from parental refusal (4.2%, n = 429), student absence on the assessment day (2.1%, n = 214), and incomplete data precluding participation (1.2%, n = 118). Of the 9,441 consenting students, four were excluded post-consent because the MINI-Kid is validated for use with those aged 6–17 years only and these participants were aged 18 years or older, yielding a full LSEBHS analytic sample of 9,437 adolescents (see Fig. 1) [8, 9]. For the present analysis, the sample was restricted to adolescents meeting DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for SUD and/or AUD, yielding an analytic subsample of n = 614.

Collection procedures

Data were collected in supervised classroom sessions lasting 50 to 70 min. Participants completed structured self-completion questionnaire booklets independently; seating arrangements and communication restrictions ensured response privacy. Trained research assistants were present throughout to address procedural questions but did not interpret individual items. No personally identifying information was recorded on survey instruments at any stage. Participants endorsing suicidal ideation or severe distress were informed, via a printed statement at the end of the questionnaire booklet, that they could voluntarily and confidentially approach the designated research assistant or school counsellor to receive information about available support services. This disclosure process was entirely self-initiated, preserving full anonymity throughout. The study received ethical approval from the Health Research and Ethics Committee of the host institution and from the Lagos State Ministry of Education.

Diagnostic assessment

Psychiatric diagnoses were assessed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI-Kid), aligned with DSM-IV criteria [13]. Modules were adapted for self-administration following prior LSEBHS procedures [8, 9], including forward and backward translation and comprehension piloting. Although the MINI-Kid is a well-validated instrument, it approximates but does not replace clinician-administered assessment [13], and formal validation studies in Nigerian adolescent populations have not yet been conducted. The cultural validity of DSM constructs in this context warrants ongoing scrutiny [8].

SUD and AUD were assessed using their respective MINI-Kid modules (past-year criteria). MDD, any anxiety disorder, conduct disorder, and ADHD were derived from their MINI-Kid modules. Suicidality was assessed using the MINI-Kid suicidality module under a past-month reference period, consistent with MINI-Kid Version 6.0 standard administration, applied to both suicidal ideation and suicidal attempt items; prevalence estimates are therefore substantially lower than lifetime or past-year figures. Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) were assessed using the 16-item Prodromal Questionnaire (PQ-16) [14]; a primary threshold of ≥ 6 items was applied a priori [15].

Latent class indicators

Five binary indicators were specified in the LCA: MDD, any anxiety disorder, ADHD, conduct disorder, and PLE (≥ 6 items). SUD and AUD defined inclusion in the analytic subsample rather than serving as class indicators, ensuring the class structure reflected patterns of psychiatric comorbidity within the substance-affected population.

Cumulative adversity

An ACE index was constructed from five binary indicators: witnessing domestic violence, frequent physical punishment, emotional abuse, parental alcohol misuse, and household instability. Each was scored 0/1 and summed (range 0–5). High adversity was defined as ACE ≥ 3, consistent with cumulative risk frameworks [16]. Domestic violence was incorporated within the composite and not entered separately into regression models.

Functional correlates

Functional impairment was assessed across academic (class repetition; current schoolwork problems), disciplinary (school suspension; physical fights), and social (social isolation; peer relationship difficulties) domains using GSHS-adapted items [17]. Maternal and paternal alcohol use were assessed by reported drinking behaviours.

Missing data

Of the 9,437 full LSEBHS participants, only n = 122 (1.2%) had incomplete data at the full-sample level, indicating exceptionally high survey completion. Within the SUD/AUD subsample, 766 participants had missing values on one or more LCA indicator variables and were excluded, yielding an analytic sample of n = 614. This reduction reflects missingness on LCA indicator variables rather than sampling exclusion or participant attrition. Comparisons between included and excluded participants are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Little’s test was consistent with Missing At Random (MAR) rather than MCAR. A logistic regression predicting missingness identified academic problems as the only significant predictor (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.01–1.47, p=.038), and academic problems is conditioned upon in both the LCA and regression models, supporting the plausibility of unbiased complete-case estimates under MAR. MICE sensitivity (20 imputed datasets) confirmed robustness of primary findings.

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated complex survey sampling weights (IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26). These weights accounted for differential selection probability across schools and clustering within the multistage sampling design.

LCA was conducted in Mplus Version 8.6 using full-information maximum likelihood with 20 random starting values. Model selection was guided by BIC, entropy, BLRT, minimum class size (≥ 5%), and interpretability. Bootstrap 95% CIs for conditional probabilities were derived from 500 parametric bootstrap samples. Multinomial logistic regression used the three-step R3STEP approach [18], with the Low Psychiatric Burden class as reference. A proof-of-concept discrimination analysis examined a three-item triad (meeting MDD diagnostic criteria, PLE- PQ-16 ≥ 6 items, and social isolation), against both Class 3 membership and an independent Composite Functional Impairment criterion (CFI: ≥2 of academic problems, domestic violence exposure, and social isolation). Ten-fold cross-validation, Brier score, Hosmer–Lemeshow calibration, and NNS (formula: 1/[sensitivity × outcome prevalence]) are reported. No formal multiple-comparison adjustment was applied [19]; findings are interpreted with emphasis on effect size and consistency.

Results

Sample characteristics

The analytic sample comprised 614 adolescents meeting DSM-IV criteria for SUD and/or AUD (SUD only: 212 [34.5%]; AUD only: 387 [63.0%]; both: 15 [2.4%]). The mean age was 15.8 years (SD = 1.4); 360 (58.7%) were male; 268 (43.6%) resided in rural areas; and 199 (32.4%) were classified as low SES. MDD was present in 309 participants (50.3%), any anxiety disorder in 295 (48.1%), ADHD in 195 (31.7%), conduct disorder in 179 (29.1%), and PLE in 252 (41.1%). Overall, 492 adolescents (80.1%) met criteria for at least one additional psychiatric disorder. Latent class model selection

Model fit statistics for two- through five-class solutions are presented in Table 2. The two-class model yielded BIC = 2,921.4 and entropy = 0.77, with two broadly separated but insufficiently differentiated classes. The three-class solution demonstrated improved fit (BIC = 2,856.8, entropy = 0.87), with all three classes exceeding the 5% minimum size criterion and the BLRT confirming significant improvement (p<.001). The four-class model produced a marginally lower AIC but higher BIC (2,874.6), reduced entropy (0.82), a smallest class of 6.9% approaching instability, and a marginal BLRT p-value (0.038), providing insufficient grounds to justify the additional class. The five-class model yielded no BIC improvement and a smallest class of 4.1%, below the 5% threshold. The three-class solution was retained on grounds of parsimony, entropy, class size, BLRT support, and substantive interpretability.

Class profiles

Conditional item-response probabilities for the retained three-class solution are presented in Table 3 (see Fig. 2 for graphical representation), with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (500 parametric bootstrap samples; full table in Supplementary Table S2). Class 1, labelled Low Psychiatric Burden (48.0%, n ≈ 295), was characterised by uniformly low probabilities across all five indicators (MDD: 0.16; anxiety: 0.19; CD: 0.14; ADHD: 0.11; PLE: 0.13). Class 2, labelled Internalising-PLE (34.0%, n ≈ 209), was distinguished by elevated MDD (0.74) and anxiety disorder (0.68) probabilities, moderate CD (0.22) and ADHD (0.17), and notably elevated PLE (0.61) : the highest PLE probability in any non-high-multimorbidity class, with its bootstrap lower bound (0.54) exceeding the Class 1 upper bound (0.17) by 37% points. Class 3, labelled High Multimorbidity (18.0%, n ≈ 110), exhibited high probabilities across all five indicators (MDD: 0.91; anxiety: 0.84; CD: 0.74; ADHD: 0.67; PLE: 0.88). Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for Classes 1 and 3 were completely non-overlapping on every indicator, confirming robust class separation. Average posterior probabilities for modal class assignment ranged from 0.86 to 0.92.

Suicidality by latent class

Suicidal outcomes assessed over a past-month reference period demonstrated a graded increase across classes (Table 4). Past-month suicidal ideation was reported by 11 participants (3.8%) in the Low Burden class, 23 (11.2%) in the Internalising-PLE class, and 20 (18.4%) in the High Multimorbidity class (χ²=28.4, p<.001). Past-month suicide attempts showed a comparable gradient: Low Burden 2.1% (n = 6), Internalising-PLE 6.4% (n = 13), High Multimorbidity 11.8% (n = 13) (χ²=14.6, p=.001). Attempt-to-ideation ratios ranged from 0.55 to 0.64 across classes, consistent with the past-month reference window; under short-interval assessment, some participants who had made a recent attempt may have resolved active ideation by the time of survey completion.

Multinomial regression: predictors of class membership (R3STEP)

Multinomial logistic regression using the three-step approach identified the following independent predictors of class membership relative to the Low Psychiatric Burden class (Table 5). Membership in the Internalising-PLE class was independently associated with female sex (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.18–2.36, p=.004) and high cumulative adversity (ACE ≥ 3; AOR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.32–2.84, p=.001). High Multimorbidity class membership was most strongly associated with high cumulative adversity (AOR = 2.88, 95% CI 1.82–4.56, p<.001), maternal alcohol use (AOR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.27–4.18, p=.006), academic problems (AOR = 2.04, 95% CI 1.35–3.07, p=.001), and older age (AOR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.12–3.36, p=.018). A comparison of modal assignment and R3STEP estimates is presented in Supplementary Table S3; the maximum AOR difference between approaches was 0.07 units, with no predictor changing direction or crossing a significance threshold.

Proof-of-concept screening analysis

The screening triad (meeting MDD diagnostic criteria, PLE (PQ-16 ≥ 6 items), and social isolation), achieved within-model AUC = 0.79 (95% CI: 0.74–0.84), sensitivity 74%, specificity 81%, PPV 54%, NPV 94%, for identifying High Multimorbidity class membership (Fig. 3). Ten-fold cross-validation yielded AUC = 0.76. To quantify circularity attributable to MDD’s dual role as class indicator and screening predictor, the triad was evaluated against an independent Composite Functional Impairment criterion (CFI: ≥2 of academic problems, domestic violence exposure, and social isolation; prevalence = 38.0%). Against this external criterion, AUC was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.68–0.76); the 0.07 AUC difference directly quantifies the circularity effect. Additional metrics against the CFI: sensitivity 64%, specificity 72%, PPV 48%, NPV 83%, Brier score 0.190 (19.4% improvement over null Brier of 0.236; Hosmer–Lemeshow p=.41). Full triad endorsement was associated with AOR = 8.36 (95% CI: 4.64–15.07) for CFI. NNS was ≈ 8 for Class 3 identification (1/[0.74 × 0.18]) and ≈ 4 for CFI (1/[0.64 × 0.38]). Results are summarised in Table 6. All findings are exploratory; replication in independent samples is required.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using PQ-16 thresholds of ≥ 3 and ≥ 7 confirmed that the three-class solution was optimal at all thresholds (BIC-preferred; entropy range 0.85–0.87). The rank ordering of class-specific PLE probabilities (Class 1 < Class 2 < Class 3) was strictly preserved. Fewer than 5% of participants changed modal class assignment (≥ 3: 4.2%, n = 26; ≥7: 3.8%, n = 23). The overall comorbidity rate varied by no more than 2.4% points, confirming that all substantive conclusions are robust to PQ-16 threshold choice (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study presents one of the first large-scale latent class characterisations of psychiatric comorbidity among Nigerian adolescents meeting diagnostic criteria for SUD and/or AUD. Before interpreting these findings, an important methodological clarification warrants reiteration: the three strata identified represent empirically derived, probabilistic configurations of indicator co-occurrence, not discrete clinical syndromes or proposed diagnostic categories. Their interpretive value lies in characterising heterogeneity in psychiatric burden across a substance-affected population, rather than in delineating replicable subtypes amenable to direct clinical application. With this framing in mind, three probabilistic strata were identified. The Low Psychiatric Burden stratum, comprising approximately half the sample, was characterised by low probabilities of all measured conditions, confirming that even within a substance-affected population, a substantial proportion does not accumulate extensive co-occurring psychiatric burden. The Internalising-PLE stratum, representing approximately one in three participants, was distinguished by elevated probabilities of MDD, anxiety disorders, and PLE, a statistical configuration that may be particularly relevant in Nigerian contexts, where internalising distress and unusual perceptual experiences frequently co-occur in the context of spiritual and communal meaning-making [20]. The High Multimorbidity stratum, comprising approximately one in five adolescents, exhibited near-universal probabilities across all five indicators, alongside the highest rates of suicidality, cumulative adversity, and functional impairment.

That 80.1% of the full analytic sample met criteria for at least one additional psychiatric disorder underscores that comorbidity is not an exception but the modal presentation among substance-affected Nigerian adolescents. The latent class structure clarifies that this aggregate burden is distributed across graded profiles rather than uniformly, a finding with implications for how psychiatric heterogeneity within substance-affected populations is conceptualised and, eventually, addressed.

Substance-specific context: tramadol and comorbidity

The Nigerian substance use landscape carries contextual features that are relevant to interpreting the comorbidity profiles identified in this study. Tramadol misuse has emerged as one of the most prevalent forms of non-medical substance use among adolescents and young adults, with some surveys reporting non-prescription use rates comparable to or exceeding those of cannabis and alcohol in certain age groups [21]. Unlike substances primarily used for euphoria or social disinhibition, tramadol is often used for perceived functional enhancement, including increased stamina for physical labour and academic demands, alongside its analgesic effects [21].

Although tramadol misuse was not directly measured in the present dataset and cannot be specifically linked to the observed comorbidity profiles, it represents an important contextual feature of the broader substance use environment within which these findings are situated. Against this background, the internalising and psychosis-spectrum configuration observed in the Internalising-PLE stratum may be consistent with the known serotonergic and opioid pharmacological properties of tramadol at non-therapeutic doses, which have been associated with mood disturbance, agitation, and, at higher doses, psychotic symptoms [21, 22]. Such interpretations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than inferential, given the absence of substance-specific measurement.

The near-universal psychiatric burden observed in the High Multimorbidity stratum may also reflect broader patterns of polysubstance use documented in Nigerian adolescent populations [23], although this cannot be directly assessed within the present data. More generally, these findings underscore the importance of disaggregating substance type in future research, as differing pharmacological profiles and usage contexts may shape distinct configurations of psychiatric comorbidity. Longitudinal designs incorporating substance-specific exposure measures will be necessary to clarify these relationships and to distinguish contextual association from causal contribution.

Suicidality and psychiatric burden

The graded increase in past-month suicidal outcomes across latent strata, with ideation rising from 3.8% in the Low Burden stratum to 18.4% in the High Multimorbidity stratum, is consistent with cumulative risk frameworks in which co-occurring psychiatric conditions amplify suicidal vulnerability beyond the contribution of individual disorders considered in isolation [24]. The monotonic pattern observed across both ideation and attempts supports the interpretation that increasing psychiatric burden is associated with progressively greater suicide risk among substance-affected adolescents.

These estimates reflect a past-month reference period and are therefore not directly comparable to studies reporting lifetime or past-year prevalence. The attempt-to-ideation ratios observed (0.55–0.64) are consistent with patterns expected under short-interval assessment, where individuals who have recently attempted suicide may no longer report active ideation at the time of survey completion. While such ratios may appear elevated relative to those derived from longer reference periods, they remain clinically interpretable within the temporal constraints of the measurement window [25, 26].

The modest gradient in attempt-to-ideation ratios across strata further supports the internal coherence of the latent class structure, insofar as it aligns with the broader severity gradient observed across psychiatric indicators. Taken together, these findings suggest that the accumulation of psychiatric burden within substance-affected adolescents is associated not only with higher prevalence of suicidality, but also with a pattern of risk that is internally consistent across related suicidal outcomes.

Adversity, family context, and comorbidity

Cumulative adversity emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of High Multimorbidity class membership, with adolescents reporting ACE ≥ 3 showing substantially elevated odds relative to those with lower exposure. This finding aligns with cumulative risk frameworks, which posit that multiple co-occurring adversities exert additive and potentially interactive effects on psychological development, increasing the likelihood of more complex and pervasive psychopathology [16]. Within the present sample, the graded distribution of adversity across latent strata suggests that psychiatric burden is systematically patterned in relation to prior exposure to adverse environments.

The operationalisation of adversity in this study included indicators such as physical punishment, domestic violence exposure, emotional abuse, parental alcohol misuse, and household instability. While this composite approach is consistent with cumulative risk models, it necessarily aggregates experiences that may differ in severity, chronicity, and sociocultural meaning. In the Nigerian context, certain forms of physical discipline may be normatively embedded within child-rearing practices, even as more severe or chronic exposures may carry distinct developmental implications. The present findings therefore support the relevance of cumulative adversity as a marker of risk, while also underscoring the need for more differentiated approaches to characterising exposure.

Maternal alcohol use was independently associated with membership in the High Multimorbidity stratum, consistent with evidence linking parental substance use to increased psychiatric vulnerability in offspring through overlapping genetic, environmental, and caregiving pathways [27]. Given the cross-sectional design, the directionality of this relationship cannot be established, and bidirectional or unmeasured confounding processes cannot be excluded.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the clustering of psychiatric conditions observed in the High Multimorbidity stratum is embedded within broader familial and environmental contexts characterised by cumulative stress exposure. This reinforces the importance of situating psychiatric comorbidity among substance-affected adolescents within multi-level risk frameworks that extend beyond individual-level factors.

The internalising-PLE profile and cultural context

The emergence of a class characterised by co-occurring internalising disorders and psychotic-like experiences warrants careful interpretation. PLE, as operationalised by the PQ-16, encompasses unusual perceptual experiences and ideas of reference that may, in the Nigerian context, overlap with culturally normative spiritual or religious experiences, or with idioms of distress that lack direct equivalents in Western nosological frameworks [28]. From a cross-cultural psychiatry perspective, such experiences may reflect culturally mediated forms of distress expression rather than indicators of psychosis risk per se, underscoring the need to distinguish phenomenological expression from nosological classification.

This distinction may be particularly difficult to operationalise using self-report instruments in sub-Saharan African contexts, where the PQ-16 has not been independently validated. Although sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the latent class structure across different PQ-16 thresholds, this does not resolve the underlying question of construct validity. The proportion of PLE endorsements attributable to psychosis-spectrum phenomena versus culturally embedded experience therefore remains uncertain.

Within this context, the Internalising-PLE stratum may be understood not as a discrete clinical subtype, but as a statistical configuration reflecting the co-occurrence of affective distress and atypical perceptual experiences within a specific sociocultural environment. Further work integrating qualitative methods, clinician-rated assessments, and culturally grounded frameworks will be necessary to clarify the meaning and implications of this profile.

Screening: circularity quantified and contextualised

The proof-of-concept discrimination analysis demonstrated AUC = 0.72 against an independent functional impairment criterion. This analysis is exploratory, demonstrating conceptual feasibility and potential signal detection within a resource-constrained setting, rather than proposing an operational screening instrument. With that scope established, the 0.07 AUC gap between within-model and external criterion analyses provides a transparent quantification of the circularity attributable to MDD’s dual role; this is preferable to acknowledging circularity in general terms without demonstrating its magnitude.

The Brier score improvement of 19.4% over null confirms that the triad’s performance is not reducible to base-rate prediction, and the NNS of approximately 4 against the functional impairment criterion is plausible for a brief first-stage indicator in a high-prevalence setting. These findings nonetheless carry the explicit limitations stated in the Methods. Screening predictors and class membership derive from the same battery; external validation in an independent cohort is required before any translational claims are warranted.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s principal strengths include a large, population-representative sample (participation rate 92.5%), standardised diagnostic assessment, person-centred statistical methodology with appropriate classification uncertainty adjustment, and explicit quantification of both circularity and calibration in the screening analysis. The use of bootstrap confidence intervals and multiple sensitivity analyses strengthens methodological robustness.

Limitations warranting careful consideration include: the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference; the school-based sampling frame, which excludes out-of-school youth likely to carry higher burden; reliance on self-completion diagnostic instruments, which approximate but do not replace clinician-administered multi-informant assessment; the absence of formal psychometric validation of the MINI-Kid and PQ-16 in Nigerian adolescent populations; elevated Type I error risk without formal multiple-comparison correction; and the potential for latent class structures to vary across populations, time points, and measurement instruments. Latent classes are statistical risk strata, not discrete or stable clinical entities.

Future directions

Longitudinal cohort studies are required to establish temporal ordering among adversity exposure, substance involvement, and psychiatric clustering, and to examine whether specific substances, particularly tramadol, are differentially associated with particular comorbidity profiles. Independent validation in separate Nigerian and sub-Saharan African cohorts will be essential before profiles can inform service frameworks. Qualitative research exploring the lived experience of adolescents in the High Multimorbidity stratum (including help-seeking pathways, the role of faith-based and traditional healing practices, and protective factors) would enrich interpretation and inform culturally responsive intervention design.

Conclusion

Psychiatric comorbidity among Nigerian adolescents meeting criteria for SUD and/or AUD is common, extensive, and heterogeneously distributed across three statistically distinct profiles. The High Multimorbidity stratum, comprising approximately one in five substance-affected adolescents, is characterised by near-universal co-occurring psychiatric burden, elevated suicidality, and strong associations with cumulative adversity and maternal alcohol use. A proof-of-concept screening triad demonstrated useful discrimination against an independent functional impairment criterion, with the circularity effect explicitly quantified at 0.07 AUC units. The distinct pharmacological properties of tramadol, the predominant substance of misuse in this population, merit specific attention in future longitudinal work examining how substance type shapes comorbidity trajectories. Longitudinal research and independent validation are required before these statistical profiles can responsibly inform clinical assessment or service planning.
