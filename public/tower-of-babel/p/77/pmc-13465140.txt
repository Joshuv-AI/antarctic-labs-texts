Comparative analysis of cold curettage and coblation adenoidectomy: surgical and postoperative outcomes with 0° and 70° endoscopic techniques
Authors: Lütfü Şeneldir, Vecide Pınar Altınışık Doğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesAdenoidectomy is a common pediatric otolaryngologic procedure. While cold curettage remains widely practiced, endoscopic coblation has been developed to enhance precision, minimize intraoperative complications, and reduce residual tissue. This study compared outcomes of conventional cold curettage adenoidectomy and coblation-assisted adenoidectomy in pediatric patients.Materials and methodsThis study was designed as a prospective, comparative, non-randomized clinical study. A total of 128 children aged 3–11 years who underwent adenoidectomy were included. The choice of surgical technique was determined by patient (family) preference after explanation of both procedures. Patients were allocated to Group 1 (cold curettage, n = 64) or Group 2 (endoscopic coblation using 0° or 70° endoscopes, n = 64). Evaluated parameters included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative pain (VAS), halitosis, peripheral tissue injury, and residual adenoid tissue assessed at 1-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up.ResultsGroups were comparable in demographics and baseline adenoid grades (p = 0.689). Mean operative time was longer with coblation (26.6 ± 5.3 min) than with cold curettage (20.6 ± 3.8 min; p < 0.001), although procedures with a 70° endoscope were shorter than with a 0° (p = 0.037). Blood loss was significantly lower in the coblation group compared to the cold curettage group (12.4 ± 2.1 mL vs 24.2 ± 3.3 mL; p < 0.001). Postoperative pain scores at 24 and 72 h were also reduced in the coblation group (p < 0.001). Halitosis was more frequent after coblation (75% vs. 31.3%; p = 0.00000173) but resolved spontaneously within one week. Minor peripheral tissue injuries occurred only with cold curettage (n = 4) without sequelae. Introduction

Adenoid disorders, which play a significant role in the background of conditions such as nasal obstruction, snoring, recurrent upper respiratory tract infections, and frequent episodes of otitis and sinusitis, are commonly encountered in pediatric otolaryngology practice. Since substantial clinical improvement is often observed in patients unresponsive to medical treatment following surgical intervention, adenoidectomy is a frequently performed procedure. Various techniques have been employed for adenoidectomy, including cold curettage, coblation, laser, microdebrider, and suction electrocautery [1]. Cold dissection (curettage) is a procedure widely used worldwide, in which hyperplastic adenoid tissue is blindly removed using an appropriately sized curette. Its short preparation time, operative time, and low cost offer significant advantages over other methods. However, due to the lack of direct visualization, this technique cannot provide complete surgical expertise [1].

Coblation," also referred to as "controlled ablation," was first described in 2001 [2] and has been used in adenoidectomy procedures since 2005 [3]. The principle of this technique involves the transmission of radiofrequency energy through a conductive medium such as isotonic sodium chloride solution to generate a plasma field. This plasma energy, at temperatures of approximately 60 °C, targets the intercellular bonds, enabling tissue disintegration while causing minimal thermal damage to the surrounding structures. Owing to this advantage, the coblation technique has gained increasing interest among otolaryngologists in recent years [2].

In the various techniques performed via transoral or endoscopic transnasal approaches, the primary objectives include complete excision of adenoid tissue, minimal intraoperative bleeding, shorter operative time, rapid postoperative recovery, and the absence of both early and late complications. Minimizing work or school absenteeism and reducing overall healthcare costs are also important considerations.

In this study, we aim to comprehensively evaluate and compare the efficacy and outcomes of traditional cold curettage adenoidectomy versus endoscopic coblation adenoidectomy in pediatric patients, examining their respective advantages and limitations from multiple clinical perspectives.

Study design and patients

This study was designed as a prospective, comparative, non-randomized clinical study conducted at a tertiary care center between August 2024 and August 2025. A total of 128 pediatric patients aged 3–11 years who underwent adenoidectomy were prospectively included, with a follow-up duration of 6–12 months. All patients were diagnosed with adenoid hypertrophy and were unresponsive to medical treatment. Indications for surgery included nasal obstruction and associated symptoms such as snoring, apnea, mouth breathing during sleep, recurrent otitis media, and hearing loss due to serous otitis media.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were offered two surgical options: conventional cold curettage adenoidectomy and coblation adenoidectomy. After being informed about both techniques, including differences in cost, the choice of surgical method was made by the patient’s family, primarily based on financial considerations rather than clinical or anatomical factors. Importantly, the operating surgeon did not influence the selection of the surgical technique; therefore, group allocation was independent of surgeon decision-making.

Patients were therefore allocated to two groups: Group 1 (cold curettage, n = 64) and Group 2 (coblation adenoidectomy, n = 64). In Group 1, patients underwent adenoidectomy with or without myringotomy and ventilation tube insertion using the conventional cold curettage method. In Group 2, the same procedures were performed using an endoscopic coblation technique. Within this group, procedures were carried out using either a 0° or 70° endoscope (n = 32 for each subgroup).

Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent concurrent tonsillectomy, dental procedures, or additional nasal surgeries such as septoplasty and/or turbinate reduction. Patients with chronic neurological disorders or cleft palate were also excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. All patients completed the scheduled follow-up visits, and no loss to follow-up was observed.

All patients underwent a detailed anamnesis and clinical examination. Following the application of a nasal decongestant spray, nasal endoscopy was performed, and adenoid size was graded from 1 to 4 using the McMurray and Clemens scoring system. In a small subset of younger or less cooperative patients, preoperative grading was based on lateral nasopharyngeal radiography due to limited cooperation during the initial assessment. However, during postoperative follow-up, cooperation improved over time through repeated clinical interaction and adaptation to the examination process. With the use of a child-friendly approach, endoscopic examination became feasible in all patients. Therefore, postoperative assessments were performed endoscopically in all cases. Preoperative grading was used only to guide clinical decision-making, whereas intraoperative endoscopic assessment was considered the reference standard for analysis. Preoperative findings, including adenoid size and anatomical variations, were not used to determine the choice of surgical technique. The choice of endoscope angle (0° or 70°) was determined intraoperatively on the day of surgery, without prior knowledge of adenoid size or specific anatomical characteristics and without predefined selection criteria, in order to minimize potential procedural bias.

Surgical procedure

Patients were assigned to two groups: Group 1 (conventional cold curettage adenoidectomy) and Group 2 (coblation adenoidectomy). In Group 2, procedures were performed using either a 0° or 70° endoscope (n = 32 for each subgroup). All procedures were performed under general anesthesia by a single experienced surgeon.

In both groups, soft palate elevation was achieved using 2–2.67 mm (06–08Ch) catheters inserted through both nasal cavities.

In Group 1, after adequate exposure with a Boyle-Davis mouth gag, adenoid tissue was palpated digitally and removed using an appropriately sized adenotome. Residual tissue suspected by digital palpation or laryngeal mirror examination was removed using a smaller adenotome. Finally, finger compression was applied and hemostasis was achieved with adenoid packing. The excised tissue was sent for histopathological examination. In Group 2, following nasal decongestion, the nasopharynx and choanae were visualized using a 2.7 mm 0° pediatric transnasal endoscope and a 70° 4 mm transoral endoscope. A punch biopsy sample was obtained under endoscopic guidance, and adenoidectomy was completed using a coblation technique (Coblator II, ArthroCare Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) with the PROcise XP plasma rod system, which can be shaped to reach all areas of the adenoid tissue (especially the upper part of the nasopharynx and the opening to the eustachian tubes) and is designed for soft tissue ablation and dissection. Power settings were 7 for ablation and 3 for coagulation. Hemostasis was achieved using the coagulation mode without the need for additional packing or bipolar cautery.

Operative time was calculated starting from the placement of the Boyle-Davis mouth gag and ending with its removal after completion of the surgical procedure and achievement of hemostasis. Intraoperative blood loss was estimated using the number and saturation level of 10 cm gauze pads. Fully saturated gauze was considered equivalent to 10 mL of blood, while partially saturated pads were estimated as 5 mL. The total blood loss was calculated by subtracting the amount of saline used from the total fluid volume in the suction canister and adding the estimated amount from the gauze. Postoperative pain was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain ever experienced) [4]. Parents were asked to rate their child's pain at the first 24 h and at the 72nd hour based on their child's behavior such as crying and discomfort. Postoperative halitosis was assessed based on parental observation. Parents were instructed to approach their child from a distance of approximately 10 cm and to record the presence or absence of unpleasant oral odor.

Postoperative complications, including bleeding, infection, velopharyngeal insufficiency, tissue injury, halitosis, and rehospitalization or reoperation, were recorded. All patients were evaluated on postoperative day 7 for early clinical assessment (general condition, pain, halitosis and velopharyngeal insufficiency). Assessment of residual or recurrent adenoid tissue was performed endoscopically at 1, 6, and 12 months using either flexible or rigid nasal endoscopy following topical nasal decongestion. In younger children, a 2.2 mm flexible endoscope was used, and examinations were successfully completed with a child-friendly approach to ensure cooperation. No radiographic methods were used during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). Quantitative variables were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, while qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages using descriptive statistical methods. The normality of data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Bar-Plot graphics.

For comparisons of non-normally distributed variables between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-Square test and Fisher’s Exact test where appropriate. Results were evaluated within a 95% confidence interval, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary null hypothesis of this study was that there would be no statistically significant difference between cold curettage and coblation adenoidectomy in terms of operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative pain, and residual adenoid tissue.

Operative time

In Group 1 (cold curettage group), the operative time ranged from 14 to 36 min, with a mean of 20.6 ± 3.8 min. In Group 2 (coblation group), the operative time ranged from 16 to 38 min, with a mean of 26.6 ± 5.3 min (Table 2). The operative times for patients who underwent coblation adenoidectomy were significantly longer than those in the conventional adenoidectomy group, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). When comparing the use of 0° (n = 32) and 70° (n = 32) endoscopes within the coblation group, surgeries performed using the 70° endoscope had shorter operative times. Specifically, in coblation adenoidectomy performed with a 0° endoscope, the operative time ranged from 16 to 38 min, with a mean of 27.9 ± 5.6 min. In contrast, with the 70° endoscope, the operative time ranged from 17 to 34 min, with a mean of 25.2 ± 4.6 min. This difference was found to be statistically significant (p = 0.037); however, the absolute difference of approximately 2–3 min is unlikely to be clinically meaningful (Fig. 2).

Postoperative pain (Visual analog scale)

In the first 24 h, pain scores in Group 1 ranged from 4 to 7, with a mean of 5.50 ± 1.0. In Group 2, scores ranged from 1 to 5, with a mean of 2.09 ± 0.90. At 72 h, pain scores in Group 1 ranged from 1 to 4, with an average of 2.08 ± 0.86. In Group 2, scores ranged from 1 to 2, with an average of 1.03 ± 0.18. The VAS scores were significantly lower in the coblation group compared to the cold curettage group at the first 24 h and at 72 h. (p < 0.001) (Table 2), (Fig. 1). No statistically significant difference was found in terms of postoperative pain between surgeries performed with 0 and 70 degree endoscopes.

Halitosis

Parents were instructed to monitor halitosis daily during the first postoperative week, and data were collected at the first follow-up visit. In Group 1, 20 patients (31.25%) were reported by parents to have noticeable halitosis, whereas this number was significantly higher in Group 2, with 48 patients (75%) affected (Table 3), (Fig. 3). Halitosis was observed to be significantly lower in Group 1.(p < 0.001) In Group 2 (coblation group), halitosis typically began between postoperative days 2 and 3 and resolved by days 5 to 7, with only a single reported case persisting up to 9 days. In contrast, in Group 1 (cold curettage group), halitosis complaints began on the evening of the operation day and lasted for a maximum of 6 days.

Residual adenoid tissue

During the follow-up assessments at 1, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, residual adenoid tissue was observed in 19 patients (29.68%) in Group 1. The difference reached statistical significance (p < 0.001) (Table 3). These remnants were located in the superior choanal region and peritubal area but did not cause airway obstruction. In contrast, no residual adenoid tissue was observed in patients from Group 2. The residual tissue in Group 1 did not result in any symptoms or complaints reported by the patients or their caregivers. No patient underwent revision adenoid surgery.

Discussion

Adenoidectomy, either alone or combined with tonsillectomy and/or ventilation tube insertion, is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures in pediatric patients. The primary aim of adenoidectomy is the complete removal of adenoid tissue in the shortest possible time, with minimal blood loss, reduced postoperative pain, and without damage to the surrounding tissues. Conventional cold curettage, although widely used, may fail to achieve complete removal, particularly in the superior nasopharynx and peritubal regions, leading to residual tissue and persistent symptoms. Furthermore, its blind nature may result in mucosal or muscular injury and complications such as velopharyngeal insufficiency [4–9].

To address the issues arising from the “blind” nature of the curettage method, various adenoidectomy techniques have been developed, including endoscopic approaches and energy-based modalities. Endoscopic adenoidectomy allows direct visualization and more precise removal of adenoid tissue, particularly in anatomically challenging regions [10–12]. Alternative techniques such as suction diathermy, carbon dioxide and KTP laser, radiofrequency, and electronic molecular resonance have been described; however, these methods may be associated with thermal injury, scarring, and risk of stenosis [13–18].

The microdebrider, widely used in endoscopic sinus surgery, enables precise tissue removal and has also been applied in transnasal endoscopic adenoidectomy [15]. In contrast, mirror-assisted techniques, used in some studies instead of endoscopic visualization, may increase the risk of injury to the choanae, torus tubaris, or nasopharyngeal mucosa [19, 20].

In recent years, endoscopy has gained significant popularity in otolaryngological practice due to its ability to provide three-dimensional visualization and ease of use in both diagnostic and surgical procedures. Consequently, endoscope-assisted adenoidectomy has become increasingly widespread [21, 22].

In parallel with these developments, coblation adenoidectomy has also become a commonly preferred technique. When combined with endoscopic visualization, coblation allows for more precise excision of the peritubal adenoid tissue located in the lateral nasopharyngeal region. The average temperature around the tissue during coblation is approximately 40–60 °C, which is significantly lower than the 100 °C observed in traditional thermal coagulation techniques. This lower thermal profile is associated with a reduced risk of tissue necrosis and postoperative infection [23, 24]. In addition, the multifunctional capabilities of the device, including ablation, coagulation, aspiration, and irrigation, contributes to a reduced incidence of potential complications [25].

When operative times were evaluated, some studies reported that coblation adenoidectomy was shorter in duration [26], whereas others have argued the contrary, suggesting that coblation requires significantly longer operative times compared to conventional or microdebrider techniques [27, 28]. In our study, the duration of coblation adenoidectomy was found to be longer than that of cold curettage (Table 2, Fig. 1). This discrepancy may be related to surgeon experience, tissue volume, and technical factors such as probe clogging or impaired visualization. In addition, in our study, the choice of endoscope angle (0° or 70°) was determined intraoperatively without prior knowledge of adenoid size or anatomical characteristics. As a result, in some cases with anatomical challenges such as septal deviation, the use of a 0° endoscope may have limited visualization and prolonged operative time. It is conceivable that selective use of a 70° endoscope in such cases could have further reduced operative duration. Importantly, although the difference in operative time was statistically significant, the absolute difference was approximately 5–6 min, which is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. With increasing surgical experience, this difference may further decrease. The choice of endoscope angle is also important; differences in ease of use and duration have been observed between 0° (n = 32) and 70° (n = 32) endoscopes. Prior to the use of a 0° endoscope, nasal decongestion, turbinate reduction, and clearance of secretions should be ensured. This angle facilitates anatomical orientation, particularly for surgeons early in their training. Nasal endoscopic assessment under general anesthesia enables clear visualization of posterior septal deviation, polyp formation, concha-septal synechiae, and the torus tubarius region. In transnasal procedures, neck extension is not required, which is especially advantageous in patients with cervical spine instability [15].

In patients with severe turbinate hypertrophy or septal deviation, the use of a 0° endoscope becomes more challenging, and a 70° endoscope via intraoral approach may serve as a better alternative. Furthermore, with 0° endoscopes, frequent lens contamination, increased risk of bleeding due to septum-turbinate trauma, and higher likelihood of synechiae formation have been noted. In cases where the adenoid tissue completely fills the choana, visualization of the coblation probe tip with a 0° endoscope becomes difficult, leading to longer operative times and greater risk of complications. In such situations, a layer-by-layer excision of adenoid tissue using coblation under a 70° endoscope has been found to be more advantageous.

The surgeon’s position also varies depending on the endoscope used; coblation with a 0° endoscope is typically performed standing, whereas a seated position is preferred when using a 70° endoscope. In our study, procedures performed using a 70° endoscope were associated with shorter operative times compared to those performed with a 0° endoscope. This difference reached statistical significance; however, the absolute difference was approximately 2–3 min, which is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. (Fig. 2). Therefore, while endoscope angle may have a modest effect on operative time, this difference does not appear to translate into a meaningful clinical advantage, and the choice of endoscope can be guided by surgeon preference and intraoperative conditions.

Pain is a common condition following head and neck surgeries, and inadequate pain control after adenoidectomy is associated with difficulties in swallowing and feeding [29]. Consistent with previous studies, coblation adenoidectomy was associated with significantly lower pain scores compared to cold curettage [30, 31]. This may be attributed to reduced thermal and mechanical tissue trauma. In the present study, pain scores in the coblation group (Group 2) were significantly lower than those in the cold curettage group (Group 1) at both 24 and 72 h postoperatively (p < 0.001; Table 2, Fig. 1). Although pain assessment relied on parent-reported VAS scores, which may introduce subjectivity, the consistent differences observed between groups support the validity of this finding.

Persistence of symptoms after adenoidectomy is generally attributed to residual tissue in the postoperative period. In the literature, residual rates of 39–95% have been reported following procedures performed with traditional techniques [11, 15]. In our study, residual adenoid tissue was detected in 19 patients (29.68%) in Group 1, particularly in the superior choana and peritubal regions, whereas no residual tissue was observed in Group 2 (Table 3). Importantly, these remnants did not cause obstruction or clinical symptoms.

Most otolaryngologists and pediatric health specialists consider halitosis to be a common problem in children with adenoid hypertrophy, although objective data in the literature remain limited [32, 33]. With respect to postoperative halitosis, studies have reported its occurrence for up to seven days following electrocautery adenoidectomy, with spontaneous resolution without requiring treatment beyond antibiotics or simple analgesics [34]. In a study by Liu et al. comparing the side effects of microdebrider and coblation adenoidectomy, halitosis was observed in all patients in the coblation group, whereas the rate was 33.2% in the microdebrider group [35]. Similarly, in the present study, postoperative halitosis was more frequent in the coblation group (75%), however, halitosis was transient, typically resolving within the first postoperative week, with only a single case persisting up to 9 days. As assessment was based on parental observation, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Although adenoidectomy is generally considered a safe and effective procedure, potential complications include bleeding, infection, and pain. The most common complication is bleeding, which usually occurs in the early postoperative period but can persist for up to two weeks [36]. In our study, one patient in Group 1 experienced minor non-active bleeding, which was managed conservatively without surgical intervention. The lower incidence of bleeding in coblation adenoidectomy is primarily due to the device’s ability to simultaneously vaporize adenoid tissue while coagulating blood vessels [3]. This creates a relatively bloodless surgical field, improves visualization, enhances surgical precision, and minimizes both tissue damage and potential complications [25, 37].

In our study, minor injuries to surrounding tissues (posterior pharyngeal wall and the region around the eustachian tube orifice) were observed in four patients in Group 1. None of these complications led to long-term sequelae. Additionally, one case of minor postoperative oozing was reported in Group 1, which resolved spontaneously without the need for further intervention (Table 3). The postoperative return to normal activities is closely related to the recovery period. To address this issue, parameters such as surrounding tissue damage, postoperative bleeding, infection, and recurrence were evaluated in both groups. The findings indicate no significant difference in postoperative recovery time between the two groups, with both methods achieving similar recovery within approximately five days. The null hypothesis was rejected, as statistically significant differences were observed between the two surgical techniques in several outcome measures.

This study has certain limitations: the relatively small number of patients, its single-center design, lack of double-blinding, absence of objective halitosis assessment, and relatively short follow-up duration. All intraoperative and postoperative assessments were performed by the same surgeon, which may introduce observer bias. In addition, halitosis evaluation was based on a subjective method (parental assessment at an approximate distance of 10 cm), and objective measurement tools would be required to improve the validity of the findings. Furthermore, postoperative pain assessment relied on parent-reported visual analog scale (VAS) scores in younger children, which may introduce subjectivity and limit accuracy compared to self-reported measures. The major strength of this study lies in its prospective design, standardized surgical approach, and complete follow-up, with all procedures performed by a single experienced surgeon. Standardized surgical procedures, consistent data collection, and prospective study design were implemented to minimize variability and potential confounding factors.

Conclusions

Endoscope-assisted coblator adenoidectomy allows access to the superior and intranasal extensions of the adenoid tissue. With a single device, both ablation and coagulation can be performed simultaneously. Direct visualization through endoscopy enables selective ablation, leading to reduced blood loss, faster recovery, and minimal damage to surrounding structures. Preservation of the Eustachian tube, nasal, and pharyngeal mucosa is associated with reduced postoperative pain. A key advantage of this technique is the ability to achieve more precise removal under direct visualization. Endoscope-assisted coblator adenoidectomy should be considered a viable alternative to conventional cold curettage adenoidectomy. However, the need for specialized equipment, surgical expertise, and the cost implications should be taken into account in clinical decision-making.
