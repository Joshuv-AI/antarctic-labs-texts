Extracellular vesicles in metabolic perspective: mechanism and targeted therapy
Authors: Qifeng Sheng, Zebin Wu, Wenming Li, Liangliang Wang, Hao Xu, Wentao Wang, Zhanjun Yan, Gaoran Ge, Yaozeng Xu, Dechun Geng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntercellular metabolic communication is essential for maintaining homeostasis and normal physiological function. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), as key mediators of cell-to-cell communication, are increasingly recognized for their close association with metabolic regulation. EVs encapsulate and transport bioactive molecules—including metabolites, enzymes, nucleic acids, and proteins—that directly influence intracellular pathways such as energy supply, RNA and protein synthesis, and organelle function. Conversely, cellular metabolic states profoundly shape EV biogenesis, cargo composition, and targeting. These bidirectional interactions position EVs as coordinators of metabolism, with dysregulation contributing to diverse diseases. In this review, we focus on the regulatory roles of EVs in metabolic processes, their pathogenic and therapeutic implications across different diseases, and their applications in metabolomics and tissue engineering. By emphasizing the metabolic perspective, this work highlights the novelty of understanding EVs as both regulators and targets of metabolism, providing a holistic view of their mechanisms and offering new insights into the development of targeted therapies.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Metabolism, Extracellular vesicles, Metabolic reprogramming, Metabolomics, Tissue engineering

Introduction

Metabolism, the fundamental life activity for cell survival and proliferation, maintains the activity of the entire living organism. Glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and other metabolic processes not only provide energy support for the realization of cellular functions but also establish the substance and environmental basis, such as the production of lactate, nucleotides, or reactive oxygen species (ROS) [1, 2]. Because of these features, metabolism disorders induce inflammation, neurodegenerative diseases, atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, and tumor evolution [3]. Therefore, metabolic regulation is pivotal to cellular function and relies on intercellular communication via hormones, signaling molecules, enzymes, metabolites, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [4]. Elements like insulin promote glucose uptake after binding to cellular receptors [5]. Although an increasing proportion of metabolism-regulating drugs like gemcitabine, methotrexate, and sulfanilamide have been developed to prevent or treat diseases, the mechanisms by which metabolism is regulated in diseases are inadequately understood [6]. However, ongoing research on EVs in recent years has unfolded their relevance in metabolism, showing that they act as carriers of biomolecules and play even more complex effects that have yet to be fully summarized.

EVs, lipid bilayer vesicles that carry cargoes of DNA, proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites, are derived from almost all cells and are widespread in many body fluids and tissues [7]. Accordingly, the carrier characteristics and transcellular communication properties of EVs have been extensively studied. However, in recent years, researchers have also demonstrated their simultaneous involvement in the regulation of intercellular metabolism [8]. MicroRNAs have been found to play a regulatory role in glucose and lipid metabolism, with EVs facilitating their transport [9]. Further, in an endocrine or paracrine manner, EVs are secreted and transferred to interact with tumor cells, regulating cancer cell lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms [10]. Enzymes, metabolic raw materials, microRNAs, and proteins with regulatory roles are transported to target cells through EVs, directly or indirectly affecting various metabolic phenotypes in cells. In contrast, the types of EVs and their numbers are also altered under diabetes, obesity, and NAFLD, suggesting that EVs are a disease marker and target [8]. Nevertheless, a relevant overview of the regulation of metabolism by EVs and their targeted therapies still needs further elaboration.

In this review, we highlight the mechanisms by which EVs regulate cellular metabolism, discuss the effects of metabolic state on the production, transport, and function of EVs, and provide an update on the use of EVs in disease diagnosis and engineered targeted therapies. Given their correlation, we conclude that EVs regulate cellular metabolic activity by transferring substances and signaling molecules and facilitating metabolic reprogramming between cells. They serve as a critical nexus for maintaining or disrupting metabolic homeostasis. In particular, they can also be regarded as an integral component of metabolic processes, playing an indispensable role in the metabolic network. Therefore, this paper aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of EVs in metabolism and to provide a new perspective for disease-targeted therapies.

EVs: emerging modulators of metabolism

As mentioned above, EVs are characterized by a lipid bilayer, which enables them to carry cargo and pass through biofilm barriers. When EVs are recognized by target cells, recipient cells engage with EVs through membrane interactions, membrane fusion, and endocytosis [11]. Notably, EVs are secreted by almost all tissue cells in the body. While originating from different cell types, these EVs share similar metabolic regulatory functions.

Concept, classification, and production of EVs

General concept of EVs

EVs, a class of cell-derived membrane structures enclosed by a bilayer lipid membrane, are nanometer- to micrometer-sized particles incapable of self-replication [12]. However, a clear consensus on the definition of terms for EVs is still lacking [13]. EVs express surface proteins, including tetraspanins, lectins, and integrins, which mediate immune responses and target recognition through receptor-ligand interactions [14]. In addition, EVs act as carriers to transport proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites (Fig. 1). Through these cargoes, EVs participate in various physiological and pathological processes, including the transfer of functional substances, cellular communication, regulation of metabolism, inflammation, tumor progression, and cellular senescence [15, 16]. Although most cells are capable of secreting EVs, and the composition and corresponding functions of EVs produced by cells of different origins vary, the processes involved in the biological production of EVs are relatively similar [17]. Based on similarities in the biogenesis mechanism, biophysical properties, and other characteristics, EVs are broadly classified into three subtypes: exosome (30–150 nm), microvesicles (MVs, 100–1000 nm), and apoptotic vesicle (500–2000 nm). Exosomes originate from the endosomal system and are enriched in tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81). MVs bud directly from the plasma membrane and carry integrins, selectins, and cytoskeletal proteins. Apoptotic bodies form during programmed cell death and contain nuclear fragments and organelles [17, 18].

Classification and corresponding biogenesis

Exosomes are generated through the endocytosis pathway, wherein the endosomal membrane buds inward to form intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) that accumulate within multivesicular bodies (MVBs). Subsequently, MVBs are either degraded through lysosomal pathways or fused with the plasma membrane to release exosomes into the extracellular space [19]. Two distinct mechanisms have been identified in exosome biogenesis: one involves the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent pathway, while the other operates through an ESCRT-independent pathway. The latter is characterized by ceramide-mediated sorting processes [20].

Microvesicles (MVs), in contrast, are formed by the outward budding of the plasma membrane, a process driven by the reorganization of membrane-associated molecules [21]. This budding is tightly regulated by the plasma membrane’s composition and intracellular Ca²⁺ levels. Changes in Ca²⁺ concentrations modulate the activity of key enzymes and cytoskeletal proteins that facilitate membrane remodeling [22].

Apoptotic vesicles are produced during programmed apoptosis by disintegrating dying cells into smaller vesicles. Emerging evidence suggests that apoptotic vesicles are primarily phagocytosed by macrophages in vivo, contributing to immunomodulatory processes by delivering apoptotic signals and cellular debris for clearance [23].

Cargoes for EVs

EVs primarily perform biological functions through cargo delivery, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites [24]. The composition of these cargoes varies depending on the cellular, metabolic, and pathological conditions under which EVs are produced, consequently altering the biological effects mediated by EVs. By analyzing the specific cargoes present in EVs, researchers can uncover the molecular pathways activated under different physiological or pathological states [25].

During membrane fusion with the plasma membrane or endocytosis by target cells, EVs release their cargo into the cytoplasm of recipient cells. This process is regulated by the metabolic state within EVs. For instance, acidic or alkaline conditions during EVs extravasation, as well as fluctuations in lipid metabolism, have been shown to influence the efficiency of cargo release [26]. Such regulation highlights the intricate interplay between EVs metabolic environments.

Emerging evidence underscores that the metabolic state of donor cells profoundly influences the composition and functional targeting of EVs. The availability of nutrients, or even alterations in a single nutrient component, can influence the biosynthesis and the function of EVs. For example, under glutamine-depleted conditions, M1 macrophages secrete increased numbers of EVs with enhanced functionality. Mechanistic analysis indicates that glutamine depletion induces reprogramming of energy metabolism and redox status, subsequently upregulating pathways involved in EV biogenesis and cargo sorting (e.g., ESCRT) [27]. Additionally, the remodelling of EVs also occurs when cells are subjected to hypoxic conditions. Hypoxic conditions markedly enhance the production of cardiomyocyte-derived EVs (CM-EVs), a process that depends on neutral sphingomyelinase 2 (nSmase2), which mediates the release of tetraspanin-positive EV [28]. Furthermore, histones localise to the surface of EVs as membrane-associated proteins during cellular secretion. Cellular stress significantly enhances the secretion of histones within EVs, concurrently with an increase in the total number of EVs released and transition towards smaller EVs volumes [29].

The ways in which EVs work

EVs are secreted by most cells and can be found in a wide range of body fluids, such as blood, urine, sweat, and saliva [30]. Moreover, EVs affect the corresponding receptor cells in these body fluids in a paracrine or endocrine manner [31]. In addition, their ability to exert biological functions relies heavily on interactions with receptor cells, which occur through distinct mechanisms. The main modes of binding are membrane interactions, membrane fusion, and endocytosis of EVs, which are not mutually exclusive but can operate simultaneously [11, 20]. Based on these modes of interaction, the functional actions of EVs can be broadly categorized as follows.

Role of cargo in EVs function

The fusion of EVs with receptor cell membranes enables the direct release of EV contents into the cytoplasm of target cells or facilitates their internalization through phagocytosis, ultimately leading to cargo delivery [32]. The cargo carried by EVs impacts the activity of recipient cells in various ways beyond merely serving as signaling molecules in intercellular communication. For example, EVs deliver miRNAs, a vitally essential cargo [33]. Upon entering the recipient cell, different miRNAs alter the cellular gene expression and effect to induce or ameliorate the disease [7]. Parallel to this, protein cargo transported by EVs can alter intracellular protein composition or modify cell membrane properties, thereby affecting cellular functions and contributing to the pathogenesis or resolution of metabolic disorders [34].

Receptor-ligand interactions in EVs signaling

The membranes of EVs contain not only lipid components but also a diverse array of proteins, the composition of which varies depending on the originating cell type, conferring unique functional properties to EVs [35]. Among these proteins are specialized transmembrane ligands that facilitate direct interaction with receptors on target cell membranes, triggering downstream signaling cascades to modulate recipient cell activity [36]. For instance, the transmembrane protein CUB-domain containing protein 1 (CDCP1) on the membrane of EVs from bone metastatic prostate cancer cells promotes osteoclastogenesis [37]. Additionally, other membrane proteins, such as CD63, have also been identified as mediators of EVs function and signaling [38].

Effects of EVs from different origins on metabolism

Traditionally, EVs were thought to be waste products of cellular metabolism, but accumulating evidence has confirmed the involvement of EVs in mediating cell-to-cell communication [39]. Furthermore, recent studies have also pointed out that EVs may have vital capacities in regulating cellular metabolic activities [9, 40]. As can be seen in these studies, EVs of cellular origin from different systemic tissues or cells act in several parallel ways to regulate the metabolism of their respective target cells. Here, a concise overview of key EVs origin and their roles in metabolism is presented (Fig. 1).

Research on neurological diseases increasingly demonstrates that EVs derived from the nervous system play pivotal roles in regulating metabolic mechanisms associated with these conditions [41]. Neurodegenerative diseases typically disrupt energy metabolism in the brain, and EVs carrying microRNAs (miRNAs) are broadly engaged in this process. For instance, neuron-derived small EVs containing miR-181c-5p promote epilepsy by modulating astrocyte glutamate metabolism [42]. Microglia-derived small EVs (sEVs) modulate glioma cell metabolism by delivering miR-124, thereby reducing lactate, nitric oxide (NO), and glutamate release [43]. Similarly, skeletal muscle derived EVs contribute to metabolic regulation, particularly during exercise. Exercise-induced EVs promote mitochondrial activity and fatty acid oxidation through protein and mitochondrial cargo delivery [44]. Moreover, cardiac tissue releases EVs containing miR-15b-5p after ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) in obese mothers, inhibiting fatty acid oxidation in myocardial tissue [45]. Furthermore, breast adipose tissue-derived EVs contribute to metabolic reprogramming in breast cancer cells by enhancing the expression of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-related genes [46].

And at the cellular level, the macrophage-derived EVs have been shown to modulate both glycolytic and oxidative metabolic pathways. For instance, macrophage-derived EVs of type M1 decreased glycolysis and oxidative metabolism in donation after circulating death (DCD) rat model [47]. In contrast, EVs derived from bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) enhance glycolysis in recipient cells under high glucose conditions [48]. Self-renewal and the generation of differentiated progeny are characteristics of stem cells, mainly divided into embryonic stem cells and adult or tissue stem cells [49]. Despite their differences, EVs derived from both types of stem cells share common mechanisms in metabolic regulation. For example, human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived EVs harbor proteins and mitochondrial subunits associated with OXPHOS, contributing to tissue repair after radiation injury [50]. Neural stem cell-derived EVs, on the other hand, deliver functional mitochondria to recipient cells, thereby modulating metabolic activity [51].

EVs in the regulation of glucose metabolism

Glucose metabolism is a central component of cellular metabolic activity, which involves key processes such as glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and OXPHOS [52]. Herein, the general process of glucose metabolism is elucidated as follows. Human cells uptake glucose via glucose transporter (GLUT) [53]. Once internalized, glucose undergoes glycolysis, producing pyruvate, which either enters the mitochondria to fuel the TCA cycle and OXPHOS under aerobic conditions or is converted to lactate under anaerobic conditions [54]. The byproducts of these metabolic pathways, including ATP, NADH, ribose-5-phosphate, and NADPH, are critical for maintaining cellular bioenergetics, redox balance, and biosynthesis [55, 56]. Furthermore, different cells have different glucose metabolic patterns. For instance, there are differences in glucose metabolism between normal cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [57]. As an essential component of cellular metabolism, glucose metabolism has long been heavily explored in terms of its mechanisms and the associated regulatory mechanisms [58].

However, emerging evidence highlights the role of EVs as active participants in glucose metabolism, influencing glucose uptake, glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and metabolic reprogramming [59, 60]. EVs function as carriers of regulatory cargo and modulators of molecular pathways directly involved in glucose metabolism. Their ability to carry diverse biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, enables them to act as intercellular messengers coordinating metabolic processes across various cell types.

First, the regulation of GLUT by EVs influences the whole process of glucose metabolism. Glucose uptake via GLUT is a prerequisite for cellular glucose metabolism, and the need for glucose in cells in the body also dictates that all cell surfaces express GLUT [61]. Internalization of GLUT-carrying EVs induces an increase in glucose uptake [62], and several studies have also pointed out that EVs transmit the phenotype directly and thus regulate glucose uptake [63]. Furthermore, glucose enters the cell and proceeds through the metabolic pathways we have previously described. Throughout these processes, EVs continue to play a crucial regulatory role.

EVs-mediated regulation of Glycolysis

Generally, regarding substance metabolism, glycolysis is the first step in glucose metabolism in all cells, converting glucose to pyruvate. In an adequate oxygen supply, pyruvate is oxidized to acetyl-CoA in mitochondria, whereas it is reduced to lactate under anaerobic conditions [54, 64]. On the other hand, the free energy generated during glycolysis forms the high-energy compound ATP, which in turn is engaged in maintaining the cellular ionic concentration gradient and the turnover of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [65]. Nevertheless, recent findings have shown that EVs are actively involved in and regulate this important metabolic link.

In recent years, there is growing recognition of EVs as pivotal modulators of pancreatic β-cell function, exhibiting bidirectional effects. By simultaneously mediating insulin secretion capacity and insulin resistance, EVs are emerging as critical players in the pathophysiology of metabolic disorders such as obesity and type 2 diabetes [66]. For example, adipocyte-derived EVs have been shown to deliver insulinotropic proteins into pancreatic β-cells, where they enhance GPCR/cAMP/PKA signaling and thereby promote first-phase glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) [60]. In contrast, EVs derived from breast cancer cells impair pancreatic β-cell function by delivering miR-122, which targets pyruvate kinase (PKM) in β-cells, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and ATP-dependent insulin secretion [67]. The regulation of insulin secretion by EVs directly impacts β-cell glycolytic activity with inhibitory EVs reducing key enzyme activity and ATP production, thereby blocking glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Conversely, pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory EVs indirectly influence glycolysis and overall glucose metabolism by modulating insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues [68].

EVs can regulate metabolic processes directly by transporting key enzymes involved in glycolysis (Fig. 2). EVs released by skeletal muscle cells contain a number of functional proteins involved in metabolic activities, and among these proteins is lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), which promotes cellular glycolysis when delivered to BMSCs [59]. Likewise, during liver fibrosis, hepatic stellate cells (HSC) stimulated by TGF-β induced palmitoylation of hexokinase 1 (HK1) and secretion through large EVs in a TSG101-dependent manner. Upon uptake of such EVs, glycolysis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells is accelerated and further deteriorated [69].

Yet, more importantly, EVs are implicated in the indirect regulation of glycolysis (Fig. 2). EVs regulate glycolysis by carrying mi-RNAs. Secreted by M2-macrophages, EVs enriched with miR-181b-5p modulate glucose uptake and glycolysis and simultaneously reduce the production of mitochondrial ROS in CC chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2)-expressing macrophages, resulting in the promotion of post-infarction myocardial repair [70]. Additionally, it has been reported that EVs produced by renal tubular epithelial cells by transmitting miRNAs and lncRNAs resulted in increased expression of HIF-1α and decreased hydroxylation of HIF-1α, thereby enhancing glycolysis in macrophages [71]. In contrast, EVs derived from human umbilical cord MSCs inhibited the expression of HIF-1α, reducing the expression of glycolytic enzymes and thus inhibiting metabolism [72]. Moreover, EVs regulate glycolysis through specialized molecular pathways. Carried by apoptotic vesicles (SHED-apoVs) obtained from stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth, programmed cell death-1 (PD1) interacts with PDL1, a specific receptor on endothelial cell (EC) membrane, thereby leading to decreased levels of glycolysis in ECs [73]. Collectively, the dual role of EVs in both promoting and inhibiting glycolysis underscores their immense potential as therapeutic tools.

EVs-mediated regulation of the TCA cycle and OXPHOS

The majority of aerobic organisms rely on the TCA cycle and OXPHOS process to generate ATP. Typically, pyruvate generated by glycolysis is transferred to the mitochondria in eukaryotic cells and undergoes the process of pyruvate oxidation to generate acetyl-CoA, after which it enters the TCA cycle [74]. Subsequently, acetyl-CoA is metabolized by the TCA cycle to produce metabolites such as NADH and FADH2 [75]. Moreover, NADH and FADH2 enter into the OXPHOS process, which is ultimately catalyzed by ATP synthase to produce ATP [76]. In addition, other metabolites such as lipids and amino acids are also involved in the TCA cycle, making the TCA cycle an important energy metabolic pathway for aerobic organisms [77]. In this process, EVs were identified to participate in regulating metabolic events in various ways.

Direct regulation by EVs involves the transport of enzymes or metabolites associated with the TCA cycle. For instance, EVs of neuroblastoma origin carry metabolic enzymes involved in the TCA cycle, potentially altering the metabolic landscape of the tumor microenvironment [78]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the direct modulation of the TCA cycle and OXPHOS by EVs are poorly understood and remain underexplored.

Indirectly, EVs influence the TCA cycle and OXPHOS by transferring regulatory molecules (Fig. 2). Loads carried by EVs contribute significantly to this process. For instance, uptake of LPS-stimulated endothelial cell-secreted EVs by bone marrow MSCs reduced isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2) expression levels. Interestingly, IDH2 reverses isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate (alpha-KG) in the TCA cycle, thereby decreasing alpha-KG levels and inducing cell epigenetic changes [79]. Similarly, the translocation of miR-155-5p, miR-10a-3p, and miR-30a-3p by EVs derived from breast adipose tissue of obese women into breast cancer cells increases the gene expression of enzymes involved in OXPHOS, such as ATP synthase, NADH, which may be associated with breast cancer development [46]. Analogous miRNA cargo is miR-146a. EVs carrying miR-146a are secreted by multiple nervous system cells as a result of inflammatory stimuli. When taken up by mixed glial cells, they induce a decrease in glycolysis and OXPHOS metabolic levels in recipient cells [80]. Furthermore, mitochondria, as sites of TCA cycling and OXPHOS, are also affected by the cargoes of EVs. For example, the mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) protein, as well as the TFAM-mRNA complex was transported by MSC-derived EVs, which ameliorated the defective OXPHOS of mitochondria in renal tubular cells [81]. From a therapeutic perspective, leveraging EVs to manipulate TCA cycle and OXPHOS activities offers exciting opportunities. However, challenges remain in ensuring the stability, specificity, and controlled delivery of these EVs to the target tissues.

EVs in the regulation of lipid metabolism

Besides contributing significantly to energy storage and metabolism, lipids are critical components of cell membranes and are implicated in cellular signaling [82]. Moreover, to the extent that fatty acids, which are the most vital component of lipids, serve as a form of energy storage, under certain conditions, they also undergo β-oxidation to generate energy [83]. Lipids also encompass phospholipids (mainly glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids) and cholesterol, fundamental to cell membrane structure and diverse metabolic functions [84]. Nonetheless, their metabolites and their biological functions are not similar. Notably, phospholipids are metabolized by phospholipases to produce a variety of biologically active lipids and are involved in membrane trafficking, signal transduction, and disease development [85]. Furthermore, cholesterol metabolism yields primarily bile acids, along with several vitamins and steroid hormones [86].

In general, lipid metabolism, as an influential part of human metabolism, has been the focus of research on its mechanisms [87, 88]. However, with growing insights into EVs, recent research has also shown that EVs exert a regulatory influence on lipid metabolism [89]. EVs act as intercellular mediators, facilitating metabolic exchanges by delivering specific components to recipient cells, thereby modulating lipid metabolic pathways [90].

As biocarriers, EVs directly transfer lipids, such as cholesterol and fatty acids, while enzymes and miRNAs can be encapsulated and translocated to engage in lipid metabolism [91]. Obesity is considered a disease caused by dysregulation of glycolipid metabolism. In investigating the mechanisms of lipid metabolism disorders in obesity, it was identified that EVs transport ncRNAs, proteins, and other factors that interfere with cellular lipids uptake, transportation, and catabolism [92]. Additionally, the ways in which EVs regulate intracellular lipid metabolism are diverse, and in this section, we will also elaborate on the approaches.

EVs-mediated regulation of fatty acid metabolism

Fatty acids, particularly long-chain fatty acids, are essential for energy production, in addition to their role in intracellular signaling and cell membrane synthesis [93]. After activation of fatty acids in the cell, they enter the mitochondria through the action of enzymes such as carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT-I is the rate-limiting enzyme for this process), where they undergo β-oxidation to form acetyl-CoA [94]. After this, acetyl-CoA is ultimately generated into ATP after entering the TCA cycle and OXPHOS [75, 76]. Notably, EVs function uniquely as mediators in the reciprocal regulation of fatty acid metabolism between cells, significantly influencing overall metabolic homeostasis and physiological functions.

EVs directly regulate fatty acid metabolism by influencing components involved in fatty acid metabolism [95]. Enzymes are an integral part of metabolic reactions, and EVs directly regulate metabolic enzymes, thereby modifying whole cellular metabolic processes. For instance, EVs secreted by neuroblastomas stimulate the cancerous transformation of recipient cells. In contrast, in proteomic studies, EVs have been found to contain a large number of metabolic enzymes, which also contain enzymes for fatty acid metabolism [78].

Nevertheless, the regulation of fatty acid metabolism by EVs is more indirect and falls into the following ways (Fig. 2). Different kinds of cargoes carried by EVs are major drivers in their regulatory effects [96, 97]. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) synthesized by palmitic acid-induced Kupffer cells, a fatty acid cargo of tumor-derived EVP, is translocated into hepatocytes and inhibits their fatty acid metabolism and OXPHOS [40]. Furthermore, human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived EVs exert cardioprotective effects through the transport of miR-20a-5p and miR-19a-3p while enhancing β-oxidation gene expression [98]. In addition, several studies have also suggested that EVs implement their functions by interfering with mitochondria [99]. After injection into oleic acid-treated ARPE-19 cells, EVs, originating from organoid-derived human retinal progenitor cells, fused to its mitochondrial network and increased levels of mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation-related proteins (CRAT and HADH) [100]. The regulation of fatty acid metabolism by EVs underscores their pivotal role in cellular communication and metabolic control. This dynamic capability suggests that EVs could be both biomarkers and therapeutic agents in metabolic disorders.

EVs-mediated regulation of cholesterol metabolism

Cholesterol is a synthesizable lipid in the body and is vital for cell membrane strength and fluidity, cell differentiation, and even individual growth and development [101]. Moreover, mammalian cells obtain cholesterol from de novo synthesis and by uptake of exogenous cholesterol from circulating low-density lipoproteins (LDL) [102]. In brief, cholesterol is initially synthesized from acetyl-CoA and NADPH, which are formed with the assistance of the key enzyme 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase [103]. In addition, the final destination of cholesterol is mainly bile acids, which also form steroid hormones [104]. The different modes of regulation during cholesterol metabolism have been studied in great detail [86, 105]. However, EVs have made advances in the regulation of cholesterol metabolism.

EVs can directly modulate cholesterol metabolism by delivering enzymes, proteins, or metabolites that impact cholesterol uptake and processing. For example, macrophages express CD36 to enable binding and internalization of LDL and microparticles for intracellular cholesterol uptake. Meanwhile, increased degradation of CD36 due to enhanced ubiquitination of CD36 by platelet-derived EVs inhibit cholesterol recruitment in macrophages [106] (Fig. 2). Through this direct modulation of cholesterol uptake, EVs then influence the subsequent whole metabolism of cholesterol within the cell.

Beyond direct interactions, the most important of indirect regulatory action is the cargo carried by EVs (Fig. 2). For instance, treating fibroblasts with EVs derived from breast cancer cells containing the metastasis suppressor genes NME1 and NME2 induced significant down-regulation of intracellular cholesterol metabolism-related gene expression [97]. Additionally, EVs may also exert regulatory effects by modulating corresponding proteins involved in cholesterol metabolism. Cow’s milk allergy (CMA) leads to lipid metabolism disorders in BALB/c mice, causing elevated serum levels of total cholesterol (TC) and LDL cholesterol (LDL-c), which were ameliorated after inhibition of EVs release. Interestingly, fewer proteins promoting cholesterol metabolism were detected in CMA mice-derived EVs. Although all of these indicate that EVs get into the regulation of cholesterol metabolism, the mechanisms underlying which active structures of EVs contribute to metabolic alterations are not yet clear [107]. This suggests that future research should focus on identifying the specific bioactive components within EVs that are responsible for modulating cholesterol metabolism, paving the way for targeted therapeutic strategies to address lipid-related disorders.

EVs-mediated regulation of phospholipid metabolism

As a group of amphiphilic lipids, phospholipids are divided into two main classes: glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids [108]. Moreover, phospholipids, together with proteins, glycolipids, and cholesterol, make up the bilayer lipid cell membrane and are the primary component, which is also responsible for the formation of signaling mediators and hormones [84]. In living organisms, phospholipid metabolism allows phospholipids to generate metabolites such as phosphoric acid, glycerol, and fatty acids through the action of various phospholipases [109]. Besides, under physiological conditions, phospholipid metabolism maintains normal cellular function, and when this process is disturbed, it can induce diseases such as depression and Alzheimer’s disease [110]. Although phospholipid metabolism continues to be explored, it has been revealed that EVs (themselves phospholipid bilayer-encapsulated particles) also participate and modulate phospholipid metabolism processes in several types of research.

For sure, EVs can intervene in the process of phospholipid metabolism, although the exact mechanism of how EVs regulate this procedure remains elusive. For example, EVs derived from Malassezia restricta inhibit the growth of Staphylococcus aureus and biofilm formation by interfering with its glycerophospholipid metabolism [111]. Likewise, Thai rat-tailed radish microgreens-derived EVs disrupt phospholipid metabolism, mainly glycerophospholipid metabolism, in HCC cells and consequently exert anticancer effects [112]. Regrettably, the composition of EVs is the source of the alterations in metabolic regulation caused during regulation, which is still unknown and requires deeper exploration.

EVs in the regulation of amino acid metabolism

As raw materials for the synthesis of proteins, amino acids maintain typical cellular structures and functions and are also essential energy substances for cells [113]. There are 20 α-amino acids in the human body that produce proteins, nine of which need to be obtained from outside the body, known as essential amino acids [114]. However, in addition to making up substances such as proteins, these amino acids also undergo energy metabolism via mainly oxidative pathways and ultimately produce urea and CO2 [115]. Amino acids enter the cell through amino acid transporters (AATs) in the cell membrane and are involved in the following metabolic processes [116]. Under the deamination by aminotransferases, alpha-KG acquires the amino group, forming glutamate, which enters the urea cycle. Meanwhile, another new product, keto acid, moves into the TCA cycle for metabolism [117].

Furthermore, amino acid metabolism is relatively complex, and much attention has been paid to the associated metabolic pathways [118, 119]. However, EVs have also been shown to have regulatory effects on amino acid metabolism in many recent studies [120]. The fact that EVs modulate intercellular metabolic exchanges through their specific mode of action is also illustrated, thus causing changes in metabolic homeostasis throughout the body. Below, we explore the roles of EVs in regulating key pathways of amino acid metabolism, including glutamine and arginine metabolism.

EVs-mediated regulation of glutamine metabolism

Glutamine, a non-essential amino acid or conditionally essential, is primarily produced in muscles [121]. Meanwhile, it is the most abundant and versatile amino acid in the body and is also involved in vital cellular activities such as metabolism and immunity. With the help of transporters such as SLC38A2 [122] and SLC1A5 [123], glutamine is taken up and moves into the mitochondria, where it is converted to glutamate by glutaminase (GLS/GLS2) [124]. Subsequently, it is transformed by glutamate dehydrogenase (GLUD) or various transaminases to alpha-KG, which enters the TCA cycle and serves as a substrate for lipid synthesis. In addition, glutamine has been implicated in the synthesis of nucleotides, aspartic acid, and glutathione (GSH) [125]. Given its central role in multiple metabolic and biosynthetic pathways, glutamine metabolism is tightly regulated to meet cellular demands under varying physiological and pathological conditions. Moreover, EVs have emerged as key modulators in this regulation, potentially providing novel therapeutic targets for metabolic reprogramming in diseases.

EVs regulate glutamine metabolism by directly affecting glutamine uptake, enzyme actions, and metabolites. For example, radiation-induced cancer cells produce sEVs enriched in SLC1A5, enhancing glutamine metabolism in unirradiated oral squamous carcinoma cells [126] (Fig. 2). In addition, EVs derived from M2-type macrophages promoted glutamine metabolism in osteoclast precursors (OCPs) by transporting glutamate, causing an increase in alpha-KG and inducing metabolic reprogramming [127].

Furthermore, EVs have been identified to influence the process of glutamine metabolism indirectly (Fig. 2). In particular, cargoes such as nucleic acids serve an essential position in the realization of the functions of EVs. For example, LINC01614, encapsulated by EVs derived from CAF tissues, was transported into lung adenocarcinoma cells and interacted with ANXA2 and p65. This process subsequently contributes to activating the nuclear factor kappaB (NF-κB) pathway, upregulated glutamine transporters such as SLC38A2 and SLC7A5, and ultimately increased glutamine metabolism in cancer cells [128]. Alternatively, EVs can interfere with gene expression to indirectly affect glutamine metabolism. EVs of ADSCs origin up-regulated the gene expression of glutamine synthetase in hepatocytes while down-regulating the expression of GLS2, which interfered with glutamine metabolism as well as the progression of hepatic fibrosis [129].

EVs-mediated regulation of arginine metabolism

Beyond being a component of multiple proteins, arginine, a non-essential amino acid and conditionally essential amino acid for the human body, constitutes a precursor for NO, urea, polyamines, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [130]. Furthermore, the physiological interactions and relationships between arginine metabolic pathways are intricate and challenging to analyze, and enzymatic catalysis represents the key factor in their regulation [131]. In general, arginine is synthesized from aspartate and citrulline in normal cells by argininosuccinate synthase 1 (ASS1) and argininosuccinate lyase (ASL) [132]. In addition, in the presence of arginase 1 (ARG 1), arginine is metabolized to urea and ornithine, which continues to participate in the urea cycle [133]. Another crucial route of arginine metabolism is the production of NO via NO synthase (NOS) as well as citrulline, which in turn is involved in arginine synthesis [134]. The intricate network of arginine metabolic pathways and their dependence on enzymatic catalysis highlights the complexity of their regulation. Understanding how external factors, such as EVs-mediated interactions, influence these pathways could provide new opportunities to manipulate arginine metabolism for therapeutic purposes, particularly in diseases where arginine homeostasis is disrupted.

EVs directly affect arginine uptake and metabolism by transporting critical proteins and nutrients. EVs originating from colorectal cancer carry cationic amino acid transporter 1 (CAT1), enhance arginine transport, upregulate downstream NO metabolism, and facilitate angiogenesis [135]. Similarly, EVs also transport proteins, thereby directly interfering with arginine metabolism. In exploring the effect of ASS1 silencing on tumor development, it was identified that EVs containing proteins and other nutrients secreted by tumor stromal cells were taken up by ASS1-deficient tumor cells and produced arginine after lysosomal degradation and recycling within the tumor cells to sustain their survival [136].

More importantly, however, EVs indirectly regulate arginine metabolism (Fig. 2). The relatively important part is the cargoes transported by EVs, which are highly valued in regulating arginine metabolism. For instance, cardiomyocyte-derived EVs, produced by stimulation of a traditional Chinese medicine Tongxinluo (TXL), transport Long Intergenic Non-Protein Coding RNA, Regulator of Reprogramming (Linc-ROR) to cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs). This cargo downregulated intracellular miR-145-5p, which activated eNOS by promoting the expression of p70s6k1, ultimately causing increased NO production [137]. In addition, EVs also regulate arginine metabolism by modulating enzyme levels. For instance, cardiosphere-derived cell-originated EVs upregulate ARG 1 expression in M1 macrophages, which also downregulates NO secretion in activated macrophages [138].

EVs in the regulation of redox metabolism

Redox reactions involving the transfer of electrons are intricately connected to cellular metabolism [139]. In vivo, redox metabolism represents a crucial component of the cellular metabolic network, operating in mammals through various electron-carrying molecules, such as NAD+, NADP+, coenzyme Q (CoQ), GSH, and Fe-S clusters [140]. These molecules participate not only in energy and material metabolism but also in the maintenance of the cellular redox state. Consequently, disruptions in redox balance can lead to metabolic dysfunctions, contributing to disease development. For example, an imbalance in the NADH/NAD+ ratio, often resulting from chronic hyperglycemia, is a key mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [141].

Furthermore, a number of recent studies underscore that EVs are involved in redox metabolism. T-lymphocytes with increased pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme 2 (PKM2) expression produce EVs that act on macrophages, contributing to lipid peroxidation, which causes an imbalance in redox metabolism and cell migration, further driving the development of abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA) development [142]. These findings highlight diverse regulatory mechanisms of EVs in redox metabolism, facilitating the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting diseases associated with redox imbalance.

EVs-mediated regulation of NAD+ and NADP+ metabolism

The redox couple, NAD+/NADH and NADP+/NADPH, maintains redox homeostasis in vivo and is critical in DNA repair, cellular energy metabolism, and apoptosis [143]. For NAD+, three significant modes of NAD+ synthesis are available: de novo synthesis, Preiss-Handler pathway, and salvage pathway. Furthermore, among these methods of NAD+ synthesis, the enzyme NAM phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), which converts nicotinamide (NAM) to NAD+, is particularly significant [144]. Similarly, NADP+ is synthesized through various intracellular pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway and NAD+ kinases [145]. Additionally, free radicals are generated through NADPH oxidases (NOXs), which cause oxidative stress [146]. Therefore, the mechanism by which NAD+ and NADP+ maintain redox homeostasis through their respective different metabolisms has become a subject of considerable interest. Meanwhile, a growing body of research on the extensive involvement of EVs in regulating NAD+ and NADP+ metabolism is emerging. These observations emphasize the importance of EVs-mediated regulation of NAD+ and NADP+ metabolism in maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly in response to physiological stressors such as exercise and aging.

EVs interact directly with key molecules, such as enzymes, in NAD+ and NADP+ metabolism (Fig. 3). For instance, in the context of NAD+ metabolism, the concentration of EVs in the bloodstream increases during exercise, accompanied by elevated levels of eNAMPT within these vesicles. This increase modifies NAD+ levels to counteract age-related declines once the EVs are internalized by recipient cells [147]. Likewise, EVs from young mouse adipose tissue MSCs transporter relatively high levels of NAMPT, which induces elevated NAD+ generation and affects SIRT1, ultimately enhancing cell activity and macrophage polarization from the SIRT1/PPARγ/PGC-1α pathway and the SIRT1/NF-kappa B p65/NLRP3 pathway, respectively [148]. Further, concerning NADP+ metabolism, EVs originating from adipose tissue in obese patients contain copies of NOX4 delivered to trophoblast cells, leading to ROS accumulation and causing severe cellular oxidative damage and senescence [149].

In addition, EVs also impact NAD+ and NADP+ metabolism through indirect regulatory mechanisms, primarily influenced by the cargo they carry (Fig. 3). For example, circulating EVs from diabetic patients have been shown to activate NOX5 by delivering miR-15a-3p, which enhances ROS generation and consequently impedes wound healing [150]. Besides, research on the regulation of this metabolic process by EVs remains limited. Expression of NOX4 and NOX2 in differentiated macrophages was suppressed after uptake of monocyte-derived EVs, and this regulation is thought to take place at the transcriptional or translational level, potentially mediated by miRNAs [151].

Moreover, considering the critical role of EVs in the antioxidant system, their regulation of the Nrf2 pathway via miRNA cargo may represent an important therapeutic target for intervening in oxidative stress and related diseases. It has been demonstrated that miRNAs enriched in cardiac-derived EVs inhibit Nrf2 selectively, thereby weakening the defense capabilities of the antioxidant system [152]. Meanwhile, human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (hASC-EVs) alleviate oxidative stress-induced damage through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, hASC-EVs activate the PI3K/Akt pathway and promote Nrf2 nuclear translocation via p62-mediated KEAP1 degradation, thereby enhancing antioxidant defense [153]. Based on these studies, EVs were shown to finely regulate cellular redox homeostasis by modulating Nrf2-associated antioxidant pathways. This not only reveals the bidirectional effects of EVs in cellular protection and signal transduction, but also provides theoretical underpinnings for EV-based antioxidant intervention strategies.

EVs-mediated regulation of iron metabolism

While iron has a critical physiological effect in oxygen transport, DNA metabolism, and energy metabolism, imbalances in iron metabolism contribute to the development of anemia, cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative disorders, inflammation, and cancer [154]. Furthermore, iron has two forms: ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric ion (Fe3+). In redox metabolism, iron reacts as a cofactor for different enzymes because of its properties. Thus, disturbances in iron metabolism, in particular, increased production of free radicals due to intracellular iron accumulation [155]. Ferroptosis is a ubiquitous non-apoptotic cell death process characterized primarily by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [156]. At the same time, alterations in various processes of iron metabolism, such as uptake, storage, and transport, also mediate ferroptosis [157]. Although these modes of regulation have been consistently focused on, the mechanisms by which EVs regulate iron metabolism and interfere with ferroptosis have begun to come to the forefront.

Ferritin, a key intracellular iron-storage protein, can be secreted via EVs or the secretory autophagy pathway. In ovarian cancer cells, the ferroptosis inducer erastin upregulates CD63, a tetraspanin involved in MVB and EV formation, and promotes EVs production. Notably, the EVs secreted by these cells contain both CD63 and the major iron-storage protein ferritin heavy chain (FtH), suggesting that ovarian cancer cells may utilize CD63⁺ EVs to secrete iron-rich ferritin and thereby evade erastin-induced ferroptosis [158]. In particular, ferritin is incorporated into CD63-positive EVs through the ferritin-specific autophagy adaptor NCOA4 and released into the extracellular environment. This mechanism of whole ferritin secretion enables cells to reduce intracellular iron levels, thereby counteracting ferroptosis [159]. These findings underscore EVs as key mediators of intercellular iron homeostasis, indicating that ferritin-containing EVs may function in modulating oxidative stress and iron-dependent cell death, and offer novel insights for cancer therapy.

Notably, via intercellular substance transport and the iron-binding receptor it carries, EVs directly regulate iron metabolism. As reported, hepatocytes increase the secretion of iron-containing EVs, causing intracellular iron depletion and enrichment in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and inducing an elevation of ROS and fibrotic activation of HSCs [160]. Meanwhile, it has also been indicated that iron-loaded cells transfer intracellular ferritin to CD63+ EVs for secretion via nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) [161]. These demonstrate the importance of EVs in intercellular iron exchange, which also influences intracellular iron concentration and the homeostasis of redox metabolism. In addition, when bacterial infection occurs, macrophages secrete more EVs, which contain iron uptake receptors that combine with ferric proteins in the circulation to reduce bacterial iron utilization, thus interfering with iron metabolism and achieving antibacterial effects [162].

Furthermore, it cannot be neglected that EVs indirectly regulate iron metabolism by interfering with gene expression and other means (Fig. 3). For instance, ceruloplasmin (CP) mRNA expression increases in TAMs, and subsequently, it is transferred from TAMs to HT1080 cells via EVs. In tumor cells, the mRNA is translated into proteins to stimulate iron release from tumor cells, thereby protecting them from ferroptosis [163]. Interestingly, EVs also rely on receptor-ligand binding modalities in order to perform their regulatory roles. EVs originate from macrophage-acquired transferrin receptors on their surface from donor cells. This receptor allows EVs to scavenge excess iron and could be therapeutically helpful as iron chelators to regulate iron metabolism after myocardial infarction [164]. Investigation of the precise signaling pathways involved in this process can offer valuable insights into the potential of targeting EVs-mediated gene transfer as a therapeutic strategy to modulate iron homeostasis and prevent tumorigenesis.

Impact of metabolic status on EVs

Previous sections mainly analyzed the modulatory effects of EVs originating from different cells and tissues on various metabolisms. Nevertheless, the production, secretion, and uptake of EVs and their roles are not static. While the metabolic state is subjected to change, EVs are inevitably affected accordingly, which means variation in their own quantity, markers, and the cargos they contain [27]. Since the function of EVs is realized mainly through their inherent properties, alterations in these features directly affect their physiological roles. Though often overlooked, the metabolic state has profound implications for EVs functionality, providing insights into disease progression and highlighting the potential of EVs as diagnostic biomarkers.

Metabolic state of oxidative stress

EVs serve as regulators in redox metabolism, which has been elucidated in previous sections. Conversely, imbalances in redox homeostasis, such as oxidative stress, significantly affect the biogenesis, secretion, and composition of EVs, thereby altering the function of EVs. For example, changes in the redox state of the cell allow oxidative modification of protein thiols, which induce calcium ion influx and subsequently generate pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant EVs and contribute to the development of chronic diseases [165] (Fig. 3). Moreover, oxidative stress results from excessive ROS generation, which damages cellular components, including DNA, proteins, and lipids [166]. ROS refers to molecular oxygen derivatives with oxidizing properties that are produced during cellular metabolism [167] and mainly for the superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and the hydroxyl radical (HO•) [168]. Meanwhile, various intracellular ROS-generating and ROS-eliminating systems work together to maintain the redox state of the cell, thus regulating cellular physiological functions and intercellular signaling [140]. However, the disequilibrium between these two systems leads to oxidative stress, which alters EVs’ biological properties to mitigate or exacerbate the condition.

The release of EVs is modulated by oxidative stress, which affects the redox metabolism of target cells (Fig. 3). For instance, oxidative stress inhibits the lysosome-associated transient receptor potential mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) channel, increasing EVs release due to disrupted lysosome-MVB interactions [169]. Similarly, elevated expression of β-hexosaminidase B (HEXB) under ROS-induced translocation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) induces more excellent stabilization of expressed HEXB proteins and disrupts the degradation process of EVs. This process ultimately leads to an augmented emission of EVs and the enhancement of tumor growth [170]. Moreover, oxidative stress modifies EVs’ cargo, enabling them to carry oxidants, antioxidants, or redox-related enzymes, thus influencing redox homeostasis in target cells either positively or negatively [171]. While these mechanisms require further elucidation, they underscore the dynamic interplay between oxidative stress and EVs-mediated functions.

Metabolic state of aging

Aging is an irreversible progression in which cellular and tissue functions deteriorate and become impaired, which may ultimately culminate in the development of age-related diseases [172]. Cellular senescence, the permanent arrest of the cell cycle, is a property of aging and evokes a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [173]. Furthermore, the alterations of the metabolic state impact cellular senescence and SASP, which in turn provoke metabolism disorders and even metabolic diseases [174]. EVs have been increasingly recognized for their role in modulating the aging-associated metabolic state, while recent studies have also examined how aging alters EVs characteristics.

The metabolic state of aging influences the release of EVs and the cargoes they carry. For instance, EVs obtained from keratinocytes in the skin of elderly individuals were released in significantly more significant quantities compared to younger individuals. At the same time, the abundance of miR-30a was also markedly higher [175]. Likewise, senescent human fat mesenchymal stromal cells in osteoarthritis produce more EVs with approximately half the miRNA-carrying capacity, leading to functional impairments [176]. Further, cellular senescence also alters the surface proteins of EVs, which then interferes with the uptake and function of EVs. For example, EVs from senescent cells are protein-rich in DPP4, which impedes the recruitment of EVs into proliferating cells [177] (Fig. 3). Notwithstanding the fact that the corresponding mechanisms are not well understood, such changed EVs in the metabolic state of aging could serve as a marker of aging and also add to the comprehension of metabolic changes in aging.

Disease pathologies and targeted therapies for metabolism regulated by EVs

EVs influence metabolic processes between cells through their own biological properties, either by directly interfering with necessary metabolic enzymes or metabolites or by affecting the expression of genes implicated in metabolic reactions. In summary, this dual functionality underscores their critical role in progressing and mitigating various diseases. Therefore, investigating the metabolic effects of EVs undoubtedly provides new perspectives for targeted therapy of diseases. This section highlights the key contributions of EVs in regulating metabolism across systemic diseases and explores corresponding therapeutic strategies.

EVs in cancer metabolism

Metabolic reprogramming is an adaptive mechanism for cancer cells to meet their requirements for rapid proliferation. Moreover, EVs transfer molecules with biological functions to receptor cells or undergo receptor-ligand binding via active molecules on membranes, reprogramming the cancer cells’ metabolism and their surrounding stromal cells to influence cancer progression, metastasis, and immunosuppression [178] (Fig. 4). For example, in tumor-macrophage symbiosis, macrophages infiltrating glioblastoma release LDHA-containing EVs that facilitate glycolysis and proliferation of glioblastoma cells [179]. Analogously, metabolite cargoes carried by EVs derived from CAFs strengthen the metabolic flux of tumor cells by providing lactate supply and TCA intermediates glutamine [180]. The indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase-1 (IDO1)-highly expressing ovarian cancer cell-derived EVs directly altered NAD+ levels in endothelial cells by translocating kynurenine, a substance required for NAD+ synthesis [181]. In addition, circTRPS1 carried by EVs derived from bladder cancer cells sponges miR141-3p and thus enhances the downstream GLS1 gene expression, which promotes glutamine metabolism in cancer cells and, hence, the proliferation of cancer cells [182].

Due to their role in the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells, EVs are considered promising targets, which can alternatively serve as markers for cancer monitoring or be designed as nanocarriers for drug delivery in cancer therapy [183]. For instance, polyamine-enriched EVs released by ferroptotic cancer cells increase neighboring cells’ sensitivity to ferroptosis. Cancer cells become hyper-sensitive to chemotherapy and radiotherapy after supplementation with polyamines, combined with the characterization of increased polyamine pools in cancer cells, which provides an exciting target for cancer therapy [184] (Fig. 4). Given their pivotal function in metabolic reprogramming and their ability to influence the tumor microenvironment, EVs are rapidly emerging as a promising tool in cancer diagnostics and therapeutics. The potential to harness EVs as biomarkers for monitoring cancer progression or response to therapy offers a non-invasive approach to cancer detection and personalized treatment strategies.

EVs in metabolic inflammation

Metabolic inflammation, characterized by chronic low-grade activation of the immune system, is a hallmark of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and related metabolic disorders. Increasing evidence indicates that EVs play a central role in this process. While cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a worldwide global health challenge, the involvement of EVs regulating intercellular communication in the physiology and pathophysiology of cells within the cardiovascular system makes them potential candidates. In addition, overexpression of MiR-30 by EVs in circulating cardiovascular cells from diabetic patients facilitates fatty acid β-oxidation and lipid peroxidation and affects microvascular function by synergistically down-regulating eNOS [185]. Meanwhile, in recent studies of atherosclerosis, the role of EVs in regulating cholesterol metabolism in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis has also been observed, and these studies suggest that there may be a deeper mechanism for the regulation of cholesterol metabolism by EVs that needs to be explored [186].

Interestingly, metabolic disorders in liver diseases have also been implicated in regulating EVs. As regulators of intercellular metabolism, EVs induce lipid accumulation, leading to disorders of hepatic lipid metabolism and further contributing to the progression of inflammation and hepatic fibrosis, whereas targeting EVs could constitute a new therapeutic strategy for the treatment of NAFL/NASH [89]. Moreover, in studying the course of acute liver failure caused by the hepatitis E virus, ASS1 was pronouncedly elevated in serum-derived EVs and closely correlated with the progression of liver failure, which may implicate a disturbance of cellular arginine metabolism [187]. Additionally, EVs-targeted therapies have showcased promising approaches for treating liver diseases. With autoimmune hepatitis, CD4+ T cells are activated and cause inflammatory liver injury due to glucose metabolism disturbances. EVs derived from MSCs may transport mitochondria-associated proteins to ameliorate the disease [188]. Preclinical evidence shows that MSC-derived EVs can improve liver function, promote tissue repair, reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhance anti-inflammatory signaling in models of acute and chronic liver injury, including liver fibrosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury [189]. In a model of primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), MSC-EVs reduced liver fibrosis, dampened immune cell infiltration, and suppressed NF-κB activation, highlighting their capacity to modulate inflammation and fibrotic pathways [190].

EVs in metabolic reprogramming during tissue injury and infection

EVs significantly influence bone and muscle diso*rders, mainly impacting cellular metabolism and inflammatory pathways [192]. For example, during osteoblast differentiation, BMSCs-derived EVs carrying miR-150-5p are taken up by ECs to induce intracellular SOX2 overexpression, which shifts cytosolic OXPHOS toward glycolysis and promotes H-type angiogenesis [193]. In addition, EVs derived from human vascular endothelial cells may mitigate inflammatory damage to myocardial tissue under IRI by replenishing deficient substances through the transport of multiple metabolites supporting metabolic activity, which offers multiple therapeutic targets for IRI treatment [191]. Moreover, platelet-derived EVs delivering miR-21/let-7b in patients with COVID-19 enhance NOX activity, elevate the number of ROS, and trigger neutrophil activation [192], highlighting the possibility of EVs as effective targets for therapeutic intervention. Similarly, EVs originating from Candida albicans exacerbate host damage by reducing ROS production by activating arginine and NO metabolic pathways to decrease bacterial death [193]. Unraveling the mechanistic basis of EVs in metabolic inflammation is expected to inform future efforts in developing targeted and personalized therapeutic approaches.

Metabolomics of EVs and their potential for disease diagnosis and therapy

Metabolites are integral to cellular function, participating directly in enzymatic reactions that sustain physiological processes. When cellular metabolism is disturbed, the associated metabolites are affected accordingly, making metabolite analysis a powerful tool to identify the various cellular activities [194]. Therefore, metabolomics refers to the chemical reflection of the relevant characterization of metabolites through different analytical chemistry techniques [195]. In addition, EVs are secreted from donor cells, encapsulating proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites. Given the heterogeneity of EVs and the fact that cargoes from EVs are not simply randomly wrapped but are packaged in a sorting mechanism, which allows for the detection of EVs and thus the analysis of disease onset and progression [196]. In general, by integrating metabolomic analysis of EVs, researchers can gain a deeper understanding of disease pathophysiology and explore innovative approaches for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

As EVs are abundantly present in various human biofluids, including urine, sweat, blood, and tissue fluids, their accessibility makes them a promising non-invasive diagnostic tool. One of the most prominent metabolomic applications of EVs is in diagnosing diseases. For example, Luo et al. performed metabolomic analysis on two subgroups of EVs, large EVs (lEVs) and small EVs (sEVs), isolated by centrifugation from pleural effusions. They observed that the metabolite differences between the tuberculosis group and the tumor group of lEVs were relatively more pronounced and determined four biomarker candidates of pleural lEVs, which also deepened the mechanism of pleural effusion [197]. In therapeutics, EVs are available to participate in the drug delivery process, and the drug as a cargo to be transported through EVs requires proper encapsulation into EVs, and this entails analyzing the composition of EVs by metabolomics for better drug delivery [194]. Beyond diagnosis and treatment, metabolomics applied to EVs also facilitates disease progression and prognosis monitoring. Wiley et al. demonstrated that quantifying biochemical variations in EVs is highly effective for early tumor detection, monitoring cancer progression, and assessing prognosis, highlighting the transformative potential of this approach [198]. In summary, the integration of EVs into metabolomic platforms for diagnostics, therapy, and prognosis represents a significant advancement in precision medicine (Fig. 5).

Engineered modified EVs targeting cellular metabolic pathways

In addition to mediating various metabolic processes, EVs mimic the functions of their donor cells while exhibiting excellent stability and reduced immunogenicity, making them up-and-coming candidates for drug delivery systems. However, the direct utilization of EVs as therapeutic agents faces certain limitations. Engineered EVs address these challenges, offering a more precise and targeted approach to modulating cellular metabolism [199]. For example, following the encapsulation of EVs encapsulating Gstm2 mRNA (EVsGstm2) derived from skin primary fibroblasts, EVsGstm2 was revealed to ameliorate cellular senescence by regulating mitochondrial OXPHOS function [200]. The functional properties of EVs stem from two primary mechanisms, and the engineering modifications of EVs are based on these pathways. Nevertheless, the various biological characteristics of EVs and their biological functions are also regulated by the cells from which they originate, suggesting that modifications to the surface proteins and their inclusions of EVs can also be achieved by intervening in the originating cells [201].

Modifications to EVs cargo

The cargoes carried by EVs are inextricably linked to realizing their functions. Consequently, engineered EVs can be formed by altering or modifying their inclusions to accomplish selective changes in function (Fig. 5). For instance, engineered small EVs loaded with miR-654-5p (miR654-EVs), derived from adipose-derived MSCs, were shown to target Heat Shock Protein Family B (small) Member 1 (HSPB1) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This feature enhanced sorafenib-induced ferroptosis, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of modifying EVs cargo for precise metabolic interventions [202]. Similarly, Zhuang et al. designed metformin-engineered EVs (Met-EVs) for the treatment of aging skin and revealed that Met-EVs caused metabolic reprogramming of senescent cells, attenuation of glycolysis and enhancement of OXPHOS and that this was achieved through the reshape of mitosis which in turn alleviated the mitochondrial damage [203]. In light of mitochondria’s pivotal function in cellular energy production and stress response, the development of EV-based delivery systems for mitochondria or mitochondrial components holds significant promise for advancing treatments in mitochondrial-related disorders and other diseases [204]. By incorporating specific metabolic regulators or therapeutic molecules into the cargo of EVs, the versatility of EVs as drug delivery vehicles can be maximized, offering targeted interventions in various diseases.

Recent advances have highlighted that EVs are not passive carriers but instead undergo selective cargo loading of RNA and proteins through tightly regulated mechanisms. Rational protein engineering has shown that directing proteins to lipid rafts at the plasma membrane can markedly enhance their incorporation into EVs, providing a generalizable platform to increase loading efficiency of diverse macromolecules [205]. Beyond mechanistic insights, selective miRNA packaging has been exploited therapeutically, exemplified by engineered EVs (iEV-150) that utilize Annexin A2 to load miR-150-3p and tumor-targeting surface peptides for melanoma therapy. Functionally, iEV-150 reprograms tumor metabolism by activating YAP signaling, upregulating ferroptosis-related genes, and inducing lipid peroxidation, while simultaneously modulating the immune microenvironment to enhance checkpoint blockade efficacy [206]. Generally speaking, such mechanisms are tightly coupled to metabolic effects: from modulating redox balance and ferroptosis sensitivity to reshaping immune metabolism in the tumor microenvironment. Understanding these selective loading pathways offers new opportunities to design EV-based therapeutics that integrate precise cargo control with targeted metabolic reprogramming.

Modifications to EVs membranes

Biological membranes are part of EVs, making them biocompatible and readily taken up by tissue cells. However, their original membranes lack specificity and are widely present in various organs. Targeted modification of EVs membranes offers an innovative approach to precisely regulating cellular metabolic patterns [207] (Fig. 5). For example, lentiviral transfection of MSCs, which was employed to engineer CD38 antigen receptor membrane-modified MSC-derived EVs (CD38-ARM-MSC-EVs) carrying fusion plasmids encoding the CD38 antigen receptor and a CD8 transmembrane fragment, enabled the targeted delivery of these EVs to alveolar epithelial type 2 cells (AEC2), where they restored intracellular NAD⁺ levels and attenuated lung fibrosis [204]. Likewise, engineering EVs to incorporate catalase within their membranes enabled targeted modulation of tumor cell redox homeostasis, inducing DNA damage and enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy [208]. The membrane modifications of EVs offer a novel and dynamic approach to fine-tuning their targeting capabilities, ensuring the precise delivery of therapeutic cargo and expanding their potential in precision medicine.

Advanced therapeutic applications

Engineered EVs have been developed to deliver CRISPR/Cas components, enabling genome editing of metabolic regulators and correcting metabolic dysfunctions. For example, BMSC-derived EVs loaded with Cas9 and sgRNA targeting KCNJ2 successfully disrupted the KCNJ2–HIF-1α feedback loop, thereby reprogramming glycolytic metabolism and attenuating osteosarcoma progression [209]. To improve the efficiency of selective cargo incorporation, multifunctional EV platforms have been engineered for glioblastoma therapy, in which surface expression of PD-1 and angiopep-2 rejuvenated exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and enhanced brain targeting, while internal delivery of Cas9/sgRNAs achieved efficient multisite gene editing, leading to apoptosis and anti-angiogenic effects [210]. Collectively, these advances highlight that engineered EVs not only enable selective loading of CRISPR components but also exert profound metabolic and immunomodulatory effects.

Moreover, engineered EVs serving as delivery platforms for therapeutic miRNAs are emerging as versatile tools across cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and inflammatory conditions. Studies have shown that stem cell-derived EVs carrying miR-1 and miR-199a can regulate apoptosis, inflammation in cardiac fibroblasts while inducing the expression of cardiomyogenic genes and ion channels, thereby promoting myocardial regeneration and functional recovery [211]. In acute lung injury (ALI) models, EVs engineered with a RAGE antagonist peptide and loaded with anti-miR-155 oligonucleotides efficiently inhibit pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and reduce cytokine levels, enabling precise modulation of pulmonary inflammation [212]. In summary, engineered EVs carrying therapeutic miRNAs can modulate key metabolic pathways, enabling targeted metabolic reprogramming and broad therapeutic effects.

Novel approaches exploit engineered EVs to deliver ferroptosis-modulating agents or to regulate iron metabolism, highlighting their potential as cancer and tissue-repair therapeutics. In oral squamous cell carcinoma, TDN-modified M1 macrophage-derived EVs (TDN@EVs) efficiently delivered Hsc70 to tumor cells, triggering GPX4 degradation, mitochondrial stress, and DNA damage, thereby inducing ferroptosis and suppressing tumor proliferation and migration [213]. Similarly, silk fibroin/collagen hydrogel carriers loaded with NGR-targeted EVs containing MFGE8 (NGR-NPs@MFGE8) prevented mitochondrial autophagy-induced ferroptosis in vascular endothelial cells, accelerated vascular repair, and enhanced wound closure in vivo [214]. Together, these strategies illustrate the versatility of engineered EVs as platforms for precise ferroptosis induction or iron metabolism modulation in both oncology and regenerative medicine.

Conclusion and perspective

EVs regulate intercellular metabolism through the transport of cargoes or functional proteins on the membrane, which is of profound significance for the targeted treatment of metabolic disorders. This review has highlighted the mechanisms by which EVs contribute to metabolic regulation, demonstrating their pivotal role in advancing disease diagnosis and treatment and underscoring their promise as a novel approach for targeted metabolic therapy.

Despite substantial progress in understanding the therapeutic potential of EVs, the mechanisms underlying their regulation of intercellular metabolism remain incompletely elucidated. For instance, while numerous studies have shown that EVs mediate specific metabolic pathways, the heterogeneity in EVs cargo sorting and their effects on recipient cells necessitates further investigation. Moreover, challenges such as achieving high-purity EVs isolation, ensuring precise targeting, and mitigating the potential adverse effects of EVs on healthy individuals remain critical hurdles to overcome. Fang et al. proposed a novel magnetic bead-mediated selective adsorption strategy for EVs separation, facilitating their use in disease diagnosis and clinical treatment [215].

Significant efforts must focus on engineering EVs to overcome their limitations as nanocarriers and achieve widespread clinical applications. Approaches such as bio-orthogonal click chemistry, surface molecular modification, and nucleic acid loading have shown promise in enhancing EV functionality, as demonstrated in the treatment of osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and neurodegenerative diseases [216]. Additionally, optimizing large-scale production techniques and establishing standardized protocols are essential for clinical translation.

Looking forward, research exploring the mechanisms of EV-mediated metabolic regulation and the development of advanced engineering technologies to achieve more precise targeting and stable drug delivery will usher in a new era for targeted therapies. Furthermore, as EV research progresses, addressing critical questions such as the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and long-term safety of EV-based treatments will provide the foundation for their integration into clinical practice. With these advancements, EVs are poised to play a transformative role in personalized medicine and multimodal therapeutic strategies.
