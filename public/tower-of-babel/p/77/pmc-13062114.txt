LC–MS-based metabolomic characterization and POM pharmacophore analysis of the bioactive methanolic extract of Ephedra alata
Authors: Eman Ramadan Elsharkawy, Salah Neghmouche Nacer, Taibi Ben Hadda, Faisal A Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEphedra alata is a medicinal plant known for its rich composition of bioactive compounds and therapeutic properties. This study explores its phytochemical profile, antioxidant capacity, and potential anticancer effects. LC-MS analysis identified a variety of flavonoids and glycosylated derivatives, with quercetin 3-D-galactoside (28.49%) and 3,3’,4,4’,5,7-pentahydroxy-6-methoxyflavanonol-3-O-glucoside-5-O-glucuronic acid (10.74%) as the most abundant compounds. These flavonoids are recognized for their strong antioxidant and anticancer properties. The extract exhibited significant metal chelation activity, assessed using an EDTA-based calibration curve, indicating its potential to combat oxidative stress. Additionally, the ORAC assay confirmed a high free radical-scavenging ability, primarily linked to the presence of flavonoids such as quercetin, rutin, and kaempferol.To evaluate its anticancer potential, the methanolic extract was tested on liver cancer cell lines (Huh-7 and HepG2) using a WST-1 assay. The results showed a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability, with HepG2 cells displaying greater sensitivity than Huh-7 cells. Cytotoxicity extract’s IC₅₀ values exceeded 100 µg/mL, indicating weak, cytotoxicity, the findings suggest that its bioactive compounds may play a role in inducing apoptosis and regulating oxidative stress in cancer cells. These results highlight Ephedra alata as a promising natural source of bioactive compounds with antioxidant potential, supporting further studies to explore its therapeutic applications.Keywords: Ephedra alata, Antioxidant, Anticancer evaluation, POM (Petra/Osiris/Molinspiration) analyses, Pharmacophore sites identificationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemical biology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Medicinal plants serve as reservoirs of bioactive compounds with significant pharmacological potential, including antioxidant and cytotoxic activities1–3. Among these, Ephedra alata, a species of the Ephedraceae family, has garnered increasing interest due to its diverse secondary metabolites and potential therapeutic applications4. Widely distributed in arid and semi-arid regions, including Northern Saudi Arabia, E. alata has been traditionally employed in the treatment of respiratory ailments, inflammation, and metabolic disorders5,6. However, despite its ethnopharmacological significance, comprehensive investigations into its phytochemical composition and biological activities remain limited.

Advancements in liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) have revolutionized the identification and quantification of plant-derived bioactive metabolites with high sensitivity and specificity7. This technique is particularly valuable for profiling extracts rich in alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols, and other phytochemicals that contribute to their pharmacological properties8. Given the complex phytochemical nature of E. alata, LC-MS-based analyses are essential for elucidating its chemical composition and biological relevance.

Oxidative stress, resulting from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and the antioxidant defense system, is implicated in the pathogenesis of several chronic diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular conditions9,10. Natural antioxidants derived from medicinal plants have emerged as promising agents for mitigating oxidative damage and preventing disease progression11. Given the historical use of Ephedra species in traditional medicine, investigating the antioxidant potential of E. alata is critical to understanding its protective effects against oxidative stress-induced cellular damage12.

The cytotoxic activity of plant-derived compounds is fundamental in the search for novel anticancer therapeutics. Bioactive constituents from medicinal plants have been shown to induce apoptosis, disrupt the cell cycle, and inhibit cancer cell proliferation13. While certain Ephedra species have demonstrated cytotoxic effects14, systematic evaluations of E. alata against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines remain scarce. Given that HCC is one of the most aggressive and prevalent malignancies globally, identifying natural compounds with potential anticancer properties is of paramount importance15.

This study aims to characterize the phytochemical composition of E. alata methanolic extract collected from Northern Saudi Arabia using LC-MS. Furthermore, we assess its antioxidant activity through various in vitro assays and evaluate its cytotoxic effects against HepG2 and Huh-7 hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines.

Materials and methods

Plant material and extraction

Fresh aerial parts of Ephedra alata were gathered from Wadi Abalkour, approximately 50 km east of Arar city in Saudi Arabia’s Northern Border Region. The collection took place in March 2019, coinciding with the plant’s flowering period. Botanical identification was performed by the Botany Department, by Dr. Suliman Mohammed Alghanem, and a voucher specimen (Sa wa 19 4-E) was preserved at the Chemistry Department, Faculty of Science, Northern Border University.

The collection and experimental use of Ephedra alata adhered to institutional, national, and international guidelines for research on plant species. No endangered or protected species were involved, and all procedures were conducted under strict ethical and scientific standards.

To retain its phytochemical properties, the plant material was shade-dried before being finely ground. A methanolic extract was prepared using maceration, where 50 g of the powdered material was immersed in 500 mL of methanol and left at room temperature for 72 h with intermittent stirring. The extract was then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator set at 40 °C. The obtained crude extract was stored at 4 °C for subsequent phytochemical characterization and biological evaluations16,17.

LC-MS analysis of Ephedra alata methanolic extract

The methanolic extract of Ephedra alata was analyzed for its phytochemical composition using a Shimadzu LC-MS-2020 system. This system was equipped with an online degasser (DGU-20A3R), an autosampler (SIL-20AXR), a column heater (CTO-20AC), and a diode array detector (SPD-M20A). The separation of compounds was performed using a C18 analytical column (150 mm × 3 mm, 3 μm particle size). The mobile phase was composed of solvent A (water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (methanol with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient elution program was designed as follows: the initial composition of 90% A and 10% B was maintained for 45 min, followed by a shift to 100% B from 45 to 55 min, and finally re-equilibration to 90% A and 10% B from 55.1 to 60 min. The system operated at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Compounds were detected using a photodiode array (PDA) detector, primarily at 350 nm, with additional scanning at 250 nm. Each sample injection volume was set to 20 µL, and peak identification was performed by comparing retention times and UV spectral patterns with those of authenticated reference standards.

For mass spectrometric analysis, the extract was examined using electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative mode to enhance the detection of phenolic and other bioactive compounds. The instrument was configured with a mass range detection of m/z 50–1500, a nebulizing gas pressure of 40 psi, a nebulizing gas flow rate of 1.5 L/min, a drying gas flow rate of 12 L/min, and a drying gas temperature of 400 °C. The [M-H] mode was applied to facilitate the detection and identification of key secondary metabolites. Mass spectra were interpreted through comparison with available spectral databases and verified reference compounds to ensure accurate structural identification18. All mass spectra were interpreted through comparison with available spectral databases and verified reference compounds to ensure accurate structural identification, in strict compliance with methodological standards.

Total antioxidant activity

Iron metal chelation assay

The ability of the methanolic extract of Ephedra alata to chelate iron ions was assessed using the Ferrozine-based iron chelation assay, adapted for a microplate format. A stock solution of Trolox (0.1 mM) was prepared in methanol, and serial dilutions were performed to obtain final concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 50 µM. The plant extract was dissolved in water at a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL19.

For the assay, 20 µL of freshly prepared ferrous sulfate solution (0.3 mM) was combined with 50 µL of either the plant extract or the Trolox standard in a 96-well microplate, with each condition tested in six replicates (n = 6). The reaction was initiated by adding 30 µL of ferrozine solution (0.8 mM) to each well. After an incubation period of 10 min at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a FluoStar Omega microplate reader. Oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay

The antioxidant capacity of the methanolic extract of E. alata was assessed using the Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC) assay, which quantifies the extract’s ability to neutralize peroxyl radicals produced by 2,2′-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH). The experiment was performed using a 96-well black microplate, with fluorescein (70 nM) serving as a fluorescent probe and Trolox (0–100 µM) as the reference antioxidant standard.

For the assay, 25 µL of the plant extract or Trolox standard was combined with 150 µL of fluorescein solution in each well and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 25 µL of freshly prepared AAPH solution (240 mM), and the fluorescence intensity was recorded at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/528 nm at one-minute intervals for a total duration of 90 min using a FluoStar Omega microplate reader20–23.

The antioxidant capacity of the extract was quantified by determining the area under the fluorescence decay curve (AUC) and expressing the results as Trolox equivalents (TE) in µmol TE per gram of extract. A calibration curve was generated for Trolox, following the equation (Y = 972.0942X + 111722.2165, R² = 0.9896), confirming the reliability of the assay.

Cytotoxic activity evaluation

The cytotoxic potential of the methanolic extract of E. alata was evaluated using the WST-1 assay, (Huh-7: Liver Cancer was obtained from Nawah Scientific Inc., (Mokatam, Cairo, Egypt)).

which determines cell viability by assessing mitochondrial metabolic activity. Huh-7 liver cancer cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well in a 96-well plate. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. They were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow proper attachment24.

After the initial incubation, cells were treated with varying concentrations of the methanolic extract and maintained for an additional 48 h. Following the treatment, 10 µL of WST-1 reagent was introduced into each well, and the plate was incubated for another hour. Absorbance readings were recorded at 450 nm using a BMG LABTECH® FLUOstar Omega microplate reader to determine cell viability. The cytotoxic effect of the extract was calculated as a percentage relative to the untreated control, and IC₅₀ values were derived from the dose-response curve.

Statistical analysis

The data collected in this study were systematically organized and analyzed using ANOVA in SPSS software. All experiments, except for the chemical compound analysis, were conducted in triplicate to enhance result reliability and accuracy.

Results and discussion

LC-MS analysis

The LC-MS analysis of the methanolic extract of E. alata revealed a diverse array of flavonoids and glycosylated derivatives, The compounds were identified and characteristic by comparing the precursor, molecular ion and fragment obtained from LC-MS/MS data with the literature data25–27.

Many of identified compounds are known for their potent pharmacological effects, particularly their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities. Among the most abundant compounds identified were quercetin 3-D-galactoside (28.487%) and 3,3/,4,4/,5,7-pentahydroxy-6-methoxyflavanonol-3-O-glucoside-5-O-glucuronic acid (10.74%). Quercetin and its derivatives have been widely studied for their ability to induce apoptosis, inhibit tumor proliferation, and modulate key cancer-related pathways, such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK28. Additionally, kaempferol (5.29%) has been reported to suppress angiogenesis and metastasis in multiple cancer models, further reinforcing the extract’s potential anticancer properties28. The extract also contained naringin (0.38%) and naringenin 5,7-di-O-glucoside (0.37%), both of which have demonstrated significant anticancer potential. Naringin has been found to suppress tumor progression by modulating apoptosis-related proteins, particularly in colorectal and breast cancer models29,30. Its aglycone form, naringenin, enhances apoptosis by activating caspases and inhibiting NF-κB signaling, a key pathway involved in tumor progression31. Additionally, myricetin-3-glucoside (0.33%) and isorhamnetin 3-O-rutinoside (0.78%) further contribute to the extract’s anticancer effects. Myricetin has been shown to induce oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis in various cancer cell lines, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma and leukemia32. Isorhamnetin, on the other hand, plays a crucial role in suppressing metastasis and cancer cell migration by targeting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes33–36.

Furthermore, the presence of chrysoeriol-7-O-rutinoside and apigenin 7-rutinoside highlights the extract’s pharmacological relevance. Apigenin, a well-known flavone, exhibits potent anticancer activity against breast, lung, and prostate cancers by inhibiting oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/Akt, STAT3, and NF-κB37. Notably, apigenin has been investigated for its ability to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy and reduce drug resistance by modulating multidrug resistance proteins38. Similarly, chrysoeriol has demonstrated anticancer effects by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting cancer cell proliferation39. These findings suggest that E. alata is a promising natural source of bioactive compounds with potential applications in anticancer therapy.

The results demonstrate a dose-dependent increase in metal chelation capacity, with inhibition values ranging from 6.25% at 2.5 µM to 82.22% at 50 µM. A strong linear relationship was observed between EDTA concentration and metal chelation activity, indicative of a direct correlation between chelator availability and its capacity to sequester metal ions. This linearity is crucial, as it enables accurate quantification of the chelating power of the tested samples by interpolating their activity within the established range.

The mechanism of metal chelation by EDTA involves the formation of stable coordination complexes with divalent and trivalent metal ions, particularly Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺. EDTA, a well-known hexadentate ligand, effectively binds metal ions through its carboxyl and amine groups, reducing their availability for catalyzing oxidative reactions. The high correlation between EDTA concentration and inhibition percentage suggests that a similar mechanism might be at play in the tested samples, wherein bioactive compounds—such as flavonoids and phenolic acids—contribute to chelation by donating electron pairs to metal ions40.

This calibration curve serves as a reference for evaluating the chelating activity of plant extracts or synthetic compounds. A sample’s EDTA equivalent can be calculated using the linear regression equation derived from this data, allowing for standardization and comparison across different studies. Given that excessive metal accumulation has been implicated in oxidative stress-related diseases—including Alzheimer’s, cardiovascular disorders, and cancer—the ability of natural compounds to mimic EDTA in chelation assays underscores their potential therapeutic relevance41.

The Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC) assay is a widely used method for evaluating the antioxidant potential of plant extracts. It measures the ability of compounds to scavenge peroxyl radicals, which are highly reactive species contributing to oxidative stress and cellular damage. In this study, the ORAC values of the methanolic extract of E. alata were expressed as Trolox equivalents (µM TE/mg sample), using a calibration curve derived from a linear dose-inhibition response (Table 3). The calibration curve showed a strong correlation between Trolox concentration and relative fluorescence unit (RFU), ensuring accuracy in quantifying antioxidant capacity. Compared to other antioxidant assays such as DPPH and ABTS, the ORAC assay uniquely measures the time-dependent antioxidant response, providing more comprehensive data on radical scavenging capacity. Flavonoids such as quercetin, rutin, and kaempferol, identified in E. alata (Table 1, chemical composition data), contribute significantly to the ORAC values due to their hydroxyl groups, which enhance radical neutralization. These compounds have been reported to inhibit cancer cell proliferation through mechanisms such as oxidative DNA damage prevention, apoptosis induction, and modulation of redox signaling pathways42,43. The presence of these polyphenolic compounds in E. alata suggests potential anticancer applications, reinforcing the importance of natural antioxidants in disease prevention.

Cell viability

Ant proliferative of Huh-7 and HepG2 cells

The present investigation assessed the antiproliferative efficacy of Ephedra methanolic extract against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines, Huh-7 and HepG2, using a WST-1 assay. The findings revealed a dose-dependent decline in cell viability, with HepG2 cells exhibiting greater susceptibility than Huh-7 cells at the highest tested concentration (100 µg/mL). Despite this cytotoxic effect, the extract demonstrated IC₅₀ values exceeding 100 µg/mL, indicating week activity its overall antiproliferative potential remains limited in the assessed conditions.

These results align with previous reports on the cytotoxic properties of Ephedra species, which have been attributed to their rich secondary metabolite composition, particularly alkaloids, flavonoids, and polyphenolic compounds44. The observed cytotoxicity may be linked to apoptosis induction, oxidative stress modulation, and disruption of cell cycle progression, mechanisms commonly associated with plant-derived bioactive compounds45.

The data presented in Table 4 illustrate a concentration-dependent decline in cell viability following exposure to Ephedra methanolic extract. Notably, HepG2 cells exhibited a more pronounced reduction in viability (77.47%) compared to Huh-7 cells (85.65%) at 100 µg/mL, suggesting intrinsic differences in cellular sensitivity. This disparity may stem from variations in metabolic activity, drug efflux mechanisms, or differential expression of apoptotic regulators within these cell lines46.

The relatively higher resistance of Huh-7 cells to the extract may be due to differences in p53 status, mitochondrial integrity, or intracellular antioxidant defenses47. Previous studies have suggested that the mutational landscape of HCC cells influences their response to phytochemicals, with TP53-mutant cell lines (e.g., HepG2) often displaying increased susceptibility to oxidative stress-induced apoptosis48.

Plant-derived bioactive compounds exert cytotoxicity through multiple biochemical pathways, including oxidative stress induction, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cell cycle arrest49. In this study, the dose-dependent reduction in viability strongly suggests an apoptotic mechanism, although further validation via caspase activation assays, mitochondrial membrane potential measurements, and flow cytometry-based apoptosis detection would be necessary50. It is well established that polyphenolic compounds and alkaloids in Ephedra exhibit anticancer activity by modulating key molecular targets such as Bcl-2/Bax, p53, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK signaling cascades51. Studies have demonstrated that flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol—compounds frequently detected in Ephedra extracts—can inhibit cell proliferation and induce apoptosis by interfering with survival pathways and promoting oxidative stress52. Moreover, given that ROS-mediated apoptosis is a fundamental mechanism of many plant-derived anticancer agents, it is plausible that the Ephedra methanolic extract exerts its effects via oxidative stress enhancement, leading to DNA damage and apoptotic cascade activation53. Future investigations should incorporate reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification assays and Western blot analyses of apoptotic markers to elucidate this mechanism further. While the antiproliferative activity observed in this study is moderate, it remains comparable to several other plant-derived extracts that exhibit IC₅₀ values in the micromolar range54. However, when compared to clinically established cytotoxic agents such as doxorubicin or paclitaxel, which exert potent effects at nanomolar concentrations, the observed cytotoxicity is relatively weaker55.

Several reports have indicated that phytochemical extracts may act synergistically with conventional chemotherapeutic agents, enhancing their efficacy while mitigating dose-dependent toxicity56. This suggests that Ephedra extracts could be explored as adjunct therapeutic agents in combination with standard chemotherapeutics for improved therapeutic outcomes in HCC (Fig. 1).

Conclusion

Flavonoids have sparked significant study interest due to their varied spectrum of biological activities, which include antioxidant effects, modification of enzyme function, reduction of cellular growth, and therapeutic potential. The objectives of this research were to outline the pertinent features of flavonoids, including their pharmacological characteristics, several recognized groups, and likely methods of action, as well as to investigate the compounds’ purportedly positive health impacts. This study focused on bioactive natural compounds that were detected by LC-MS in E. alata methanol extract.
