Long-term occupational outcomes in major depressive disorder: a 10-year follow-up study of symptom and comorbidity predictors
Authors: Ching-I Hung, Kuan-Yi Wu, Le-Yung Wang, Ching-Hui Yang, Mei-Chu Tsia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNo study has simultaneously investigated the associations of anxiety comorbidities, persistent depressive disorder (PDD), depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms at baseline with the total and longest durations of paid employment (DPE) as well as the total number of reasons for occupational impairment (OI) over a 10-year period among patients with major depressive disorder (MDD). The study aimed to investigate these issues.MethodsAt baseline, 290 subjects with MDD were enrolled. MDD and anxiety comorbidities were confirmed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR. The Depression and Somatic Symptoms Scale and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale were employed. At the 10-year follow-up point, 113 subjects were investigated. The total and longest DPE were clarified and recorded. A 28-item list was used to investigate the subjective reasons for OI. Multiple linear regression models were used for statistical analysis.ResultsFor the total and longest DPE, 14.2% and 40.7% of the subjects were less than five years, respectively. An increase of one anxiety comorbidity in subjects was associated with a decrease of 4.6 months in the total DPE compared to those without. Subjects with greater somatic symptoms, PDD, and the female gender at baseline reported more reasons for OI. Fatigue, somatic discomfort, decreased motivation, and sensitivity to comments were common reasons for OI.ConclusionsIn the treatment of depression, clinicians should pay more attention to the above treatable factors, including anxiety comorbidities, PDD, fatigue, decreased motivation, and somatic symptoms, to improve OI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07504-w.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, Somatization, Vocation, Outcome

Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability and occupational impairment (OI) in the general population [1]. Most of the burden of MDD comes from OI [2]. Employment rates decrease both one year before and two years after the diagnosis of depression [3]. One study reported that reduced earnings continued for ten years after experiencing a major depressive episode (MDE) [4].

Depression and occupation interact, and some factors related to occupation might increase the risks of depression and anxiety [5]. Involuntary employment exit, job insecurity, repeated job strain, and low reward are important factors for predicting MDD [6–9]. Patients with MDD employed in high-middle occupations may have a poorer prognosis after treatment [1]. Unemployment was found to be associated with a poor treatment prognosis of depression [10]. Occupational and cognitive-behavioral therapies might improve depression symptomology [11, 12]. Therefore, investigations of factors related to OI should be important among patients with MDD.

Several studies have investigated factors related to OI or sick leave among patients with depression, including perceived stressful work, inability to enjoy work, low resilience to work adversities, older age, low educational level, a history of childhood abuse, duration of a current MDE, comorbidity, receipt of social assistance, greater depression and anxiety severities, and low job control [2, 13–17]. Some of these studies were cross-sectional. Long-term follow-up studies are rare [4, 15–17]. One follow-up study found that one-fifth of patients with depression were granted a disability pension within five years [15]. Another five-year study reported that depressive patients with labor-based jobs at baseline spent one-third of the follow-up duration off work [16]. One 11-year follow-up study reported that 15.7% of patients with depression had been granted a disability pension [17]. Most of these studies used disability pensions as the index of OI.

Previous studies have reported that anxiety comorbidities, persistent depressive disorder (PPD), and somatic symptoms have negative impacts on the prognosis of MDD [18–23]. Most previous studies related to depression and occupational functioning were conducted in the general population or enrolled subjects with depression [1, 2, 4, 8–10, 13–17]. Rare studies simultaneously considered the impacts of anxiety and depression [5, 6]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has simultaneously investigated the associations of anxiety comorbidities, PPD, and the severities of depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms at baseline with the DPE among patients with MDD over a 10-year follow-up period. The duration of employment should be an important index for occupational functioning. This study used the total and longest durations of paid employment (DPE) to quantify occupational functioning over a 10-year follow-up period. The longest DPE was defined as the longest duration of a single PE over the past 10 years. The total DPE was calculated by adding the duration of every PE over the past 10 years. Moreover, the total number of reasons (TNR) for OI was also used as an index because a higher TNR might indicate that subjects encountered more difficulties and experienced greater OI.

Investigations of factors related to OI and the DPE might be helpful for occupational outcomes among patients with MDD because some factors associated with OI might be treatable, such as depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms. Therefore, the study aimed to investigate the associations of anxiety comorbidities, PPD, and the severities of depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms at baseline with the total and longest DPE and the TNR for OI over a 10-year follow-up period among patients with MDD. We hypothesized that anxiety comorbidities, worse depression, and more somatic symptoms at baseline were associated with a reduced DPE and more TNR for OI at the 10-year follow-up point among patients with MDD based on previous studies [18–23].

Methods

Subjects

At baseline, the study was performed from January 2004 to August 2007 in the psychiatric outpatient clinics of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital at Linkou, a medical center in northern Taiwan. Consecutive outpatients aged 18 to 65 years were considered eligible participants if they (1) fulfilled the criteria of MDD based on the DSM-IV-text revision (TR) and were experiencing a current MDE and (2) had not taken antidepressants or other psychotropic medications over the past four weeks. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR Axis I Disorders (SCID) was used [24] to confirm the diagnoses of MDD, MDE, and anxiety disorders. To avoid confounding depressive symptoms, three exclusion criteria were established: (1) a history of substance dependence or abuse without full remission in the past four weeks; (2) severe psychomotor retardation, psychotic symptoms, or catatonic features; and (3) chronic medical diseases such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and other diseases.

At baseline, 290 subjects with MDD were enrolled (Fig. 1). The subjects were followed up at the 10-year point (from August 2014 to December 2016). This study was an observational study. Psychiatric pharmacotherapy was not controlled over the 10-year period. Therefore, subjects might intermittently accept or quit pharmacotherapy as they chose. The total duration of psychiatric pharmacotherapy, both in our hospital and other hospitals, over the past 10 years was recorded at the 10-year follow-up point.

A flowchart for the enrollment of subjects with major depressive disorder

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants based on the guidelines regulated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnoses of anxiety disorders and PDD

At baseline, the SCID was used to diagnose the lifetime history of six anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, agoraphobia, specific phobia, social phobia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder [25]. Because of difficulty in making a reliable diagnosis, the SCID allows a diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder only if it is currently present (i.e., within the past six months) [24]. The investigators were blind to other data in making the diagnoses. In the statistical analysis, the total number of anxiety disorders was calculated for each subject.

At baseline, certified psychiatrists clarified the longitudinal course of depression. The duration of this MDE was recorded. Subjects with a course of depression of more than two years were classified into three types: current MDE superimposed on antecedent dysthymic disorder; persistent MDE for more than two years; and current MDE with previous intermittent MDE without full remission for more than two years. Based on the DSM-V [26], the three types were categorized as PDD.

Evaluation of depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms

The Depression and Somatic Symptoms Scale (DSSS), which is a self-administered and free scale, simultaneously evaluates depression and somatic severity [27]. The reliability and validity of the Chinese, English, and Korean versions of the DSSS have been established [28]. The DSSS includes a 12-item depression subscale (DS) and a 10-item somatic subscale (SS). The DS score is correlated with the scores of the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, and Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale [28]. The SS includes five pain and five non-pain somatic symptoms, and the SS score is correlated with the scores of the physical subscales of the Short-Form 36 and the somatization subscale of the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised [28].

The anxiety subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A), which includes 7 items for anxiety symptoms, was used to evaluate the severity of anxiety [28, 29]. The HADS-A can evaluate anxiety without being confounded by somatic symptoms because it does not include any somatic symptoms.

A higher score on the three subscales represented a greater severity. The scores ranged from 0 to 36, 0–30 and 0–21 for the DS, SS, and HADS-A, respectively.

Investigation of the reasons for OI and the duration of employment

A 28-item list of reasons for OI was established to investigate the reasons for OI. To draw up this 28-item list, a preliminary study was performed in which 62 patients with MDD (20 men and 42 women) in psychiatric clinics were requested to freely report any reasons related to their OI. Moreover, previous studies associated with OI were reviewed [15, 30, 31]. Common reasons for OI reported from patients with MDD in the preliminary study and previous studies as well as criteria of MDD in the DSM-IV-TR were selected to form the 28-item list (Table 2). Subjects were told that they could choose any reason (one or more) related to OI at the top of the sheet. Based on the forced-choice method, subjects were requested to choose one of the above reasons for OI as the major reason for OI at the end of the sheet. The 28-item list was completed by subjects at the 10-year follow-up point.

At follow-up, the subjects were requested to report the duration of every PE over the 10-year period. Then, investigators interviewed the subjects and clarified the self-reported duration of every PE and other details related to these PEs to avoid memory bias. The total and longest DPE were calculated and recorded. The TNR for OI in the 28-item list was calculated. The total DPE, longest DPE, and TNR for OI were considered the three indices in this study.

The study aimed to investigate factors related to the duration of PE. Some subjects might not have needed to work for the following reasons: (1) subjects were retired or students; (2) the major tasks of subjects in their daily life were to care for families and do housework; and (3) subjects had good financial support from families. If subjects reported that it was not necessary for them to work and their reasons were confirmed by investigators, they were excluded from the analysis because they might not truly understand their difficulties or impairments in occupations.

Statistical methods

SPSS for Windows 20.0 was used for statistical analysis. Statistical methods were used in appropriate situations, including independent and paired t tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, sign tests, McNemar tests, chi-square tests, and Pearson’s correlations. A principal-axis factor analysis with varimax rotation was used to investigate which reasons for OI were clustered together.

Multiple linear regressions with the forward method were used to test the associations of the three indices with clinical variables at baseline. The dependent variables in the regression models were the total DPE, longest DPE and TNR for OI. Twelve variables at baseline were put into the regression models as independent variables, including age, gender, marital status, PE or not, duration of education, duration of this MDE, age at the first MDE, number of anxiety disorders, PDD or not, DS score, SS score, and HADS-A score.

PE at baseline might be a strong factor in predicting the duration of PE at follow-up and might conceal other important factors related to the duration of PE. Therefore, the independent variables in the second regression model included the same variables at baseline in the first regression model, except for PE at baseline.

A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Subjects

Two-hundred and ninety subjects with MDD in a current MDE were enrolled at baseline. At the 10-year follow-up point, 153 (52.8%) subjects did not participate in the follow-up for the following reasons: 99 (34.1%) could not be contacted by mail or phone; 49 (16.9%) refused to attend the follow-up program; 3 (1.0%) had expired due to medical diseases; and 2 (0.7%) had a diagnosis that was transformed to schizophrenia. Among the 137 subjects attending the follow-up, data from 24 subjects were excluded because they reported that they did not require PE. In the subsequent analysis, data from 113 subjects were analyzed. Among the 113 subjects, 15 (13.3%) had panic disorder, 14 (12.4%) had agoraphobia, 27 (23.9%) had specific phobia, 29 (25.7%) had social phobia, 16 (14.2%) had obsessive–compulsive disorder, 12 (10.6%) had post-traumatic stress disorder, and 7 (6.2%) had generalized anxiety disorder.

Table 1 shows the five demographic variables, the number of anxiety disorders, the percentage of PDD, and psychometric scores at baseline and follow-up. There were no significant differences in the five demographic variables, psychometric scores, or other clinical variables at baseline between the subjects included (n = 113) and excluded (n = 177) in further analysis, except for the HADS-A score at baseline (p = 0.01). Compared with the DS, SS, and HADS-A scores at baseline, the three psychometric scores at follow-up had significantly decreased (p < 0.001). The percentage of PE (85.0%) at follow-up had significantly increased compared with that (61.1%) at baseline (p < 0.001).

Subjective reasons for OI among patients with MDD

Table 2 shows the reasons for OI. For subjects who reported any reason for OI, fatigue (80.5%) was the most common reason, followed by somatic discomfort (67.3%), lack of motivation (66.4%), care for colleagues’ comments (66.4%), inability to concentrate (62.8%), and insomnia (61.9%). For the major reason for OI, fatigue (15.9%) and somatic discomfort (15.9%) were the most common reasons, followed by insomnia (8.8%), decreased memory (8.0%), lack of motivation (7.1%), and inability to concentrate (6.2%).

The factor analysis found that several reasons for OI were clustered together. Factors 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 were associated with anxiety and difficulties in interpersonal relationships, cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms, hypersomnia, and somatic symptoms, respectively (Table 2 and Supplement Table 2).

Discussion

This study found that anxiety comorbidities were associated with a reduced total DPE. Several studies reported that anxiety comorbidities were associated with a poor prognosis among patients with MDD [20, 21, 32]. One study reported that subjects with both depression and anxiety rated by scales have a poorer work performance and more sickness absence compared to all other groups [33]. Patients with panic disorder might suffer from panic attacks, which can suddenly trigger severe somatic discomfort [25]. Additionally, social phobia might increase difficulties in interpersonal relationships [25]. Subjects with PDD were associated with increased reasons for OI. In fact, MDD with PDD at baseline was found to be associated with a poor long-term prognosis of depression [22, 34]. Previous studies reported that subjects with a history of PDD in adolescence had a lower income across early to middle adulthood compared with peers without depression [35], and the duration of a depressive episode was an important factor related to long-term disability among patients with depression [16].

Subjects with greater somatic symptoms reported more subjective reasons for OI. Somatic discomfort was the second most common reason for OI (Table 1). One study reported that a greater somatic severity was associated with prolonged sickness absence, health-related job loss, and enduring disabilities [31]. Moreover, MDD patients without employment have greater somatic symptoms than those with employment [14]. Fatigue and somatic discomfort were more common than depressed mood and cognitive impairment in this study. Fatigue was the most common reason for OI in the study. It occurred in over 90% of patients with MDD and was a common residual symptom of MDD [36, 37]. Previous studies reported that fatigue was associated with OI in MDD [38] and was more predictive of OI than other depressive symptoms [30].

The regression models demonstrated that having a PE at baseline was the most important factor associated with increased total and longest DPE. Previous studies reported that unemployment was associated with a poor prognosis of depression [10, 39, 40]. MDD patients with employment showed a significant functional improvement in mobility, cognition, and participation over a three-year period [40]. One study reported that a good history of working was a significant predictor of employment at a three-month outcome among patients with severe mental illnesses [41]. Subjects with a married status were associated with an increased longest DPE. Marital status is associated with depressive severity [42, 43]. Moreover, a married status at baseline might imply a better functional status because an unmarried status was found to be associated with PDD among patients with depression at long-term follow-up [17].

Several subjective reasons for OI are worth discussing. (1) Although depressive mood is the core symptom of MDD, it is not the most common reason for OI. Lack of motivation was the third most common reason for OI. Work motivation is significantly and negatively associated with depression [44]. Motivation to work is an important factor to facilitate employment among patients with MDD [45]. (2) Inability to concentrate and decreased memory were the fifth most common reason for OI and the fourth major reason for OI, respectively. Cognitive deficits were found to be a major mediator of OI in remitted MDD patients [46]. Neurocognition is positively associated with social-occupational function in patients with anxious depression [47]. (3) The NTR for OI reflected subjective perception of OI and might be affected by the severity of mood symptoms. The total and longest DPE were objective employment outcomes. When patients with MDD reported more NTR for OI, this might imply that they suffered from greater depressive and somatic severity and met more difficulties in their occupations. Although the TNR for OI was significantly correlated with the total and longest DPE, the correlation degree was low (r = −0.22 and − 0.20, respectively). This implied that the TNR for OI was only weakly associated with the total and longest DPE.

Several points are worth noting. (1) In regression model II, DS at baseline was negatively associated with the total and longest DPE (Table 5). Our results (Table 3) demonstrated that greater severities of depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms at follow-up were associated with more reported reasons for OI. One study reported that improvement of depression was associated with an increased possibility of becoming employed at follow-up among depressed subjects without employment at baseline [48]. (2) In regression model II, an older age at the first MDE was associated with an increased longest DPE. Previous studies reported that early onset of depression was associated with greater severities of depression and functional impairment [49, 50]. (3) In this study, female subjects with MDD were associated with more TNR for OI. This might result from these reasons: (a) Women with MDD were associated with a younger onset of depression and higher lifetime anxiety comorbidities as compared with men with MDD [51]. (b) At baseline, women tended to have more severe depressive symptoms than men (DS score: 25.4 ± 5.3 vs. 23.9 ± 4.6, p = 0.13). (c) Potential gender differences in willingness to report reasons for OI might exist. Although women reported more TNR, gender was not an independent variable related to total and longest DPE in the two regression models. 4)The total duration of psychiatric pharmacotherapy was negatively and positively correlated with the total DPE and TNR for OI, respectively (Table 4). This meant that longer pharmacotherapy was associated with a shorter total DPE and more TNR for OI. This might imply that some MDD subjects who had a poor treatment response to pharmacotherapy and needed longer pharmacotherapy had a shorter total DPE. In fact, previous studies reported that newer antidepressants have positive impacts on occupational function [52, 53]. 5) Our study found that PDD was associated with reported more reasons for OI, and greater depressive severity at baseline was associated with a reduced DPE. PDD and greater depressive severity at baseline might be associated with treatment-resistant depression, which might cause the negative impacts on patients’ occupational functioning, social integration, and long-term prognosis [54]. In future studies, investigators should simultaneously consider the associations of PPD and treatment-resistant depression with OI.

The study had several limitations. (1) At follow-up, 61.0% of the subjects were not enrolled in the statistical analysis, including 153 (52.8%) subjects without follow-up and 24 (8.3%) subjects who did not need to work. This might introduce bias. Moreover, sample attribution might cause unstable statistical results, and some statistical methods could not be used. (2) Pharmacotherapy was not controlled over the 10-year period because the study was an observational study. Moreover, a lack of details related to pharmacotherapy might limit the interpretation. (3) Although the investigators have clarified the self-reported DPE, possible memory bias might exist. (4) The unit of total and longest DPE was one month. Sick leave for several days and decreased work efficiency due to depression were neglected in this study. (5) This study did not control for workplace characteristics, such as the types of occupations, working hours, and supportive systems. (6) This study enrolled subjects from outpatients in a single center. This might limit generalizability. (7) Some reasons related to OI might overlap. For example, decreased memory and inability to concentrate might be related to difficulty in learning new skills. (8) The reasons for OI were self-reported and subjective. Bias might exist.

Conclusion

In conclusion, 14.2% and 40.7% of subjects had a total and longest DPE of less than 5 years, respectively. An increase of one anxiety comorbidity in subjects was associated with a decrease of 4.6 months in the total DPE compared to those without. Greater somatic symptoms, PDD, and the female sex at baseline were associated with more reported reasons for OI. Fatigue, somatic discomfort, lack of motivation, and care about colleagues’ comments were the four most common reasons for OI. In the pharmacological and psychological treatment of depression, clinicians should pay greater attention to improving fatigue, motivation, and interpersonal sensitivity. Our findings suggested that clinicians should routinely assess and address these treatable factors, including anxiety comorbidities, PDD, fatigue, and somatic discomforts, to enhance vocational recovery.
