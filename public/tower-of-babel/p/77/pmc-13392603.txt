Price trend, clinical benefit and within-class competition of drugs for metastatic Non–small cell lung cancer in China: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xin Du, Xingxian Luo, Hefan Zhang, Tianyi Shen, Xinru Wang, Qixiang Guo, Xufeng Lv, XinYi Zhao, Yiwen Pu, Yue Yang, Lin Huang, Yi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe affordability and clinical benefit of novel anticancer drugs are critical determinants of equitable access to cancer care in China and globally. In China, a recent influx of innovative drugs for metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has occurred alongside major pricing policy reforms. However, it remains unclear whether this expanded availability has reduced prices and aligned costs with clinical benefit. This study aims to assess the price trends of metastatic NSCLC drugs in China, evaluate how within-class competition influences these trends, and determine the relationship between drug pricing and clinical benefit.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included all domestic and imported targeted agents for metastatic NSCLC approved in China between January 1, 2005, and March 15, 2026 based on approval data from the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA). Pivotal trial characteristics were identified from drug labels, regulatory review reports, and published literature. Price information at launch and post-approval was collected from the provincial medical insurance bureau procurement platforms. Clinical benefit was assessed using the European Society for Medical Oncology–Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) v2.0. We included drug prices from 2019 to 2026 to calculate monthly treatment costs, and to estimate the average annual reduction rate (AARR) and total treatment reduction rate (TTRR). The primary outcomes were monthly treatment cost, AARR, and TTRR; secondary outcomes included the association between price reduction magnitude, competition index, National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) status, and the correlation between ESMO-MCBS scores and monthly treatment costs. Spearman correlation analyses were used to evaluate the associations between monthly treatment costs (both initial and latest) and ESMO-MCBS scores, and between the competition index and AARR or TTRR. Univariable and multivariable regression analyses were performed to assess the effects of NRDL status and the competition index on the magnitude of price reduction.FindingsA total of 51 targeted drugs for metastatic NSCLC were included, comprising 16 EGFR inhibitors, 11 PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, 8 ALK inhibitors, 5 MET inhibitors, 4 ROS1 inhibitors, 3 KRAS inhibitors, 2 RET inhibitors, and 2 HER2 inhibitors. The median monthly treatment price decreased substantially from launch to the latest prices, with NRDL drugs showing the largest reductions (median $7058.9 to $2547.2) compared with non-NRDL drugs (median $1,2173.2 to $1,1526.0). The AARR was largest during NRDL negotiation (median −54.8%; IQR, −68.1% to −38.1%), followed by post-inclusion declines (median −3.6%; IQR, −16.0% to −0.6%), whereas pre-NRDL reductions were minimal (median −0.05%; IQR, −1.08% to 0.2%). The median Spearman correlation coefficient values were 0.997 (IQR, 0.775–1.000) for EGFR inhibitors, 0.942 (IQR, 0.765–1.000) for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, 0.689 (IQR, 0.456–0.787) for ALK inhibitors, and 1.000 (IQR, 0.507–1.000) for MET inhibitors. Spearman correlation analyses showed that the competition index was not significantly correlated with the magnitude of price reductions either before or after NRDL inclusion. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses indicated that NRDL inclusion significantly reduced prices, whereas within-class competition had no significant impact on the extent of price reductions. There was no significant association between either launch prices (r = −0.084, P = 0.54) or the latest prices (r = −0.001, P = 0.99) and clinical benefit for NSCLC drugs in China.InterpretationOur findings demonstrate that NRDL negotiations are the primary driver of price reductions for targeted lung cancer drugs in China, whereas market competition alone has a limited effect on lowering prices. Neither launch prices nor current prices of NSCLC drugs correlate strongly with clinical benefit. Incorporating value-oriented clinical benefit assessment into reimbursement decisions may help prioritize drugs with substantial clinical benefit and ensure that drug pricing more closely reflects therapeutic value.FundingThis work was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (72374115) and Tsinghua University Initiative Scientific Research Program (20257020014).Keywords: Cancer drugs, Launch and the latest price, Clinical benefit, Within-class competition

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The affordability of innovative anticancer drugs remains a critical public health challenge worldwide. Over the past decade, China’s regulatory reforms have accelerated the approval of numerous within-class cancer drugs while establishing a specialized national reimbursement negotiation pathway to improve patient access. We searched PubMed, Web of Science for peer-reviewed original studies published from database inception through March 2026, using the search terms “anticancer drugs”, “drug pricing”, “treatment cost”, “clinical benefit”, “within-class competition”, “me-too drugs”, and “China”. While the impact of National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) negotiations on drug affordability in China is well-documented, evidence regarding the specific role of within-class competition—the market entry of multiple drugs targeting the same pathway—remains scarce. International literature, primarily from the United States, suggests that the introduction of within-class drugs often fails to lower prices and may even be associated with annual price increases. In contrast to the post-launch price increases seen in the United States, although some European countries such as the United Kingdom, Germany, and Switzerland have experienced declines in inflation-adjusted monthly treatment costs, the median reduction five years after launch did not exceed 6%. In China, drug regulatory reforms have accelerated the approval and market entry of numerous within-class drugs targeting the same pathways, particularly in the field of lung cancer. However, it remains unclear whether this expanded availability fosters significant price reductions through market competition. Furthermore, studies evaluating the relationship between the price and clinical value of these within-class anticancer drugs remain limited.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first concurrent evaluation of the interplay between within-class competition, institutional negotiation, and clinical benefit for 51 targeted drugs approved for NSCLC in China. Our findings suggested that the prices of within-class drugs for NSCLC in China have shown an overall downward trend, with significant price correlations among these drugs. However, the price reductions of these within-class drugs were not driven by market competition but were largely attributable to the role of national reimbursement negotiations. Furthermore, the prices of within-class drugs were not correlated with their clinical value as measured by ESMO-MCBS, regardless of whether initial prices or the most recent prices were considered.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of our study indicate that in China’s centralized healthcare system, the mere presence of multiple therapeutic alternatives is insufficient to drive affordability through traditional market forces. Instead, within-class competition serves primarily as an institutional catalyst that enhances the payer’s bargaining power during formal negotiations. The “decoupling” of market competition from price reductions implies that the NRDL negotiation model remains the primary driver of substantial price reductions.

Notably, the observed misalignment between drug pricing and clinical benefit in the NSCLC drugs highlights a critical need for reform. To ensure a sustainable and equitable healthcare ecosystem, Chinese policymakers should transition toward a value-based pricing model. Integrating standardized, clinical benefit assessment frameworks (e.g., ESMO-MCBS or ASCO-VF) directly into reimbursement decision-making would enable healthcare systems to prioritize innovative drugs with substantial clinical benefit, ensuring that national expenditures more closely reflect demonstrated therapeutic value.

Introduction

The affordability of innovative anticancer drugs remains a critical public health issue worldwide.1 Cancer drugs are among the most expensive medications, and their escalating costs contribute to substantial “financial toxicity”, including reduced treatment adherence, and even bankruptcy, especially among patients with inadequate insurance coverage.2,3 According to a recent IQVIA report, global spending on oncology drugs reached $223 billion in 2023, an increase of $25 billion from 2022, and is projected to rise to $409 billion by 2028.4 In response, several countries have encouraged the market entry of within-class agents within the same drug class, a strategy based on the principle that competition among multiple drugs with overlapping mechanisms of action and similar indications would lead to price reductions.5, 6, 7 However, previous studies have shown that, in the pharmaceutical market, the presence of more products does not necessarily result in lower drug prices.8, 9, 10 For instance, one study demonstrated that despite the approval of numerous within-class agents for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in the United States, drug prices have continued to rise faster than inflation.10

Beyond the challenge of high prices is the persistent question of clinical benefit. Substantial concerns exist regarding whether new agents within the same therapeutic class deliver meaningful improvements over existing treatments.2 The previous study indicates that the magnitude of clinical benefit varies widely across anticancer agents, with a considerable proportion providing minimal or no added value compared to existing therapies.11 The relationship between a drug’s price and its clinical benefit remains controversial and poorly aligned. Rationalizing health resource allocation by linking costs to meaningful outcomes is essential for improving health system efficiency. In this context, the frequent misalignment between prices and the clinical benefit of anticancer drugs has emerged as a critical driver of resource misallocation. Consequently, efforts by health authorities to enhance value-for-money in cancer drug provision are coming under increasingly scrutiny. Multiple studies have demonstrated that monthly treatment costs for anticancer drugs show no consistent association with the level of clinical benefit, a pattern observed not only in the United States and several high-income European countries, but also across Asian nations including Japan, China, and South Korea.2,12

To address challenges in access to anticancer drugs, many countries have implemented diverse strategies to contain rising costs of oncology drugs.3 In the United States (US), the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 empowers Medicare to negotiate prices for a select number of drugs that have been on the market for an extended period, with considerations based on clinical benefit, unmet medical needs, and research and development costs.13,14 In Europe,15 Canada,16 and Japan,17 national regulatory frameworks govern both initial launch pricing and subsequent post-launch adjustments through formal health technology assessments, price negotiation mechanisms, and periodic reassessments of value. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of these regulatory interventions in curbing cancer drug expenditure varies considerably across health systems.

In China, a series of drug regulatory reforms have been initiated since 2015. These reforms, including priority review pathways, a 60-day silent approval mechanism for investigational new drug (IND) applications, and the encouragement of global multi-center clinical trials, have substantially accelerated the approval of targeted anticancer drugs.18,19 As a result, the number of innovative oncology drugs approved increased from fewer than 5 in 2015 to 135 drugs between 2020 and 2025, particularly in lung cancer, which carries the highest incidence and mortality rates in China.20 Concurrently, to enhance treatment affordability, the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) introduced a centralized national price negotiation system for anticancer drugs in 2018, with annual updates to the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL).21 In China, the basic medical insurance system covers over 95% of the population, with reimbursement rates determined by multiple factors including insurance type (e.g., urban employee vs. resident), hospital level, and whether the drug or service falls within the NRDL. Patients are required to pay deductibles and a percentage of costs out-of-pocket, with rates varying across regions and clinical contexts. Consequently, the NRDL negotiation mechanism, which determines both coverage and pricing, directly shapes the actual financial burden shouldered by patients. Early evaluations indicate that these policies have been instrumental in lowering drug prices in China, notably by facilitating the introduction of domestic anticancer agents that are substantially more affordable than imported alternatives. However, existing studies have largely focused on the price-reducing effect of reimbursement negotiations, whereas the role of within-class competition in shaping oncology drug prices in China remains underexplored.22

Furthermore, whether the prices of within-class drugs align with their clinical value remains uncertain. As in other countries, the pricing of approved anticancer agents in China shows no clear correlation with their clinical benefit.2 Previous studies have demonstrated no significant correlation between price and value for anticancer medicines before and after their inclusion in China’s NRDL.23,24 Conversely, other investigations have suggested a potential association between initial pricing and clinical value for targeted oncology therapies in China.25 Critical questions remain regarding the initial pricing and subsequent price trajectories of within-class anticancer drugs in China, particularly whether these agents deliver superior clinical benefit, and whether an alignment exists between price and benefit. This study aims to evaluate the impact of reimbursement negotiations and market competition on the pricing of innovative cancer drugs, and to further examine the association between drug pricing and clinical benefit among within-class NSCLC drugs, thereby addressing an important evidence gap. Our findings may provide critical evidence to inform clinical value-oriented drug development ecosystem and improve the affordability of within-class anticancer medicines in China and beyond.

Methods

Data sources and extraction

This analysis focused on novel anticancer drugs, defined as new molecular entities (NMEs), approved in China for metastatic NSCLC between January 1, 2005, and March 15, 2026. The targeted anticancer drugs were identified via the NMPA database,26 following a methodology consistent with previous studies.2,27 Drugs were classified as within-class competitors if they shared the same mechanism of action and were approved for NSCLC indications (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors for NSCLC). For targeted agents with multiple approved lung cancer indications (e.g., ensartinib for both first-line and later-line settings in advanced disease), only the first lung cancer indication granted approval by the NMPA was included in the analysis. For each drug, we extracted key characteristics including drug class, approval pathway, approval date, and molecular target (Appendix pp 2). The approval pathway was classified as standard approval and expedited approval, with the latter encompassing Breakthrough Therapy Designation, Conditional Approval, Priority Review and Special Approval (This study does not cover).

We obtained the initial and latest (as of March 15, 2026) list prices for the first approved NSCLC indication, as well as pricing trends from 2019 to 2026, in accordance with an earlier study.11 Data were sourced from the PharmCube database, a widely referenced commercial source in China, as well as from publicly available records (Appendix pp 11).20 Monthly treatment costs at launch and for subsequent time points were calculated based on the official list prices and the labels specified in the NMPA-approved prescribing information for the first NSCLC indication. In this study, we assumed a body weight of 70 kg and a body surface area (BSA) of 1.7 m2 for adult patients to simplify the calculations similar to the previous study.6 Where multiple drug strengths were available, the option yielding the lowest treatment cost was selected. Initial prices were adjusted for inflation to 2026 values using health-specific purchasing power parities (PPP), and then converted to U.S. dollars at an exchange rate of 7.2.28 Besides, we calculated the average annual reduction rate (AARR) and total treatment reduction rate (TTRR) of monthly treatment prices for each anticancer drug price. The AARR was defined as the sum of the reduction rates divided by the number of years, a method consistent with a previous study.29

We identified all pivotal trials for each NSCLC drug from NMPA review reports.23 Considering that these reports may contain only partial efficacy and safety data (e.g., lacking data on the incidence of serious adverse events), we supplemented our data extraction with published literature based on the pivotal trials. When review reports for some targeted drugs were unavailable, we identified their pivotal clinical trials based on the latest drug labels issued by the NMPA through March 15, 2026, consistent with a previous study.6 We extracted primary efficacy endpoints, trial design, enrollment numbers, safety profiles, and quality of life measures from the pivotal trials. The common primary efficacy endpoints include objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), response rate (RR), disease-free survival (DFS), and time to progressive (TTP). When multiple trials were relevant, preference was given to trials focusing on Chinese populations or those demonstrating the best clinical outcomes.

The validated European Society for Medical Oncology Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) V2.0 was used to evaluate the clinical benefit of each drug at both the initial listing and the most recent time point.30 Updated ESMO-MCBS scores for solid tumors were retrieved from the ESMO website, which regularly revises these scores.31 For indications where no updated scores were available, two independent evaluators with oncology backgrounds reassessed the scores based on the latest trial evidence, following the official ESMO-MCBS evaluation forms and online tutorials. Any disagreement between the two reviewers was resolved by a third party. The same procedure was applied to determine the initial ESMO-MCBS scores, based on the clinical evidence available at the time of the initial listing. In accordance with the scale, scores of 4 or 5 substantial clinical benefit, a score of 3 indicates intermediate clinical benefit, and scores of 1 or 2 reflect low clinical benefit, aligning with the criteria used in the previous study.32

To evaluate the impact of within-class drug competition on price reductions, we introduced a competition index. Specifically, the competition index was calculated by plotting the cumulative number of within-class drugs approved or launched over time and measuring the area under the resulting curve (AUC). A larger AUC value reflects a higher level of competitive intensity, as it indicates a greater cumulative exposure to competing drugs over the observation period. All data used in this study were derived from publicly accessible sources. Therefore, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman rank correlation was used to assess price correlations within-class, associations between clinical benefit scores and prices, and the relationship between competition intensity and price reduction outcomes (AARR and TTRR). Affordability was contextualized by comparing monthly treatment costs with the average monthly disposable income per capita in China in 2026.

Additionally, we employed univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses to evaluate the effects of within-class drug competition (measured by the competition index) and NRDL inclusion (drugs successfully included NRDL by national price negotiations) on the AARR and TTRR of anticancer drugs. To assess whether the effect of the competition index on price reductions varies by NRDL inclusion, we included an interaction term between the two variables (model specifications are provided in the Appendix) similar to the previous study.24 Given the skewed distribution of the dependent variable, we applied log-transformation prior to regression.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Included drugs characteristic

Our study cohort included a total of 51 brand-name drugs that received regulatory approval from the NMPA (eTable 1, Appendix pp 3–5). The cohort comprised 16 EGFR inhibitors (erlotinib, gefitinib, icotinib, afatinib, osimertinib, dacomitinib, almonertinib, furmonertinib, befotertinib, sunvozertinib, rezivertinib, zorifertinib, rilertinib, limertinib, mifanertinib, and lazertinib); 11 PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, durvalumab, camrelizumab, tislelizumab, sintilimab, atezolizumab, sugemalimab, toripalimab, serplulimab and penpulimab); 8 ALK inhibitors (crizotinib, ceritinib, alectinib, brigatinib, lorlatinib, ensartinib, iruplinalkib, and envonalkib); 2 RET inhibitors (pralsetinib and selpercatinib), 5 MET inhibitors (savolitinib, glumetinib, vebreltinib, tepotinib, and capmatinib); 4 ROS1 inhibitors (entrectinib, unecritinib, repotrectinib and taletrectinib); 3 KRAS inhibitors (glasdegib, fulzerasib and glecirasib); and 2 HER2 inhibitors (trastuzumab rezetecan and trastuzumab deruxtecan). Among these agents, 30 (58.8%) were domestically developed and 21 (41.2%) were imported. More than half (27, 52.9%) received at least one form of expedited programs. The majority (43, 84.3%) were approved for initial indications, while 8 (15.7%) were for supplemental indications (Table 1).

Price trends among within-class NSCLC drugs

Among the 51 anticancer drugs, the median monthly treatment price decreased from an initial $7834.5 to a latest price of $2989.4 (P < 0.001). For NRDL-listed drugs, prices declined from $7058.9 to $2547.2 (P < 0.001), while prices for non-NRDL-listed drugs declined from $12,173.2 to $11526.0 (P = 0.82). Significant reductions occurred across drug origins, approval pathways, and indication types. EGFR, ALK, and MET inhibitors showed significant price decreases (all P < 0.010); changes for PD-1/PD-L1, ROS1, KRAS, RET and HER2 inhibitors were not significant. All ESMO clinical benefit categories showed significant price reductions (all P < 0.001) (Table 1, Fig. 1).

Over the 8-year study period, monthly treatment costs for all lung cancer targeted therapies showed a consistent downward trend, with an overall median TTRR of −50.0% (−68.8%, −17.5%). Specifically, the AARR for monthly treatment costs was −20.9% (IQR, −34.7% to −8.7%) for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, −18.7% (IQR, −26.8% to −8.0%) for EGFR inhibitors, −19.6% (IQR, −24.7% to −5.8%) for ALK inhibitors, and −22.7% (IQR, −43.4% to −12.4%) for other targeted agents (Fig. 1). Analysis of pricing changes relative to reimbursement status showed that the AARR was minimal prior to NRDL inclusion (median −0.05%; IQR, −1.08% to 0.2%). A substantial one-time price reduction occurred during NRDL negotiation (median, −54.8%; IQR, −68.1% to −38.1%), followed by a continued decrease after inclusion (median AARR, −3.6%; IQR, −16.0% to −0.6%) (Table 2).

Price correlation among within-class NSCLC drugs

Analysis of pricing correlations revealed consistently high correlations across multiple therapeutic classes (Table 3). For EGFR inhibitors, the median Spearman correlation coefficient was 0.997 (IQR, 0.775–1.000), with erlotinib, gefitinib, and icotinib demonstrating near-perfect correlation (r ≥ 0.996). Among PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, the median correlation coefficient was 0.942 (IQR, 0.765–1.000), with nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, sugemalimab, toripalimab, and serplulimab showing complete correlation (r = 1.000). ALK inhibitors exhibited a median correlation of 0.689 (IQR, 0.456–0.787), where crizotinib maintained strong correlations with ceritinib (r = 0.952) and brigatinib (r = 0.931). MET inhibitors exhibited a median correlation of 1.000 (IQR, 0.507–1.000) demonstrating complete correlation.

When AARR were used as the dependent variable, univariate regression analysis revealed that NRDL inclusion was significantly associated with the magnitude of price reduction (β = −0.342, P = 0.006, adjusted R2 = 0.128), whereas the competition index was not a significant predictor (β = 0.003, P = 0.093, adjusted R2 = 0.037). Multivariate regression analysis further confirmed these findings, with NRDL inclusion remaining a significant independent predictor (β = −0.362, P = 0.040) and the competition index showing no significant association (β = 0.003, P = 0.040) (adjusted R2 for the model = 0.185). In the interaction model, no significant interaction effect was observed between NRDL inclusion and the competition index (β = 0.002, P = 0.87, adjusted R2 = 0.168), indicating that the effect of reimbursement status on price reduction did not vary with the level of market competition (eTable 2). When TTRR were used as the dependent variable, univariate regression analysis revealed that NRDL inclusion was significantly associated with the magnitude of price reduction (β = −1.011, P < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.391), whereas the competition index was not a significant predictor (β = −0.004, P = 0.19, adjusted R2 = 0.016). Multivariate regression analysis further confirmed these findings, with NRDL inclusion remaining a significant independent predictor (β = −0.993, P < 0.001) and the competition index continued to show no significant association (β = −0.003, P = 0.22) (adjusted R2 for the model = 0.398). In the interaction model, no significant interaction effect was observed between NRDL inclusion and the competition index (β = −0.007, P = 0.61), indicating that the effect of reimbursement status on price reduction did not vary with the level of market competition (eTable 3).

Clinical benefit and ESMO-MCBS score

This study included a total of 51 pivotal clinical trials used to support the market approval of NSCLC drugs (eTable 4). Of these, 22 (43.1%) were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 29 (56.9%) were single-arm trials. Among the pivotal trials, only four (7.8%) used OS as the primary endpoint, whereas 24 (47.1%) adopted ORR, and 23 (45.1%) used PFS. Regarding the RCTs, 10 employed chemotherapies as the control, four used a placebo, and eight utilized an active comparator (eTable 4). Analysis of clinical benefit using the ESMO-MCBS 2.0 revealed a median score of 3.0 (IQR 2.0–4.0) across all drugs, with substantial variation observed between therapeutic classes. Among EGFR inhibitors, 18.8% (3/16) were classified as substantial clinical benefit (ESMO-MCBS score ≥4), 62.5% (10/16) as intermediate clinical benefit (score 3), and 18.8% (3/16) as low clinical benefit (score ≤2). Among PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, 81.8% (9/11) were substantial clinical benefit, 10% (2/11) were intermediate, with no low clinical benefit agents. For ALK inhibitors, 62.5% (5/8) were substantial clinical benefit, 12.5% (1/8) intermediate, and 25% (2/8) low clinical benefit. None of the agents targeting KRAS, MET, RET, HER2 or ROS1 were graded as substantial clinical benefit.

Association between clinical benefit and price of NSCLC drugs

No significant association was observed between monthly treatment price and ESMO-MCBS scores of NSCLC drugs, either in terms of the initial price (r = −0.084, P = 0.54) or the latest price (r = 0.001, P = 0.99) (Figs. 3 and 4a). This lack of correlation was consistent regardless of whether the drugs were covered by NRDL. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed across different drug targets. Among EGFR-targeted agents, the correlations between ESMO scores and the initial and latest prices were −0.250 (P = 0.35) and r = −0.150 (P = 0.58), respectively. For PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, the corresponding values were r = 0.620 (P = 0.042) and r = 0.572 (P = 0.066). Discussion

This study provides the first concurrent evaluation of the impact of reimbursement negotiations and market competition on drug pricing, and their association with clinical benefit, among within-class NSCLC drugs in China. Advances in oncology drug innovation have led to the availability of numerous within-class drugs across global markets, offering patients a broader range of treatment options. Our findings show that between 2019 and 2026, the prices of 51 NSCLC therapeutic analogues declined continuously, with a median overall reduction of −50·0%. By contrast, post-launch price increases for within-class innovative cancer drugs in the US market have often outpaced inflation, while median price reductions in selected European markets were less than 6% five years after market entry.33,34 China has witnessed substantial and consistent price reductions for NSCLC drugs, driven largely by its NRDL negotiation mechanism.35 Phase-specific analysis showed that the largest price reduction occurred at NRDL negotiation (median reduction: −54.8%), followed by post-inclusion price declines driven by renegotiations for additional indications or contract renewals (median AARR: −3.6%), whereas the pre-NRDL decline mediated by market competition was modest (median AARR: −0.1%) (Fig. 2). These comparative findings underscore the substantial success China has achieved in controlling anticancer drug prices through its NRDL negotiation mechanism, an outcome that stands in stark contrast to the limited effect observed under free-market models.

Whether market competition among drugs within the same class leads to price reductions was examined by analyzing price correlations within each therapeutic class using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Consistent with the findings of Desai et al. (2022) in the US market, our analysis revealed consistently high correlations across multiple drug classes.10 For example, EGFR inhibitors showed a median Spearman correlation of 0.997 (IQR, 0.775–1.000), with erlotinib, gefitinib, and icotinib achieving near-perfect correlation (r ≥ 0.996). These high correlation coefficients are indicative of little price competition among within-class drug prices.

To isolate the effect of therapeutic competition from that of NRDL-driven price reductions, we assessed its impact on prices before negotiation and after NRDL inclusion. Our results suggest limited price competition among manufacturers of within-class drugs prior to their inclusion in China’s NRDL. Spearman correlation analyses showed no significant association between the competition index and either the AARR or TTRR. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses further confirmed that NRDL negotiation was a significant driver of price reductions for targeted therapies (β = 0.516, P < 0.001), whereas the competition index showed no statistically significant association. These findings indicate that, in the absence of NRDL negotiation, market competition among within-class drugs has a negligible effect on price reductions. This finding aligns closely with China’s unique institutional design for NRDL access. Reimbursement listing in China is based on an institutional negotiation framework, in which the inclusion mechanism depends on the competitive landscape: exclusive drugs enter through price negotiation, whereas drugs with generic or same-active-ingredient alternatives enter through competitive bidding, which typically results in deeper price cuts.36 Under this institutional framework, the presence of within-class alternatives does not automatically trigger price competition in a free market and might have only a very limited role in enhancing the payer’s bargaining power.37 Therefore, the observed positive price correlation among within-class drugs reflects the institutional outcome of price convergence under the NRDL negotiation mechanism.

Despite these pronounced price reductions, drug affordability remains a serious concern. In 2025, the median monthly cost of lung cancer drugs in China ($2992.0) accounted for 265.9% of the monthly per capita GDP, indicating severe financial toxicity. Taking the reimbursement rates for urban and rural residents at tertiary hospitals in Beijing in 2025 as an example, patients are required to pay 23%–25% out of pocket, amounting to $688.2–748.0, which is equivalent to 61.6%–66.5% of per capita GDP.38 This stands in stark contrast to the affordability index of 9.02% in the US.11 The financial burden is particularly acute for patients relying on lung cancer drugs not yet included in the NRDL, who face substantial out-of-pocket payments.

Expanding therapeutic options by increasing the number of within-class drugs has sparked considerable global debate, as it remains uncertain whether such growth translates into better clinical benefit.31,39 Our study found that later-approved NSCLC drugs within the same class did not show a trend toward greater clinical benefit; some were priced higher without offering superior clinical value. The challenge of aligning price with clinical benefit is not unique to China; it has been observed across various health-care systems, including those in Japan, the US, and several European countries. Multiple studies have reported a weak or absent correlation between clinical benefit and oncology drug prices in both the US and Europe.12,40 Even countries with well-established health technology assessment systems, such as Germany, the UK, and Switzerland, continue to struggle with linking oncology drug prices to therapeutic benefit.41, 42, 43

In response, both the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) introduced value assessment frameworks in 2015, the ASCO Value Framework (ASCO-VF)44 and the ESMO-MCBS, respectively.45 These frameworks are now widely used in clinical guideline development, and the ESMO-MCBS has been promoted by the WHO for international use.31,46 To promote value-oriented pricing, the US has introduced several legislative measures, including the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022, which authorises Medicare to negotiate prices directly for certain high-cost drugs, and the proposed Fair Prescription Drug Prices for Americans Act (2025), which seeks to establish a “most-favoured-nation” pricing model linking US drug prices to the average of those in other developed countries.47 To further advance value-based pricing, it is essential to systematically integrate quantitative benefit assessment frameworks (e.g., ESMO-MCBS) into pricing policy, alongside conventional pharmacoeconomic evaluations during reimbursement negotiations. In February 2024, China’s NHSA issued a draft policy aimed at establishing an initial pricing mechanism for new chemical drugs that emphasises clinical benefit and evidence-based practice, signaling a shift toward value-driven pricing.48,49 While international frameworks such as ASCO-VF and ESMO-MCBS offer valuable references, differences in national epidemiology, clinical evidence, and health priorities suggest that developing a China-specific framework may be necessary to achieve truly value-oriented pricing in the Chinese context.

To support value-based pricing of innovative oncology drugs, the ESMO-MCBS and ASCO value frameworks are widely used internationally. These frameworks assign benefit grades based on clinical trial data for specific indications. Consequently, the same drug may receive different benefit grades across different trials depending on the treatment context—for instance, osimertinib showed substantial clinical benefit (grade 4) in the LAURA study but only intermediate clinical benefit (grade 3) in the FLAURA 2 study.35 This nuance underscores the importance of interpreting benefit grades within their specific clinical contexts. However, these value frameworks have inherent limitations, as they do not restrict the choice of comparator in clinical trials, a factor that is critical to the assessment of clinical benefit. There is growing international consensus that head-to-head trials against active comparators are more informative when within-class alternatives are available. The US FDA, in its guidance on effectiveness evidence, has encouraged but not mandated head-to-head trials against standard therapies.50 Similarly, China’s NMPA has explicitly endorsed a clinical development model centered on patient needs and clinical benefit, emphasising direct comparative evidence using optimal controls, such as standard of care.51 Going forward, a coordinated approach between innovation and reimbursement policies should be established to support and, where appropriate, require head-to-head comparisons against current standard therapies. Such an approach would strengthen the evidence base for benefit assessment and price negotiation, ultimately directing resources towards truly transformative innovations and ensuring their appropriate market recognition.32,52

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was limited to 51 cancer drugs approved by the NMPA and did not include drugs that were not approved in China. Second, pricing data was based on publicly available sources and may not reflect confidential discounts. This is particularly concerning for recently launched drugs, for which publicly available price data cover only a short period of time. Third, clinical benefit was assessed using the ESMO-MCBS v2.0, which reflects the benefit demonstrated in the specific pivotal trials supporting an indications rather than the inherent benefit of the drug itself. As evidenced by our analysis of osimertinib, the same drug may receive different benefit grades across different treatment contexts, and this nuance should be considered when interpreting our findings. Fourth, in China, drug reimbursement prices are currently determined at the product level rather than by specific indication. Fifth, due to the limited sample size, only a restricted set of covariates, including NRDL listing status and the competition index of within-class drugs, was included in the univariate and multivariate regression analyses to preserve statistical power. Sixth, consistent with previous studies, we did not perform sensitivity analyses on our assumed standard patient parameters (70 kg body weight, 1.7 m2 BSA), which may affect the precision of individual-level cost estimates. Finally, our analysis focused on list pricing and market availability rather than actual drug utilization or patient-level access barriers.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that NRDL negotiations constitute the principal driver of price reductions for within-class lung cancer drugs in China, whereas market competition alone has a limited independent effect on lowering prices. Currently, neither the launch nor latest prices of NSCLC drugs show a strong correlation with clinical benefit as measured. To ensure a sustainable and value-oriented healthcare ecosystem, policymakers should consider incorporating standardized clinical benefit assessment frameworks directly into reimbursement decision-making. Such integration could help prioritize therapies with substantial clinical benefit and better align national drug expenditures with demonstrated therapeutic value.
