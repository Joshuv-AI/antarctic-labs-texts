Machine learning-based prediction of compressive energy absorption in shoe soles with different features
Authors: Mohammad Mahdi Mohammadi, Amir Nourani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study conducted 537 force-controlled compression tests on shoe soles to measure strain energy. Various features were studied, including midsole and outsole structures, sole geometry, sole hardness, and loading conditions including applied compression force, angle, and loading speed. Strain energies during compression tests were recorded, followed by correlation analysis, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Tukey tests. Subsequently, different machine learning (ML) algorithms—i.e., simple linear regression (SLR), support vector regression (SVR), random forest (RF), and multi-layer perceptron (MLP)—were applied for curve fitting. The correlation matrix revealed that strain energy exhibits a strong dependence on the applied force (correlation = 0.89), which stands out significantly compared to other features. This is followed by geometry and shoe hardness, both of which show a correlation of approximately 0.25. To validate these findings, ANOVA tests were conducted, and all p values were significant (p < 0.05). Additionally, it was found that auxetic midsole structures achieve similar strain energy values as simple midsoles (mean = 2.978 J), while maintaining a lighter overall weight. In contrast, weight-reducing holes were not as effective (mean = 2.080 J). It was also shown that initial contact position (i.e., changed with the compression angle) can affect the strain energy; that is, initial contact with the heel generates less strain energy (mean = 2.643 J) compared to the first touch with the toes (mean = 2.839 J). After tuning ML hyperparameters through grid search, RF outperformed other models (MSE = 0.0089), followed by SVR with polynomial kernel (MSE = 0.0105), SVR with RBF kernel (MSE = 0.0146), and MLP (MSE = 0.0251). The developed models offer a practical tool for shoe manufacturers to optimize sole designs and prevent injury during high-stress activities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20864-3.Keywords: Compression test, Flexible soles, Curve fitting, SVR, MLP, Random forestSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Orthopaedics

Introduction

Research shows that selecting footwear with optimal characteristics may significantly influence human movement and, consequently, prevent injuries1–3. When ground reaction forces are not adequately absorbed by footwear, they are transmitted to the upper body—particularly the lower limbs (foot, knee, and hip) and even the lumbar spine. Even during gait, pressure on the medial calcaneus can rise to 4.5 times the body weight, concentrated in the rearfoot area. The ability to manage these extreme forces is closely linked to the performance of the footwear4 .One of the key mechanical parameters governing this behavior is strain energy, defined as the compressive energy absorbed by the sole during specific activities. Soles engineered with optimized strain energy distributions—such as auxetic or off-loading designs—are commonly used in athletic footwear to absorb impact and reduce force transmission to the upper body, thereby lowering the risk and severity of injuries to the wearer without affecting the nature of the sport5,6. Such soles are particularly effective in reducing heel pressure during the gait cycle7 and alleviating lumbosacral disc pressure in high-demand activities8.

Building on this, researchers have explored advanced sole structures to enhance footwear performance by modifying human kinematics and kinetics. One promising innovation is the integration of an auxetic rotating triangles structure, as seen in Nike Free RN Flyknit sports shoes9,10. In vivo testing conducted by Nourani et al.8 demonstrated that these auxetic soles significantly increase maximal hip (13.4%), knee (9%), and ankle (13.2%) flexion angles. This enhanced range of motion contributes to a reduction in maximal L5-S1 compression during high-impact activities by 11.8%.

However, these tests did not isolate the effects of sole geometry and hardness, making it difficult to attribute the observed benefits solely to the auxetic structures. To address this limitation, Nourani et al.11 employed a finite element (FE) approach to analyze the strain energy in midsole and outsole structures independently. They compared the strain energy of four ethylene–vinyl acetate (EVA) shoe designs: (1) control shoes, (2) shoes with midsoles featuring re-entrant auxetic structures similar to unit cells from Behbahani et al.12, (3) shoes with midsoles containing circular holes in the sagittal plane as a non-auxetic design, and (4) shoes with an outsole resembling the Nike Free RN10. The results showed that midsole modifications significantly enhanced performance: re-entrant auxetic structures and circular holes increased strain energy by 153% and 157%, respectively, compared to control shoes, indicating increased strain energy and potential benefits for cushioning and running economy. In contrast, the modified outsole had a minimal impact, with only a 7% increase in strain energy, suggesting a limited role in energy-related performance. Despite the strengths of this study, it also presents several limitations. Notably, it did not consider variations in material properties, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other common sole materials such as polyurethane (PU), thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), or different grades of EVA. Additionally, the analysis was limited to static loading conditions, which may not accurately reflect the behavior of these structures under more demanding dynamic or impact scenarios.

While previous studies have focused on optimizing sole designs for general motion patterns, another approach involves analyzing an individual’s gait when wearing various footwear and suggesting the one that is better suited to their gait. Machine learning (ML) algorithms have recently emerged as a promising approach to footwear selection tailored to desired kinematic outcomes. To our knowledge, only one study was conducted by Onodera et al.13 utilizing ML to distinguish midsole resilience and shoe dorsum features. They captured kinematic data with six cameras and kinetic data using two force plates, resulting in a dataset of 1080 entries (10 trials × 4 footwear types × 27 participants) with 1254 variables. Through ML analysis, they identified key features from kinematic, kinetic, and 42 shoe-specific parameters. Using artificial neural networks, an accuracy of 84.8% was achieved in distinguishing between two midsole materials with 5% and 55% resilience, while 93.9% accuracy was accomplished in identifying two shoe dorsum structures.

Robust mechanical or biomechanical models for predicting strain energy in shoe soles, or for optimizing sole parameters, are currently lacking. However, machine learning (ML) models developed by Xu et al.14,15 demonstrate considerable potential for adaptation to this domain with additional data from diverse activities and sole designs. In one study, Xu et al.15 developed an ML model to estimate anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) forces during single-leg landings by integrating the sparrow search algorithm (SSA) with extreme learning machine (ELM) and long short-term memory (LSTM) techniques, using ankle motion data as input. This model achieved exceptional accuracy (SSA-ELM: coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.9992, Mean square error (MSE) = 0.0023; combined SSA-ELM/SSA-LSTM: R2 = 0.9947, MSE = 0.0076), although its applicability was limited by the exclusive use of male participants and a single landing maneuver. In another study, Xu et al.14 proposed a metaheuristic optimization approach to select an optimal set of 10 gait features, including six ankle-related and four knee-related features. These features were evaluated using classifiers such as K-nearest neighbors, random forest, support vector machine, and artificial neural networks. The approach achieved a gait recognition accuracy of 99.81% (± 0.53%), and enabled waveform analysis that revealed distinct stance-phase patterns. However, the study’s reliance on conventional kinematic and kinetic data may overlook opportunities for simpler, video-based feature extraction methods to reduce system complexity.

Previous studies have explored various shoe designs during specific activities, yet the critical sole features influencing performance remain unclear. Moreover, previous studies have not independently examined key parameters. As a result, their findings are confounded by multiple interacting factors, making it difficult to isolate the effects of specific midsole and outsole properties. This study addresses these gaps by systematically investigating three primary hypotheses: (1) auxetic sole structures are effective in increasing strain energy, (2) loads are more critical when initial contact occurs with the heel compared to the toes, and (3) wider geometry, and reduced hardness can significantly increase strain energy. To test these hypotheses, 537 mechanical compression tests were systematically conducted to assess the influence of midsole and outsole structures, sole geometry ,and hardness on strain energy absorption. These parameters were examined in varying loading conditions—including compression amplitude, angle, and loading rate. Furthermore, predictive models were developed to evaluate the strain energy behavior of EVA soles with varying configurations under diverse loading scenarios. By isolating and analyzing these parameters, the study provides a clearer understanding of the factors that govern strain energy absorption in shoe soles, offering valuable insights for optimizing footwear design to reduce impact forces and prevent injuries. Notably, this research is the second study, following Onodera et al.13, to apply ML in the footwear industry. While Onodera et al. used ML to predict shoe parameters based on gait patterns, this study adopts a mechanical perspective, focusing on predicting strain energy in soles under various loading conditions.

Materials and methods

Sole production

Material selection was the first step in this experiment. Among commonly used midsole materials—polyurethane (PU), thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), and ethylene–vinyl acetate (EVA)—EVA was selected for its lightweight, flexible structure and superior ability to store mechanical energy during loading. While PU and TPU offer higher strength and durability, their greater stiffness limits their capacity for strain energy absorption. In contrast, EVA’s lower stiffness allows greater deformation under load, enabling it to absorb and store more strain energy in the sole16,17. This makes it more effective for tests focused on reducing energy transfer to the body by maximizing strain energy within the shoe, which aligns with the objective of this study8,18. Although EVA has known disadvantages, such as lower strength and durability, these can be mitigated by incorporating TPU layers in the midsole or outsole; however, this strategy falls outside the scope of the present study. Furthermore, in this study, EVA soles with 40 and 50 Shore A hardness were developed, as this range aligns with the common hardness levels of EVA soles reported in the literature and industry8,18.

After selecting EVA as the material for this study, two sole geometries commonly used in the industry were chosen: wide and narrow soles. The wide-toe soles were similar in geometry to Nike’s design10, while the narrow-toe soles were similar to the geometry of soles fabricated in Shima Company, Iran. Dimensions of these types of soles are provided in Fig. 1.

This project utilized midsoles with re-entrant structures, incorporating both widely spaced (Re-entrant 1) and tightly spaced (Re-entrant 2) configurations (Fig. 1 and 2), as well as weight-reducing holes (Fig. 3). For soles featuring a two-part design (separate outsole and midsole), the re-entrant structure was created in the midsole using laser cutting, when necessary (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the weight-reducing hole structure was integrated into the mold, allowing the sole to remain a single, unified piece.

Besides, the outsole structure was designed with a focus on enhancing flexibility, durability, and traction. Instead of using molds, grooves were precisely cut into the outsole using a hot cutter (Fig. 4B), ensuring the correct depth and alignment. This cut grooves structure (Fig. 4B), 1 cm deep, was inspired by the geometric design of the Nike Free RN Flyknit 2017 shoe10, known for its segmented and flexible outsole that promotes natural foot movement.

Compression testing

Force-controlled compression tests were conducted to evaluate the mechanical behavior of shoe soles under controlled conditions. These tests measured the strain energy of the soles using a size 42 prosthetic foot from Tehran University of Medical Sciences. A custom fixture was designed to enable testing at two foot angles and three compression velocities (Fig. 5).

Fixture of the compression test.

The selected compression angles were designed to accurately simulate distinct loading scenarios based on biologically relevant foot strike patterns observed in human locomotion. Two configurations were implemented to reflect these patterns. The first configuration—designated as toe-first contact (0° compression angle)—involved positioning the prosthetic foot at a 0° (neutral) angle, allowing simultaneous engagement of the forefoot and rearfoot, thereby replicating forefoot-initiated landings. The second configuration—referred to as heel-first contact (12° compression angle)—introduced a 12° inclination from the vertical axis, which represents the maximum permissible deviation for the prosthetic foot-fixture assembly, ensuring initial ground contact occurred at the heel. These specific angles were deliberately chosen to model the two predominant modes of foot–ground interaction during dynamic movements such as jumping, aligning with established biomechanical principles.

To prevent damage to the prosthetic foot and permanent deformation of the soles, a maximum force limit of 1200 N was applied during the tests, which were conducted at three velocities: 7, 80, and 800 N/s. These velocities are similar to the loading rates used by Dickson et al.19, who performed compression tests at 5, 50, and 500 mm/min under displacement control. In EVA compression testing, a 12 mm displacement is typically used for a 1200 N load. Assuming a linear relationship between displacement and force, this corresponds approximately to loading rates of 8.3, 83.3, and 833.3 N/s, which are closely aligned with the selected test velocities.

All tests were conducted using the Amsler HCT 25–400 Zwick/Roell machine equipped with an ACT 2000 actuator. A sampling frequency of 50 Hz was used for tests conducted at a loading rate of 7 N/s. For higher loading rates of 80 N/s and 800 N/s, a higher sampling frequency of 500 Hz was applied due to the high speed of these tests and concerns about insufficient data resolution.

The results provided a comprehensive dataset for analyzing different design parameters. A total of 537 tests were conducted, systematically varying loading conditions, midsole and outsole structures, sole materials, and hardness. Results

This section presents the results of the correlation analysis, the application of linear and nonlinear regression models, and a comparison of their performance. The SLR model serves as a linear baseline, while SVR, RF, and MLP are implemented as nonlinear models to capture more complex patterns in the data. In the end, the mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R2) are presented in detail, and permutation feature importance is applied.

Correlation matrix

The correlation matrix, presented in Fig. 6 heatmap, highlights the factors influencing strain energy. In the heatmap, yellow indicates strong positive correlations, purple indicates strong negative correlations, and cyan represents low or negligible correlations. Based on the correlation matrix, strain energy exhibits a strong dependence on reaction force, which stands out significantly compared to other features. This is followed by geometric features and shoe hardness, both showing a correlation of approximately 0.25. While most midsole structures demonstrate correlations below 0.15, the Re-entrant 2 structure exhibits a relatively higher correlation of 0.22. Compression angle also correlates with strain energy, although its impact is weaker compared to reaction force, geometry, hardness, and the Re-entrant 2 structure. Other features, such as outsole design and loading speed, show negligible correlations, with values below 0.1.

Support vector regression (SVR)

This algorithm was executed using four cores with five-fold cross-validation applied to all cores. By tuning the hyperparameters through a search algorithm, the optimal values shown in Table 5 were identified, corresponding to the lowest MSE for each core. Among the kernels tested, the sigmoid kernel performed poorly. This is likely due to its inherent limitations. The sigmoid function compresses input values into a narrow output range (typically [0,1] or [− 1,1]), which restricts its ability to represent functions with wide variations. Furthermore, the sigmoid kernel offers a relatively shallow representation of the input space and often struggles to capture the complex, nonlinear relationships typical in strain energy modeling. This can lead to poor separation and generalization, especially when support vectors are unevenly distributed. Additionally, in extreme input regions, the sigmoid kernel becomes saturated—its output changes very little in response to input changes—resulting in reduced sensitivity to subtle but important variations in the data. In contrast, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel, derived from the Gaussian function, effectively models localized and nonlinear patterns by forming smooth, flexible decision surfaces. Consequently, SVR with an RBF kernel yielded substantially better results. The highest accuracy was achieved using a quartic polynomial kernel (Fourth-degree polynomial model), which offered greater nonlinear expressiveness and matched the complex physical behavior of the dataset. Importantly, the same trend observed in MSE was also reflected in MAE: SVR models with the RBF and polynomial kernels produced the lowest average absolute errors, while the sigmoid and linear kernels exhibited much higher MAE values. This reinforces the conclusion that more expressive kernels are better suited for capturing the intricacies of strain energy modeling.

Random forest (RF)

This algorithm, like the previous ones, was executed on the training data with five-fold cross-validation, and its hyperparameters were determined through a grid search. As shown in Table 6, the RF model outperformed the others, achieving the lowest mean squared error (MSE). This superior performance can be attributed to the ensemble nature of RF. The model constructs multiple decision trees, each trained on a different subset of the data generated through bootstrapping. During prediction, it aggregates the results of all individual trees—typically via averaging—which helps smooth out the variance of individual trees and reduces the risk of overfitting. Additionally, RF is well-suited to capturing complex, nonlinear interactions between features, which are common in physical modeling tasks like strain energy estimation. By leveraging both ensemble averaging and randomized feature selection, the model achieves high predictive accuracy and generalization.

Discussion

Predicting the strain energy of shoe soles in various loading conditions is crucial for understanding how they absorb mechanical stresses during movement. It directly impacts comfort, durability, and injury prevention by ensuring that forces are properly managed to reduce excessive pressure on joints and muscles. The effects of midsole and outsole structures, sole geometry, sole hardness, and loading conditions—including applied force, compression angle, and loading speed—were examined in this study.

Effect of loading conditions

Applied force magnitude

The results (Fig. 6) clearly showed that applied force had the strongest influence on strain energy, with a significant increase as force magnitude increased (Table 2, p < 0.05). Consequently, shoes that perform well in energy absorption under specific loading conditions may not be effective in others. Optimizing sole geometry and hardness, which greatly affect strain energy, can help manage this variability.

Compression angle

In addition to force magnitude, the compression angle was another loading condition examined in this experiment and was found to significantly affect strain energy (Table 2, p < 0.05). Variations in this angle may reflect the specific region of the foot that first contacts the ground—whether the forefoot or rearfoot—which plays a crucial role in human biomechanics. In jumping activities, humans typically aim to initiate ground contact with their toes, followed by the heels pressing down. To our knowledge, the present article is the first study to examine heel-first contact (12° compression angle) and toe-first contact (0° compression angle) from a mechanical perspective. From a biological perspective, Huang et al. (2024)21 investigated the effects of initial forefoot and rearfoot contact in 23 male participants; it was found that forefoot contact can reduce stance time by 9% without compromising jump height, potentially lowering the risk of non-contact anterior cruciate ligament injuries. This research expands on this by analyzing strain energy differences, providing further biomechanical insight into the effects of foot contact patterns in jumping. Our Tukey test results showed a significant increase in strain energy in the toe-first configuration compared to heel-first, confirming the mechanical advantage of forefoot contact (Table 3). This could be due to the structure being more fully compressed when the toes make initial contact. The tendency to prioritize toe-first contact may stem from an adaptive mechanism honed over generations to prevent potentially harmful situations associated with lower strain energy during heel-first landings. However, athletes are at risk of injury if accidental heel-first contact occurs, as it reduces strain energy and increases the likelihood of harm. To address this issue, footwear designers must carefully consider these risks and develop sports shoe soles that mitigate potential injuries, especially in heel-first contact, while also ensuring safety in toe-first contact.

Loading speed

Loading speed is another loading condition that can significantly impact strain energy (Table 2), highlighting the need to consider the typical loading speed of intended activities when designing footwear. As the loading speed increases, strain energy decreases; however, according to the Tukey test, this reduction is more pronounced at very low loading speeds (Table 3). Supporting this, Alonzo et al.18 observed similar trends in their in vivo tests, where increasing loading speed led to higher ground reaction forces and a greater risk of injury in athletes.

Midsole and outsole structure

In addition, studies have indicated that midsole and outsole structures and their cut orientation may influence human biomechanics by altering strain energy22. Using the Tukey test (Table 3), this study found that midsole horizontal auxetic structures—those oriented in the horizontal plane and extruded normal to it—absorbed almost the same strain energy compared to simple shoes. Moreover, it was revealed that decreasing the spacing between these structures and, consequently, increasing their quantity does not significantly enhance the energy absorption potential. These outcomes may be attributed to the trade-off between the unique deformation behavior of auxetic structures and the reduction of sole material. While auxetic structures exhibit a negative Poisson’s ratio, which tends to increase the capacity of strain energy absorption, they reduce the material in the heel area, ultimately lowering strain energy. These opposing effects may result in approximately constant strain energy across different structure spacings and between auxetic and simple shoe designs. On the other hand, Nourani et al.11 demonstrated the benefits of vertical auxetic structures—applied in the sagittal plane and extruded normal to it— in strain energy absorption of shoe soles through FE analysis. Their findings showed that vertical auxetic structures enhanced strain energy absorption by 153%, unlike the horizontal structures examined in the current study. These results highlight the critical role of structure orientation in strain energy absorption. In contrast, we found that horizontal weight-reducing holes may not be as effective, as they fail to result in a strain energy at a level comparable to that of simpler designs. However, Nourani et al.’s FE results11 suggest that vertical weight-reducing holes, unlike the horizontal ones examined in this study, may offer an advantage, increasing the strain energy by 157%. Nourani et al.11 also found that the strain energy in shoes with a cut-groove outsole, like the Nike RN 2017, was nearly the same as in simple shoes, differing by only 7%. The present analysis further confirmed this similarity, showing just a 3% difference (Table 3).

Sole geometry and hardness

Hardness and geometry of soles are other sole parameters that may be beneficial in optimizing strain energy of soles. According to the Tukey test results presented in Table 3, soles with reduced hardness can increase strain energy by 19%. Wider designs, similar to Shima shoes, can increase it by 22% (Table 3). Both modifications have the potential to enhance performance and comfort. Similarly, in an in vivo study on midsole hardness, Alonzo et al.18 found that a softer midsole increased peak forefoot ground reaction force by 5%, which in turn contributed to greater strain energy. The enhanced strain energy observed in wider and softer soles may be due to their ability to distribute pressure more evenly across the foot, thereby reducing localized stress. Notably, while previous research has explored midsole hardness, this study is the first to isolate the effects of sole geometry while rigorously controlling confounding variables.

Correlation between loading speed and hardness

The correlation between Loading speed and hardness has long been a significant concern in the literature. In this research (Fig. 6), it was found that hardness (correlation = 0.25) exhibited a stronger correlation compared to Loading speed (correlation = 0.08). However, Alonzo et al.18 reported that midsole hardness had a lesser impact compared to Loading speed. Notably, their study used two relatively hard midsoles (50 and 60 Asker C), whereas the present analysis examined softer midsoles (40 and 50 Asker A), which may explain the difference in findings.

ML model performance

In order to prediction purposes and the involvement of different influential parameters in the energy absorption of shoe soles, various ML algorithms were evaluated to assess their effectiveness in curve fitting based on the research data. Among the tested algorithms, the RF algorithm outperformed others, with SVR using polynomial and RBF kernels and MLP providing the next best results (Fig. 8—A). SVR offered competitive performance, though its reliability can be sensitive to small dataset sizes and the choice of kernel parameters. Optimal hyperparameter choices further enhanced its results, and its performance would likely improve with a larger dataset. SLR has low complexity, making it less vulnerable to overfitting, but also less responsive to subtle patterns in the data. Notably, even SLR achieved good accuracy, making it a viable choice for simpler tasks. The MLP model was also effective in capturing non-linear trends, but its performance can vary due to sensitivity to hyperparameters. In small datasets like ours, MLPs are also more prone to overfitting. However, in our implementation, validation checks confirmed stable generalization (see section “Metrics summary”). The RF algorithm delivered the highest accuracy at the expense of increased computational time due to its multiple decision trees and recursive partitioning. For the dataset in this study, the execution time of RF remained manageable, but this issue could become more significant when applied to larger datasets. In terms of feature importance, almost all models ranked force significantly higher than other features in influencing strain energy, consistent with the raw correlation matrix. This was followed by hardness, geometry, and midsole structures. For more details, see section “Permutation feature importance”.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to incorporate such a broad set of features within a single investigation. To the best of our knowledge, it also represents the largest experimental dataset of its kind reported in the literature and the first to apply such a wide range of regression algorithms in shoe mechanical testing. In future studies, it will be possible to test soles with vertical structures such as re-entrant auxetic structures and honeycomb auxetic structures. Although these structures have a complex manufacturing process, they seem to have a significant impact on the strain energy of various soles. Additionally, optimizing the shape of these lateral structures using FE software could be another avenue for exploration. Future research on strain energy prediction must prioritize integrating physics-informed neural networks with data augmentation techniques. This synergy is essential to overcome experimental data constraints and enable robust predictions for next-generation sole designs. This is especially important for auxetic geometries subjected to complex, multi-directional loading scenarios.

Conclusions

This research successfully developed several curve-fitting models to estimate the strain energy of shoe soles, incorporating key factors such as speed, force, loading angle, sole stiffness, sole geometry, and the structure of the midsole and outsole (all p values < 0.05). As the first study to achieve this milestone, it provides valuable tools for shoe manufacturers to predict the strain energy of their soles and compare it against optimal thresholds. If a sole fails to meet the required threshold, manufacturers may adjust critical parameters, such as geometry and hardness (Correlation = 0.25), to achieve the desired performance. The findings of this study are particularly relevant for researchers and practitioners focused on high-demanding activities, as shoes with appropriate strain energy may play a significant role in mitigating damage caused by ground reaction forces (Correlation = 0.89), ultimately enhancing the safety of soles. Current research also revealed that specific design choices, namely wider sole geometries and softer midsoles, significantly enhance strain energy absorption. This is particularly crucial in high-demanding activities involving high speed heel-first contact. However, because both the statistical analyses and the developed models were based on a limited dataset, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings beyond the tested conditions. Future studies should incorporate larger and more diverse samples to validate these effects and enhance the generalizability of the results.
