On-demand rotational manipulation of microparticles and zebrafish larvae via orthogonally phased BAW acoustofluidics
Authors: Junjun Lei, Li Lin, Feng Cheng, Peter Glynne-Jones, Martyn Hill, Zhigang Huang, Zhen Yao, Maodan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rotational manipulation of microscale objects or living organisms is crucial for various applications in microsystems and biological engineering, especially in high-precision imaging. However, achieving precise rotation of individual biological samples at specific angles remains a challenge. This study introduces a novel and cost-effective acoustofluidic approach for on-demand rotation of microparticles and biological specimens, such as zebrafish larvae, to desired angles. The system consists of piezoelectric transducers arranged orthogonally on a structured glass capillary. By applying sinusoidal signals with a defined phase difference (φ) to these transducers, we can adjust the ultrasonic resonant field in the capillary’s microchannel to rotate the acoustic pressure nodal plane at the cross-section. This rotation creates an acoustic radiation force that traps and aligns suspended objects along the rotated pressure node. Within a specific operational range, specimens are securely trapped along the channel axis and rotated at a controlled angle α relative to φ (following the relationship α ≈ φ/2), demonstrating stepwise rotational control. By dynamically adjusting the phase difference φ from 0 to 4π, we can achieve controlled full 360° rotation of a trapped object. Further parametric numerical studies are conducted to showcase the effectiveness of this technique at various operating parameters for the controlled rotational manipulation of objects suspended in a fluid. The use of a glass capillary and external piezoelectric transducers offers a smooth and biologically permissive environment, simplifying fabrication and showing significant potential for applications in micro-robotics, single-cell analysis, and organism-level studies in fields like developmental biology.

Introduction

The precise, contactless manipulation of microscopic entities—from synthetic particles to biological cells and living organisms—is a cornerstone capability in modern microsystems research and lab-on-a-chip technology1–3. Particle manipulation forms the foundational basis for a vast array of applications, including high-throughput cell sorting, ultrasensitive diagnostic assays, the assembly of advanced materials, and the fundamental study of cell-cell interactions. As these technologies strive for greater functionality and miniaturization, the demand for sophisticated control schemes that extend beyond simple translation has grown substantially. In this context, rotational manipulation has emerged as a critical yet challenging frontier. The ability to precisely orient a single cell, a particle-based micro-tool, a developing embryo, or a living organism unlocks transformative possibilities: enabling comprehensive three-dimensional imaging, facilitating single-cell biopsy and analysis, studying cellular mechanical properties via rotational shear, controlling microscopic robotic elements, and observing developmental processes in model organisms from any angle.

Acoustofluidics, which leverages acoustic forces for contactless control within fluidic environments, has emerged as a leading platform for manipulating objects4–7. While techniques for patterning8,9, sorting10,11, trapping12,13, routing14,15, focusing16,17, gripping18,19, shaking20, translating21,22, settling23, and separating24,25 particles using acoustic forces are well-established, generating controlled rotational motion poses specific challenges. Current acoustofluidic rotation strategies primarily rely on two physical phenomena, both of which typically operate in continuous modes. One common approach involves using acoustic streaming vortices26, typically created by oscillating bubbles27–30 or solid structures31–33, to generate localized fluid circulations that impart a viscous shear stress on objects. Another method involves establishing a rotational acoustic torque on suspensions, often through complex transducer arrangements or patterned substrates in wide fluids34–38. While effective, these approaches have limitations. Streaming-based methods may compromise stable positional trapping, leading to unwanted translation during rotation. Techniques requiring intricate channel geometries or surface excitations can increase fabrication complexity, cost, and limit biocompatibility. Furthermore, these techniques typically rotate suspensions continuously, making it challenging to orient them to a specific angle when necessary. Therefore, there is a need for a more direct, robust, and simplified mechanism that combines stable trapping with precise rotational control in a single, biocompatible platform.

In this work, we introduce a novel and fundamentally distinct approach that addresses this need by directly rotating the acoustic pressure node or Gor’kov potential field itself. We present a simple system composed of orthogonal pairs of phase-controllable PZT transducers (PZTs) mounted on a structured glass capillary. By applying sinusoidal signals with a defined phase difference (φ) to these transducers, we dynamically steer the orientation of the standing bulk acoustic wave (BAW) field’s pressure node—the site of stable particle trapping—within the capillary’s cross-section. Suspended objects are first captured at the pressure node by the primary acoustic radiation force and then compelled to align with and follow these rotating nodes. We establish that the rotation angle (α) of a trapped microparticle cluster or organism is approximately equal to half the electronic phase difference (α≈φ⁄2), offering a straightforward digital control paradigm. It is worth noting that the present mechanism produces reorientation of trapped anisotropic objects following the rotated acoustic potential well, rather than continuous spinning induced by acoustic radiation torque. This distinction is fundamental: spherical particles, lacking a preferred orientation, would not exhibit observable rotation, whereas the intended biological targets (e.g., zebrafish larvae, cells, and embryo-like structures) are inherently anisotropic and thus amenable to this reorientation strategy.

This node-rotation mechanism provides significant advantages. It enables 360° rotations, both in steps and continuously, while maintaining excellent positional stability. The use of a smooth glass capillary and externally attached transducers offers an environment that is well-tolerated by biological specimens like zebrafish larvae, while simultaneously reducing fabrication costs and complexity compared to etched or lithographically defined chips. By successfully demonstrating the controlled rotation of both inert microparticles and live zebrafish larvae, this work bridges a critical gap between fundamental micromechanical control and impactful biological applications. Our platform not only introduces a new principle for rotational acoustofluidics but also provides a versatile and accessible tool for advancing research in microrobotics, single-cell analysis, and developmental biology, where precise orientation control is paramount. Concrete application scenarios enabled by this rotational manipulation capability include: (i) 3D tomographic imaging of zebrafish larvae, where rotating the specimen allows multi-angle image acquisition for 3D reconstruction without physical contact; (ii) phenotype analysis in developmental biology, where specific morphological features (e.g., heart looping, fin curvature) can be examined from optimal viewing angles; (iii) micro-robotic assembly, where anisotropic building blocks can be oriented prior to joining; and (iv) single-cell biopsy, where precise orientation of cells enables targeted membrane penetration with minimal damage.

Material properties

Polystyrene particles

In the experiments, fluorescent polystyrene particles (Yuan Biotech) with a diameter of 10 µm were tested. We used a polystyrene particle solution with a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. This solution was prepared by diluting 10 μm polystyrene particles, initially at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, with distilled water.

Zebrafish larvae

The zebrafish larvae utilized in the experiment were from the TU strain and were cultured in a formulated nutrient solution containing 0.25 mg NaHCO3, 0.5 mg KCl, 1 mg CaCl2, and 35 mg NaCl per 1 L of total volume. After being cultured in a petri dish at room temperature, zebrafish embryos gradually developed into zebrafish larvae. Zebrafish larvae with a bent body morphology were deliberately selected for the rotation experiments because their asymmetry facilitates visual identification of orientation changes. Larvae with straight bodies are equally trappable but show less pronounced changes in dual-plane images during rotation. The average length of selected larvae (0–5 dpf) was approximately 4 mm, with head and abdomen diameters of approximately 0.6 mm. This study employed the MS-222 buffer solution to induce reversible anaesthesia in zebrafish larvae. Larvae exhibiting excessive movement (even under anaesthesia) were excluded. A pipette was used to aspirate the zebrafish larvae and transfer them to the channel of the capillary. A syringe connected to the opposite end of the capillary served as a negative pressure generator, allowing fine control over the axial position of the zebrafish larva. The larva was positioned approximately midway along the 50 mm capillary length to minimize end effects on the acoustic field.

Trapping operation

Acoustic trapping of 10 μm polystyrene particles was conducted first, followed by the rotational manipulation of microparticles and zebrafish larvae. The solutions were introduced into the channel of the glass capillary using a 20 mL syringe pump. Without ultrasonic excitation, the particles were monodispersed in water. When the PZT was activated at the channel resonance, microparticles experienced the strongest acoustic forces and moved fastest towards the acoustic pressure nodes or Gor’kov potential minima. This can result in the formation of regular patterns in the cross-section of the channel41. In this study, a cross-sectional mode was excited by activating each PZT individually or both simultaneously. Specifically, for the most fundamental cross-sectional mode, 10 μm polystyrene microparticles suspended in water can be stably trapped at the centre of the channel’s cross-section along the axis under the influence of acoustic forces42.

Characterization of orientation and circumferential rotation of trapped objects

Two sinusoidal signals, both initially with zero phase, were generated from a dual-channel signal generator and applied to actuate the two PZTs. By adjusting the phase of the signal in one channel, a phase difference between the two PZTs and their resulting acoustic fields was introduced. The subsequent changes in the orientation of trapped objects, including a microparticle cluster and zebrafish larva within the channel, were then observed.

It was found that during the rotation of the orientation of the trapped objects, their overall structure remained relatively unchanged. By utilizing the dimensions of trapped objects (e.g., a cluster of microparticles or zebrafish larvae), we can estimate their orientation and calculate the rotation angle at each operating phase difference. Flow conditions during experiments

All trapping and rotation experiments were performed under static fluid conditions. After introducing the particle suspension or zebrafish larva into the capillary, the syringe pump was turned off, and both ends of the capillary were left open to the atmosphere to equalize pressure. This static condition ensures that the primary acoustic radiation force is the dominant mechanism governing particle motion, with negligible contribution from background flow. For zebrafish larvae, the static condition also minimizes stress on the specimen. The absence of flow was verified by observing the absence of drift of free particles before acoustic actuation.

Numerical

A two-dimensional numerical model was employed to simulate acoustofluidic fields and the rotation of acoustic pressure nodes and trapped microparticles in the cross-section of a glass capillary channel using COMSOL. Let (r, θ) and (x, y) be the polar and Cartesian coordinate systems defined in the cross-section of the channel, with the origin at the centre, as depicted in Fig. 3a. Initially, the acoustic field in the circular channel of the capillary was simulated by implementing a coupling model that considered both the glass and fluid layers. The impact of various operating parameters on the outcomes was then investigated. The model, as illustrated in Fig. 3a, represents a cross-section of the glass capillary used in experiments. The initial geometry of the cross-sectional model consisted of a circular inner domain with a diameter of 900 μm, modelled as water (with density ρf = 998.2 kg/m3, sound speed cf = 1481.4 m/s, and dynamic viscosity μf = 1.01 mPa·s), and an outer domain bounded by a square with side of 1500 μm, modelled as quartz glass (with density ρg = 2200 kg/m3, sound speed cg = 5720 m/s, Young’s modulus E = 72 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.17). By incorporating the glass layer, the model captures the effects of its interaction with the fluid on the resulting acoustofluidic fields and acoustophoretic motion of suspended particles in the fluid. Previous studies have shown that the cross-sectional acoustic resonance in a glass capillary with a circular channel surrounded by glass with a square outer cross-section may differ when simulated using a coupling model that considers both the glass and fluid layers, compared to the theoretical resonant mode derived from a reduced-fluid model that only considers the circular fluid layer (see, e.g., Lei et al.43, who demonstrated that the glass layer shifts the resonant frequency and modifies the pressure node patterns in Chladni figure formation).

Briefly, the model couples pressure acoustics in the frequency domain, creeping flow for acoustic streaming, and particle tracing for microparticle motion. The governing equations for the acoustic pressure field, streaming field, and particle motion follow standard formulations from previous studies42,44 and are provided in full in supplementary material (Section S2).

An incident pressure field boundary condition was applied to two exterior orthogonal glass walls to represent the excitation of PZTs, while the remaining walls were set as impedance boundary conditions (with impedance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Z}_{n}={\rho }_{{\rm{air}}}{c}_{{\rm{air}}}$$\end{document}Zn=ρaircair) to represent the physics of the glass-air interface, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The equivalence between the physical PZT excitation and the incident pressure boundary condition in the numerical model is established as follows. Each PZT, when actuated by a sinusoidal voltage, generates a longitudinal strain that is transmitted to the adjacent glass wall as a time-varying normal stress. For a thickness-expansion mode PZT bonded to a glass wall, the dominant coupling mechanism is pressure loading normal to the interface, with minimal shear contribution due to the low shear coupling of the adhesive layer. Therefore, we model each PZT as a uniform incident pressure wave applied over the 1.5 mm width of the glass wall. The numerical resonant frequency was identified using a frequency sweep approach by sweeping frequencies around the theoretical resonant frequency fmn (presented in equation (S4))45 to find the frequency that gives the maximum acoustic energy density in the fluid domain.

Results and discussion

Numerical simulations

In this section, we will present the numerical simulation results of a two-dimensional glass-water model with the same dimensions as the capillary cross-section. Figure 3b–d display the modelled acoustic pressure, acoustic streaming, and acoustophoretic motion of polystyrene particles with a diameter of 10 μm for φ = 0 and β = 1, where β = A2/A1 is the amplitude ratio of the pressure waves generated by the two PZTs (A1 and A2 are the pressure amplitudes at the two excitation boundaries), at the (1, 0) mode. Experimentally, β = 1 corresponds to driving both PZTs with the same peak-to-peak voltage. Further discussion on the effect of β values on the simulation results is included in Section 3.4.1 below.

It is observed that the modelled acoustic pressure field in the fluid domain is not perfectly symmetric to the pressure nodal plane (annotated by a white dashed line in Fig. 3b), deviating from the symmetric theoretical (1, 0) mode described in equation (S3) or those simulated using a reduced-fluid model (e.g., in ref. 42). This asymmetry in acoustic resonance, primarily resulting from the non-axisymmetric boundary conditions and the glass layer, leads to a slight distortion of the four-vortex acoustic streaming pattern (Fig. 3c) and asymmetric trapping of microparticles (Fig. 3d) in the circular fluid domain. On the pressure nodal plane, the acoustic radiation force on 10 μm polystyrene particles is greater than the drag force induced by acoustic streaming, which results in centre-focusing, similar to the findings from a reduced-fluid model in a previous study42. A small number of particles, particularly those near pressure antinodes, were observed to be propelled towards the water-glass interface by the acoustic radiation force. However, it is evident that most particles within the fluid domain tend to align along the pressure nodal plane, forming a line in this two-dimensional model or a thin sheet of particles in three-dimensional space when considering the volume of the particles. The Gor’kov potential46 minima (dark regions in Fig. 3d) align with the pressure nodal plane (black regions in Fig. 3b), confirming that the primary acoustic radiation force, derived from the gradient of Gor’kov potential, is responsible for trapping particles at the pressure node. The rotation of the pressure nodal plane thus directly rotates the potential energy landscape, compelling trapped particles to follow the moving minima. An animation of the microparticle focusing process shown in Fig. 3(d) can be found in the supplementary material (Video 1).

The influence of the phase difference φ on the total pressure pt for β = 1 is illustrated in Fig. 3e, showing the evolution of the acoustic pressure field at different values of φ. It is observed that there is a stepwise rotation of the overall resonant mode with increasing φ. This rotation results in a 180° change in the orientation of the pressure node as φ (in radians) increases from 0 to 2π and a 360° rotation for every change of 4π in φ. The rotation process, as quantitatively shown in Fig. 3f, reveals that the angle of rotation, α, generally moves along the linear relationship α = φ⁄2 as φ increases. The numerical results follow the linear relationship consistently when φ = kπ, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{\mathbb{\in }}{\mathbb{Z}}$$\end{document}k∈Z, while differences are observed at other values of φ. Specifically, it is interesting to note that the results presented in Fig. 3g indicate that the slight difference between α and φ/2 varies sinusoidally as φ increases. Despite the complex variations, the modelling results suggest that this method provides a robust technique for rotating pressure nodes, allowing for an on-demand, stepwise manipulation in the orientation of objects that are trapped along the pressure nodal plane. An animation showing the rotation process of the simulated acoustic pressure node due to phase adjustment, as presented in Fig. 3e, can be found in the supplementary material (Video 2).

It is worth discussing further the physical origin of the asymmetric acoustic resonance observed in Fig. 3b. Unlike reduced-fluid models that treat the fluid domain with idealized rigid or pressure-release boundaries, our coupled glass–fluid model accounts for the finite acoustic impedance of the quartz glass layer (characteristic impedance Zg = ρgcg ≈12.6 × 106 kg·m−2·s−1, compared to Zf = ρf cf ≈1.48 × 106 kg·m−2·s−1 for water). The impedance mismatch at the glass–water interface leads to partial wave reflection and transmission, while the non-axisymmetric boundary conditions—incident waves applied only on two adjacent exterior walls—break the rotational symmetry of the theoretical (1, 0) mode described by equation (S3). Consequently, the pressure nodal plane becomes slightly tilted and distorted, as shown in Fig. 3b. This asymmetry is essential for the rotation mechanism: when a phase difference φ is introduced, the interference pattern between the two orthogonally propagating waves shifts continuously, rotating the nodal plane. The rotation follows α≈φ/2 because the phase difference directly modulates the relative phase of the two standing wave components, and the nodal plane orientation is determined by the condition that the total pressure field vanishes, which yields a linear relationship for small distortions.

Experiments: controlled rotation of microparticle clusters

Figure 4 demonstrates the acoustic trapping of a microparticle cluster and the controlled rotation of its orientation in our orthogonally phased BAW acoustofluidic device. The consecutive frames in Fig. 4a show how the orientation of a trapped cluster of microparticles can be rotationally manipulated by adjusting the phase difference between the two actuated transducers. It can be observed that under different phase difference conditions, the particle cluster exhibits different characteristics when viewed from both the xz and yz planes of the channel. From a single planar observational perspective, the trapped particles undergo a cyclic transformation between a cluster-like shape and a linear shape. This suggests a rotation in the spatial orientation of the particle cluster, assuming the cluster behaves as a rigid body. The three unfocused stripe‑like patterns observed in the central region of each frame in Fig. 4a are optical artefacts arising from the 45°‑rotated square capillary and the prism‑based dual‑plane imaging configuration. They correspond to multiply‑reflected light at the glass–air and glass–water interfaces: the central line corresponds to the light that passes straight through the capillary, while the two outer lines arise from total internal reflection at the glass–air interfaces of the square capillary’s outer walls (see also annotated schematic in Fig. 2a). Error bars of ± 10° are included to account for the uncertainty in determining the orientation of the cluster. The error bars of ± 10° represent the cumulative uncertainty arising from multiple sources: (i) finite image resolution; (ii) subjective delineation of cluster boundaries during manual annotation; (iii) optical misalignment in the experimental setup; (iv) manufacturing tolerances inherent to the 67.5° prisms; and (v) possible non-rigid deformation of the cluster structure, as elaborated in the subsequent section. Despite this magnitude of uncertainty, the experimental data consistently follow the trend of the numerical simulations, with a linear relationship α≈φ/2 across the full range of phase differences.

Several factors may contribute to the minor discrepancies between the experimental rotation angles and the ideal linear relationship α≈φ/2 observed in Fig. 4b. (1) First, the acoustic field along the capillary channel may not be perfectly uniform in the axial direction due to slight variations in channel geometry or PZT attachment, leading to position-dependent rotation behaviour. (2) Second, the two PZTs, although nominally identical, may exhibit small differences in their electromechanical response, resulting in a non-unity amplitude ratio β (see Section 3.4.1) and introducing a systematic offset in the rotation. (3) Third, the measurement of cluster orientation from dual-plane images relies on manually identifying the widths wx and wy, which is subject to human error, particularly for irregularly shaped clusters. The error bars of ± 10° reflect these combined uncertainties. (4) Finally, the assumption that the microparticle cluster rotates as a rigid body may not hold perfectly. The cluster experiences collective trapping: inter-particle attractive forces (secondary acoustic forces and possible depletion interactions) create an effective cohesion that resists relative motion. As a result, the cluster behaves as a metastable aggregate that rotates together rather than disassembling and reforming. This is supported by the observation that the cluster’s overall shape (e.g., the concave features noted in Fig. 4a) is preserved after a 2π rotation. However, in reality, individual particles within the cluster may undergo relative motion—such as sliding or repositioning—as the acoustic potential well rotates, particularly if the acoustic trapping force is not uniformly distributed across the cluster. Such internal rearrangements could cause the overall shape of the cluster to evolve during rotation, introducing errors when determining orientation from the widths wx and wy. This effect likely contributes to the scatter observed in Fig. 4b and is partially accounted for by the ± 10° uncertainty.

Experiments: controlled rotation of zebrafish larvae

We will now demonstrate the acoustic trapping and controlled rotational manipulation of zebrafish larvae in the device. Without ultrasonic excitation, a zebrafish larva will naturally settle at the channel bottom due to the effect of gravity. However, when a (1, 0) resonant mode is created in the channel’s cross-section, a zebrafish can be levitated along the channel axis as the magnitude of the acoustic radiation force overcomes gravity. Further experimental results indicate that on-demand rotation of zebrafish larvae can be effectively achieved by adjusting the phase difference between the ultrasonic excitations.

This indicates a shared rotational mechanism between zebrafish and microparticle clusters, further confirming the capability of this technique in manipulating microparticles and millimetre-scale objects.

It is important to note that while the dual-channel imaging under a π/2 phase difference in Fig. 5a allowed for ideal lateral and top-view observations of the zebrafish larva simultaneously, this method may not be universally applicable to all individuals due to variations in body shape. Different body shapes have different positions of the centre of gravity, which can result in varying force balance postures despite having the same distribution of acoustic radiation force. However, this is not a problem in our orthogonally phase-adjustable BAW acoustofluidic system, where continuous rotation changes in the sound field allow for precise observation of zebrafish from any arbitrary angle by adjusting the phase difference accordingly.

To assess potential effects of acoustic trapping on biological activity, we monitored zebrafish larvae immediately after acoustic exposure (typical duration 3–10 min per larva) and after transfer to fresh culture medium. Larvae exposed to the acoustic field at 1.073 MHz with peak-to-peak voltage 14 V resumed normal swimming behaviour within several minutes after cessation of anaesthesia (MS-222 washout). No visible morphological damage (e.g., fin or eye abnormalities) was observed in any of the larvae tested. Moreover, larvae that were returned to the culture dish and raised for an additional 24 h showed normal developmental progression (swimming and feeding responses) indistinguishable from control larvae that were not acoustically trapped. These observations suggest that the acoustic trapping and rotation conditions used in this study are non-damaging to zebrafish larvae at the developmental stages tested (0–5 dpf). However, we note that a systematic quantitative study of long-term viability was beyond the scope of this work.

Parametric numerical studies

Effects of amplitude ratio of the two incident waves on the node rotations

Although the two PZTs were designed to be identical, their actuating efficiency may not be exactly the same, leading to a potential discrepancy in the pressure amplitude generated by each one when actuated individually at the same voltage. Therefore, it would be interesting to know how the particles perform when the two pressure amplitude components are different, i.e., when β does not exactly equal one. In this section, we will numerically investigate how the distribution of the resonant acoustic pressure field and its rotation with respect to φ are related to the value of β.

The impact of the phase difference φ on the total pressure pt for four different β values, namely 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5, is shown and compared in Fig. 6a. It is demonstrated that, for all the values of β considered, there is a gradual rotation of the overall resonant mode with increasing φ. A 2π rotation is observed for all the presented cases as φ increases from 0 to 4π, while the specific relationship between α and φ slightly varies among them. Numerical results indicate that the discrepancy between the modelled (1, 0) mode and the theoretical pattern intensifies as φ increases, and the desired smooth 2π rotation of the pressure nodal plane was not observed when β is greater than approximately 3 or less than approximately 1/3. The transition from smooth to abrupt rotation as β increases beyond approximately 3 can be understood by examining the relative contributions of the two orthogonal acoustic fields. When β = 1, the two incident waves have equal amplitude, and the total pressure field is the sum of two comparable orthogonal standing waves. The nodal plane orientation then varies continuously with φ. However, when β is large (e.g., β = 2.5), the wave from the stronger PZT dominates the total pressure field. In this regime, the weaker wave can only perturb the nodal plane orientation when its relative phase φ is such that it constructively or destructively interferes with the dominant wave. As φ varies, the nodal plane remains locked near the orientation of the dominant wave’s nodal plane for most φ values, then rapidly ‘snaps’ to a new orientation when the interference condition favours a switch. This behaviour resembles a bifurcation in the phase response of a weakly driven nonlinear oscillator. For β > 3, the weaker wave’s contribution is insufficient to overcome the trapping effect of the dominant wave, and no complete 2π rotation of the nodal plane is achieved.

Furthermore, it should be noted that assuming the two PZTs have identical actuating efficiency, the results presented in Fig. 6a also predict the rotation performance of the BAW acoustofluidic system when the PZTs are driven by different peak-to-peak voltages.

Effects of fluid properties on resonant modes and rotation

While all experiments in this study were conducted in water, the generality of the rotation mechanism can be extended to other fluids by considering how key parameters—density (ρf), sound speed (cf), and viscosity (μf)—influence the acoustic field. The resonant frequency for a given cross-sectional mode scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{{mn}}\propto {c}_{f}/d$$\end{document}fmn∝cf/d (equation (S4)), so fluids with higher sound speed (e.g., water–glycerol mixtures, cf≈1500–1900 m/s) shift the resonance upward, while lower sound speed fluids (e.g., oils, cf≈1200–1400 m/s) shift it downward. The pressure node distribution depends primarily on the ratio of the fluid’s acoustic impedance to that of the glass wall (Zf/Zg). For fluids with impedance close to water (e.g., biological buffers, cell culture media), the glass–fluid coupling remains similar, and the rotation mechanism should perform comparably. For fluids with significantly higher viscosity (e.g., glycerol solutions with μf > 10 mPa·s), acoustic streaming is attenuated, which may reduce streaming-induced drag on particles but also slow the focusing time. For very low viscosity fluids (e.g., organic solvents like ethanol, ρf≈790 kg/m3, cf≈1140 m/s), the acoustic radiation force is reduced due to lower density and compressibility contrast, and stable trapping may require higher actuation voltages.

Distinction between reorientation and acoustic torque-induced rotation

It is important to clarify that the rotation mechanism presented in this work is a reorientation of the trapped object following the rotated acoustic pressure nodal plane, not a continuous spinning motion driven by an acoustic torque. The primary acoustic radiation force traps anisotropic objects (e.g., particle clusters or elongated zebrafish larvae) at the pressure node, and as the node rotates, the object rotates with it to minimize its potential energy. For a perfectly spherical particle, which has no preferred orientation in a rotationally symmetric potential well, the proposed method would not produce any observable rotation—the sphere would remain stationary while the nodal plane rotates around it. However, this does not limit the utility of the method for the intended applications, which involve imaging and manipulation of biological specimens (which are almost always non-spherical) and anisotropic synthetic particles.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed an acoustofluidic system using phase-controlled PZTs on two orthogonal sides of a structured glass capillary. This design enables the rotational manipulation of microparticles and zebrafish larvae in water, with precise control over rotation angles. By activating the PZTs with signals of a phase difference (φ), we can rotate the acoustic pressure node at the channel’s cross-section, generating an acoustic radiation force that moves the trapped objects within the fluid. This alignment along the pressure nodal plane enables accurate trapping and rotation of trapped objects, with rotation angles closely related to half of the phase difference (φ/2). By adjusting the phase difference from 0 to 4π, zebrafish larvae or microparticles can be rotated 360° in steps or continuously. This method provides a direct and controlled alternative to continuous rotating mechanisms driven by acoustic streaming vortices or viscous torque. Parametric numerical studies showed that controlled stepwise rotation can be achieved in glass capillaries using different operating frequencies, amplitude ratios, and glass dimensions and shapes. The platform is characterized by its simplicity, low cost, and excellent control, effectively decoupling rotational from translational manipulation.

Moreover, the use of a glass capillary and the normal recovery of zebrafish larvae after acoustic exposure indicate that the system is well-tolerated by biological specimens, supporting its utility for organism-level studies. Potential applications of this platform include high-throughput 3D morphological screening of small model organisms, orientation-controlled micro-assembly for tissue engineering, and on-chip phenotyping of drug responses in zebrafish larvae. The simplicity and low cost of the glass capillary design also make it suitable for integration with standard laboratory microscopes without specialized microfabrication facilities.
