Barriers to accessing cervical cancer prevention services among out-of-school young women in Ibadan
Authors: Olayemi Oluwadamilola Sangoniyi, Magbagbeola David Dairo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer accounts for an estimated 604,127 cases and 341,831 deaths. 84% of cervical cancer morbidities and 88% of cervical cancer-related mortality in such areas are attributed to women in developing countries. Out-of-school young women are a vulnerable and underserved population in public health, having limited access to health education and prevention services. This study aims to identify the willingness and barriers to accessing cervical cancer prevention services which is key to developing strategies that enhance the uptake among out-of-school young women.MethodsIn a community-based cross-sectional study among 356 out-of-school young women aged 15–24 years in Ibadan North Local Government Area, eligible respondents were selected using a multistage sampling technique. Data was collected using structured questionnaires and analysed with SPSS version 27.ResultsMost respondents (n = 224, 62.9%) were aged 15–19 years, with the mean age being 18.9 ± 2.42, and unemployed (n = 327, 91.9%). Main barriers to accessing cervical cancer prevention services include lack of awareness of the strategy (HPV vaccine (n = 302/356, 84.8%, Pap Smear − 306/356, 86%), absence of services centre (HPV vaccination centre - n = 115/356, 32.3%, Pap smear screening centre− 103, 28.9% and HPV test – n = 89, 25%), fear of positive result (HPV test − 104/356, 29.2%) and high cost of screening (HPV test − 303/356, 85.1%).ConclusionLow knowledge, high cost, lack of awareness of services and fear of positive results significantly impede access to cervical cancer preventive services. Educational campaigns to increase awareness and uptake of cervical cancer prevention services are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04651-4.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cervical cancer prevention service, Reported barriers, Willingness, Out-of-school young women

Background

Cervical cancer is a disease of the female reproductive organ caused by 15 high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV), in which the cells of the cervix become malignant [1]. Cervical cancer has remained a global public health concern, accounting for an estimated 604,127 cases and 341,831 deaths, according to the report by GLOBOCAN 2020 [2], mostly affecting women from poor economic backgrounds residing in developing countries, and this is known to account for about 84% of cervical cancer morbidities and 88% of cervical cancer-related mortalities in such areas [3]. It is the second leading cancer after breast cancer and the second most prevalent cancer among women of reproductive age (15–44 years) in Nigeria [1]. For more than three decades in developing countries, the rate at which Cervical cancer affects young women has been alarming and has increased from 10% to 40% [4]. Although HPV has been proven to be the main cause of cancer of the cervix [5], its risk factors include exposure to sexual intercourse at an early age, early marriage, multiple childbirths, immune suppression, smoking, exposure to human papillomavirus (HPV), oral contraceptives, multiple sexual partners, recurring sexually transmitted diseases (STDs), and poor socioeconomic status [5, 6]. Cervical cancer prevention services are a major public health approach to cervical cancer control and have helped reduce the burden of the disease in developed countries [6]. Aside from the (Pap) test, other cervical cancer prevention strategies include vaccination against the human papillomavirus [7]. However, cervical cancer is treatable when detected early [8].

In Africa, the incidence and mortality of Cervical cancer are highest despite the establishment of a national cancer control policy that co-opts Cervical cancer awareness, the implementation of HPV vaccination programmes, and HPV-DNA (Human Papillomavirus Vaccine-Deoxyribonucleic acid) testing or VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic acid), with the treatment of pre-cervical lesions [1, 9]. Research has shown poor acceptance of cervical cancer prevention initiatives in sub-Saharan Africa [10]. On a global scale, only 6% of girls have received the HPV vaccine, leaving more than 90% of girls without access to this vaccination [11]. In addition, recent studies showed that the utilisation rate of cervical cancer screening services ranges from 3 to 54.1% [12]. There is also wide variation in the cervical cancer screening coverage; which is estimated to be between 2 and 20% and 0.4–14% in the urban and rural regions of sub-Saharan Africa, respectively [13]. Refusal or non-acceptance of HPV vaccine has significant consequences for the future sexual, physical, and reproductive health of young women [14]. The barriers young women face in assessing cervical cancer prevention services include fear of receiving unfavourable results of screening, a low perception of personal risk, apprehension about potential embarrassment [15], fear of side effects from the HPV vaccine, and fear of the unknown [16]. Lack of knowledge and poverty are factors that hinder the broad uptake of human papillomavirus vaccines among adolescents aged 10–18 years, of which young women are inclusive [17]. Nigeria as a country is also faced with challenges of accessibility and utilisation of cervical cancer screening services.

Young women are a vital target group for HPV vaccine promotion. This is because of the high prevalence of HPV infection reported in this age group, low HPV vaccination rates, increased engagement with healthcare services due to reproductive activities, and their increased capacity to independently make health decisions [18]. It is therefore crucial to recognise the challenges that are peculiar to out-of-school young women regarding the utilisation of cervical cancer screening services such as HPV vaccination and screening.

Methods

Study area

This study was carried out in some selected communities within Ibadan North Local Government in Ibadan, the state capital of Oyo State, Southwest Nigeria. The communities were Isale-afaa, Yemetu, Idi-ogungun, and Sango communities of Ibadan North Local Government Area, located in the urbanized area of Ibadan. It is located within the geographical coordinates of latitude E003.45, 033 to E003.9500 and longitude N07.1810 to N07.2350, covering an area of 27 square kilometers. The administrative headquarters of this area is in Agodi. It is bordered by Ido and Ibadan North-West Local Governments to the west, while it shares its eastern boundaries with Lagelu, Egbeda, and Ibadan South-East Local Governments. Also, to the north, it is bordered by Akinyele Local Government [19]. The commonest religious practices in this locality are Christianity and Islam, with some adherence to indigenous traditional religions. It is ethnically diverse but predominantly inhabited by Yoruba-speaking people. The area covers a landmass of 27 square kilometers and consists of 12 political wards and 93 communities. The population of Ibadan North Local Government Area is approximately 308,118 people, consisting of 152,608 males and 155,511 females, according to the National Population Census conducted in 2006. The city is served by multiple primary healthcare centers, in addition to the state hospital and tertiary health facilities like Adeoyo Maternity Teaching Hospital (AMTH) and University College Hospital (UCH), which provide specialized care for women, children, and adults, including oncology services. Cervical cancer screening services including HPV vaccine is available at the University College Hospital Ibadan. In addition, cervical cancer screening is also available in a private health facility within the local government area. The primary healthcare centers offer various preventive health services, including antenatal care, vaccination, health education, and treatment of minor ailments [17].

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study was carried out among out-of-school young women aged 15–24 in Ibadan North Local Government.

Eligibility criteria

The study targeted mentally and physically fit young women aged 15–24 years who had either never been to school, had discontinued schooling, or had been out of school for over one year. Participants are either married or unmarried, reside in Isale-Alfa, Yemetu, Idi-ogungun, and Sango communities, and include individuals like apprentices, sales assistants, and street vendors, amongst others, who are willing to take part in the survey.

The targeted sample size was 424, however only 356 questionnaires were valid and analyzed (84% response rate). This is because some respondents were unwilling to continue the study, while some skip responses. These incomplete responses make the questionnaires unfit for analyses. However, the respondents indicated no reason for the decision to terminate their participation other than that there have been several students coming to interview them on several health topics. The feedback from these students were not received and they are not comfortable wasting their time with the researchers. This shortfall however is unlikely to have substantially affected precision or power. There is no information that shows that these non-responders differ from the respondents in this study.

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was used to select respondents in this study. The selection of the out-of-school young women recruited into this study was carried out in three (3) stages:

Stage 1

A simple random sampling technique by balloting without replacement was used to select four (4) out of the twelve (12) political wards in Ibadan North Local Government. The selected political wards included in this study are Oke Are, Yemetu, Agodi, and Sango.

Stage 2

A simple random sampling technique by balloting without replacement was used to select a community from each of the four selected political wards. A total of four communities were selected, and these are: Isale Aafa, Yemetu, Idi-ogungun, and Sango.

Stage 3

A cluster random sampling technique in which all available and willing women within the age group 15–24 were enrolled into the study. A cluster is the community selected for the study. To obtain the participants enrolled in the study the investigator identifies the center of the community. By convenience, a direction of the street is decided by spinning a bottle. The direction in which the bottle settles after spinning is chosen as the direction of the street in which selection of the women will be made. After deciding the direction of the street, the first house is located and all eligible women in the house are invited. By simple balloting one woman is selected from each house and interviewed. Only one woman is interviewed in each household. Subsequently houses were chosen consecutively and one woman interviewed in each of them. These women were recruited into the study until the desired sample size was attained. These women have to meet the specified eligibility criteria which are being a mentally and physically fit young women aged 15–24 years who had either never been to school, had discontinued schooling, or had been out of school for over one year.

Data collection instrument and procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire developed by the researchers based on a review of relevant literature. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Yoruba, the predominant language in Ibadan, and back-translated into English to ensure accuracy. The instrument included items assessing respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of cervical cancer and its prevention, willingness to utilize cervical cancer prevention services, and perceived barriers to accessing these services.

Data were collected using printed questionnaires administered by trained interviewers. A copy of the English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Pretest and pilot study

Research assistants who were engaged in this study were duly trained on the data collection and data management processes while ensuring that research ethics were maintained. Pretesting of the research instrument (pilot study) was conducted among out-of-school young women in Oremeji community of Ibadan North East Local Government, a locality that has similar socio-demographic characteristics and settings with the selected communities for this study, using 10% of the calculated sample size (43) before the commencement of the main study. The purpose of this was to determine the accuracy, reliability, and validity of the questions. The pre-tested questionnaires were then modified.

Study variables

The dependent variables are reported barriers to accessing cervical cancer prevention services and willingness to access cervical cancer prevention services. The independent variables are age, utilization of cervical cancer prevention services, and knowledge of cervical cancer prevention services.

Data management and analysis

A serial code was assigned to each questionnaire for identification. A separate record of the identification was kept by the researcher for reference purposes and to easily track missing questionnaires. Questionnaires that were not filled were counted as void and scored as non-response; hence, such questionnaires were not analyzed, while filled questionnaires were kept safe to avoid missing questionnaires. Additionally, the data were sorted and cleaned using the MS-Excel 2013 software, then entered and analyzed using the Statistical Software Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. Descriptive data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, such as a frequency distribution table, to summarize the data about the different variables.

Knowledge of cervical cancer as a disease was graded on a 26-item scale. For each item on the disease knowledge scale, a score of 2 was awarded for correct responses and 0 for incorrect and “I don’t know” responses. The minimum knowledge score was 0, and the maximum score was 56. Thereafter, the mid-point of the knowledge score was used as a cut-off for categorization of respondents into those with good and poor levels of knowledge on cervical cancer. Those with scores below the mid-point were categorized as having poor knowledge.

Similarly, for questions regarding the level of knowledge towards cervical cancer prevention services, it was graded on a 11-item scale. A score of 2 was assigned to correct responses and 0 for incorrect responses. The minimum knowledge on cervical cancer prevention score was 0, and the maximum score was 22. Thereafter, the mid-point score for cervical cancer prevention was used as a cut-off for categorization of respondents into those with good and poor levels of cervical cancer preventive score. Those with scores below the mid-point were categorized as having poor cervical cancer preventive knowledge.

Also, adopting the grading scale used by Tekle et al. (2020) [21], willingness to utilize cervical cancer prevention services was assessed using Likert-scale items comprising both positively and negatively worded statements. Responses were initially rated on a 5-point scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree). For positively worded items, “strongly agree” and “agree” were considered correct responses and assigned a score of 5, while all other responses were scored 0. For negatively worded items, the scoring was reversed such that “strongly disagree” and “disagree” were considered correct responses and assigned a score of 5, while the remaining responses were scored 0. Mid-point scores were obtained for the level of willingness towards the utilization of the HPV vaccine, Pap smear and HPV test, which was set as a cut-off mark. Respondents who had scored less than the cut-offs were considered as having “poor level of willingness”, and respondents having a with the cut-off score and above were considered as having “good level of willingness”.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 356 out of 424 questionnaires administered were completed, giving a response rate of 84%. About two-thirds, 224 (62.9%) of the respondents were between the ages of 15 and 19 years, and the mean age was 18.85 ± 2.42. Nearly three-quarters, 252(70.8%) of the respondents were Christians. The majority of the respondents, 334(93.8%), belonged to the Yoruba ethnic group. Most 340(95.5%) of the respondents had a secondary school education, while less than one-tenth, 15(4.2%) of the respondents had no formal education. A preponderance of 341 (95.8%) of the respondents were single. Most 327(91.9%) of the respondents are unemployed, while less than one-tenth, 29(8.1%) of the respondents belonging to the work force were self-employed, of which 11(3.1%) and 13(3.6%) of the respondents were skilled and partly-skilled respectively (Table 1).

Reported barriers to cervical cancer prevention services utilization

The majority, 302(84.8%) of the respondents were not aware of the HPV vaccine. Similarly, about one-third 115(32.3%) of the respondents were not aware of any HPV vaccination facility in their community, and more than one-fifth 86(24.2%) of the respondents reported that there was no HPV vaccination facility in their community.

More than three-quarter 306(86.0%) of the respondents were not aware of the Pap smear. Also, one-third 113(31.7%) of the respondents were not aware of any health facility providing Pap smear screening service in their community, and more than one-fifth 103(28.9%) of the respondents reported that there was no Pap smear screening centre in their community.

Most 303(85.1%) of the respondents reported that the HPV test is expensive. More than one-fifth 89(25.0%) of the respondents were not aware of any health facility providing HPV testing service in their community, and nearly one-third 104(29.2%) of the respondents feared having a positive result upon screening (Tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

The findings of this study show that out-of-school young women in Nigeria have limited knowledge of cervical cancer and its prevention services, revealing a significant gap in health education. Respondents who were aware of cervical cancer preventive services, it was found that awareness does not translate to willingness or actual utilization of these services, as observed in this study.

This gap between awareness and willingness presents a critical challenge. In contrast, a study conducted among in-school young women at Lagos State Polytechnic reported that 54.8% of the respondents were willing to receive the HPV vaccine [22]. This comparison highlights how access to information and formal education can positively influence willingness to engage in preventive health services.

This study also identified major barriers that prevented young women from using these services. The most common barrier was a lack of awareness; 84.8% of the respondents had never heard of the HPV vaccine. In addition, 32.3% of the respondents did not know where to get vaccinated, and 24.2% reported that no vaccination facility existed in their community. Cost was another significant hindrance. Although only 7.0% mentioned the vaccine as expensive, the price before December 2023 (NGN15,000 per dose) was likely too high for most, especially given that 91.9% of the participants were unemployed. Also, considering the value most Nigerians place on free and opportunistic programmes [23], most of these young women would prefer to wait for such moments when there is a free vaccination campaign before getting vaccinated.

This study highlights significant barriers to the uptake of Pap smear and HPV testing among out-of-school young women in Nigeria, which are consistent with findings from previous literature. The majority of the respondents (86.0%) had never heard of Pap smear screening, 31.7% were unaware of any health facility offering the service, and 28.9% reported the absence of such screening centres in their locality. The cost of screening was also noted as a constraint, particularly when payment was made out-of-pocket. These findings align with Okunade et al., who identified poor awareness, inadequate health education, and limited access to services as predictors of low Pap smear uptake among women in Lagos [9]. Similarly, this study found that 85.1% of participants considered HPV testing too expensive, 25.0% did not know where to obtain the test, and 29.2% reported fear of receiving a positive result. These factors reflect broader challenges reported by Beddoe, who emphasized that cervical cancer elimination efforts in low-resource settings are often impeded by high costs, infrastructural deficiencies, and psychosocial barriers such as fear and stigma [24].

Limitations of the study

The scoring of items in the analysis is not validated using psychometric tests. While the use of psychometric tests to validate the scales is desirable, there are reports within the scientific literature using non-validated questions to assess knowledge and other parameters. There are no validated scales available for measuring the construct of knowledge of cervical cancer preventive practices. Thus, it is considered a further realm of research to study the psychometric properties of instruments for assessing knowledge of cervical cancer and its prevention.

Conclusion

The interplay of poor knowledge, low level of willingness, negligible utilization, and significant barriers poses a serious public health concern. This situation renders out-of-school young women at high risk of developing cervical cancer, a largely preventable disease. Without access to school-based health education or regular contact with health services, many of these young women miss out on vital health information and care. There is an urgent need for targeted and practical interventions. Addressing the gap between intention and action is key to reducing the impact of cervical cancer and improving access to care for out-of-school young women in Ibadan.
