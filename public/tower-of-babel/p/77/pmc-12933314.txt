Gastrointestinal bleeding from an arterial-enteric fistula postcoil embolisation of GDA pseudoaneurysm after pancreatico-duodenectomy
Authors: Saimuralidhar Srinivasan, Gregg Franco, Annmarie Joyce, Anasuya Gunturi, Suman Koganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPostoperative bleeding from a gastroduodenal artery (GDA) pseudoaneurysm is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication following pancreatoduodenectomy. While it can often be controlled with timely intervention, it carries the risk of rare, challenging secondary complications. We discuss in this report an elderly male who underwent a pancreatico-duodenectomy. Fourteen months postsurgery, he presented with bleeding and abdominal pain. Following an immediate CT angiogram, endovascular coil embolisation of the GDA pseudoaneurysm, along with the placement of a covered stent in the common hepatic artery (CHA), was performed. The bleeding had stopped. Unfortunately, 2 months later, he endured another gastrointestinal bleed. An Esophagogastroduodenoscopy and a CTA were done, which showed a clot and confirmed the coils in the afferent limb of the jejunum at the pancreato-jejunal anastomosis site. An endoscopic attempt at retrieval was unsuccessful, and the coils eventually egressed spontaneously. This case underscores the clinical relevance of pseudoaneurysms in postpancreatico-duodenectomy patients and highlights the potential complications associated with interventions.

Background

Gastroduodenal artery (GDA) pseudoaneurysm (PSA) is a rare complication that arises postpancreatoduodenectomy (PD), in comparison to its counterparts that arise from the pancreaticoduodenal branches. A systematic review conducted by Brodie et al indicated the occurrence of GDA PSAs in 55 patients out of a total of 4227 patients who underwent pancreatic operations, leading to a reported incidence of 1.3%.1 The GDA is a branch of the common hepatic artery (CHA), which begins from the coeliac plexus of the descending aorta. The GDA supplies the greater curvature of the stomach, the first part of the duodenum and the head of the pancreas. GDA PSAs were once corrected primarily via an open approach. Over the past years, endovascular coil embolisation as well as stent graft approach over the open repair method has been the standard of care as reported in a systematic review by Lee et al.2 This case is unique, as it not only highlights the sequence of events surrounding GDA PSA rupture but also illustrates rare complications that can arise from the intervention and how to monitor and manage them postintervention. Here, we present the case of an elderly male who developed a GDA PSA following a PD and the complications associated with it.

Case presentation

An elderly patient in his early 80s with past medical history of atrial fibrillation (A-Fib) on low-dose aspirin and right foot osteomyelitis presented with painless jaundice, pruritus and weight loss and was diagnosed to have distal cholangiocarcinoma. He underwent pylorus-preserving pancreatico-duodenectomy with portal vein resection. Portal vein resection was done to achieve R0 resection. Pathology showed invasive, moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma involving the common bile duct and invading the pancreas. The tumour size was 3 cm. All margins were negative. 36 lymph nodes were negative. Final stage was pT1N0. Mismatch repair was intact. He received adjuvant Xeloda (gemcitabine) for 6 months, which had to be stopped due to grade 3 diarrhoea and grade 2 palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia. Ultimately, he chose not to resume chemotherapy and was started on surveillance since that time. More than an year later, he presented to the ED with abdominal pain and intra-abdominal bleeding.

Investigations

CTA was done immediately and demonstrated bleeding (figure 1) from a GDA PSA. He underwent coil embolisation of PSA followed by hepatic artery angioplasty with covered stent placement. He seemingly recovered from the bleed and was discharged home. However, shortly after, he presented with another episode of GI bleed in three months. EGD demonstrated clots in the pancreato-jejunal limb. CTA abdomen was notable for artefacts from coils in the upper abdomen with no active GI bleeding. The embolisation coils appeared to be present in the lumen of the afferent limb of the jejunum (figure 2).

Differential diagnosis

Based on the location of the coils on the CT and the clinical presentation of coffee ground emesis, we were reasonably certain that the bleeding was intraluminal and likely from fistulisation of the PSA, although mucosal ulceration with bleed from the afferent limb at the gastro-jejunostomy or hemosuccus from the pancreatico-jejunostomy was considered less likely.

Treatment

He underwent a repeat EGD that confirmed the coils in the afferent limb with an attempted removal, which was aborted in view of the brisk bleeding (figure 3). He developed transient hypotension with a drop in haemoglobin to 6.2. He was resuscitated with 2 units of packed cells and close haemodynamic monitoring in the Intensive care unit, from which he quickly recovered. He was subsequently planned for emergency exploration to undergo coil retrieval and revision of the anastomosis. A bedside KUB X-ray was done to identify the location of the coil prior to exploration. However, the X-ray showed that the coils had egressed distally (figure 4) without causing an obstruction and circumvented any need for an operative revision. The coils eventually passed out spontaneously with a bowel movement. He had a follow-up CT scan that demonstrated resolution of the PSA with an intact stent graft in the CHA (figure 5).

Outcome and follow-up

He subsequently recovered well and was discharged home. He was followed up in the office at 3, 6, 12 and 18 months postcoil passage. He had made a complete recovery. He is currently asymptomatic and is doing well with no clinical consequences.

Discussion

This report describes an elderly male in his early 80s who underwent a pylorus-preserving PD and developed a GDA PSA that had ruptured and was coil embolised. He later developed a complication related to the intervention that was successfully managed. Unlike a true aneurysm, which involves dilation of all three layers of the arterial wall (intima, media and adventitia), a PSA does not contain all the histologic layers of the vessel wall. Consistent blood flow through the aneurysm without immediate management may further cause dilatation of the pseudoaneurysm and can lead to rupture with catastrophic bleeding.

Postoperative bleeding after pancreatic surgery is most commonly due to PSAs arising from the CHA, its branches or the GDA stump.3 GDA PSAs most commonly arise in the setting of chronic pancreatitis (47%), followed by alcohol abuse (25%), peptic ulcer disease (17%) and cholecystectomy (3%). Additional causes include liver cirrhosis, fibromuscular dysplasia, polyarteritis nodosa, abdominal trauma, septic emboli and postoperative states, particularly following PD.4 5 Less frequent associations involve connective tissue disorders, vascular interventions and liver transplantation, all of which can compromise arterial wall integrity and predispose to PSA formation.

PSAs typically result from an injury to the arterial wall due to surgery, trauma or inflammation. Given the history of a prior PD in our patient, the most probable cause for the PSA could be postoperative inflammation, causing erosion and weakening of the vessel wall. It is said that, even without overt intraoperative injury, thermal or inflammatory damage, for example, from electrocautery, scalpels, pancreatic or biliary secretions, etc, can compromise vessel integrity.6 In pancreatitis, PSAs form due to pancreatic enzymes eroding the adjacent vessels, most often involving the splenic artery, with GDA involvement being comparatively less common.7 8 Though symptoms are often non-specific, key features include abdominal pain, bleeding into adjacent structures and obstruction of nearby organs such as the pancreas, biliary tree or gastrointestinal tract.2 9

For diagnosis, CT angiography is now considered the gold standard due to its 100% sensitivity and superior ability to delineate vascular anatomy and surrounding structures.9 With this in mind, we immediately ordered a CTA to identify the cause of the bleed, which showed the presence of a PSA of the GDA. While there are methods such as an abdominal colour Doppler ultrasound, it has a comparatively much lower sensitivity (~50%) but remains a useful adjunct in detecting PSAs within the gastrointestinal tract.9 Endoscopic evaluation, depending on the symptoms, both upper and lower endoscopy play a vital role in excluding alternative sources of gastrointestinal bleeding, especially when the aetiology is unclear.9 In patients with a history of prior gastrointestinal surgery, targeted upper endoscopy is particularly valuable for visualising the target site. It is a useful tool to assess associated mucosal ulceration and sources of active bleeding, just like the eroded coils in our case.

Open surgery was traditionally the standard for treating visceral artery aneurysms and PSAs. However, recent advancements favour endovascular interventions, especially in haemodynamically stable patients. Lee et al,2 in their publication, reported that endovascular methods such as transarterial embolisation (TAE), thrombin injection and stent placement were used in most cases, with surgery needed in only about 25%. These minimally invasive techniques offer lower morbidity and mortality, quicker recovery and shorter hospital stays compared with open surgery, as noted by several authors.5 9 Common options include coil embolisation, stent grafts and thrombin injection.3 6 9 When anatomy permits, endovascular therapy is preferred over open repair, though preservation of distal perfusion through collateral vessels is essential.10 11 For instance, hepatic artery flow must be maintained during coil embolisation of the pancreaticoduodenal arcade to avoid liver dysfunction.11 Li et al12 emphasised that covered stents like the Viabahn are useful in preserving perfusion but require favourable vascular anatomy to prevent complications like ischaemia of end organs. The choice of technique, coil embolisation versus stent-graft placement, depends on vessel size, lesion length and anatomical feasibility. Izumi et al13 described three cases where initial selective embolisation failed, requiring endovascular trapping. They noted that slow stent occlusion may allow collateral formation, reducing rebleeding. Coil embolisation or stent deployment is preferred in non-ruptured or stable ruptured aneurysms, while unstable patients are best managed surgically with ligation or bypass.4 Embolic agents such as cyanoacrylate glue, gelatine foam or ethanol can also be employed.4 8 Lim et al8 reported embolising a GDA PSA with an arterioportal fistula (APF) using TAE and backing it with a balloon and stent to prevent coil migration into the portal vein. Li et al12 stressed that treatment must consider vessel anatomy, disease aetiology and patient status for optimal outcomes.

With these advantages and concerns in mind, endovascular coil embolisation of the GDA PSA was performed, and a stent graft across the CHA was deployed to maintain adequate liver perfusion and avoid ischaemic complications in our patient. Despite its therapeutic advantages, endovascular coil embolisation carries inherent risks, particularly in patients with altered anatomy or compromised vascular integrity. A rare but serious complication is coil migration, as observed in our case. This tends to occur in PSAs adjacent to gastrointestinal structures or surgical anastomoses, such as in post-PD patients. Migration can result in rebleeding, tissue erosion or embolisation into critical vessels, such as the portal vein. Lim et al8 described a case of coils migrating through an APF into the portal vein, managed successfully with a balloon catheter and stent to control outflow. In high-risk anatomical zones, adjunctive strategies—including covered stents, balloon-assisted techniques and the use of liquid embolic agents—may help mitigate this risk.8 12 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of fistula from GDA PSA into the afferent limb of jejunum.

Although the exact mechanism of coil migration remains unclear, several studies have identified potential risk factors such as infection, malignancy or local inflammation around the PSA. These conditions may weaken the vessel wall, allowing the coil to erode through and migrate to adjacent organs. The complex anatomical configuration post-Whipple procedure with close proximity of the GDA stump and the PSA to the afferent limb of jejunum could have been a predisposing factor. Despite considerable variation in PSA and coil sizes, no consistent correlation between coil size and migration risk has been established.14 15 The time interval between embolisation and coil migration ranges widely, from weeks to as long as 10 years. In our case, migration occurred unusually early, within 2 months. Although intraprocedural injury may accelerate this process, no endovascular damage or extravasation was evident on postembolisation angiography.15

There is no standardised treatment for migrated coils due to the rarity of reported cases, but management depends largely on the location and associated symptoms. There are some cases where coils found in the common bile duct have been removed via endoscopic retrograde cholangioscopy, percutaneous cholangioscopy or open surgery. Some situations where the coil migrated to the jejunum have been successfully treated by upper endoscopic retrieval using hot biopsy forceps. Others have resolved spontaneously, with coils passing through the gastrointestinal or urinary tract. In our patient, the initial bleed after the EGD was self-limited, and the coils egressed spontaneously after the initial endoscopic attempt at retrieval.15

Some other things to look out for during this repair are hepatic ischaemia, which is a major concern when the hepatic artery is embolised without sufficient collateral supply, especially in patients with prior surgeries, liver disease or portal hypertension. In such cases, concurrent hepatic artery stenting may preserve perfusion, though complications like stent thrombosis or rebleeding are not uncommon. Viabahn stents help maintain flow but show variable 1-year patency rates (40%–80%), highlighting the need for long-term surveillance. Other risks include non-target embolisation leading to infarction, particularly in patients with vascular abnormalities or prior interventions. PSA recurrence from incomplete occlusion or collateral revascularisation may also require repeat treatment. These complications stress the need for tailored procedural planning and vigilant postprocedure monitoring, especially in anatomically complex or high-risk patients. Close monitoring of these patients is important even after discharge, as complications and recurrence are not uncommon. Our patient had a successful follow-up for over 18 months postintervention and is currently doing well.
