Design and Validation of a Molded Silicone Task Trainer for Simulating Peritonsillar Abscess Drainage
Authors: Peng You, Madeline Green‐Holland, Kathryn Roth, Christopher J Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeritonsillar abscess (PTA) is a common and essential otolaryngologic presentation, and bedside incision and drainage is a core procedural skill for otolaryngology residents. We report the design and preliminary validation of a low‐cost, anatomically realistic simulation model for PTA drainage. The model integrates a molded silicone oropharynx with a drainable abscess cavity into a commercial dental task trainer. Thirty‐eight otolaryngology trainees and faculty performed simulated drainage and completed a postsimulation survey evaluating realism and educational value. Face validity was supported by high realism scores (mean ≥4/5) and strong internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.85). Participants reported significant increases in procedural knowledge (mean increase: 1.17 points, P < .05) and confidence (mean increase: 1.13 points, P < .05). This modular, reusable model offers a scalable solution for simulation‐based training in the development of this common and essential procedural skill in otolaryngology.Keywords: 3D printing, peritonsillar abscess, simulation, task trainer

Peritonsillar abscess (PTA) is a common presentation encountered by otolaryngologists, and bedside incision and drainage is a core procedural skill. 1 Opportunities for early learners, however, are limited by patient safety concerns, time constraints, and procedural complexity, particularly trismus and proximity to vital structures. 2 Simulation provides a safe, controlled environment to build confidence and competence. 3 Although cadaveric models have been used to teach PTA drainage, they are limited by their cost and availability. 2 In response, the objective of this study was to design a low‐cost, high‐fidelity, and reproducible PTA task trainer and to gather preliminary validity evidence for its realism and educational impact.

Methods

The simulation model replicates PTA drainage using a modular oropharyngeal insert embedded in a commercial dental task trainer (Dental training manikin model JG‐C6‐1; JingleMed Equipment Co.; retails approximately $200 USD). A 3D oropharyngeal model with a right‐sided PTA and leftward uvular deviation was created using computer‐aided design software. A negative mold was printed with polylactic acid filament on an AnkerMake M5 3D‐printer (Anker Innovations). A secondary mold was created for a back plate to seal the abscess cavity, and a core insert was used to create space for the abscess (Figure 1). Silicone (Shore 00‐30) was molded to produce a flexible, elastic oropharyngeal insert. A paint‐filled balloon simulated the abscess. The silicone back plate sealed the cavity with silicone adhesive, and Velcro® fasteners attached the insert to the dental task trainer, allowing easy interchangeability (Supplemental S2, available online). Each model required approximately $20 USD in consumable materials. The 3D printed molds and dental task trainers are reusable. Silicone inserts are batch‐cured at room temperature for approximately 5 hours, followed by individual assembly requiring approximately 5 minutes per insert.

Illustration of the peritonsillar abscess task trainer. (A) Mold and insert fabrication with casting core and back plate; (B) dental task trainer with adjustable mouth opening; (C) the assembled simulator with insert affixed in position.

The model was used in the 2024 National Emergencies in Otolaryngology boot camp at Western University. Participants received a brief instructor‐led orientation on PTA drainage and practiced hands‐on with the simulator.

Participants completed a presimulation questionnaire on PTA‐related confidence and knowledge. Following the simulation, they completed a 20‐item survey covering anatomical and tactile realism, procedural utility, teaching quality, and perceived educational impact. Questions used 5‐point Likert scales, educational utility was rated on a 100‐point visual analog scale, and free‐text was provided for optional narrative feedback (Supplemental S1, available online).

Survey responses were anonymized and analyzed using descriptive statistics. Internal consistency of grouped items was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. The Mann‐Whitney U test was used for comparisons, given presumed nonparametric distribution. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 (GraphPad Software). The study received approval from Western University's institutional research ethics board.

Results

Thirty‐eight participants completed the simulation and survey: 31 otolaryngology junior residents (PGY1 or 2), 6 staff surgeons, and 1 physician assistant. Baseline self‐ratings indicated moderate confidence (mean 2.97/5) and knowledge (mean 3.28/5) in managing PTA.

Following the use of the simulator, high realism ratings were reported by all. Staff surgeons reported high fidelity (means ≥4.0 across items), indicating strong agreement that the model accurately simulates PTA anatomy and drainage (Table 1). Resident ratings were similarly favorable, though slightly more variable. Participants also rated the model highly for teaching value, particularly for abscess localization, tool navigation, and drainage technique. Internal consistency was strong across grouped items (Cronbach's α = 0.85 for realism; α = 0.86 for utility).

Discussion

This study describes the development and preliminary validation of a novel, 3D‐printed simulation model for PTA drainage. While several PTA simulators have been described, a recent review highlighted their variability and emphasized the need for models that balance realism with reproducibility and standardization. 4 Cadaveric models offer high fidelity but are limited by cost, availability, and scalability. 2 Synthetic and manikin models address some issues but vary in design and durability. 5 , 6 Our model differs by integrating a molded silicone oropharynx with a drainable abscess cavity into a dental task trainer. Its modular design enables rapid resets between learners, making it suitable for bootcamps and simulation courses.

Messick's unified validity framework defines validity as a multifaceted construct supported by multiple sources of evidence. 7 In this case, content validity was supported by expert review and positive ratings across key teaching domains, including anatomy, instrument handling, and procedural utility. A standardized survey supported response process validity, but participants were from one session, with no formal performance assessment. Internal structure was demonstrated through high internal consistency among grouped survey items (Cronbach's α ≥ 0.85). 8 Relations with other variables, such as differences in scores based on training level, prior experience, or clinical exposure, were not explored but represent a potential direction for future work. Lastly, consequential validity was supported by observed improvements in self‐reported knowledge and confidence following the simulation, suggesting meaningful educational impact on learners and supporting the model's value in teaching early‐stage trainees.

The study is limited by its reliance on self‐reported outcomes and the absence of objective performance assessments. However, Messick's framework supported validity through multiple sources of evidence. Future work should explore transfer validity, objective performance assessment, and real‐world outcomes. Inclusion of learners outside otolaryngology, such as Emergency Medicine, would further assess generalizability. Nevertheless, this task trainer combines low material cost and rapid reset capability, with preliminary validation in a modest sample size supporting its potential as a practical teaching tool.
