A Comprehensive Report on the Self-Reported Chronic Kidney Disease Patients in the NHANES Cross-Sectional Cohort from 2001 to 2020
Authors: Muhammad Sohaib Asghar, Muhammad Nadeem Ahsan, Sadia Iqbal, Afsana Ansari Shaik, Maria Duharte, Mohammed Mahmmoud Fadelallah Eljack, Moustafa Hegazi, Chad K. Brands
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study was a comprehensive review of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES) databases. The primary objectives were to ascertain patterns of demographic and clinical data among chronic kidney disease (CKD) and dialysis patients.MethodsThis study examines participants from the CDC NHANES database to identify the population diagnosed with CKD or dialysis patients from 2001 to 2020. The total cohort of 51,743 patients were included in each group; i.e., CKD group included 1,509 patients (out of which 173 patients have received dialysis in the past 12 months), while non-CKD group included 50,234 patients. Exclusion criteria included age >85 years (n = 624), pregnancy (n = 1,375), and history of malignancy (n = 5,575). Logistic regression was performed to identify conditions that were more frequently observed with CKD and those on dialysis with reported odds ratios (ORs). Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to calculate diagnostic accuracies of different cut-offs for urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR).ResultsThe mean age of study participants (n = 51,743) was 48.50 ± 17.43 years. Age was significantly higher in CKD group, but there was no difference in dialysis versus nondialysis patients (p = 0.833). There was no gender predisposition in CKD patients; however, males were more likely to undergo dialysis with 13.3% vs. 9.8% in females (p = 0.032). Among racial differences, non-Hispanic Whites were predominantly affected by CKD (37.0%) followed by non-Hispanic Blacks (29.3%), but non-Hispanic Blacks are mostly undergoing dialysis (48.6% vs. 17.3%). BMI (kg/m2) was significantly higher in CKD group (p < 0.001), but lower in dialysis group (p = 0.039). Among urinary complaints, history of kidney stones was prevalent in CKD patients (7.7% vs. 2.6%, p < 0.001). Frequent coexisting comorbidities were congestive heart failure (OR: 9.8), diabetes (OR: 5.4), coronary artery disease (OR: 5.0), and hypertension (OR: 4.7). UACR (>30 mg/g) is able to categorize CKD patients (AUC: 0.73), at a sensitivity of 44.9% and a specificity of 88.8%.ConclusionCertain clinical factors were identified as highly associated with CKD, as disseminated by the NHANES database findings. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to address CKD disparities and inform public health strategies.

Introduction

The incidence and prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) are on the rise globally according to the global burden of disease with uptrending mortality rates and disability-adjusted life years [1]. Within the USA, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) WONDER mortality data from death certificates reveal death rates in the USA as multiple causes of death between a crude rate of 28.7 per 100,000 population and an age-adjusted mortality rate of 26.3 per 100,000 population [2]. This study utilized data from the CDC National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database to explore the prevalence and characteristics of CKD in the USA [3]. NHANES, a representative cross-sectional survey, provides comprehensive data on CKD based on clinical and laboratory markers such as estimated glomerular filtration rate and albuminuria. The findings from NHANES were analyzed and compared with published regional, national, and international data to evaluate trends, disparities, and the broader context of CKD burden.

The NHANES data revealed a self-reported CKD prevalence within the Kidney Questionnaire of approximately 2.54% among US adults >20 years of age (which according to NHANES study weights represents a total population of over 221 million from 2001 to 2020), consistent with prior national estimates [4]. This prevalence aligns with findings from other national databases, including the United States Renal Data System (USRDS), which similarly reported a persistent CKD prevalence in recent decades [5]. However, variations were observed across subpopulations, with higher prevalence among individuals with diabetes, hypertension, and older adults. Such trends underscore the role of comorbid conditions in driving CKD burden and reflect national epidemiological patterns previously described [6].

When compared with region-specific studies within the USA, NHANES provides a broader, standardized snapshot of CKD prevalence. Regional studies, such as those conducted in the Southeastern USA, report even higher CKD prevalence rates, often attributed to the higher regional prevalence of risk factors like obesity, diabetes, and socioeconomic disparities [7]. These discrepancies may reflect geographic and demographic differences or regional healthcare access disparities. Internationally, NHANES data provide a benchmark for CKD prevalence that aligns closely with other high-income countries but differs significantly from low- and middle-income countries. For example, studies from Europe, such as the German CKD (GCKD) study and from developing countries like sub-Saharan Africa, reported a CKD prevalence comparable to NHANES findings [8, 9]. The primary objectives were to ascertain patterns of demographic and clinical data among CKD and dialysis patients who are at risk for chronic disease to fill the knowledge gaps.

Methods

Design

This is a large, population-based, cross-sectional study from CDC NHANES databases to identify the lifestyle patterns, dietary habits, physical activity, and stressors of CKDs and dialysis.

Cohort

Patients were identified by searching the online CDC portal (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/) for the enrolled individuals in NHANES databases dated from 2001 to 2020 [3]. A subset of this patient database was then identified and then interrogated as “Kidney Conditions – Urology.”

Patients (or Participants)

Patients (or participants) were self-reportedly diagnosed with CKD and/or have been dialysis patients in the past 12 months. These individuals were 20–84 years of age. The population studied include patients who answered the question “Ever told by a doctor that you had weak/failing kidneys.” Those who answered “yes” were considered having CKD (self-reportedly), while the comparison group includes the patients whose answer is “no” to the above question. The total NHANES cohort reviewed 116,876 patients, among whom 59,433 were eligible for screening (the rest included the pediatric age group among other reasons), out of which 51,743 were included in the study (Fig. 1).

NHANES database screening and inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Interventions/Exposures Measured

Demographic data, other comorbidities, urinary symptoms, kidney stones, health insurance, housing, dietary habits, lifestyle activities, use of medications, smoking status, alcohol intake, hospitalization in last year, mental health, health insurance, vaccination, sleep cycle, BMI, blood pressure, heart rate, and laboratory investigations were explored as explained in depth in the online supplemental methods section (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551875).

Main Outcome Measure(s)

The main outcome measures were difference of demographic and clinical data between CKD and non-CKD groups and a subset analysis between dialysis and nondialysis patients. Logistic regression was performed to identify conditions that were more frequently observed with CKD and those on dialysis with reported odds ratios (ORs). Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to calculate diagnostic accuracies of different cut-offs for urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR).

Secondary Outcomes/Measure(s)

Mortality data provided by the National Death Index (NDI) were linked to CDC NHANES to obtain follow-up mortality outcomes in this patient population [10–12].

Propensity Matching Analysis

The propensity score matching analysis was performed using the Python version 3.13, by using Kernel’s method, and stratified with closest neighbor match 1:1 for age. Matching was only performed for age, since onset of most diseases is mainly determined by age and both groups (with or without CKD) were propensity matched to nullify to confounder effect of age on these comorbidities. Post-age-matched (adjusted) analysis was showcased by using a Love plot (pre- and post-matching).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed on Statistical Package of Social Sciences (SPSS) by IBM Corporation (Armonk, NY) version 25.0. Additionally, Microsoft Excel version 2013 was used to sort and assimilate datasets for merging variables and combining datasheets into one source file. For weighed data analysis, which was obtained from the study weights provided by CDC for national-level estimates, STATA version 17.0 was used to incorporate weights, strata, and primary sampling units which were adjusted and combined for all the study years. For receiver operating characteristic analysis, MedCalc® Statistical Software version 22.023 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2024) was used additionally for automated validation of diagnostic accuracy of UACR.

Results

Baseline Data and Sociodemographics

The mean age of study participants (n = 51,743) was 48.50 ± 17.43 years. Age was significantly higher in the CKD group (59.15 ± 16.30 vs. 48.18 ± 17.56 years, p < 0.001), but there was no difference in dialysis versus nondialysis patients (p = 0.833). The age groups of CKD patients divided into categories (20–45 years, 46–65 years, and 66–85 years) were presented in online supplementary Figure 1. There was no gender predisposition in CKD patients with 2.8% being male and 3.0% female patients. However, males were more likely to undergo dialysis with 13.3% vs. 9.8% in females (p = 0.032). Among racial differences, non-Hispanic Whites were the predominantly representative sample of CKD (37.0%) in this study population followed by non-Hispanic Blacks (29.3%), but non-Hispanic Blacks are mostly undergoing dialysis (48.6%) as compared to non-Hispanic Whites and Mexican Americans (17.3%) each as shown in online supplementary Table 1.

Comorbidity Analysis

Among CKD patients (n = 1,509), the odds of having coexisting comorbidities are congestive heart failure (OR: 9.85 [8.58–11.30]), history of myocardial infarction (5.44 [4.71–6.29]), anemia (5.43 [4.71–6.27]), gout (5.20 [4.34–6.23]), coronary artery disease (5.03 [4.33–5.85]), past stroke (4.98 [4.28–5.79]), and angina pectoris (4.87 [4.08–5.83]), as shown in Figure 2a. Among dialysis patients (n = 173), having anemia (OR: 4.63 [3.29–6.51]), blood transfusion (3.76 [2.67–5.24]), diabetic retinopathy (3.41 [2.87–4.06]), overnight hospital admission in the past year (3.14 [2.27–4.33]), and hypertension (2.73 [1.84–4.06]) are more frequent, as shown in Figure 2b.

Univariate analysis for factors associated with patients having CKD (a) and on dialysis (b).

Among urinary complaints, history of kidney stones is prevalent in CKD patients (7.7% vs. 2.6%, p < 0.001), and urine leakage during physical and nonphysical activities, urinary urgency, and nocturia are more common in CKD patients (p < 0.001) but less common in dialysis subset (p < 0.001) as shown in online supplementary Table 2. Since CKD cohort has higher age at baseline as shown in online supplementary Table 3 and online supplementary Table 4, age of onset for chronic diseases was assessed in online supplementary Figure 2 with propensity-matched age adjustment showing earlier age of onset for most comorbidities in CKD group as shown in online supplementary Table 1. Online supplementary Tables 5–10 provide nonresponse bias analysis. Propensity adjustment of age also depicts how proportion of different comorbidities differs within the matched group in CKD patients as shown by the Love plot (online suppl. Figure 3). Table 1 shows UACR (>30 mg/g) is able to categorize CKD patients with highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.73), at a sensitivity of 44.9% and a specificity of 88.8% with different cut-offs assessed as shown in Figure 3. Online supplementary Figures 4–6 showcase health questionnaire, physical exam findings, and other laboratory examinations, respectively.

Discussion

Main Findings

The NHANES database highlights significant variations in CKD prevalence across sociodemographic factors and progression across different patient groups. The current study also evaluated different cut-off values associated with CKD for UACR for better diagnostic accuracy. The study findings suggest that the currently practiced cut-offs of 30 mg/g show the highest sensitivity and specificity, in comparison with lower UACR values.

Regional Disparities in CKD Prevalence and Outcomes within the USA

Previous studies highlight regions in the USA with particularly high prevalence of CKD, especially in the Southeast, often referred to as the “Kidney Disease Belt.” Studies have demonstrated that CKD prevalence in Southeastern states such as Mississippi, Louisiana, and Alabama exceeds the national average, driven largely by higher rates of diabetes, hypertension, and obesity in these regions [13, 14]. For instance, a study from the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) cohort identified a 20–30% higher prevalence of CKD in the Southeast compared to other US regions, which aligns with the observed geographic disparities in NHANES data [15].

Socioeconomic and healthcare access disparities further exacerbate regional differences. In underserved rural areas, limited access to nephrologists and preventive care contributes to delayed diagnosis and more advanced CKD at presentation [16]. Moreover, variations in public health infrastructure and state-level healthcare funding impact the availability of screening and early interventions, particularly for vulnerable populations. These geographic disparities emphasize the need for region-specific strategies to address CKD risk factors and improve access to care.

International Disparities in CKD Prevalence and Outcomes

NHANES findings on CKD prevalence and risk factors provide an important benchmark for comparisons with international populations. In high-income countries, CKD prevalence is often similar to or slightly lower than that reported in the USA. For example, the prevalence of CKD stages 1–4 in European countries such as Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden ranges between 10% and 13%, according to studies like the GCKD study and the EPIC-Norfolk study in the UK [17, 18]. European countries promote early diagnosis and management, potentially mitigating the progression of CKD compared to the USA, where healthcare access disparities persist [19].

Despite similar prevalence rates, differences in CKD outcomes are notable. High-income countries with strong primary care systems report lower rates of progression to end-stage kidney disease and better survival rates among dialysis and transplant patients compared to the USA [9]. In contrast, CKD prevalence in many LMICs is underreported due to limited population-based studies and restricted access to laboratory testing [20]. However, emerging evidence suggests a growing burden of CKD in regions such as South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America, where the prevalence of noncommunicable diseases like diabetes and hypertension is rapidly rising [21]. For instance, community-based studies in India and Pakistan estimate CKD prevalence between 7% and 15%, depending on the population studied [5]. Similarly, a study from sub-Saharan Africa reported CKD prevalence ranging from 10% to 16%, with substantial variability between rural and urban populations [7].

Several unique risk factors contribute to CKD in LMICs, particularly in agricultural communities in Central America and South Asia [22]. Additionally, infectious diseases such as HIV, hepatitis B, and schistosomiasis remain significant contributors to the CKD burden in sub-Saharan Africa, unlike in high-income settings where noncommunicable diseases dominate [23]. Healthcare system limitations in LMICs exacerbate CKD disparities. Mortality rates among end-stage kidney disease patients are disproportionately high due to the unaffordability or inaccessibility of life-saving kidney replacement therapies [24]. Moreover, the financial burden of CKD care in LMICs often falls heavily on individuals, further limiting access to treatment and increasing the risk of catastrophic health expenditures [25].

Addressing Regional and Global Disparities

Efforts to reduce regional and international CKD disparities should focus on improving access to early detection and affordable care. In high-burden regions like the Southeastern USA and LMICs, targeted public health campaigns to address modifiable risk factors (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, and obesity) are critical. Expanding the availability of CKD screening programs, particularly in rural and underserved areas, and ensuring equitable access to evidence-based treatments such as renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors are essential steps toward improving outcomes. On a global scale, harmonizing CKD diagnostic criteria and developing cost-effective screening tools suitable for low-resource settings could improve CKD surveillance and enable more accurate comparisons of prevalence and outcomes across countries. In our study, UACR with an optimum cut-off showed highest diagnostic accuracy for CKD. Collaborative international initiatives, such as the Global Kidney Health Atlas by the International Society of Nephrology, play a pivotal role in advocating for policy changes and capacity building in resource-limited settings [26].

The circulatory disease risk factors analyzed in this study are also closely linked to the development of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a disorder with interconnected heart disease, kidney pathologies, obesity, and diabetes mellitus [27]. In the year 2021, CKM syndrome was identified as the leading cause of death in the USA. During 2011–2020, approximately 90% of the US adult population met the criteria of CKM syndrome stage 1 or higher, and almost 15% of the adults had advanced stage CKM syndrome, with none of these patients demonstrating improvement during this period. Trends analyzed during the same period indicated greater prevalence of advanced CKM syndrome stages in adults aged ≥65 years, in Black adults, and in males [28]. Early identification and management of CKM risk factors including dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus are recommended to mitigate the disease burden and improve treatment-related outcomes [29].

Strengths and Limitations

The NHANES database offers significant strengths, including its nationally representative sample and standardized data collection methodology, enabling robust comparisons across populations. However, there are inherent limitations. Cross-sectional data restrict causal inference, and self-reported comorbidities may introduce recall bias. Additionally, differences in CKD definitions, diagnostic criteria, and screening methods between NHANES and other datasets may contribute to variations in prevalence estimates. For instance, the CDC reports a CKD prevalence of 14% among US adults from 2017 to 2020 NHANES databases [30], by using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration GFR estimating equation, which includes serum creatinine, age, and sex, while we relied upon self-reporting of CKD by the study participants during the interview. The estimates based on a single measure of albuminuria or serum creatinine do not account for the persistence of albuminuria or elevated creatinine as indicated by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes recommendations; hence, we could not rely solely on the laboratory data. Furthermore, variables like sleep cycle and mental health are not assessed by a validated questionnaire and hence are more subjective.

Conclusion

The study was able to identify several risk factors for CKD patients who were undergoing dialysis. Certain populations like non-Hispanic Blacks were identified to be highly exposed to CKD implications and will help disseminate the NHANES database findings and formulate recommendations that in the future must inform decisions and policies for improving population risk factors, patient care, health outcomes, and resource allocation, as appropriate. The NHANES data underscore the significant burden of CKD in the USA, particularly among high-risk subpopulations, and provide valuable insights for global comparisons. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to address CKD disparities and inform public health strategies. Future studies should aim to harmonize CKD definitions and methodologies across regions and countries to enable more precise global comparisons.
