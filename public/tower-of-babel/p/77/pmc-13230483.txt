New records and a new species of Erysiphe (Erysiphaceae, Ascomycota) from Mexico
Authors: Rosario Gregorio-Cipriano, Gerardo Rodríguez-Alvarado, Susumu Takamatsu, Uwe Braun, Siska AS Siahaan, Jamjan Meeboon, Marlene Díaz-Celaya, Nuria Gómez-Dorantes, Alfredo Reyes-Tena, Yolanda Leticia Fernández-Pavía, Alejandro Soto-Plancarte, Michael Bradshaw, Patricia Silva-Sáenz, Sylvia Patricia Fernández-Pavía
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMexico hosts a wide diversity of plant species, which is reflected in the high number of powdery mildew species present in the country. Nevertheless, many of them have not yet been detected and are often undescribed. The present work provides records of 13 species belonging to the genus Erysiphe (including sect. Erysiphe, sect. Microsphaera, and sect. Uncinula) from Mexico. In most cases, only asexual morphs have been found, which require molecular phylogenetic analyses based on ITS+28S rRNA sequences in addition to morphological examinations for accurate identifications. Five new reports: E. ampelopsidis, E. intermedia, E. peckii, E. rumicicola, and E. russellii and two new hosts for Mexico, viz., Casuarina equisetifolia (E. diffusa) and Lupinus elegans (E. intermedia), are amongst the new collections. The new species Erysiphe burserae, on Bursera kerberi and Bursera sp., is introduced, illustrated, and discussed.Keywords: Burseraceae, Helotiales, Powdery mildew

1. Introduction

Powdery mildews (Erysiphaceae, Ascomycota) are a large group of obligate biotrophic fungi, causing detrimental plant diseases of almost worldwide distribution (Bradshaw et al., 2025; Braun & Cook, 2012). The white mycelium, giving rise to a characteristic anamorph, and black sexual reproductive structures (chasmothecia) on the surface of the host (stems, leaves, flowers, fruits) allow this pathogen to be easily distinguishable (Takamatsu et al., 2013; Yarwood, 1957). Powdery mildews currently comprise about 900 recognized species occurring on a wide range of spermatophytes, excluding gymnosperms (Braun & Cook, 2012). Amongst its more than 10,000 known host species, there are numerous economically important species causing plant diseases, such as agricultural crops (cereals, fruit trees, vegetables, flowers) and ornamental species. Powdery mildews may affect the appearance of the hosts concerned and can diminish their economic value (Bradshaw et al., 2025; Braun, 2011; Braun & Cook, 2012; Havrylenko & Takamatsu, 2005; Putnam & Glawe, 2007).

Erysiphe is the most diverse genus with about 450 species, including many species only known by their anamorphs (Bradshaw et al., 2023, 2025; Braun & Cook, 2012; Takamatsu et al., 2015). The genus Erysiphe, in its current monophyletic circumscription, comprises species exhibiting morphologically diverse sexual forms. However, all share a synapomorphy on their unique anamorphs, previously assigned to the anamorph-typified genus Pseudoidium, characterized by having lobate hyphal appressoria and conidia produced singly (Bradshaw et al., 2023, 2025; Braun & Takamatsu, 2000; Mori et al., 2000). Based on different morphological types of chasmothecial appendages, Braun & Cook (2012) divided Erysiphe into five morphological (non-monophyletic) sections: sect. Erysiphe, sect. Californiomyces, sect. Microsphaera, sect. Typhulochaeta, and sect. Uncinula.

In recent years, there has been an increase in studying powdery mildews in Mexico (e.g., Félix-Gastélum et al., 2013, 2014; Gregorio-Cipriano et al., 2022; Márquez-Licona et al., 2018; Solano-Báez et al., 2018; Tovar-Pedraza et al., 2018). However, these studies are not proportional to the high diversity of potential host plants that exist in Mexico, a country which has 25,314 species of native and introduced vascular plants, including 23,824 angiosperms (Villaseñor, 2016; Villaseñor & Meave, 2022). Thus, it is necessary for more research to evaluate the diversity of powdery mildew species, their distribution, and their host ranges in Mexico. The present study aimed to identify powdery mildews caused by Erysiphe species on host plants of importance for agriculture, ornamental purposes, forestry, or ecology in Mexico, based on morphological and molecular methods.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Collections

Specimens analyzed in this study were collected between Dec 2013 and Mar 2018 (including a single specimen collected in 2007, and others from 2020 to 2024) in different localities in Mexico, under different growing conditions, including commercial crops, gardens, potted plants, and wild species. Most samples came from locations in the State of Michoacán, supplemented by some specimens from other Mexican states, such as Guanajuato, Guerrero, Mexico City, and Puebla. Herbarium specimens are deposited at the Universidad Michoacana de San Nicolás de Hidalgo Herbarium (EBUM; Michoacán, Mexico) and Mie University Mycological Herbarium (TSU-MUMH, Tsu, Japan; now transferred to TNS, Tokyo, Japan and NCSLG, Raleigh North Carolina).

2.2. Morphological examinations

Morphological examinations were carried out using standard light microscopy (Leica DME model 13595XXX). Fresh samples were mounted in 3% KOH, and dried material in lactic acid, either by placing a piece of adhesive clear tape on the sample or by scraping the fungal structures from the surface of the leaves with a scalpel. Measurements of 25 to 30 conidia and conidiophores of each specimen were performed.

2.3. Molecular identification

Whole-cell DNA was extracted from mycelia using the chelex method as described in Hirata & Takamatsu (1996). The 5′-end of the 28S rDNA (including D1 and D2 domains) and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions were amplified and sequenced separately following the methods in Yamaguchi et al. (2021).

For the specimens on Bursera hosts, the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the ITS and LSU regions was carried out using the primer pair PM10/PM28R (Bradshaw & Tobin, 2020). Each 26 µL reaction contained: 6 µL of a 0.1 dilution of the DNA extract solution, 5.148 µL of 5 × and colorless buffer, 2.106 µL of 25 mM MgCl2, 0.546 µL of a deoxynucleotide triphosphate mix (10 mM each one; dNTP Mix U151, Promega, Madison, WI, USA), 0.13 µL of DNA polymerase enzyme (GoTaq® Flexi DNA Polymerase, Promega), 0.135 µL of each primer (100 pmol), and 11.8 µL of molecular-grade water. Amplifications were performed in an Eppendorf Mastercycler 96-Well Gradient (Eppendorf, Hauppauge, NY, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min (denaturation), 57 °C for 2 min (annealing), and 72 °C for 2 min (extension), with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels, and the bands of interest were excised and purified using a commercial kit (Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System, Promega). Purified amplicons were sent to Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea) for sequencing using the primer pair PM10/PM28R (Bradshaw & Tobin, 2020).

The new sequences were analyzed using BLAST against the GenBank database to determine their closest similarities. The phylogenetic analysis was performed only for the new species on Bursera spp. ITS region sequences of specimens on Bursera spp. were compared with sequences of selected taxa from Uncinula lineage, based on Bradshaw et al. (2023). Arthrocladiella mougeotii was selected as the outgroup taxon, as it is divergent from the Uncinula lineage of Erysiphe. Sequences were aligned and edited using Geneious Prime 2025.0.3. A GTR+G+I evolutionary model was used for phylogenetic analyses, as it is the most inclusive model of evolution and includes all other evolutionary models (Abadi et al., 2019). The phylogeny was inferred using Bayesian analysis using a Yule tree prior (Gernhard, 2008) and a strict molecular clock, in the program BEAST v.1.10.4 (Suchard et al., 2018). A single MCMC chain of 107 steps was run, sampling every 1000th generations, with a burn-in of 25%. Posterior probabilities were calculated from the remaining 7500 sampled trees. A maximum clade credibility tree was produced using TreeAnnotator v.1.10.4 (part of the BEAST package). Stationarity was confirmed by running the analysis multiple times, which revealed convergence between runs. The resulting tree was visualized using FigTree v.1.3.1 (Rambaut, 2009). A maximum likelihood analysis was accomplished using raxmlGUI (Silvestro & Michalak, 2012) under the default settings with a GTR+G+I evolutionary model. Bootstrap analyses were conducted using 1000 replications (Felsenstein, 1985).

3.2. Taxonomy

Erysiphe ampelopsidis (Peck) M. Bradshaw, U. Braun & Pfister, Mycologia 115: 6, 2023.

Mycelium on leaves, amphigenous, forming patches, sometimes causing pink to reddish discolorations visible on both sides of the leaf; hyphae 3-7 µm wide, thin-walled, hyaline; hyphal appressoria 5-9 µm diam, lobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores arising from the upper surface of mother cells, frequently 2-4 on one mother cell, erect; foot cells (26-)30-60 × 6-9 µm, twisted, flexuous or slightly sinuous at the base, rarely straight, followed by 2 shorter cells, frequently, the first one almost twice larger than the other, forming conidia singly; conidia cylindrical, cylindrical-ellipsoidal, or ovoid, 27-43 × 11-19 µm, length/width ratio 1.9-2.8(-3.2); germ tubes terminal, short, apical appressorium slightly lobed.

Specimens examined: MEXICO, on Parthenocissus quinquefolia (Vitaceae), Michoacán, Tarímbaro, in an urban garden, 3 Jun 2016, S.P. Fernández-Pavía (EBUM27937, MUMH6799), anamorph; Posta-Veterinaria UMSNH, in a suburban garden, 30 Jan 2020, M. Díaz-Celaya (EBUM28786), anamorph.

Distribution: This species has previously been reported on Parthenocissus cuspidifera, P. quinquefolia, P. tricuspidata, and P. vitacea in Canada and the USA (Bradshaw et al., 2023).

Note: This is the first report of Erysiphe ampelopsidis in Mexico. The phylogeny and taxonomy of this species was treated in Bradshaw et al. (2023).

Erysiphe australiana (McAlp.) U. Braun & S. Takam., Schlechtendalia 4: 17, 2000.

Mycelium amphigenous, mainly on inflorescences, forming dense white patches or covering the entire surface, on leaves only with small patches causing yellow discolorations; hyphae 3-7 µm wide, thin-walled, hyaline, smooth; hyphal appressoria 5-6 µm diam, slightly lobed to multilobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores erect, arising from the upper surface of mother cells, central position, solitary; foot cells 23-39(-45) × 6-10 µm, straight or slightly curved, followed by 1-3 shorter cells, forming conidia singly; conidia 23-38 × (8-)11-18 µm, ellipsoidal to cylindrical-ellipsoidal, rarely ovoid or doliiform, length/width ratio 1.6-3.1; germ tubes terminal or subterminal, short, lobed or not.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Copándaro municipality, Copándaro de Galeana, in an urban garden, on Lagerstroemia sp. (Lythraceae), 16 Jun 2017, A. Reyes-Tena (EBUM27954, MUMH7073), anamorph.

Distribution: Erysiphe australiana, confined to Lagerstroemia species (L. floribunda, L. indica, L. ovalifolia, L. parviflora, L. speciosa, and L. subcostata), is distributed in Africa (South Africa and Egypt), Asia (China, India, Iran, Japan, Nepal, Pakistan, Russia, South Korea, Taiwan, Turkey, Uzbekistan), Australia, Europe (Croatia, Italy, Portugal, Spain, Switzerland, Ukraine, United Kingdom), New Zealand, North America (USA, Mexico), South America (Argentina, Brazil, Venezuela) (Asif, 2013; Attia & Reyad, 2014; Baiswar et al., 2009; Bradshaw et al., 2023; Braun & Cook, 2012; Chhetri et al., 2010; Cunnington et al., 2003; Fonseca et al., 2016; Gautam & Avasthi, 2018; Khodaparast, 2016; Kim, 2021; Liberato & Barreto, 2004; Nabieva et al., 2021; Novak et al., 2011; Zafar et al., 2025b). This species was recently reported on L. indica from Mexico City, Mexico (Márquez-Licona et al., 2018).

Note: This is the first report of E. australiana in the state of Michoacán.

Erysiphe betae (Vaňha) Weltzien, Phytopathol. Z. 47: 127, 1963.

Mycelium on leaves amphigenous, effuse or in dense patches, frequently covering the entire leaf surface; hyphae 3-7 µm wide, thin-walled, hyaline; hyphal appressoria (3-)5-10 µm diam, slightly lobed to lobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores single or two on a hyphal cell, arising from the upper surface of mother cells, position central to non-central, erect, foot cells (18-)25-92.5 × 5-11 µm, cylindrical, straight, followed by 1-2 cells, the first one commonly longer than or about as long as the foot cell (29-75 µm), rarely shorter, second one (if present) always shorter, forming conidia singly; conidia (26-)29-55 × 10-22 µm, cylindrical, ellipsoidal, length/width ratio (1.5-)1.8-3.9; germ tubes terminal, short (about half of the conidial length) or longer (almost six times as long as the conidial length).

Specimens examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Copándaro municipality, Copándaro de Galeana, in a family production area, on Beta vulgaris var. cicla (Amaranthaceae), 5 May 2015, N. Gómez-Dorantes (EBUM27925, MUMH7078), anamorph. MEXICO, Michoacán, Morelia municipality, Uruapilla, in a commercial tomato greenhouse, on Dysphania ambrosioides (Amaranthaceae), 6 Aug 2007, G. Rodríguez-Alvarado (EBUM27909, MUMH6797), anamorph; MEXICO, Morelia municipality, Morelia, in a commercial nursery, on Dysphania ambrosioides, Mar 2016, N. Gómez-Dorantes (EBUM27910, MUMH6796), anamorph.

Distribution: Erysiphe betae is confined to host species in the Amaranthaceae, mostly Beta, sometimes Dysphania, and Spinacia oleracea (Braun & Cook, 2012). On Dysphania ambrosioides this powdery mildew has been reported in Argentina, India, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Pakistan, Romania, and Taiwan (Anwar et al., 2020; Braun & Cook, 2012; Park et al., 2012), on Beta vulgaris it is known almost worldwide wherever sugar beets are grown (Braun & Cook, 2012; Vakalounakis & Kavroulakis, 2017; Weltzien, 1978; Zhang et al., 2014). Erysiphe betae was previously recorded on Chenopodium ambrosioides (= D. ambrosioides) in the states of Guanajuato and Mexico (Yáñez-Morales et al., 2009); and recorded on Beta vulgaris var. cicla in the state of Puebla (Solano-Báez et al., 2023).

Note: Specimens on both hosts have the typical morphology of conidiophores and conidia of E. betae, with its characteristically long cylindrical conidia and foot cells followed by 1-2 cells, the first of them typically longer or of the same length as the foot cell. However, specimens on D. ambrosioides have a wider range in the length of the foot cells, (26-)30-92.5 × 5-11 µm, vs. foot cells 18-50 × 7-12 µm of E. betae (Braun & Cook, 2012). Braun & Cook (2012) assigned the D. ambrosioides powdery mildew to E. betae, owing to the morphological similarity between specimens on Beta and Dysphania. In the ITS+28S phylogenetic analyses, sequences of powdery mildew on B. vulgaris and D. ambrosioides cluster together in a single clade (Bradshaw et al., 2025). A multilocus approach is necessary to disentangle the powdery mildews on the two hosts, as well as other powdery mildews, such as E. heraclei which falls in this complex.

This is the first report of Erysiphe betae in the state of Michoacán.

ITS+28S sequence ex-holotype: PV997901.

Mycelium amphigenous, in dense patches on leaves, white; hyphae branched, 4-7 µm wide, straight to flexuous, hyaline, septate, thin-walled, smooth; hyphal appressoria 8-13 µm diam, lobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores erect, single or rarely two per hyphal cell, arising from the upper surface of mother cells; foot cells 10-21(-26) × 7-11(-13) µm, straight, sometimes showing a slight swelling near the basal septum, followed by 1-2 shorter cells, forming conidia singly; conidia 22-40(-43) × 11-15(-17) µm, cylindrical, length/width ratio 1.8-3.3(-3.6); germ tubes terminal. Chasmothecia gregarious or scattered, 122.5-210 µm diam; peridium cells irregularly polygonal, 7.5-12.5 µm wide; appendages 21-45 per chasmothecium, ± equatorial, straight to curved, sometimes dichotomously branched near the apices, tips uncinate-circinate, 22.5-87.5 µm long and ± uniformly 2.5-7.5 µm wide throughout, occasionally with a single septum at the base, hyaline, thin-walled, smooth, walls somewhat thicker at the base; asci 5-10, broad ellipsoid-ovoid, ellipsoid, 40-70 × 25-42.5 µm, mostly sessile, sometimes short-stalked; ascospores not developed.

Additional specimens examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Morelia municipality, Morelia, in the Municipality nursery, on Bursera kerberi (Burseraceae), May 2013, N. Gómez-Dorantes (EBUM28778 = MUMH7193), anamorph. MEXICO, in Michoacán, Morelia municipality, Morelia, in an urban garden, Bursera sp., Dec 2023, A. Soto-Plancarte (EBUM000979), anamorph.

Note: There are no previous records of powdery mildews on species of the genus Bursera. Erysiphe garugae on Garuga pinnata in India is the only Erysiphe species so far reported on a host belonging to the family Burseraceae (Braun & Cook, 2012). Unfortunately, there are no molecular sequences retrieved from type material of E. garugae, the only existing material of this species, for comparison; however, clear morphological differences in both the teleomorph and anamorph between the specimens on Bursera in Mexico and those on Garuga in India suggest the involvement of two separate species. In the phylogenetic tree, Erysiphe sp. on Bursera forms a separate clade located in the Uncinula lineage, genetically quite different from all other powdery mildew species (Fig. 1). The new species ITS region was extremely divergent and aligned ~88% with the closest powdery mildew species (Erysiphe adunca).

Erysiphe diffusa (Cooke & Peck) U. Braun & S. Takam., Schlechtendalia 4: 7, 2000.

Mycelium on leaves, effuse, white; hyphae 4-5 µm wide, thin-walled, hyaline; hyphal appressoria 6-8(-10) µm diam, slightly lobed to lobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores, solitary, slightly curved, arising from the upper surface of mother cells; foot cells 22-46 × (5-)6-8 µm, straight, rarely slightly sinuous at the base, followed by 1-2 shorter cells, forming conidia singly; conidia (25-)27-36(-40) × 12-16(-18) µm, ellipsoid to subcylindrical, ovoid, rarely doliiform, length/width ratio 1.8-2.9(-3.5); germ tubes not seen.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Tarímbaro municipality, Posta-Veterinaria, UMSNH, on Casuarina equisetifolia (Casuarinaceae), 4 Aug 2016, A. Soto-Plancarte (EBUM27950, MUMH6841), anamorph.

Distribution: Previously, Erysiphe diffusa was only known to cause powdery mildew diseases on hosts of the Fabaceae family (Ajithkumar et al., 2022; Baiswar et al., 2016; Braun & Cook, 2012; Fu et al., 2015; Kelly et al., 2021; Luz et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2024). However, based on the results of sequence analyses, E. diffusa (s. lat.) has recently been demonstrated to be able to infect Carica papaya (Braun et al., 2017; Tsay et al., 2011; Vielba-Fernández et al., 2019), Bergera koenigii (Moparthi et al., 2023), and Hibiscus sabdariffa (Yeh et al., 2025), members of the plant families, Caricaceae, Rutaceae and Malvaceae, respectively. In Mexico, E. diffusa has, so far, only been known on two different legumes, viz., on Pachyrhizus erosus in the state of Guerrero, where both the anamorph and teleomorph were detected (Yáñez-Morales et al., 2009), and on Phaseolus vulgaris in Sinaloa (Félix-Gastélum et al., 2011).

Note: A few records of powdery mildew worldwide are known on Casuarina spp., and almost all of them were recorded as Oidium sp. (Braun & Cook, 2012; USDA Fungal databases, 2025), except for Erysiphe aquilegiae reported on Casuarina cunninghamiana in Argentina (Wolcan et al., 2012). The anamorph on C. equisetifolia in Mexico resembles the conidial state of E. aquilegiae as to its ellipsoid to subcylindrical or ovoid conidia, and foot cells straight, rarely slightly sinuous at the base. However, it differs in having slightly shorter and narrower conidia, (25-)27-36(-40) × 12-16(-18) µm, vs. (25-)28-50 × (12-)16-22(-24). The ITS+28S sequences retrieved from the Mexican specimen on C. equisetifolia align with E. diffusa (s. lat.) sequences (AB078803 = 99.86%, PP681011 = 99.84%), suggesting that this host may be infected by the latter powdery mildew species. The morphological characteristics match better with E. diffusa, characterized by having ellipsoidal-cylindrical to doliiform conidia, 25-35 × 11-17.5 µm. However, the conidiophore foot cells of the powdery mildew on C. equisetifolia are longer and narrower [22-46 × (5-)6-8 µm] in comparison to foot cells in E. diffusa, described to be 25-38 × 7.5-10 µm (Braun & Cook, 2012).

This is the first report of E. diffusa on a species of the genus Casuarina (Casuarinaceae), and the first report of powdery mildew in Casuarina in Mexico.

Erysiphe euphorbiicola U. Braun & S. Takam., Schlechtendalia 4: 8, 2000, s. lat.

Mycelium amphigenous on leaves and commonly on bracts, forming effuse or dense white patches; hyphae 4-6 µm wide, flexuous, branched, septate, thin-walled; hyphal appressoria 4-10 µm diam, mostly solitary, rarely in opposite pairs, lobed; conidiophores erect, arising from the upper surface from mother cells; foot cells flexuous, 25-53(-57) × 7-11 µm, followed by 1-2(-3) cells, forming conidia singly; conidia ellipsoidal-ovoid, or cylindrical-doliiform, 20-38(-47) × 9-15(-17) µm, length/width ratio 1.7-4.2; germ tubes terminal, short (about half as long as the conidial length) to large (about three to four times as long as the conidial length), simple to lobed at the end with appressoria or not.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Guanajuato, San Miguel de Allende municipality, in an urban garden, on Euphorbia pulcherrima (Euphorbiaceae), 12 Mar 2017, S.P. Fernández-Pavía (EBUM28766, MUMH7075), anamorph.

Distribution: There are previous reports of this species on poinsettia in Mexico City and Michoacán (Braun & Cook, 2012; Yáñez-Morales et al., 2009). The taxonomy of this species, including the tentative synonymy of Pseudoidium poinsettiae based on ITS sequences, has recently been discussed in Bradshaw et al. (2025). The phylogenetic-taxonomic status of the poinsettia powdery mildew (P. poinsettiae) is not yet final and requires phylogenetic multilocus analyses. Erysiphe euphorbiicola on Euphorbia spp. is known from North America to South America and some European countries (Bradshaw et al., 2025; Braun & Cook, 2012).

Note: This is the first report of E. euphorbiicola (including Pseudoidium poinsettiae) in the state of Guanajuato.

Erysiphe fallax C. Blomq., S. Rooney-Latham & Fernández-Pavía, Mycosphere 8(9): 1411, 2017.

Mycelium on leaves, in dense patches, amphigenous, white; hyphae 4-7 µm wide, straight to somewhat flexuous, hyaline, septate, thin-walled, branched; hyphal appressoria 8-11 µm diam, nipple-shaped or lobed, solitary; conidiophores slightly curved, single or two on a hyphal cell, arising from the upper surface of mother cells; foot cells 35-55 × 7.5-10 µm, straight, followed by 1-2 cells, mostly shorter than or about as long as the foot cell, occasionally basal septum at the junction with the mother cell elevated up to 8 µm, forming conidia singly; conidia (32.5-)37.5-55 × 15-20 µm, cylindrical, ellipsoid, length/width ratio 1.9-3.2; germ tubes terminal or subterminal, unlobed.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Chilchota municipality, Ichan, in a suburban garden, on Carica papaya (Caricaceae), 12 Apr 2015, R. Gregorio-Cipriano, (EBUM28775, MUMH7190), anamorph.

Distribution: Erysiphe fallax is only known on C. papaya in North America (USA, Mexico) (Braun et al., 2017; Poudel & Zhang, 2019). Mexican records are known from Guerrero, State of Mexico, Tabasco, and Veracruz (García-Álvarez, 1976, 1981; Saldaña et al., 1985; Yáñez-Morales et al., 2009). According to Braun et al. (2017), Erysiphe fallax was previously reported from Mexico on C. papaya as E. polygoni (s. lat.) and Oidium caricae.

Note: Braun et al. (2017) showed that at least five different Erysiphe spp. can infect papaya: E. caricae, E. caricae-papayae, E. diffusa, E. fallax, and E. necator. The asexual morphs of these species, except for E. necator, are morphologically barely distinguishable, i.e., they can only be identified with certainty by molecular sequence analyses. The morphological characters of the new specimen found in Mexico, with exception of those of the conidiophore foot cells that are slightly longer (35-55 µm, vs. 25-50 µm), are concordant with the ones described for E. fallax in Braun et al. (2017). In addition, ITS+28S rDNA sequences from the Mexican specimen aligned with E. fallax (LC228608 = 100%).

This is the first report of E. fallax in Michoacán.

Erysiphe heraclei DC., Flore Française 6: 107, 1815.

Mycelium on stems and leaves, amphigenous, covering the entire surface, dense; hyphae 4-7 µm wide, hyaline; hyphal appressoria 7-10 µm diam, lobed, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores erect, arising centrally from upper surface of the mother cell; foot cells (17-)20-47 × 5-10 µm, straight, followed by 1-3 shorter cells or frequently second cell longer (55-64 µm), sometimes second cell about as long as the foot cell, forming conidia singly; conidia (24-)30-46 × 10-16 µm, cylindrical or doliiform, length/width ratio 1.7-3.5(-3.8); germ tubes terminal.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Copándaro de Galeana municipality, in a commercial crop farm, on Coriandrum sativum (Apiaceae), 5 May 2015, R. Gregorio-Cipriano (EBUM28776, MUMH7191), anamorph.

Distribution: Erysiphe heraclei has a worldwide distribution, reported in Africa, Asia, Australia and New Zealand, Caucasus, Europe, North and South America, on numerous genera and species, mostly in the Apiaceae family (Braun & Cook, 2012). Coriandrum sativum is one of the hosts of E. heraclei, especially in Asia (Amano, 1986; Braun & Paul, 2009). Recently, it has been reported on C. sativum in the USA (Chinnaiah & Gao, 2025). In Mexico, this species was previously recorded on C. sativum in Puebla (Yáñez-Morales et al., 2009), Daucus carota in Michoacán (Rodríguez-Alvarado et al., 2010), Foeniculum vulgare in the State of Mexico (Moreno-Velázquez et al., 2020), Hedera helix in the State of Mexico and Mexico City (Tovar-Pedraza et al., 2016a), and Ammi majus in the State of Mexico (Tovar-Pedraza et al., 2016b).

Note: The ITS+28S sequences of this specimen aligned with E. heraclei (LC010021 = 99.85%). Based solely on ITS data, Erysiphe heraclei constitutes a heterogeneous complex. This specimen exhibits two morphometric differences in comparison to the anamorph of E. heraclei described by Braun & Cook (2012): Appressoria wider (7-10 µm diam, vs. 3-8 µm diam), foot cells of the conidiophores shorter [(17-)20-47 × 5-10 µm, vs. 20-70(-90) × 8-10(-12) µm].

Erysiphe intermedia (U. Braun) U. Braun, Mycotaxon 112: 175, 2010.

Mycelium on leaves, amphigenous, effuse; hyphae 3-8 µm wide, hyaline, septate, thin-walled; hyphal appressoria 3-10 µm diam, nipple shaped to lobed, solitary; conidiophores erect, straight to slightly curved at the base, single, arising from the upper surface of mother cells, position central; foot cells (25-)37.5-63 × 5-8 µm, straight, cylindrical, slightly curved or sinuate at the very base, followed by 2 shorter cells, forming conidia singly; conidia 33-47(-50) × 16-21 µm, cylindrical, doliiform to ellipsoid-ovoid, length/width ratio 1.7-2.8; germ tubes terminal.

Specimens examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Hidalgo municipality, Los Azufres, in wild landscape, on Lupinus elegans (Fabaceae), 13 Aug 2016, N. Gómez-Dorantes (MUMH7056), anamorph; same locality, 9 Sep 2023, A. Soto-Plancarte (EBUM28789).

Distribution: This species is now confined to North American collections on diverse Lupinus spp. (Bradshaw et al., 2022). European specimens previously assigned to E. intermedia (Braun & Cook, 2012) pertain to E. trifoliorum s. lat. (Bradshaw et al., 2022; 2025).

Note: Several Erysiphe species have been recorded causing powdery mildew on Lupinus spp. worldwide: E. diffusa, E. intermedia, and E. pisi (Braun & Cook, 2012), as well as E. lupini and E. trifoliorum s. lat. (Bradshaw et al., 2022). Erysiphe diffusa and E. trifoliorum are easily distinguishable from the Mexican collection by their shorter conidiophore foot cells [25-38 × 7.5-10 µm and (15-)25-45(-55) × 6.5-10(-12) μm, respectively] and slightly narrower conidia (11-17.5 µm) in E. diffusa, whereas E. intermedia, E. lupini, and E. pisi have anamorphic characteristics close to those of Erysiphe intermedia from Mexico, i.e., it is very difficult to separate these species based on the anamorphic morphology. In any case, anamorphs of Erysiphe spp. on Lupinus spp. can only be identified with certainty based on sequence analyses. Our analyses confirmed the close affinity of “Erysiphe sp.” on L. elegans in Mexico to E. intermedia, a species which is now confined to North American collections on diverse Lupinus spp. (Bradshaw et al., 2022).

This is the first report of E. intermedia in Mexico.

Erysiphe necator Schwein., Trans. Amer. Phil. Soc. 4: 270, 1834.

Mycelium on leaves, amphigenous, effuse, causing yellow discolorations; hyphae 2-6 µm wide, hyaline; hyphal appressoria 4-8 µm diam, mostly slightly lobed to lobed, sometimes nipple-shaped, solitary or in opposite pairs; conidiophores flexuous in the lower part, single, arising from the upper surface, position central; foot cells (20-)28-60 × 5-8(-13) µm, twisted at the base, flexuous, sinuous, followed by 1-2(-5) shorter cells, forming conidia singly; conidia 25-35(-40) × 10-18 µm, cylindrical, ellipsoid-ovoid rarely doliiform, length/width ratio 1.4-3.5; germ tubes terminal.

Specimen examined: MEXICO, Michoacán, Tarímbaro municipality, in an urban garden, on Vitis vinifera (Vitaceae), 18 Jul 2016, S.P. Fernández-Pavía (EBUM27943, MUMH7184), anamorph.

Distribution: Erysiphe necator is distributed worldwide on numerous species of the genus Vitis and some on other genera in Vitaceae (Ampelopsis brevipedunculata, A. glandulosa var. heterophylla, Causonis and Cissus) in Africa, Asia, Australia, Europe, New Zealand, North and South America (Braun & Cook, 2012; Yeh et al., 2025). Although accidental infections on hosts of other plant families must also be taken into consideration, as exemplified by records of E. necator on Carica papaya (Caricaceae) and Caryocar sp. (Caryocaraceae) (Braun et al., 2017). This species has been reported in nine states of Mexico based solely on morphological characters: Aguascalientes, Baja California, Chiapas, Coahuila, Durango, Guanajuato, Mexico City, Sonora, and Zacatecas (García-Álvarez, 1976; 1981; Mendoza-Zamora & Pinto-Cortez, 1982).

Note: The sequence retrieved from the Mexican specimen on V. vinifera collected in Michoacán aligned with E. necator (LC028996 = 99.78%), confirming the identification based on morphology. Likewise, the morphological characters are concordant with E. necator (Braun & Cook, 2012).

This is the first report of Erysiphe necator in Michoacán, and the first molecular confirmation of its presence in Mexico.

Erysiphe peckii (U. Braun) U. Braun & S. Takam., Schlechtendalia 4: 12, 2000.

4. Discussion

Morphological and molecular analyses of the 13 Erysiphe species collected from both native and introduced hosts across different localities in Mexico provide new insights into the diversity and distribution of powdery mildews in the region. It is noteworthy that our study includes the description of a new species, Erysiphe burserae, occurring on Bursera spp. In addition, five species―E. ampelopsidis, E. intermedia, E. peckii, E. rumicicola, and E. russellii―are reported for the first time in Mexico, expanding the known range of these taxa.

The discovery of E. burserae is particularly significant, as it represents the first powdery mildew recorded on any Bursera species and only the second known powdery mildew infecting a member of the Burseraceae family (Braun & Cook, 2012). This finding highlights the potential for previously undocumented mildew-host associations within this plant family and underscores the importance of continued surveys and integrative taxonomic approaches in uncovering fungal diversity. Bursera is a genus comprised of about 105 species of trees or shrubs in arid and semiarid regions, as well as tropical forests, distributed from the southern United States of America to Peru and the Caribbean in South America, with ~ 85% species endemic to Mexico (Barrales-Alcalá & Bonfil, 2024; Becerra et al., 2012; Rzedowski et al., 2005). The infected Bursera trees were found in an urban garden in Morelia, which is an area with a subhumid temperate climate. Future research should aim to collect powdery mildew on Bursera species in natural habitats to find out the possible diversity of powdery mildews on species of this plant genus.

Amongst all powdery mildew species in this work, E. burserae was the only one with developed teleomorph beside the anamorph. The teleomorph was seen only in Bursera kerberi but not in Bursera sp., even though both were located near to each other, and were sampled the same day. Similar to this finding, in northwestern Mexico, Podosphaera xanthii shows only sporadic development of sexual morphs (chasmothecia). These were observed on Cucurbita spp. (squash) and Cucumis melo, but not on other cucurbits sampled in the same region and season (Félix-Gastélum et al., 2005). McGrath (1994) suggested that the chasmothecial formation does not depend exclusively on the presence of both compatible mating types and environmental conditions, but may also be influenced by other factors related to the host species.

The discovery of E. diffusa infecting Casuarina equisetifolia is likely a case of a host jump, which is commonly observed in powdery mildews (Beenken, 2017). The confirmation of Lupinus elegans as host of E. intermedia is a significant contribution to the knowledge of host range and distribution of the latter species (Bradshaw et al., 2022). Among the other collected hosts, there are several economically important crops such as Beta vulgaris var. cicla (chard), Carica papaya (papaya), Coriandrum sativum (coriander), Dysphania ambrosioides (epazote), and Vitis vinifera (grape), as well as notable ornamental plants, like Euphorbia pulcherrima (poinsettia), where powdery mildew infections can cause significant losses in the production. Farmers in Mexico often apply large amounts of fungicides at different stages of the crop to prevent or control various fungal diseases, including powdery mildew.

Although only a few plant genera infected with powdery mildews are included in the present study, it is noteworthy that some represent new records for Mexico. Additional studies are needed to gain a better understanding of the diversity of powdery mildews in Mexico, a biodiversity hotspot in regards to plant species.
