Acid–base and electrolyte changes in dogs after packed red blood cell transfusion
Authors: P Bou, I Mesa, R F Ferreira, C Torrente, E G Manzanilla, L Bosch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPacked RBC (pRBC) transfusions are often necessary to enhance organ perfusion and tissue oxygenation in cases of severe anemia.ObjectivesWe aimed to describe changes in acid–base and biochemical parameters in dogs after transfusion of pRBC and potential effects on the outcome.MethodsThe prospective observational study included anemic dogs requiring pRBC transfusions. Venous blood gas and electrolytes were measured pre‐transfusion and repeated within 1 h post‐transfusion. Signalment, pre‐existing conditions and underlying disease(s), number of days spent of hospitalization, the quantity of transfused pRBC units, and the outcome were also recorded. Associations between analytical values and all other parameters before and after transfusion were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. A generalized linear model was generated for each of the blood gas parameters post‐transfusion, including the pre‐transfusion values and the characteristics of the animal as fixed effects. The effect of the blood gas parameters on the mortality and hospitalization length was assessed using logistic regression and generalized linear models, respectively.ResultsTwenty‐six dogs with different causes of anemia were included in the study, with dogs remaining hospitalized for a median of 3 days (range: 0–11). Pre‐transfusion heart rate, respiratory rate, Angap, base excess, and lactate were higher than post‐transfusion values. Post‐transfusion results showed an increase in hematocrit, hemoglobin, HCO3‐, PCO2, tCO2, and chloride. Pre‐transfusion hypophosphatemia and receiving an increased volume of transfused pRBC were associated with a longer hospital stay, while higher pre‐transfusion lactate values were associated with higher mortality.ConclusionspRBC transfusion in anemic dogs was associated with an improvement in perfusion and acid–base parameters. Pre‐transfusion lactate may be an indicator of mortality in dogs.

INTRODUCTION

In cases of moderate‐to‐severe anemia, early detection of tissue hypoxia and subsequent treatment with packed RBC (pRBC) transfusion is often necessary to enhance organ perfusion and tissue oxygenation, thereby reducing patient morbidity and mortality. Customary vital signs such as heart rate and blood pressure may not reliably indicate hypoperfusion, and it is common to examine severely anemic patients with vital signs within the normal reference range; this is a condition known as occult hypoperfusion. 1 When oxygen delivery is insufficient to maintain normal tissue oxygenation, anaerobic metabolism ensues, thus leading to metabolic acidosis. This metabolic acidosis is manifested by a decrease in pH, bicarbonate, and base excess (BE). 1

In human neonatal patients, pRBC transfusion is associated with an improvement in pO2 and pH and also with a reduction in pCO2.2, 3 Changes in BE can be used to aid guidance of fluid resuscitation in critically ill patients, aiming to restore effective circulatory volume and enhance oxygen delivery.4, 5 Also, in both human and veterinary medicine, base excess serves as a marker for potential transfusion.6, 7 Despite its benefits, the administration of pRBC transfusions carries various risks and potential adverse effects. During storage, RBC generate by‐products through anaerobic cell metabolism, altering the biochemical composition of stored pRBCs and simultaneously decreasing pH, glucose, and sodium levels. 8 It has been reported that the administration of large volumes of pRBCs may induce changes in the patient's plasma biochemical parameters.2, 3 Additionally, potassium concentration increases in the additive solution during storage2, 9, 10 and after transfusion can potentially lead to hyperkalemia11, 12, 13, 14 and impact the acid–base status of a critically ill patient.8, 15

Although the pH of stored pRBCs progressively decreases, patients often exhibit metabolic alkalosis rather than the anticipated metabolic acidosis after a pRBC transfusion. The use of sodium citrate, a prevalent anticoagulant used during blood collection, is thought to be the reasoning behind using this. Under normal circumstances, the liver efficiently metabolizes sodium citrate into bicarbonate, leading to an elevation in pH. 9 Therefore, the ultimate shift in the patient's pH is influenced by both the rate and volume of transfusion, as well as the metabolic function of the liver. 16

To the authors' knowledge, no studies have evaluated how stored pRBC transfusion affects perfusion biochemical markers, acid–base balance, and electrolyte concentrations in a population of anemic dogs. Furthermore, there is a lack of research examining potential associations between these variations and outcomes. Identifying clinically significant changes in electrolyte concentration and acid–base balance associated with stored pRBC transfusion could enhance clinicians' decision‐making regarding transfusion, thus helping to prevent or treat potentially life‐threatening complications.

The aim of this study was to assess the effect of stored pRBC transfusion in anemic dogs by examining specific blood gas parameters, acid–base balance, and electrolyte concentrations pre‐ and post‐transfusion. We hypothesized that changes in these values could affect the length of hospitalization and mortality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient inclusion criteria

This study was performed in a veterinary teaching hospital, Fundació Hospital Clinic Veterinari‐Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, where a mixture of referral and primary cases are attended. After obtaining full owner consent, dogs were able to join the study between August 2020 and November 2021. Anemic patients needing RBC replacement and patients only transfused with stored pRBC were included in the study. Ethics approval was not considered necessary by the ethical general board committee of our institution since all the data collected for the study did not translate into further interventions, aside from the ones already included as per protocol in our hospital.

Packed red blood cell units

Whole blood was collected from 26 healthy client‐owned dogs weighing more than 25 kg that had been regularly vaccinated and dewormed. Complete blood counts and biochemistry profiles were performed, and dogs were included only if the results were within reference intervals. All dogs tested negative for Leishmania spp., Ehrlichia spp., Anaplasma spp., Babesia spp., Dirofilaria spp, and Brucella spp. Dogs were placed in lateral recumbency, and the puncture area over a jugular vein was clipped of hair and aseptically prepared (chlorhexidine and alcohol). Jugular venipuncture was performed, and blood was allowed to flow by gravity into a commercial quadruple blood bag (Fresenius Kabi, Hesse, Germany), consisting of a primary bag for the collection of 450 ± 10% mL of blood with 63 mL of CPD anticoagulant (tri‐sodium citrate, sodium phosphate, and dextrose), an empty bag to collect the whole blood after leukocyte filtration, an empty bag for plasma storage and one bag with 100 mL of additive solution SAG‐MAN containing adenine, dextrose, mannitol, and sodium chloride. Collected whole blood units were immediately stored using refrigerated butanediol plates (Compocool, Fresenius Kabi, Hesse, Germany), keeping a constant temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and processed within 12 h. Whole blood units were gently inverted and placed in the centrifuge (Megafuge 40R; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA.) cups, eliminating void spaces by using manufactured plastic adaptors. Weight differences under 0.3 gr between opposite cups were tolerated. Units were centrifuged at 2000 g for 15 min at 20°C with 80 s of acceleration and 110 s of deceleration. Plasma was completely expressed into the transfer bag using a manual plasma extractor (Terumo Medical Corporation, New Jersey, USA). A volume of 100 mL of SAG‐MAN was added to the pRBC bag. Packed RBC units were stored at 4°C in a dedicated refrigerator (Fiocchetti, Luzzara, Italy) until their clinical use.

All units were stored for 5–31 days until they were used. Storage time was recorded for every unit used in the study and correlated with measured parameters and outcomes.

Collection data

Physical examination parameters were recorded immediately pre‐ and post‐transfusion in all patients. Venous blood samples (1–2 mL) were collected from the jugular vein of anemic patients 0–60 min prior to and 1 h after completing the transfusion, conforming to the hospital's routine clinical evaluation protocol. All patients were stabilized depending on their status upon admission. No additional samples were drawn for this study. After obtaining baseline blood samples, pRBCs were administered at a rate of 2.5–5 mL/kg/h for 2–4 h. The pRBC volume and infusion rate were determined based on individual responses to treatment and at the discretion of the treating clinician.

All samples were promptly analyzed under anaerobic conditions using a point‐of‐care testing machine (VetStat Electrolyte Blood Gas Analyzer; IDEXX Laboratories SL, Madrid, Spain). The recorded parameters included pH, PCO2, PO2, HCO3‐, BE, SO2, tCO2, Angap, lactate, potassium, sodium, and chloride. Albumin and phosphorus were measured using a commercial biochemical analyzer (Catalyst DX; IDEXX Laboratories SL, Madrid, Spain). Additionally, patient signalment, underlying disease(s), number of days of hospitalization, volume of pRBC administered, and mortality rates were documented.

STATISTICAL METHODS

All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). No cases were excluded from the statistical analysis. The normality of data was assessed using graphic and statistical methods, and data were log‐transformed when required. The alpha level for the determination of significance was 0.05. Differences between the values for all variables pre‐ and post‐transfusion were calculated using the Wilcoxon test (two related samples) irrespective of normality. A generalized linear model was generated for each of the blood gas parameters after the transfusion, including the value of the parameter prior to the transfusion and the characteristics of the patient (ie, age, sex, body weight, pRBC total volume transfused, transfusion speed, pRBC units, and storage time) as fixed effects. The effect of the blood gas parameters pre‐ and post‐transfusion on the mortality and hospitalization length was assessed using logistic regression and generalized linear models, respectively, using in both cases stepwise selection. For the model analyzing hospitalization length, animals that died after transfusion were removed from the database.

RESULTS

Twenty‐six dogs, with a mean age of 8 ± 3.54 years and a mean body weight of 20.9 ± 9.43 kg, were enrolled in the study. Underlying causes of anemia included immune‐mediated hemolytic anemia (9/26), acute bleeding due to neoplasia (9/26) such as splenic hemangiosarcoma (5), hepatic carcinoma (2), histiocytic sarcoma (2) and other causes: (8/26) such as renal disease (2), gastrointestinal bleeding (2), infectious disease (2), hemolytic anemia due to onion intoxication (1), and unknown origin (1). Dogs received an average of 15 mL/kg ± 4.52 of pRBC and remained hospitalized for a mean of 3 days (range: 0–11). During the study period, four patients died.

DISCUSSION

This prospective study aimed to assess the effect of stored pRBC transfusion in anemic dogs by comparing specific blood gas parameters and electrolyte concentration values pre‐ and post‐transfusion. Our results support an improvement in perfusion parameters and acid–base status after transfusion and a new potential prognostic indicator of mortality, such as an increased lactate value before transfusion. We also found that hypophosphatemia prior to transfusion implies longer periods of hospitalization.

Studies in human medicine, 2 assessing the metabolic effect of stored pRBC transfusion on preterm neonates showed that pRBC transfusions were effective in improving anemia, increasing pH and pO2. Anemic patients develop a compensatory response, which increases their stroke volume, heart rate, and oxygen extraction ratio, and shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right. 17 When oxygen delivery is inadequate to maintain normal tissue oxygenation, anaerobic metabolism, and subsequent metabolic acidosis occur, leading to acidosis that can manifest as a change in pH, HCO3, or BE. 7

In this study, we observed an improvement in anemia post‐transfusion; however, we did not detect an increase in pH. This lack of change to the pH could derive from various factors.

Fundamentally, only six dogs in our study exhibited a pH level below 7.3 pre‐transfusion. Among these, 4 out of 6 were suffering from ongoing bleeding, while the remaining two dogs had decompensated immune‐mediated diseases. It is highly likely that the observed acidemia in these patients resulted from the severe hypoperfusion and hypovolemia they were experiencing at the time. The remaining 19 out of 25 dogs within our study presented with a pH level within the normal reference range. All patients were considered normovolemic, yet displayed signs of decreased oxygen delivery. The limited number of patients with abnormal pH levels pre‐transfusion may have hindered the ability to obtain results comparable to those observed in human medicine.

Our study exhibited a statistically significant difference in HR, RR, Agap, lactate, and BE post‐transfusion, though all these parameters were higher pre‐transfusion. These findings are to be expected due to the response in volume expansion and improvement in oxygen delivery to the tissues. 18

In human medicine, BE is a great prognostic indicator for the requirement of a blood transfusion and a significant increase in mortality when the initial BE is less than −8 mEq/L. 6 However, in canine models, BE has shown potential as an indicator of mortality and oxygen deficit that correlates with blood pressure, cardiac output, or serum lactate level. 43 A previous study in dogs with blunt trauma showed that admission BE was an independent predictor of mortality and blood transfusion requirement within 24 h. Animals who require a red blood cell transfusion had significantly lower admission BE values than those patients who did not require one (median BE −8.4 vs −4.7, respectively). In addition, the patients that died or were euthanized had significantly lower admission BE than those that survived (median BE −7.3 vs −4.9, respectively). 7 Furthermore, a study by Ozakin 19 revealed that elevated lactate levels and low BE measured at the time of admission served as prompt and effective markers for predicting risk factors associated with the necessity for blood transfusion. 19 Our study exhibited similar results to these previous studies, and the mean BE pre‐transfusion was −6.2, which is similar to the values in the other studies. However, in the present study, it wasn't associated with mortality.

We observed a significant improvement in various parameters pre and post‐transfusion, including Hct, Hb, HCO3‐, pCO2, tCO2, and Cl‐, where all these parameters exhibited an increase post‐transfusion. The anticipated rise in Hct and Hb post‐transfusion aligns with the administration of RBC. A similar elevation in chloride, as reported in human literature, was also evident in our study. 20 Perhaps a possible explanation for the increase in chloride for patients with IMHA could be the presence of distal renal tubular acidosis 20 or even the administration of post‐transfusion fluid therapy (Nacl 0.9%) given that the samples were extracted 1 h after ‐transfusion. 21 We also found that the Hct and Hb increase post‐transfusion was related to the quantity of pRBC administered. This was not unexpected, as theoretically, every mL/kg of pRBCs increases 1% the Hct, 22 and patients treated within the study received a range of 2.5–5 mL/kg of pRBCs. Therefore, those who received a higher ml/kg had a larger increase in Hct and Hb.

Contrary to expectations, an increase in PCO2 and tCO2 was noted in our patients. This conflicts with previous studies in humans with severe hemorrhage after trauma, which demonstrated a decrease in these parameters following transfusion. 23 Blood transfusion, in hypovolemic patients, improves cardiac output, tissue blood flow, and metabolic acidosis, and as a result, pCO2 decreases. One explanation for our finding could be that not all patients included in this study were hypovolemic. Also, many patients in our study had a markedly increased respiratory rate pre‐transfusion secondary to severe anemia, resulting in respiratory alkalosis, which resolved after transfusion, and therefore, we noted an increase in CO2. Another potential explanation is that venous samples rather than arterial samples were used in our study, and this could have impacted our results. However, it is important to note that patients initially presenting with abnormally high PCO2 levels, exhibited a decrease in levels post‐transfusion with the improvement of tissue blood flow following transfusion and amended the metabolic acidosis in some cases, which is also consistent with study findings in human medicine. 24 It is also possible that the limited number of patients enrolled in this study may have contributed to our inability to detect these changes in PCO2.

We observed a statistically significant association between mortality and pre‐transfusion hyperlactatemia, indicating that higher pre‐transfusion values were associated with higher mortality. Lactate is a product of anaerobic metabolism, and it has been extensively used as an early biomarker of tissue hypoperfusion.25, 26 However, hyperlactatemia could be related to other factors such as neoplasia, drugs, and thiamine deficiency.27, 28, 29

In human medicine, hyperlactatemia is an indication for blood transfusion, 25 and in veterinary medicine, hyperlactatemia can occur commonly in critically ill patients with disorders such as shock, post‐cardiac arrest, severe sepsis, and neoplasia, among other reasons. 30 However, there is growing evidence in veterinary medicine that lactate clearance over the initial 24 h is more important than a single value.31, 32 In this study, the initial lactate value indicated mortality rather than the clearance over the patient's hospitalization stay. However, most of the patients in the study presenting with a higher pre‐transfusion value of lactate had a final diagnosis of neoplasia. Therefore, we think it is possible that a combination of type A and B hyperlactatemia was the main reason for the higher values. In addition, all the patients diagnosed with neoplasia in this study obtained a poor prognosis, thus correlating with the higher mortality rate observed. Pre‐transfusion hyperlactatemia in anemic patients has been previously described as a potential negative prognostic indicator in dogs with IMHA. 33 Nevertheless, our study includes different types of anemia, and in our opinion, this result should be considered, but given the low number of patients included, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Hypophosphatemia and length of hospitalization were negatively associated. In human medicine, hypophosphatemia is among the most frequently encountered electrolyte metabolic disturbances in critically ill patients, with an incidence prevalence ranging between 2.4 and 100%. 34 Human studies have found an association between hypophosphatemia and degree of illness35, 36 and a prolonged stay in ICU.37, 38

Also, an association between hypophosphatemia and increased mortality seems to be linked. However, it remains unclear whether hypophosphatemia contributes to mortality or merely is a marker of illness severity.38, 39 In our study, patients who had hypophosphatemia had a prolonged LOH. This could be secondary to the severity of illness.

This study has several limitations; firstly, the relatively small sample size may account for the failure to detect further changes in acid–base parameters and electrolyte levels. Secondly, blood samples were collected from a jugular vein both pre‐ and post‐transfusion. This approach was chosen for its practical clinical simplicity. Numerous studies in dogs have investigated and compared blood gas values among central venous, peripheral venous, and arterial blood samples. The findings across these studies consistently indicated that pH, PCO2, HCO3, and BE correlated well in both hemodynamically stable and hypovolemic dogs. As a result, our results are unlikely to be significantly influenced by the chosen sampling method.40, 41, 42 Lastly, despite the fact that blood samples were drawn on admission without fluid therapy. There was a lack of control for other treatments given between samples, and there was diversity in the underlying disease process. Additionally, only one unit per patient (15 mL/kg ± 4.52) of pRBC was administered, and a larger volume is sometimes required in severe hypoxemic situations. 43

CONCLUSIONS

This is the first published study in dogs that evaluates the effect of stored pRBC transfusion on clinical parameters, acid–base balance, and electrolyte concentration in anemic dogs. The transfusion of stored pRBC improves perfusion and acid–base parameters. Higher values in pre‐transfusion lactate may be a new negative prognostic indicator in anemic dogs that require blood transfusions, while pre‐transfusion and hypophosphatemia could be associated with prolonged hospitalization. However, further research is warranted. No conflicts of interest have been declared.
