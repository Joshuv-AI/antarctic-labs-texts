Impact of RSV Preventive Strategies on Hospitalizations for Bronchiolitis in Infants
Authors: Marine Tarizzo, Julie Mesinèle, Blandine Prevost, Sophie Denamur, Elora Peulier‐Maitre, Loïc Guillot, Nadia Nathan, Harriet Corvol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the leading cause of bronchiolitis and hospitalization in infants. Since 2023, France has implemented two preventive strategies aimed at reducing RSV burden: long‐acting monoclonal antibody nirsevimab and RSV vaccine for pregnant women, the latter becoming available from September 2024. This study aimed to assess the impact of RSV preventive strategies on the clinical severity and viral epidemiology of bronchiolitis in hospitalized infants.Material and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study at Armand Trousseau University Hospital, including infants under 12 months hospitalized for a first episode of bronchiolitis between October and March during four winter seasons (2021–2025). Clinical and virological data were compared between the pre‐intervention (2021–2023) and post‐intervention (2023–2025) periods. Subgroup analyses were performed based on RSV infection and prophylaxis status.ResultsA total of 509 infants were included (292 pre‐intervention, 217 post‐intervention). Post‐intervention infants were older (median, 3.2 vs. 1.9 months, p = 0.001), required less oxygen therapy (58.5% vs. 70.9%, p < 0.001), and had shorter hospital stays (median, 3.0 vs. 5.0 days, p < 0.001). Compared with untreated infants (n = 140), those who received nirsevimab (n = 67) had significantly lower rates of RSV infection and viral co‐infections (OR: 0.15 (95%CI: 0.08–0.29), p < 0.001; and 40.4% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.035), while human metapneumovirus was detected more frequently (OR: 11.00 (95%CI: 3.50–34.55), p < 0.001).Discussion and ConclusionDuring the period following the introduction of RSV preventive strategies, a reduced hospital burden, milder clinical courses, and shifts in viral epidemiology were observed. These findings support the continued implementation of RSV immunoprophylaxis and underscore the importance of ongoing surveillance to monitor its long‐term impact.Keywords: acute bronchiolitis, hospitalization, infant, maternal vaccination, nirsevimab, RSV

Introduction

Bronchiolitis is an acute lower respiratory tract infection and remains the leading cause of hospitalization for respiratory illness in infants under 12 months of age. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the primary causative agent, identified in approximately 60%–80% of bronchiolitis cases during seasonal peaks, owing to its high transmissibility, predictable seasonality, and potential to cause severe disease in young infants [1, 2, 3].

Other viral pathogens are also frequently implicated, including rhinoviruses, human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza viruses, adenoviruses, and, more recently, seasonal coronaviruses and SARS‐CoV‐2 [4, 5]. Co‐infections are relatively common, although their impact on disease severity remains an area of investigation.

In temperate regions such as France, the annual rise in bronchiolitis incidence typically begins in late October and peaks in mid‐December, resulting in a significant increase in primary care visits and a surge in emergency department admissions [6, 7]. Each year, bronchiolitis affects an estimated 30% of children under 2 years of age in France [8]. Among them, approximately 2%–3% of infants under 12 months require hospitalization for severe bronchiolitis necessitating oxygen therapy, nasogastric feeding, or intensive care support.

Until recently, RSV immunoprophylaxis was limited to high‐risk infants eligible for monthly injections of palivizumab. However, in 2023, two major advances in pediatric RSV prevention were approved in Europe, the United Kingdom, and North America [9]. The first, Sanofi's nirsevimab/Beyfortus, is a long‐acting monoclonal antibody targeting the RSV fusion (F) protein, designed to provide season‐long protection with a single dose administered to all infants during their first RSV season [10, 11]. The second, Pfizer's Abrysvo, is a bivalent prefusion F protein vaccine approved for administration during late pregnancy [12]. Nirsevimab and the maternal RSV vaccination are two immunoprophylactic strategies designed to confer passive immunity to newborns.

In France, nirsevimab was incorporated into the national immunization program starting with the 2023–2024 RSV season, while maternal RSV vaccination became available in 2024 for pregnant women in their third trimester [13, 14]. Together, these interventions mark a paradigm shift in RSV prevention, offering the potential for broad population‐level protection against severe bronchiolitis in infancy [15, 16]. Beyond reducing the burden of RSV infection, these strategies may also alter the clinical and viral landscape of bronchiolitis, potentially by reducing RSV circulation and shifting the etiological spectrum toward less virulent pathogens.

The objective of this study was to assess the impact of RSV preventive strategies, nirsevimab and maternal RSV vaccination, on the clinical severity and viral epidemiology of bronchiolitis in infants hospitalized at a tertiary pediatric center, by comparing pre‐intervention (2021–2023) and post‐intervention (2023–2025) periods over 4 consecutive winter seasons (2021–2025).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective, observational, monocentric study was conducted in the Pediatric Pulmonology Department of Armand Trousseau University Hospital (Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris) in Paris, France. It included infants under 12 months of age who were admitted for a first episode of acute bronchiolitis during the autumn/winter seasons over a 4‐year period, from 2021 to 2025. The study covered the 2021–2022, 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025 RSV seasons, each defined as spanning from October 1 to March 31. Exclusion criteria were: age over 12 months, a history of previous bronchiolitis episodes, and/or recurrent wheezing episodes, in accordance with French national guidelines for the management of bronchiolitis [17]. Admission criteria, thresholds for initiation of oxygen therapy, and discharge policies remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Patient selection was based on ICD‐10 diagnostic codes for bronchiolitis. Three physicians independently reviewed medical records and extracted demographic, clinical, and laboratory data using a standardized data collection form. For the 2021–2023 pre‐intervention period, previously collected and published data from our center were used [4]. For the 2023–2025 post‐intervention period, defined as the period following the implementation of RSV preventive strategies, the same variables were collected, including age and weight at the time of hospitalization, sex, and the presence of underlying conditions (such as prematurity, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital heart disease, esophageal atresia, sickle cell disease, intrauterine growth restriction, diaphragmatic hernia, or genetic disorders). Virological data were obtained from PCR testing of nasopharyngeal swabs or aspirates to identify causative pathogens. In addition, clinical course data were recorded, including length of hospital stay, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), type of respiratory support (oxygen therapy, non‐invasive ventilation, invasive ventilation, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), and nutritional support modalities.

For the 2023–2025 post‐intervention period, the analysis was further expanded to include severity markers at hospital admission—specifically respiratory rate, oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry, and signs of respiratory distress. Information on RSV preventive strategies, including administration of nirsevimab and maternal RSV vaccination, was obtained from each infant's electronic medical record.

Throughout the study period (2021–2025), admission criteria, oxygen thresholds, and discharge policies remained unchanged. Clinical decisions regarding hospitalization, initiation of oxygen therapy, and discharge were based on standardized institutional protocols aligned with national French guidelines for bronchiolitis management [17] and were applied consistently across all seasons. Similarly, virological testing practices did not change over the study period: PCR testing was routinely prescribed for infants hospitalized with bronchiolitis, with sample collection and processing performed by the same healthcare staff using consistent procedures and laboratory methods.

This study was conducted in a post‐COVID epidemiological context previously characterized by increased bronchiolitis severity and RSV burden, as described in our earlier work [4]. The current analysis extends this observation period to assess changes following the implementation of RSV preventive strategies. The study period spans years marked by substantial disruptions in RSV epidemiology related to the COVID‐19 pandemic, including altered seasonality and atypical inter‐seasonal circulation, as previously reported in France and other European countries [18]. In particular, reduced RSV activity during the 2020–2021 winter and increased out‐of‐season circulation during subsequent summers have been described [18]. RSV seasons were defined from October 1 to March 31 for all study years, in accordance with the French bronchiolitis surveillance system and consistent with routine surveillance and hospitalization practices in France. Although this approach may not fully capture atypical inter‐seasonal RSV circulation, it allows for standardized period‐based comparisons across consecutive years.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of the French Society for Respiratory Medicine (Société de Pneumologie de Langue Française, #CEPRO_2020‐080). In accordance with French laws for observational studies, the requirement for written informed consent was waived (study BronChioVID N°20201119185601).

Statistical Analysis

We described patient characteristics and viral epidemiology across two distinct periods, before and after the implementation of RSV preventive strategies. The pre‐intervention period included the 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 seasons (Oct 1 to Mar 31), while the post‐intervention period included the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 seasons, over the same months. Descriptive statistics are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and as counts and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann‐Whitney test due to non‐normal distributions. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square (χ 2) test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

A focused analysis was then conducted for the 2023–2025 post‐intervention period. Two main analyses were performed, each based on a distinct exposure. First, to assess the impact of RSV preventive strategies, the exposure was defined as RSV preventive therapy status, with infants classified into three groups: (i) no preventive therapy (reference group), (ii) maternal RSV vaccination, and (iii) nirsevimab. Second, a complementary analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of virological status, using RSV infection status (RSV‐positive vs. RSV‐negative) as the exposure. The outcomes of interest included markers of disease at admission (respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and signs of respiratory distress) and indicators of clinical course during hospitalization, including length of stay, need for oxygen therapy, admission to the ICU, and use of non‐invasive ventilation, invasive ventilation, or extracorporeal circulation. To assess the association between exposures and outcomes, regression analyses were performed. Linear regression models were applied for continuous outcomes (respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and length of stay), with results reported as beta (β) coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). For binary outcomes (signs of respiratory distress, oxygen therapy, admission to the ICU, non‐invasive ventilation, invasive ventilation, and extracorporeal circulation), odds ratios (OR) and 95%CI were estimated using logistic regression models.

Regression models were adjusted for age, sex, presence of comorbidities, and, when appropriate, RSV status or RSV preventive therapy status. Age, sex, and comorbidities were selected a priori as potential confounders because they may influence both disease severity and the likelihood of exposure to RSV preventive strategies and/or RSV infection status. In particular, eligibility for nirsevimab is age‐dependent, preventive uptake may vary according to patient characteristics, and infants with comorbidities are more likely to be targeted for preventive interventions and to experience more severe disease [17, 19, 20]. RSV status or RSV preventive therapy status was additionally included as an adjustment variable when not considered the primary exposure, given its potential association with clinical severity and hospitalization outcomes.

For PCR‐based viral identification, infections caused by Coronavirus 229E, SARS‐CoV‐2, Coronavirus NL63, and Coronavirus OC43 were infrequent and therefore grouped into a single category labeled “Coronavirus.” Patients with missing PCR results were excluded from virological subgroup analyses but were retained in descriptive and clinical analyses when appropriate.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2, http://www.R-project.org/), with a p‐value ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical Characteristics of the Infants and Viral Epidemiology Over 4 Years (2021–2025)

Between 2021 and 2025, a total of 1,157 hospitalizations were recorded during winter seasons (Oct 1–Mar 31). The overall number of hospitalizations decreased across successive winter seasons, from 333 in 2021–2022 and 297 in 2022–2023 to 295 in 2023–2024 and 232 in 2024–2025. A total of 648 hospitalizations were excluded, and exclusions due to age older than 12 months increased over time (136 during the 2021–2023 period vs. 182 during the 2023–2025 period). After applying the study inclusion criteria, 509 individual infants were retained for analysis: 292 in the pre‐intervention period (2021–2023) and 217 in the post‐intervention period (2023–2025), corresponding to an average of 146 and 108 infants per winter season, respectively. The number of infants analyzed decreased across successive winter seasons, from 151 in 2021–2022 and 141 in 2022–2023 to 120 in 2023–2024 and 97 in 2024–2025 (Figure 2).

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics, clinical course, and viral epidemiology of these patients. There were no significant differences between the two periods in terms of sex distribution (p = 0.719) or the proportion of infants with comorbidities (p = 0.095). However, infants hospitalized during the 2023–2025 post‐intervention period were significantly older than those in the 2021–2023 pre‐intervention period (median of 3.2 months vs. 1.9 months, p = 0.001). Notably, infants aged 0 to 3 months represented 64.4% of hospitalizations in the 2021–2023 pre‐intervention period, compared to 47.5% in 2023–2025 post‐intervention period, a statistically significant difference in age‐group distribution (p = 0.001). Moreover, 54.5% of children in the pre‐intervention period were born shortly before or at the beginning of the RSV season (September, October, November), compared with 41.4% in the post‐intervention period. In the latter group, birth months were more evenly distributed across the second half of the year (July to December).

Regarding hospitalization outcomes, no significant differences were observed between the 2 periods for the proportions of infants requiring ICU admission or respiratory support (non‐invasive or invasive ventilation) with p‐values ranging from 0.094 to 0.445. Infants who received prophylaxis were significantly younger at the time of hospitalization than those in the non‐prophylaxis group (median, 3.7 months, IQR: 1.6–5.6): median 0.7 months (IQR: 0.5–1.7) for the maternal RSV vaccination group (p = 0.001), and 2.4 months (IQR: 1.0–4.0) for the nirsevimab group (p = 0.008). Compared to the no preventive therapies group, the proportion of infants aged 6–12 months was significantly lower in the nirsevimab group (OR: 0.19 (95% CI: 0.06–0.58, p = 0.004). Infants in the nirsevimab group were more likely to be male and to present with comorbidities (OR: 1.94 (95% CI: 1.07–3.52), p = 0.030, and OR: 8.20 (95% CI: 3.08–21.86), p < 0.001, respectively).

Of the 217 infants hospitalized during this period, only 7 did not undergo PCR testing, with no significant differences between groups. Viral distribution differed markedly based on RSV preventive therapy status (Table 2A and Figure 3B). RSV and rhinovirus were the most frequently identified viruses in the 3 groups; however, RSV was detected in 83.8% in those without therapy compared to only 43.8% of infants who had received nirsevimab (OR: 0.15 (95%CI: 0.08–0.29), p < 0.001), and 50% in the maternal RSV vaccination group (OR: 0.19 (95%CI: 0.05–0.72), p = 0.015). In the nirsevimab group, human metapneumovirus was the third most common virus (25.0%), versus only 2.9% in the non‐prophylaxis group (OR: 11.0 (95%CI: 3.50–34.55), p < 0.001). Additionally, they had fewer co‐infections with two or more viruses (25% vs. 40.4%, OR: 0.49 (95%CI: 0.25–0.95), p = 0.035). Among the 10 newborns in the maternal RSV vaccination group, 4 viral species were identified, with coinfections for 2 infants. These differences in viral distribution are illustrated in Figure 3, which also shows that the pattern observed in untreated children during 2023–2025 closely resembled that of the pre‐intervention period (2021–2023).

While oxygen saturation at admission did not differ significantly between the three groups, the proportion of infants requiring oxygen therapy during hospitalization was significantly lower among those who had received nirsevimab (40.3% vs. 67.1% in the non‐prophylaxis group, OR: 0.44 (95%CI: 0.21–0.89), p = 0.023). No significant differences were observed in the use of non‐invasive ventilation or ICU admission rates for both preventive therapy groups. The average length of hospital stay was similar in the non‐prophylaxis group compared to prophylaxis groups (median, 3.0 days (IQR: 2.0–5.0) vs. 2.0 days (IQR: 1.2–3.0) days for maternal RSV vaccination (p = 0.149), versus 3.0 days (IQR: 2.0–5.0) days for nirsevimab (p = 0.102)) (Table 2B).

Discussion

This study assessed the impact of the introduction of RSV preventive strategies, nirsevimab and maternal RSV vaccination, on the clinical and virological characteristics of infants hospitalized for acute bronchiolitis over 2 consecutive winter seasons (2023–2025), compared to the pre‐prophylaxis period (2021–2023).

First, our findings suggest period‐related differences in RSV‐related hospitalizations, the need for oxygen therapy, and length of hospital stay. We observed a significant decrease in the number of hospitalizations for bronchiolitis during the post‐intervention period, with a mean of 108 infants admitted per season compared to 146 previously. While multifactorial, this reduction coincides with the introduction of large‐scale RSV preventive measures in France and may reflect their early potential benefit at the population level, as previously reported [7, 10, 21]. Additionally, infants hospitalized in the post‐intervention period were older on average, and the proportion of admissions among those aged 0–3 months, a group particularly vulnerable to severe RSV infection, decreased substantially. This finding is consistent with the known efficacy of nirsevimab and maternal RSV vaccination in protecting the youngest infants during their first RSV season [22, 23].

Oxygen therapy was significantly less frequently required in the post‐intervention cohort and hospital stays were shorter by more than 1 day on average. Previous studies have suggested that preventive strategies may not only reduce the number of RSV infections but also attenuate disease severity among breakthrough cases, potentially by reducing RSV burden or shifting infections toward less virulent pathogens [24]. Our findings do not allow such conclusions, as ICU admission rates and the need for ventilatory support remained stable across the two periods. Moreover, no difference in hospital length of stay was observed between the prophylaxis and non‐prophylaxis groups during the 2023–2025 period, and reduced use of oxygen therapy was limited to the nirsevimab group.

Although our analyses were adjusted for several key confounders, unmeasured factors and broader secular trends may still have influenced the findings. Our previous work showed that the post‐COVID period (2021–2023) was associated with increased bronchiolitis severity and a higher prevalence of RSV infections [4]. RSV circulation during this time also followed atypical patterns, with a progressive increase during the 2021 interseason, an early peak in 2021–2022, and a gradual return toward pre‐pandemic seasonality in 2022 [18]. In addition, the implementation and subsequent relaxation of non‐pharmaceutical interventions during the COVID‐19 pandemic may have contributed to these epidemiological changes [25]. Reduced RSV exposure during the pandemic, followed by renewed viral circulation after relaxation of these measures, may have increased susceptibility among immunologically naïve infants, a phenomenon often referred to as “immunity debt” [26, 27]. These factors may also have influenced healthcare‐seeking behavior and hospitalization patterns independently of RSV preventive strategies [28].

The present study captures a later phase of this post‐COVID rebound, coinciding with the implementation of RSV preventive strategies. As such, part of the observed reduction in hospitalizations and differences in clinical parameters may relate to evolving epidemiological dynamics independent of prevention. Importantly, local clinical practices and hospitalization patterns are guided by the French national guidelines for the management of bronchiolitis, updated in 2019 and unchanged throughout the study period [17]. No major modifications occurred in admission criteria, clinical management, or virological testing recommendations, reducing the likelihood that shifts in healthcare‐seeking behavior, admission thresholds, or surveillance intensity fully explain the observed trends. Consequently, admission thresholds, clinical management strategies, and virological testing practices were stable over time, making it unlikely that these factors alone explain the observed reductions. While residual confounding related to secular trends cannot be entirely excluded, the consistency of the associations across multiple clinical outcomes and subgroups strengthens the potential contribution the of RSV preventive strategies, particularly nirsevimab, to the observed trends.

Second, differences in clinical and virological outcomes were observed according to vaccinal status during the prophylaxis period (2021–2023). Given the small number of infants in the maternal RSV vaccination group (n = 10), this precludes drawing conclusions regarding this preventive strategy.

Regarding the nirsevimab strategy, our virological data indicate a lower proportion of RSV detection among infants who received nirsevimab (43.8%) compared to untreated infants (83.8%). Interestingly, the viral distribution in non‐prophylaxed infants during 2023–2025 closely mirrored that of the pre‐intervention years, supporting a role of RSV immunization in shaping epidemiological trends.

Conversely, human metapneumovirus was more frequently observed in this group, while co‐infections were less prevalent. The observed increase in the proportion of non‐RSV viruses, particularly human metapneumovirus, should be interpreted with caution. As this study is hospital‐based and lacks population‐level denominators, these findings likely reflect proportional redistribution within the viral landscape of hospitalized bronchiolitis, rather than absolute increases in viral circulation. Reduced RSV dominance following implementation of preventive strategies may have shifted the relative contribution of other respiratory viruses, a phenomenon previously described in post‐COVID respiratory epidemiology. Since the COVID‐19 pandemic, concurrent circulation of RSV and human metapneumovirus has been increasingly documented, with overlapping seasonal peaks reported in several countries [29, 30]. Such co‐circulation raises the possibility of viral interference mediated by the innate immune response, as well as ecological competition between respiratory pathogens, which may shape the observed epidemiological patterns. By reducing the dominance of RSV through widespread immunoprophylaxis, a redistribution of the viral landscape may occur, favoring the proportional detection of other viruses such as human metapneumovirus and rhinovirus. The higher rate of co‐infections among non‐immunized infants is highlighted as a particularly relevant finding, as it may reflect decreased susceptibility to multiple respiratory viruses in the presence of RSV immunoprophylaxis. The clinical implications of these shifts remain uncertain; although we observed a reduced need for oxygen therapy among infants who received nirsevimab, no differences were found for other markers of disease severity, including ICU admission, invasive or non‐invasive ventilation, or extracorporeal circulation. Multicentre studies are warranted to further characterize outcomes in the context of evolving viral ecology and frequent viral co‐infections.

Finally, our data show that a substantial proportion of hospitalized infants had received nirsevimab, especially among those with comorbidities, those under 3 months of age and male. The overrepresentation of infants with comorbidities likely reflects appropriate implementation of national recommendations and targeted outreach to high‐risk populations rather than a causal relationship. Similarly, the higher proportion of male infants in this group may be explained by the well‐documented male predominance in bronchiolitis hospitalizations [19], and by previous reports highlighting differences in uptake of preventive interventions across clinical and sociodemographic groups [20].

Real‐world data emerging from multiple high‐income countries provide mixed evidence regarding the effectiveness of RSV preventive strategies. Whereas several studies suggest a benefit of nirsevimab [15, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37], evidence regarding maternal RSV vaccination is also supportive [36, 37, 38, 39] although uncertainty remains regarding the durability of its protection [37]. These international experiences highlight the transformative impact of RSV immunoprophylaxis programs and emphasize the importance of sustained implementation and monitoring.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective and observational design, which precludes causal inference, as well as its monocentric design, which may restrict generalizability. Furthermore, this study was not designed as a prospective birth cohort and includes only cases of bronchiolitis requiring hospitalization, which may limit the representativeness of the findings. We did not analyze outpatient visits or hospital avoidance, which could further illustrate the impact of preventive measures. In addition, the lack of data on potential genetic risk factors, such as parental history of asthma, may have led to residual confounding, as these factors could influence the severity of respiratory infections [40, 41, 42]. Importantly, as our analysis is based on period comparisons, the observed differences may also be influenced by secular trends unrelated to RSV preventive strategies. In particular, changes in healthcare‐seeking behavior, admission thresholds, and clinical practices in the post‐COVID context may have affected hospitalization patterns and disease severity independently of immunoprophylaxis. Although local clinical protocols remained stable during the study period, broader changes in healthcare utilization cannot be fully excluded. Lastly, the epidemiology of respiratory infections is inherently dynamic, with viral circulation patterns fluctuating annually [8]. Moreover, variability in vaccination coverage for RSV preventive therapies, which appears to stem from socioeconomic deprivation and barriers to accessing healthcare, may have influenced our findings [43, 44].

In conclusion, the implementation of RSV preventive strategies is associated with a reduction in RSV‐related hospitalizations, lower oxygen therapy requirements, and changes in viral epidemiology. These findings support the continued rollout of immunoprophylaxis and highlight the need for ongoing surveillance to assess long‐term impacts, optimize vaccine targeting, and understand evolving patterns of respiratory infections in infancy.
