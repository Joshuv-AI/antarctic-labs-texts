Centromere protein I facilitates breast cancer tumorigenesis and disease progression through modulation of Wnt/β-Catenin signaling
Authors: Chaoshen Wu, Yibing Zhou, Yuxiao Mu, Yijie Cheng, Wenqian Xu, Mengna Huang, Jingyuan Xu, Ming Li, Jianfeng Gu, Xuli Meng, Da Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer (BCa) is a major contributor to female mortality worldwide. Treatment resistance and tumor heterogeneity contribute to the lack of effective therapeutic targets, posing a significant challenge in BCa management. CENPI, a core centromere protein involved in chromosome segregation, has emerging evidence implicating it in oncogenesis across diverse malignancies. However, its functional and molecular mechanisms in BCa remain unclear.MethodsWe analyzed CENPI expression and its clinical significance by using the BCa dataset from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and immunohistochemical staining of 3 human BCa tissue samples. Cellular functional assays and mice xenograft models were utilized to assess the effects of CENPI on BCa growth. RNA sequencing combined with bioinformatics analysis was conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CENPI function, with further validation through Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and TOP/FOP flash assays.ResultsCENPI was aberrantly overexpressed in BCa, with elevated expression levels strongly associated with disease progression and poor prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that CENPI significantly promoted breast carcinogenesis in both cellular and animal models. Mechanistically, CENPI increased BCa progression and malignant phenotypes by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin axis.ConclusionsCENPI is a critical oncogene in BCa, driving tumorigenesis and disease progression via the Wnt/β-catenin axis, which represents a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for BCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04001-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BCa) is characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation and malignant transformation of breast epithelial cells under the influence of multiple carcinogenic factors, and represent the most common female malignancy with approximately 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2022. The incidence rate continues to rise annually, and the BCa accounted for 665,000 female deaths, representing 15.4% of all female cancer-related mortality despite advancements in treatment [1, 2]. The classification of BCa is based on molecular and histological characteristics, with four major subtypes recognized, namely, Luminal A (HER2−, HR+, and low Ki67 expression), Luminal B (HER2+/−, HR+, and high Ki67 expression), HER2-enriched (HER2+, HR−), and triple-negative BCa (TNBC; HER2−, HR−).

The therapeutic landscape for BCa has expanded significantly, with treatment strategies including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical resection, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy, all of which contribute to improved survival outcomes [3]. The prognosis is influenced by various factors, including traditional clinical pathological variables such as tumor grade, size, and lymph node metastasis. Clinically, progesterone receptor (PR), estrogen receptor (ER), and HER2 are widely used as biomarkers, while genetic mutations such as those in PIK3CA, TP53, and BRCA1/2 are frequently observed in BCa [4, 5]. Among these, ER is overexpressed in approximately 70–80% of BCa cases [6, 7], making it a critical therapeutic target. Selective inhibition of ER signaling has been one of the most effective treatment strategies in oncology [8]. However, drug resistance is a major issue and is linked to mortality in BCa patients [9]. Moreover, BCa exhibits significant heterogeneity, leading to variations in clinical manifestations among individuals. Given the prevalence and mortality of the disease, elucidating the mechanisms driving BCa progression and identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets remain pressing priorities.

Chromosomal instability, arising from persistent errors in chromosome segregation during mitosis, is an important hallmark of cancer. Studies have shown that TNBC, in particular, exhibits a high frequency of chromosomal instability [10]. The precise segregation of sister chromatids during mitosis relies on centromere integrity, which is maintained by centromere-associated proteins. Centromere Protein I (CENPI), located at the q22.1 locus of the human X chromosome, is a key component of the CENP-H/I/K complex. CENPI is involved in centromere stabilization through its direct interaction with centromeric DNA, ensuring accurate chromosome pairing and segregation, and maintaining normal mitotic progression [11–13].

Recent studies have highlighted that CENPI and its abnormal expression in various human malignancies are strongly implicated in initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance. High levels of CENPI are found in colorectal cancer where they are correlated with malignancy. CENPI, as an oncogene, is involved in regulating migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in colorectal tumors [14]. Yu et al. reported that CENPI may drive the progression of castration-resistant prostate cancer by inducing chromosomal instability, suggesting its use as a diagnostic and prognostic marker [15]. Similarly, Wang et al. reported upregulation of CENPI in gastric cancer, where it was linked with poor prognosis and enhanced tumorigenic behavior in cells [16]. In lung adenocarcinoma, Yang et al. identified CENPI as a significant prognostic indicator with potential implications for immunotherapy [17]. These reports demonstrate the importance of CENPI in cancer, and suggest its potential as biomarker for both diagnosis and prognosis. However, the precise functional impact, mechanistic drivers, and therapeutic relevance of CENPI specifically in BCa remain undefined.

The Wnt signaling pathway is a conserved and strongly regulated cascade involved in embryonic development, cell differentiation, and tissue homeostasis [18]. Aberrant Wnt signaling activation is frequently observed in BCa progression [19]. The Wnt signaling is classified into three major branches: the canonical Wnt/β-catenin, the non-canonical Wnt-planar cell polarity (PCP), and the Wnt-Ca²⁺ pathways. Among these, the canonical pathway is the most extensively studied, and it regulates gene expression by controlling the β-catenin stability and nuclear translocation.

Motivated by this critical gap, we investigated the specific function of CENPI in BCa and demonstrated for the first time that it drives tumor initiation and progression by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin axis. The results suggest the potential of CENPI as a biomarker and target for treating BCa, providing a foundation for novel diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) is a web server that comprehensively analyzes the immune infiltration in different types of cancer, containing multiple data types to evaluate the relationship between tumor and immunity (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) [20]. Data on mRNA expression and clinical phenotypes of BCa cases were retrieved from TCGA (http://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). As this information is publicly available, the study adhered to the TCGA access policies and publication guidelines.

Patients tissue samples

Human BCa samples (n = 3) and corresponding para-tumor tissues were acquired from the Pathology Department of Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital. The clinicopathological features of the patient were presented in Supplementary Table 1. CENPI expression was derived from the combined assessment of staining intensity (graded as 0: negative; 1: weak; 2: moderate; 3: strong) and the proportion of positively stained cells (scored as 1: ≤ 25%; 2: 26–50%; 3: 51–75%; 4: ≥ 76%). Two experienced pathologists blinded to all patient characteristics independently performed the assessment. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Management Committee of Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital (No. L2025010).

Cell culture and reagents

The human BCa MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines were obtained from the ATCC (USA). Cells were grown in DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal calf serum at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The Wnt signaling agonist SKL2001 (Selleck, USA) was used for pathway activation experiments. The cell proliferation inhibitor Mitomycin C (MCE, USA) was used in the wound healing assay.

RNA interference and lentiviral infection

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting CENPI (CENPI-sh1: GGACTTCACAAGGTAGTAGTA; CENPI-sh2: GGAATTCTCTCTCAGTTATAC) and a negative control shRNA (shControl: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT) were cloned into pLV3 lentiviral vector (GenePharma, China). Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting the shRNA constructs into 293 T cells with packaging plasmids (P1-VSVG, P2-REV, and P3-RRE). BCa cells were transduced with the lentiviruses, followed by selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Solarbio, China) for two weeks to establish stable CENPI-knockdown lines.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Vazyme, China) as directed, followed by evaluation using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qRT-PCR (+ gDNA wiper). qRT-PCR was conducted on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The qRT-PCR primers were presented in Supplementary Table 2. β-actin represented the internal standard. The primers mentioned above were purchased from Sangon Biotech.

Western blotting (WB), immunofluorescence, and antibodies

The total cellular protein after harvest was isolated with the RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) and quantified using a BCA kit (Beyotime, China). Equal protein amounts were electrophoresed on SDS-PAGE, transferred to NC membranes (Millipore, USA), and blocked with skim milk. Membranes were treated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China) for one hour at ambient temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL reagent (NCM Biotech, China) and imaged using the Tanon 5200 automatic chemiluminescence imaging analysis system. GAPDH and Lamin A/C represented internal standards. Cell fractionation was undertaken with Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (KeyGEN, China). The antibodies used for WB were anti-CENPI (ab168778) from Abcam, anti-GAPDH (10494-1-AP), anti-E-cadherin (20874-1-AP), anti-Vimentin (10366-1-AP), anti-Wnt3a (26744-1-AP), anti-GSK3β (22104-1-AP), anti-β-catenin (51067-2-AP), anti-c-Myc (10828-1-AP), anti-Cyclin D1 (26939-1-AP), and anti-Lamin A/C (10298-1-AP) from Proteintech.

For immunofluorescence, cells were grown in 12-well plates and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (SparkJade, China), followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China). After blocking with 5% BSA for one hour, cells were treated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at 37 °C. DAPI (KeyGEN, China) was utilized for nuclear staining and imaging was performed with the EVOS M7000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher, USA). The antibodies utilized were against E-cadherin (20874-1-AP), Vimentin (10366-1-AP), and β-catenin (51067-2-AP) from Proteintech. The secondary antibody was Cy3 Conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (BOSTER, China).

CCK-8 and clonogenic assays

The viability of the cells was assessed using CCK-8 assays (APExBIO, USA). Cells (5 × 103/well) were inoculated in 96-well plates. After 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well with 100 µL of complete medium and incubated for two hours. Absorbances at 450 nm were read in a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA).

For clonogenic assays, cells (1 × 10³/well) were inoculated into 6-well plates and cultured until visible colonies formed. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet (Solarbio, China), and manually quantified.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were grown in 6-well plates until approximately 80% confluence confluent. They were harvested using trypsin without EDTA to yield single-cell suspension (1 × 106/mL) which were fixed overnight with 70% chilled ethanol before staining with a PI/RNase A working solution for 30 min away from light (KeyGen, China). Red fluorescence at 488 nm was recorded. For apoptosis assessments, single-cell suspensions (5 × 105/mL) were stained with Annexin V-kFluor647 and PI (KeyGen, China). Flow cytometry was performed using an Agilent NovoCyte Advanteon (USA) to assess both cell cycle distribution and apoptosis levels.

Wound healing and transwell invasion assays

For the wound healing assay, cells were inoculated in 6-well plates and grown to a confluent monolayer. Mitomycin C (10 µg/mL) was added for 2 h of incubation, followed by scratching the monolayer with the tip of a sterile 200-µL pipette. Detached cells were removed by washing with PBS, and the remaining adherent cells were grown in serum-free medium. Images of the wound were recorded at specific time points using a light microscope, enabling calculation of the migration of cells from the wound.

Cell invasion ability was evaluated using Matrigel-coated transwell chambers (Corning, USA). A total of 2 × 105 cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 µL of medium with 30% serum was placed in the lower compartment. Following 48 h of incubation, cells on the upper membrane were collected using a cotton swab, while the invading cells on the lower part were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Images were captured, manually enumerate invading cells. Normalize the counts to the proliferation rate of the respective cell line to quantify invasive capacity.

Mice xenograft model

All animal protocols were approved by the Ethics Management Committee of Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital (No.L2025021). A total of 12 nude mice (BALB/c, female, 4 weeks old) were kept under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with 6 nude mice were randomly divided into each group. For the orthotopic xenograft model, equal numbers of CENPI knockdown cells (3 × 106) and negative control cells were administered subcutaneously into the mammary fat pads of the animals. Tumor growth was assessed each week, and tumor volumes were determined as: TV = length×width2 × 0.5. Tumor measurements were taken by investigators blinded to group allocation. On completion of the experiment, the nude mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation to confirm that their breathing and heartbeat had stopped. Then tumors were excised and weighed to evaluate the effects of CENPI knockdown on tumor growth.

Immunohistochemical staining, H&E staining, and TUNEL staining

For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent initial baking at 65 °C for two hours, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through an ethanol gradient. Antigen retrieval was conducted in a pressure cooker using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After allowing it to cool naturally, 3% H2O2 was incubated with the cells for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activities. The sections were then washed thrice with PBS and blocked with 5% BSA at RT for one hour. Primary antibody incubation was performed at 4 °C overnight. Sections were then rinsed with PBS and probed with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for one hour. After another PBS wash, staining was performed using diaminobenzidine (DAB), and counterstaining was carried out with hematoxylin. Dehydration was completed using graded ethanol, followed by clearing with xylene and fixation with neutral gum. The primary antibodies used for immunohistochemistry were anti-CENPI (Thermo Fisher, PA5-100717) and anti-Ki67 (Servicebio, GB111499).

For H&E staining, paraffin-embedded sections were baked at high-temperature, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin for five minutes, followed by differentiation in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for a few seconds and bluing in 1% ammonia solution for one minute. The cytoplasm and extracellular matrix were stained with eosin, followed by dehydration in a graded ethanol series. The sections were then cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral gum.

Apoptotic cells in tumor tissues were detected using a TUNEL staining kit (Beyotime, China). After baking and deparaffinization, tissue sections were permeabilized with Proteinase K solution. DNA break ends were labeled with TdT enzyme and fluorescently labeled dUTP. Following PBS washes, slides were stained with DAPI and sealed with neutral gum. All sections were visualized and photographed using a fluorescence microscope.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analyses

An RNA-Seq library was constructed and sequenced by BGI Company using the PE150 strategy on the DNBSEQ platform. The quality of the library was assessed using the Phred + 33 scoring system. Reads of low quality, those containing adapter contamination, and those with excessive N bases were removed. The high-quality reads were then aligned with the human reference genome (GRCh38.p14) using HISAT2 (2.2.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (1.36.0). Functional GO and KEGG analyses were conducted using ggplot2 (3.3.6), while Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (4.4.4).

TOP/FOP flash assay

Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) was used to co-transfect TOP flash or FOP flash plasmids (GenePharma, China) along with the Renilla TK-luciferase vector (Promega, USA) into cells cultured in 12-well plates. Transfection was performed, and after the designated incubation period, Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (APExBIO, USA), with Renilla activity representing the control for normalization. The TOP/FOP ratio was calculated to assess β-catenin transcriptional activity within the Wnt signaling pathway.

Plasmid transfection

The degradation-resistant β-catenin mutant (ΔN90-β-catenin, lacking N-terminal 90 amino acids) was constructed by GenePharma (China) and transfected into cells cultured in 6-well plates using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). The construct was verified by Sanger sequencing and WB (anti-β-catenin, Proteintech, 51067-2-AP).

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and SPSS 22.0. Data obtained from TCGA and RNA-Seq analyses were processed using R software (4.2.1). Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 represented significance (NS, P ≥ 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Results

The expression of CENPI is abnormally raised in BCa and is associated with disease progression and poor prognosis.

To investigate CENPI levels in BCa, the “DiffExp” module of the TIMER website was used, which provides comprehensive gene expression profiles for tumor and paired normal tissues. The analysis revealed that CENPI showed differential expression across 19 tumor types, including BCa (Fig. 1A). To further explore its association with BCa progression and prognosis, data from TCGA were analyzed, comprising 1069 BCa tissue samples and 111 normal breast tissue samples. CENPI was significantly overexpressed in BCa (P < 0.001, Fig. 1B). Moreover, the relationship between CENPI expression in BCa tissue and clinicopathological features was examined by analyzing its levels across different pathological stages, including T, N, and M stages. CENPI levels were positively linked to BCa progression, with higher CENPI levels in advanced stages (Fig. 1C–F). These findings indicate that elevated CENPI levels are associated with aggressive disease characteristics in BCa. Furthermore, to assess the prognostic significance of CENPI, BCa cases were allocated to low- and high-expression groups according to the median level of CENPI expression. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated that the high-CENPI group had comparatively worse overall survival (OS) (Fig. 1G). Similar trends were observed for progression-free interval (PFI) and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 1H–I). Moreover, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to assess the diagnostic potential of CENPI, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.921 (Fig. 1J). This indicates high sensitivity and specificity of CENPI in BCa diagnosis. Immunohistochemical staining of clinical BCa tissue samples further confirmed increased CENPI expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 1K). These results suggest that CENPI overexpression is strongly associated with BCa progression and poor prognosis, highlighting its potential role in tumorigenesis and disease development.

CENPI knockdown blocks BCa cell proliferation and promotes cell cycle arrest and apoptosis

To examine the function of CENPI in BCa, MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cells were infected with two pairs of shRNAs targeting CENPI. Following selection with puromycin, stable CENPI knockdown cell lines were successfully established. They were designated as CENPI-sh1 and CENPI-sh2, along with a negative control. The knockdown efficiency of the stable cell lines was confirmed using qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 2A). The impact of CENPI silencing on proliferation and colony formation was assessed using CCK-8 and clonogenic assays, respectively. A marked reduction in proliferation was seen in both cell lines following CENPI knockdown (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, CENPI-knockdown cells exhibited a significant reduction in clonogenic capacity which is a key indicator of tumorigenic potential (Fig. 2C). To determine whether CENPI affects cell cycle progression and apoptosis, flow cytometry was performed. The results demonstrated that CENPI silencing led to cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 and induced apoptosis in both BCa cell lines (Fig. 2D–E). These findings indicate that CENPI has oncogenic functions in BCa by promoting proliferation and impeding apoptosis.

CENPI knockdown reduces the migration and invasion of BCa cells

To further explore the role of CENPI in BCa progression, its effects on cell migration and invasion were examined. Wound healing assay revealed that CENPI knockdown significantly impaired the migratory capacity of BCa cells (Fig. 3A–B). In agreement with these findings, transwell invasion assay demonstrated marked reductions in the invasive abilities of BCa cells following CENPI silencing (Fig. 3C–D). These data indicated that CENPI serves as a facilitator in the migration and invasion of BCa cells. To examine the mechanism involved, qRT-PCR analysis revealed CENPI knockdown altered core EMT marker expression. Epithelial markers (CDH1, CLDN1, KRT19) were significantly upregulated, whereas mesenchymal markers (VIM, ZEB1, SNAI1) showed pronounced downregulation (Fig. 3E-F). Consistent with transcriptional changes, WB and immunofluorescence staining confirmed elevated E-cadherin (encoded by CDH1) and reduced Vimentin (encoded by VIM) protein levels in CENPI knockdown cells (Fig. 3G-H). These findings show that CENPI induces the EMT in BCa cells, facilitating migration and invasion.

CENPI silencing mitigates BCa tumorigenicity in vivo

Following the in vitro analysis of CENPI function in BCa cells, subcutaneous tumor formation experiments in nude mice were conducted to assess its role in tumorigenesis in vivo. Xenograft models were established using CENPI knockdown and control cells. Each mouse was evaluated regularly, and tumor volumes were determined. The tumors from CENPI-knockdown cells grew more slowly and were markedly smaller in volume compared to the controls. At the study endpoint, tumor excision and measurement revealed marked decreases in both tumor volumes and weights in the CENPI knockdown group (Fig. 4A–C). Histological and molecular analyses of the tumors were conducted using H&E staining, Ki67 immunohistochemical staining, and TUNEL assay to assess cell proliferation and apoptosis. H&E staining demonstrated an increased presence of vacuolar structures in the CENPI knockdown group, indicative of apoptosis. Ki67 staining revealed a significantly lower number of proliferative tumor cells in the CENPI knockdown tumors compared to controls (Fig. 4D–F). Moreover, the TUNEL assay indicated markedly higher numbers of apoptotic cells in the CENPI knockdown group (Fig. 4G–H). These findings from the in vivo xenograft mouse model support the in vitro results, confirming that CENPI promotes BCa growth.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of CENPI in BCa

A differential gene expression analysis was undertaken to assess the functions of CENPI in BCa. 1113 DEGs were found, including 772 up- and 341 down-regulated genes. The top 15 genes are visualized in heatmaps (Fig. 5A). Functional annotation, GO enrichment, and KEGG pathway analyses revealed that CENPI-associated DEGs were involved in processes such as chromosome segregation, meiotic cell cycle, positive regulation of the cell cycle, and DNA replication (Fig. 5B). Then, GSEA of CENPI-related DEGs was performed to identify signaling pathways affected by CENPI overexpression in BCa. The results indicated enrichment of pathways involved in the cell cycle, chromosome maintenance, DNA repair, apoptosis, transcriptional regulation by TP53, TCF-dependent signaling in response to Wnt, and β-catenin-TCF transactivating complex formation in the high-expression group of CENPI (Fig. 5C–I). RNA-Seq further validated genes affected by CENPI depletion. Differential expression analysis using DESeq2 identified distinct gene expression patterns in CENPI-sh2 cells compared to controls (Fig. 5J–K). CENPI was associated with various biological processes, including the Wnt signaling pathway, regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, regulation of the mitotic cell cycle, apoptosis, cell cycle, and regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 5L). These findings indicate the involvement of CENPI in chromosome segregation, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis, and its expression is associated with the Wnt signaling. Therefore, CENPI may promotes BCa progression through its regulatory role in Wnt signaling.

CENPI promotes the progression of BCa by regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Previous bioinformatics analyses revealed a strong association between CENPI expression and the Wnt signaling pathway. To further elucidate the role of CENPI in this signaling cascade, the expression of key regulatory proteins was analyzed. In MDA-MB-468 cells, CENPI knockdown resulted in a reduction in Wnt3a, GSK3β, and β-catenin protein levels. Since Wnt signaling regulates transcription of several critical genes, CENPI knockdown also induced marked downregulation of its downstream targets Cyclin D1 and c-Myc (Fig. 6A). To validate the impact of CENPI on Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity, the TOP/FOP dual luciferase reporter assay was performed. A significant decrease in TOP/FOP activity was observed upon CENPI knockdown, indicating reduced β-catenin mediated transcription (Fig. 6B). In addition to assessing β-catenin protein expression levels, the subcellular localization of β-catenin is crucial for assessing Wnt signaling activation. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that β-catenin was expressed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of BCa cells. When CENPI was knocked down, the expression of β-catenin was significantly inhibited (Fig. 6C). Moreover, nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation revealed a decrease in β-catenin levels in both compartments following CENPI knockdown (Fig. 6D). To further investigate the interaction between CENPI and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, the Wnt signaling activator SKL2001 was administered. The results demonstrated that the inhibition of Wnt signaling caused by CENPI knockdown was reversed by SKL2001 treatment, confirming that CENPI acts upstream of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Fig. 6E).

To substantiate our mechanistic findings, a stable and non-degradable β-catenin was exogenously expressed in CENPI knockdown cells. Successful overexpression was confirmed by WB (Fig. 7A). Notably, the impaired proliferative capacity resulting from CENPI depletion was significantly rescued upon β-catenin reconstitution (Fig. 7B). Migration and invasion capacities, previously suppressed by CENPI knockdown, recovered to near baseline levels with β-catenin reconstitution (Fig. 7C-F). Collectively, these findings establish β-catenin as the critical downstream mediator of CENPI, demonstrating that CENPI promotes BCa progression and a multitude of malignant phenotypes by regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Discussion

Over the past few years, advancements in BCa therapy have led to the development of drugs such as paclitaxel, tamoxifen, and palbociclib, which have shown clinical efficacy. Furthermore, immunotherapy has gained prominence, particularly with the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). For instance, the KEYNOTE-522 clinical trial reported that chemotherapy combined with pembrolizumab significantly improved the pathological complete response (pCR) rate in early-stage TNBC [21]. However, treatment resistance and disease progression remain significant challenges, particularly in advanced BCa, where effective treatment strategies are still lacking. Thus, identifying novel molecular targets with high specificity and sensitivity is essential for developing targeted therapies that can improve BCa patient survival.

As a member of the CENP family, CENPI is closely associated with tumor onset and progression due to their crucial biological functions. Dang et al. demonstrated that CENPI is involved in repairing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), and its absence leads to DSB formation and an increased rate of chromosomal aberrations [22]. In this study, analysis of the TCGA BCa dataset revealed abnormal CENPI overexpression, which was closely linked to disease progression and poor prognosis. Moreover, immunohistochemical staining of clinical BCa tissue samples and corresponding adjacent tissues confirmed the bioinformatics findings. Stable CENPI knockdown cell lines were established by infecting human BCa cell lines with two pairs of shRNAs targeting CENPI to investigate its role further. Functional assays, including the CCK-8 assay, clonogenic assay, and flow cytometry analysis, demonstrated that CENPI regulates BCa cell growth. Given the link between the EMT and multiple aspects of malignancy, including invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance, the regulatory role of CENPI in EMT was investigated [23]. qRT-PCR, WB, and immunofluorescence staining revealed that CENPI knockdown influenced the expression of major EMT-related markers, including Vimentin and E-cadherin. Furthermore, a subcutaneous tumor formation experiment in nude mice confirmed that CENPI promotes BCa tumorigenesis in vivo, consistent with the in vitro findings.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CENPI function in regulating the occurrence and development of BCa, RNA-Seq and bioinformatics analyses were performed to identify downstream genes and signaling pathways associated with CENPI. The findings suggested that CENPI may regulate the biological behavior of BCa through the Wnt signaling pathway. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a major branch of Wnt signaling and also represents a pivotal signaling cascade, and its dysregulation has been linked to a variety of cancers, including solid tumors, hematological malignancies, and sarcomas [24]. This pathway is documented to be involved in BCa progression. Zhu et al. reported that PROX1 is up-regulated in BCa and interacts with hnRNPK to inhibit its ubiquitination, thus activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoting invasion and metastasis in BCa [25]. Chen et al. showed that the tumor suppressor CMTM7 interacts with CTNNA1 to modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling and inhibit BCa progression [26]. Moreover, Fbxw7 has been reported to suppress tumorigenesis and stemness in TNBC by ubiquitinating and degrading CHD4, blocking Wnt/β-catenin signaling [27]. Given the established role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in BCa, the relationship between CENPI expression and this pathway was investigated. WB, immunofluorescence staining, and the TOP/FOP flash assay consistently demonstrated that CENPI is closely related to the Wnt signaling and can regulate BCa development by modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Subsequently, treatment with the Wnt/β-catenin activator SKL2001 reversed the inhibitory effects of CENPI knockdown on Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Moreover, an exogenous stable and non-degradable β-catenin was expressed in CENPI knockdown cells. β-catenin reconstitution rescued CENPI knockdown-induced deficits in proliferation, migration, and invasion, definitively positioning CENPI upstream of β-catenin in the signaling hierarchy. The abnormal overexpression of CENPI stabilizes β-catenin, allowing it to amass in the cytoplasm and transfer to the nucleus, a process recognized as a hallmark of Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation [28]. In the nucleus, β-catenin interacts with TCF/LEF, promoting the transcription of various genes including Cyclin D1 and c-Myc [29]. These findings suggest that CENPI exerts oncogenic effects in BCa by affecting Wnt/β-catenin signaling, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

This investigation systematically evaluated, for the first time, the characteristics and biological functions of CENPI in BCa, elucidating its role in tumor progression through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings address a research gap and provide new perspectives on BCa pathogenesis, with significant translational implications. It suggests that CENPI may serve as a novel diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for BCa. Future work should prioritize development of therapeutic agents targeting the CENPI-Wnt/β-catenin axis and verify their efficacy in patient-derived organoids.

However, there are still some limitations. The primary research samples were derived from BCa cell lines and nude mouse models, with a relatively small number of clinical samples, which may limit the universality of the results. Expanding the clinical sample size in future studies is essential to further verify the association between CENPI expression and BCa prognosis and its potential as a therapeutic target. Furthermore, the precise molecular mechanism through which CENPI regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling has not been fully explored. On one hand, CENPI is a known centromeric protein involved in chromosome separation and mitotic progression. Its silencing leads to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. The observed changes in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling in our study might be an indirect consequence of these significant cellular effects. On the other hand, whether CENPI directly interacts with β-catenin or other Wnt signaling components, or whether it involves additional intermediate factors or cross-regulation with other signaling pathways, all of these remain to be further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CENPI is aberrantly overexpressed in BCa and significantly promotes tumor growth and EMT. By modulating the expression of key target genes through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, CENPI functions as a key oncogenic driver in BCa progression. These findings suggest the potential of CENPI as a potential biomarker and a crucial target for treating BCa.
