Employment and epilepsy disclosure: A neglected research priority
Authors: Muqaddas Arshad, Samia Arif, Ayesha Shafqat, Abdul Haseeb Hasan, Saira Naz
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder marked by recurring seizures, which frequently include loss of consciousness, major motor phenomena, urinary incontinence, and occasionally tongue biting or involuntary inappropriate behavior. The condition affects approximately 50 million people worldwide, with 80% of them living in low-income countries. The World Health Organization classifies epilepsy as a permanent brain disorder that develops throughout life and ranks among the most common neurological disorders worldwide. Proper diagnosis and treatment enable 70 percent of epilepsy patients to reach seizure control, but substantial treatment gaps remain because many patients in low- and middle-income areas lack proper medical care. People with epilepsy (PWE) face a threefold higher chance of early death than those without the condition. 1 However, they remain vulnerable to stigma, including low self-esteem and discrimination at school or work. Therefore, a comprehensive approach to the management of epilepsy, including both medical treatment and optimization of quality of life, has been emphasized.1,2

Employment improves the quality of life for PWE by fostering independence and social integration. One of the critical challenges faced by PWE in employment is disclosure in the workplace. The importance of disclosure is evident because it can lead to workplace modifications and safety precautions that are essential to prevent any injury to PWE themselves and their co-workers. However, this can also make individuals vulnerable to stigma. The pilot study of the Australian Epilepsy Project has demonstrated that epilepsy is associated with impaired quality of life and work productivity. The study demonstrated that PWE experience major effects that arise from employment challenges through their need for care when work-related problems with disclosure and stigma affect their job performance and overall well-being, which forces them to seek additional care. 3

Recent studies show that disclosure decisions among PWE are shaped by both individual and social factors. 4 Unlike earlier research that focused only on stigma and employment outcomes, newer work examines disclosure itself. Strategies such as timing, selective disclosure, and normalizing the condition to reduce panic highlight that choices are strongly influenced by anticipated stigma. 5 A qualitative study with HR managers and occupational physicians also revealed a lack of systemic guidance for managing disclosure in the workplace. 6

Future research should compare various disclosure strategies to document which most effectively reduces stigma. Only a few longitudinal studies examine the impact of disclosure on employment retention, career advancement, or quality of life. There is also minimal evidence on cultural differences, workplace policies, and legal protections for PWE. Very few intervention studies have tested whether policy reforms and employer education regarding epilepsy lead to career growth and improved quality of life in PWE. 4

It is therefore essential to prioritize epilepsy and employment disclosure as a research domain. Workplace policies and legislation must be implemented by authorities, and intervention studies must be conducted to assess their impact on workplace advancement. Without such research, decisions regarding disclosure may continue to be driven primarily by personal beliefs and experiences rather than evidence-based guidance regarding anticipated consequences, leading to employment barriers and reduced quality of life in PWE.
