Neuroendocrine Tumor of the Terminal Ileum Presenting as Refractory Crohn's Disease: A Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Mohammad Abuassi, Saar Peles, Bellal Alfar, Aiman Obed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeuroendocrine tumors (NETs) can mimic inflammatory bowel diseases like Crohn's, often delaying diagnosis. We report a case of a 56-year-old woman with abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss initially treated as Crohn's disease. Despite immunomodulatory therapy, symptoms persisted. Imaging revealed ileal strictures and a suspicious mass. Surgical resection was performed, and histopathology confirmed a Grade 1 well-differentiated ileal NET (pT4 pN1) with lymphovascular and perineural invasion. The patient was referred for oncology follow-up. This case highlights the need to consider NETs in patients with gastrointestinal symptoms that mimic Crohn's disease but fail to respond to standard therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are a rare and heterogeneous group of neoplasms originating from neuroendocrine cells and can occur in various organs, most commonly the gastrointestinal tract. These tumors often present diagnostic challenges due to their nonspecific symptoms and mimicry of more common gastrointestinal diseases, such as Crohn's disease.1 Studies suggest that up to 70% of NET patients initially had a misdiagnosis of a different gastrointestinal, respiratory, metabolic, or dermatologic condition.2 The diagnostic dilemma is further complicated by the overlapping clinical and radiological features between gastrointestinal NETs and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), particularly Crohn's disease, which can lead to significant delays in accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.3

Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract characterized by transmural inflammation and a variety of systemic manifestations.4 Differentiating Crohn's disease from other pathologies like NETs is crucial because treatment strategies diverge significantly. While immunosuppressive therapies are the cornerstone of treatment of Crohn's disease, they are ineffective and potentially harmful when an unrecognized malignancy such as a NET is present.5

The rarity and complexity of NETs requires a high degree of suspicion, especially in patients who do not respond to conventional Crohn's disease treatments. Advanced imaging techniques and targeted biopsies are often required to establish a definitive diagnosis.6 This case highlights the importance of considering a broad differential diagnosis when patients present with atypical symptoms or when they exhibit an inadequate response to standard treatments.7

CASE REPORT

A 56-year-old woman with a presumed diagnosis of Crohn's disease presented to our outpatient clinic with an approximately 12-month history of diffuse, intermittent colicky abdominal pain, which did not radiate and was temporarily relieved by analgesics. She also reported vomiting, fatigue, fever, chronic watery diarrhea, anorexia, and significant weight loss. These symptoms coupled with magnetic resonance imaging findings for stricture and obstruction was strongly suggestive of an exacerbation of inflammatory bowel disease or an alternative gastrointestinal pathology.

She had been diagnosed with Crohn's disease 12 months earlier at another institution. At that time, her clinical presentation and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a terminal ileal stricture with features of partial obstruction. Colonoscopy with intubation of the terminal ileum revealed erythema, edema, friability, and luminal narrowing with stenosis. Histopathological examination of mucosal biopsies taken during that procedure showed chronic inflammation with segmental crypt architectural distortion, findings interpreted as suggestive of Crohn's disease.

Despite extensive treatment with immunomodulatory therapies, her symptoms remained unimproved. She initially received prednisone and azathioprine for approximately 1 year with only transient improvement, followed by adalimumab for 1 month using the standard induction regimen, without additional clinical benefit.

Abdominal computed tomography (illustrated in Figure 1, showed intestinal dilatation and ileal stricture, and identified a suspicious mass in the ileal region. The presence of this mass suggested a potential complication or an alternative diagnosis that could explain the patient's persistent symptoms despite intensive treatment.

Contrast-enhanced axial computed tomography images demonstrating a soft-tissue mass in the terminal ileum (arrows) with associated bowel wall thickening and luminal narrowing.

Given the persistence of her symptoms and the concerning imaging findings, surgical intervention was deemed necessary. A mini laparotomy was performed, during which the affected section of the terminal ileum was resected, and primary anastomosis was established. Figure 2 shows the ileal stricture observed during surgery, which confirmed the severity of the patient's symptoms. The histopathological examination of the resected tissue revealed a Grade 1, well-differentiated unifocal NET, measuring 1.5 cm in diameter, and staged as pT4 pN1, Ki-67 labeling index < 3% and mitotic rate <2 per 2 mm2. This tumor demonstrated invasive behavior into the muscularis propria, subserosal fat, and visceral peritoneum, and exhibited lymphatic, vascular, and perineural invasions, features that indicate an aggressive phenotype with a high risk of recurrence and metastasis. Importantly, the surgical margins, including proximal, distal, and mesenteric, were free of tumor, confirming the complete excision. However, significant involvement was found in the regional lymph nodes, with 6 out of 12 recovered nodes having metastatic tumor.

Intraoperative image showing the terminal ileum with a focal area of stricture and thickening (indicated by the arrow), corresponding to the site of the neuroendocrine tumor.

In addition to the primary tumor, the histopathological report also identified a small intestinal diverticulum lined by benign small intestinal epithelium. Furthermore, there was notable congestion in the mucosa both proximal and distal to the obstruction.

The patient was referred to oncology for surveillance and further management and remains clinically stable under follow-up.

DISCUSSION

The diagnosis of a well-differentiated NET in a patient initially managed for Crohn's disease highlights the complexity and necessity of a thorough diagnostic evaluation in patients presenting with atypical gastrointestinal symptoms. NETs, particularly those located in the gastrointestinal tract, are notorious for their ability to mimic more common inflammatory conditions such as Crohn's disease, leading to misdiagnosis and delayed appropriate treatment.8

The overlap of symptoms between Crohn's disease and gastrointestinal NETs includes abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss. Imaging may reveal strictures or mass-like lesions, but these findings do not reliably distinguish inflammatory from neoplastic processes.9 NETs are frequently submucosal and may present as subtle nodules or strictures with overlying inflammatory changes, which can resemble active ileitis or proctitis, leading to misinterpretation.10 Many NETs are also relatively small and submucosal, making them difficult to detect with superficial mucosal biopsies.11 On endoscopy, both NETs and IBD can involve the same bowel segments, particularly the terminal ileum or rectum, and may present with overlapping macroscopic features such as segmental erythema, edema, friability, ulcerations, and strictures.12,13 Laboratory markers can add to the challenge. Fecal calprotectin, for example, may be elevated in both IBD and NETs, and current evidence does not support its use as a reliable discriminator between these conditions.14

Despite the similarities, NETs can be suspected by a presentation of a distinct mass on imaging, lack of improvement with IBD therapy and in rare cases carcinoid syndrome presenting with symptoms such as bronchospasm, wheezing and flushing not seen in IBD.15,16

In our case, persistent symptoms despite immunomodulatory therapy, together with identification of an ileal mass on imaging, prompted re-evaluation of the initial diagnosis. The surgical intervention in this case not only provided definitive histological diagnosis but also therapeutic relief. Histopathological analysis revealed invasion into the muscularis propria and the presence of lymphatic and vascular invasions, which are indicative of higher risk of recurrence and metastasis.17 These findings required a shift in management strategies from immunosuppressive therapy to oncologic surveillance and potential adjuvant treatment.6,18
