Response to the Letter Regarding “Determining Physical Function Measures for Estimating VO2Peak in Patients on Hemodialysis”
Authors: Kenneth Lim, Nolan W Groninger, Monique Campos
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We thank Professor Torres-Castro for his thoughtful commentary on our recently published study “Determining physical function measures for estimating VO2Peak in patients on hemodialysis.”

We agree that physical capacity (cardiovascular functional capacity) and physical function are distinct constructs.1 In our original article, we introduced the concept that physical function tests differ from cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) in that they are submaximal and do not involve maximal exercise stress testing.2 A methodological advantage to CPET is the continuous measure of ventilatory and hemodynamic variables in response to an incremental workload. These measures enable an objective evaluation of an individual’s physiological ceiling and assists with determining the underlying mechanisms of functional impairment.3 In contrast, standard physical function tests do not provide direct physiological measurements; rather, they assess performance of functional tasks such as walking, standing, balance, and agility. Thus, although CPET and physical functional assessments differ, they provide complementary information on an individual’s functional status.3

Professor Torres-Castro highlighted the need for extreme caution correlating CPET results with submaximal tests such as the 30-second sit-to-stand; we are in complete agreement. As stated in our original article, a major goal of the study was not to equate submaximal physical function testing with CPET or to propose their substitution, but rather to examine whether traditional physical function measures demonstrate measurable associations with VO2Peak in patients undergoing hemodialysis. For these reasons, our original a priori hypothesis for the study was that physical function tests would demonstrate limited prognostic utility for estimating gold-standard VO2Peak. This is also reflective of the limitations of traditional physical function measures to unveil subclinical cardiovascular impairment, that is often unmasked only under the stress of exercise.4 The findings of our study therefore promotes caution when using these tests clinically in patients on hemodialysis, rather than its clinical substitution.1

However, we respectfully disagree with Professor Torres-Castro’s comment that the association of 30-second sit-to-stand with VO2Peak finding, is “nearly anecdotal.” Of the 10 physical function measures evaluated, only the 30-second sit-to-stand remained significantly associated with VO2Peak (β = 0.21, SE = 0.10; P = 0.04) after adjustment for multiple predictors and this was consistent across all models. This observed association is supported by several prior studies that have consistently shown an association between 30-second sit-to-stand and VO2Peak in patients undergoing hemodialysis treatment or older adults.5,6 Importantly, VO2Peak reflects not only central oxygen delivery but also peripheral oxygen extraction, a process dependent on skeletal muscle integrity. This is particularly relevant in patients on hemodialysis where the burden of sarcopenia is unacceptably high and reflected by an impaired 30-second sit-to-stand.4,7 We agree that equating physical function with cardiovascular functional capacity may lead to misinterpretation of cardiovascular risk. Nonetheless, the observed association between the 30-second sit-to-stand and VO2Peak highlights the important role of skeletal muscle health as a determinant of VO2Peak in patients on hemodialysis and the need for further studies to define the role of functional variables in improving risk stratification.
