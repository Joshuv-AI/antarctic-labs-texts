High-resolution mapping reveals a profound gap between existing rooftop solar deployment and potential in Africa
Authors: Dongmei Huang, Ling Tang, Wei Hu, Mingkun Jiang, Huan Zhou, Linbo Han, Pengfei Zhang, Wendong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRooftop photovoltaics offer a flexible and scalable pathway for expanding electricity access and advancing power system transitions in Africa. However, spatially explicit tracking of rooftop photovoltaic installations at a continental scale remains a critical bottleneck, hindering efforts to characterize deployment patterns, assess regional untapped potential, and inform investment decisions. Here, we used satellite imagery and a U-Net semantic segmentation model to construct a geospatial database of rooftop photovoltaic installations across Africa. Results showed that total rooftop photovoltaic installations have reached 3.37 GW, with capacity predominantly concentrated in South Africa (∼71%). Strikingly, existing installations represented less than 1% of the continent’s total capacity potential. Moreover, the analysis revealed that rooftop photovoltaic deployment is primarily driven by local electricity demand, with solar irradiance playing a relatively limited role. These findings offer an empirical foundation for optimizing renewable energy policy and guiding infrastructure planning in Africa.

Introduction

Rooftop photovoltaic systems offer a transformative pathway to expand electricity access and support power system transitions across Africa, where persistent electricity shortages coexist with abundant solar irradiance.1,2,3,4,5 Due to their minimal reliance on centralized grids6,7,8 and the smaller initial investment required for individual installations compared to utility-scale projects,9,10,11,12 these systems represent a flexible and scalable electricity supply option across the continent.13,14,15,16 Despite this potential, however, their deployment remains markedly uneven across regions and households, driven largely by significant disparities in income and financing access.17,18

The development of rooftop photovoltaics in Africa has attracted growing attention in recent years. Several previous studies have assessed the theoretical and technical rooftop photovoltaics potential across the continent.19,20,21,22,23 For example, Zhang et al.19 employed remote sensing imagery combined with machine-learning techniques to quantify rooftop area and estimate the potential for rooftop photovoltaics deployment in Africa, reporting a total installable capacity of 1,188 GW. Similarly, Joshi et al.20 projected that the electricity generation potential of African rooftop photovoltaic systems could reach as high as 2.9 PWh yr−1. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that Africa possesses substantial potential for rooftop photovoltaic installations and electricity generation. However, a critical data gap persists. Due to the inherent monitoring complexities of distributed solar assets, reliable information on the actual scale and spatial distribution of existing rooftop photovoltaic installations is unavailable in most African nations.24,25,26,27 While recent studies have utilized deep learning and multi-source data fusion to identify rooftop photovoltaic systems,28,29 these efforts have largely been confined to urban scales. This is primarily because accurate rooftop photovoltaic identification necessitates sub-meter resolution imagery, which introduces substantial computational overhead when scaled to larger regions.

In this study, we integrated high-resolution Google Earth imagery with a U-Net semantic segmentation model to identify existing rooftop photovoltaic installations across Africa, thereby constructing a spatially explicit geospatial database of rooftop photovoltaic installations at the continental scale. The U-Net architecture excels at local feature extraction and pixel-level spatial detail delineation, making it highly suited for identifying photovoltaic arrays that are typically clustered within localized image regions.28,29 Furthermore, compared with more recent transformer-based approaches, the U-Net architecture offers higher computational efficiency with lower overhead, and it yields robust performance without requiring massive data pre-training. Consequently, this framework strikes a practical balance among accuracy, training data requirements, computational efficiency, and scalability for continental-scale mapping. Based on this newly established dataset, we quantified the corresponding installed capacity, annual electricity generation, and the development rate of rooftop photovoltaic potential. In addition, using nighttime light data and solar irradiance, we examined the key drivers of rooftop photovoltaic expansion and identified priority regions for future deployment. Collectively, this study advances the understanding of Africa’s rooftop photovoltaics landscape and provides a data-driven foundation for future deployment. By bridging critical information gaps, our findings facilitate the dual objectives of expanding regional electricity access and catalyzing the energy transition; meanwhile, they support multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including SDG 13 (Climate Action) and SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy).

Results

Identification of rooftop photovoltaic installations in Africa

This study utilized high-resolution satellite imagery and a U-Net semantic segmentation model29 to identify the location and quantify the area of rooftop photovoltaic installations. To focus the analysis, African residential and built-up areas were delineated as the study domain using the multi-temporal, high-resolution Global Urban Boundary dataset derived from the Global Artificial Impervious Area (GAIA) product.30 For model training, we constructed a training set comprised of expert-annotated ground-truth samples, encompassing rooftop photovoltaic installations of diverse spatial scales, geometries, and partial occlusion conditions; detailed training parameters are provided in the Methods section. Throughout the continental-scale implementation, more than 3 TB of high-resolution satellite imagery were processed. During the inference stage, the U-Net model automatically detected rooftop photovoltaic installations from the raw imagery and delineated their spatial boundaries with high precision31 (Figure S1).

The labeled training samples were used to train the U-Net semantic segmentation model, thereby enhancing its capacity to detect photovoltaic arrays under complex background conditions. Figure 1 presents the spatial distribution of identified rooftop photovoltaics across Africa. The predicted masks align well with actual rooftop photovoltaic features across diverse human settlements, including dense commercial districts, industrial structures, and rural dwellings. These results demonstrate the robustness of our workflow for large-scale mapping across heterogeneous built-up environments. Spatial distribution of rooftop photovoltaics across Africa

At the spatial scale, the identified rooftop photovoltaic installations were aggregated into standard 10 km × 10 km grids. Across the African continent, approximately 16.83 km2 of rooftop photovoltaic installations were detected. National-level rooftop photovoltaics area data are provided in Table S3. Additionally, the rooftop photovoltaic installed capacity for Africa was estimated (Figure 2), along with the rooftop photovoltaic installed capacity for each country (Figure S3). The results indicate that the total existing rooftop photovoltaic capacity across Africa reached approximately 3.37 GW, yielding an annual electricity generation of 7.37 TWh. The spatial distribution of rooftop photovoltaics is highly uneven, with installations concentrated within a small cohort of countries. South Africa led with a rooftop photovoltaics area of approximately 11.98 km2, corresponding to an installed capacity of 2.40 GW and accounting for 71.18% of Africa’s total capacity. This was followed by Nigeria (4.37%), Morocco (3.73%), Egypt (3.48%), and Kenya (2.32%), which collectively comprised approximately 85% of the continent’s total rooftop photovoltaics area. In contrast, installed capacity across the vast majority of other African countries remained negligible, highlighting a pronounced spatial polarization and a largely nascent market across the wider continent.Figure 2Spatial distribution of rooftop photovoltaic installed capacity across Africa, with insets highlighting the four countries with the largest installed capacity

To characterize the detected rooftop photovoltaic installations, we summarize their capacity distributions and provide representative examples (Figure S4 and Table S4). Most installations are small-scale, with 88% below 30 kW, indicating that the inventory successfully captures fine-scale distributed arrays rather than being biased toward large-scale installations. However, small rooftop photovoltaic systems are inherently more susceptible to omission errors due to their restricted spatial footprints and elevated sensitivity to roofing material heterogeneity, cast shadows, and localized image degradation. These uncertainties mainly affect local detection accuracy and are unlikely to alter large-scale spatial patterns of rooftop photovoltaic distribution across regions.

Rooftop photovoltaics potential development rate in Africa

Africa holds a vast and largely untapped rooftop photovoltaics potential. The continent’s technically exploitable rooftop photovoltaics potential area is estimated at 5,926.77 km2, which, based on average performance coefficient and solar irradiance conversion, can generate approximately 2,070 TWh of electricity annually. This theoretical output is 2.5 times Africa’s total electricity generation recorded in 2023.32 However, this immense solar resource remains largely unexploited; our geospatial inventory reveals that the existing installed capacity accounted for a mere 0.28% of this total technical potential.

Furthermore, we systematically evaluated the development rate of rooftop photovoltaics potential across Africa (Figure 3A). Results show that relatively high development rates are concentrated primarily in South Africa and other high-demand urban areas. In South Africa, rooftop photovoltaics deployment was more prominent in the Johannesburg–Pretoria metropolitan area, Cape Town, and coastal port cities such as Durban. Beyond South Africa, relatively high development rates were also observed in the Nile Delta region of North Africa, the Mediterranean coastal urban agglomerations of Morocco and Algeria, and the coastal economic corridors along the Gulf of Guinea in West Africa. These regions are characterized by high population densities, advanced urbanization stages, and intensive electricity demand, positioning them as the key hotspots for rooftop photovoltaics deployment.Figure 3Rooftop photovoltaics development rates in Africa(A) Spatial distribution of rooftop photovoltaic development rates.(B) Top ten countries by total rooftop photovoltaic area.

At the national level, South Africa led the continent with the highest rooftop photovoltaics development rate, having deployed approximately 1.60% of its exploitable rooftop area—a figure roughly six times the continental average (Figure 3B). This was followed by Namibia (1.11%), Mauritius (0.38%), Botswana (0.31%), and Chad (0.29%). In contrast, most other African countries had rooftop photovoltaic development rates below 0.10%, reflecting the overall underutilization of rooftop photovoltaic resources. These results underscore Africa’s enormous rooftop photovoltaics potential and highlight the substantial gap between current deployment and technically feasible capacity, indicating considerable scope for future expansion.

Spatial coupling analysis of solar resources and electricity demand in Africa

To assess the spatial alignment between solar resources and electricity demand across Africa, we employed Night-Time Light (NTL) data from VIIRS33 and Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) from ERA5 reanalysis data34 as proxies for electricity demand and solar resources, respectively. Africa possesses abundant solar resources, with a continental mean GHI of approximately 2,054 kWh/m2/year. The top 25% and 50% of regions exceed 2,099 and 2,088 kWh/m2/year, respectively. Spatially, high values are concentrated within the Sahara–Sahel region in the north and the Kalahari Basin in the south, whereas lower GHI levels are observed in the humid equatorial belt and some coastal areas (Figure 4A). NTL data indicate that electricity demand is highly concentrated in specific regions, primarily clustered along the Mediterranean coast of North Africa, the Gulf of Guinea arc in West Africa, the East African Rift Valley corridor, and the urban centers of the South African plateau, which collectively constitute Africa’s major electricity load centers (Figure 4B).Figure 4Regional categories of Africa based on nighttime light (NTL) and global horizontal irradiance (GHI)(A–E) Spatial distributions of GHI (A) and NTL (B), and the resulting 16 regional categories (C). To characterize the continental variations in solar resource endowment and electricity demand, we categorized African regions into a 16-group classification framework. Specifically, NTL and GHI were each segmented into three discrete tiers—high (H), medium (M), and low (L)—defined by their respective terciles (Table S5), with the unlit regions (N) incorporated as an independent fourth category. The cross-tabulation of these four levels for both metrics yields a matrix of 16 unique regional classifications (Figure 4C). Crucially, the nine categories that exclude ‘N’ account for 11% of the total grid cells across the African continent (Figure 4D).

Existing rooftop photovoltaic installations were predominantly concentrated in regions with high electricity demand, as indicated by NTL intensity. Specifically, over 97% of the total installed rooftop photovoltaic capacity was located within high-demand regions. Among these, the vast majority (81.1%) was concentrated within H–L (high demand, low solar radiation) regions, despite these areas representing a mere 2.3% of the total grid cells across the African continent. In contrast, roughly 18% of the installations were situated in high-demand regions characterized by medium-to-high solar radiation, with H–M and H–H regions accounting for 16.2% and 1.8%, respectively (Figure 4E), which together account for 1.3% of the continent’s grid cells. This disproportionately high capacity clustering in low-irradiance regions demonstrates that solar resource conditions are not the primary driver of rooftop photovoltaic deployment in Africa.

Furthermore, we compared the rooftop photovoltaic development rates across different regional categories to identify the primary drivers of deployment. Overall, regions with higher electricity demand exhibited higher development rates, even in regions where solar radiation is less prominent. Specifically, the highest development rate (0.44%) was observed in H–L regions, followed by H–M (0.28%) and H–N (0.24%) regions. This also highlights a substantial opportunity for future expansion in regions characterized by high electricity demand and medium-to-high solar radiation (i.e., H–M and H–H regions). Notably, regions with low-to-medium demand showed minimal development rates, regardless of their favorable solar resources; for instance, the rates for M–H, M–M, L–M, L–H, and M–L are all below 0.02%. These findings further confirm that electricity demand, rather than solar endowment, is the main factor shaping regional photovoltaic adoption.

Discussion

In Africa, approximately 600 million people still lack access to a stable electricity supply.35 Given the continent’s abundant solar endowment and the inherent advantages of rooftop photovoltaic systems,36,37,38 rooftop photovoltaics stands as a critical approach for accelerating regional electrification and decarbonizing the energy structure.15,39 By establishing a spatially explicit inventory of rooftop photovoltaic installations, this study provides a critical empirical foundation for future research, policy design, and investment analysis regarding distributed solar development across Africa.40,41

Our results show that the development rate of rooftop photovoltaic potential in Africa remained extremely low, with existing installations accounting for only 0.28% of the continent’s technically exploitable potential. Furthermore, this deployment is highly uneven geographically: over 70% of the total installations were concentrated within South Africa, whereas deployment in other regions remained limited. This vast untapped potential indicates opportunities to improve living standards and support progress toward the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) through expanded solar deployment. It should be noted, however, that standalone rooftop photovoltaic systems are generally insufficient to satisfy household electricity demands. Given the inherent intermittency of solar resources, rooftop photovoltaics typically require integration with energy storage systems to secure basic power services for households or communities. Achieving a more adequate and stable power supply requires connection to the centralized grid infrastructure.

By integrating GHI and NTL data, our analysis suggests that observed rooftop photovoltaic deployment across Africa is primarily driven by local electricity demand, while regional solar irradiance conditions have not yet exerted a substantial influence on distribution patterns. Notably, over 80% of existing rooftop photovoltaic capacity was concentrated in high-demand regions characterized by relatively low solar irradiance. To facilitate further expansion, we suggest that policymakers and industry managers prioritize future deployment in regions that combine medium-to-high solar irradiance with high electricity demand.

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the reliance on Google Earth high-resolution imagery introduces temporal discrepancies, as tile updates frequently lag within certain African regions, thereby constraining the capacity of our inventory to reflect the most contemporary, real-time development trajectories.42 Second, the pixel-level rooftop photovoltaic extraction remains susceptible to uncertainties arising from variations in image acquisition geometries, transient cloud cover, roofing material heterogeneity, and localized viewing angles, as well as potential cross-regional model-transfer biases.43,44 Third, this study does not include system-level evaluation of rooftop photovoltaic installations. We conduct pixel-level rather than system-level assessment because system boundaries cannot be reliably defined from satellite imagery, as a single building may contain multiple spatially separated photovoltaic arrays, while adjacent arrays may not correspond to distinct systems. Notably, small systems are more likely to be under-detected due to their limited spatial extent and elevated sensitivity to roofing texture interference, cast shadows, and localized image degradation. Fourth, the estimation of photovoltaic potential is based on a two-dimensional approximation that does not account for three-dimensional building features (e.g., roof slope, orientation, and shading) and on-site obstacles,45,46,47,48 which may lead to overestimation of available area and irradiance utilization efficiency. Finally, using nighttime light NTL data as a proxy for electricity demand has inherent limitations, including digital number saturation in urban cores, signal loss from observation noise or cloud cover, and “dark” grids that nevertheless exhibit demand.49 Future research should further address these limitations to improve the accuracy and timeliness of rooftop photovoltaic assessment and better support solar energy development across Africa.

Method details

Deep learning-based rooftop photovoltaics identification

In this study, high-resolution RGB satellite imagery was obtained via the Google Satellite API. The imagery has a spatial resolution of approximately 0.3 m and is widely used in urban analysis and infrastructure mapping. Based on the geographic boundaries provided by the Global Urban Boundaries (GUB) dataset, large-scale imagery covering target residential areas was downloaded in batches using QGIS.

Dataset preparation and annotation

A total of 2,957 image tiles were manually annotated to construct the annotated dataset. All images had a size of 512 × 512 pixels and contained pixel-wise labels of rooftop photovoltaic systems. The annotations were generated using the “Export Training Data for Deep Learning” tool in ArcGIS Pro and exported in classified raster format. The dataset was split into training (84%), validation (8%), and test (8%) subsets.

Notably, to enhance the generalization capability and cross-regional adaptability of the model, the training samples were compiled across diverse geographic regions worldwide, encompassing North America, Europe, East Asia, Southeast Asia, Australia, the Middle East, South America, South Africa, and other African regions. Among the training samples, 10.3% were from Africa; of these African samples, 49.0% were from South Africa and 51.0% were from other African regions. This distribution closely aligns with the empirical application landscape, where South Africa accounts for 71% of Africa’s total rooftop photovoltaic capacity, thereby ensuring sufficient training density for the continent’s dominant application hub while preserving broader regional adaptability across other African nations. These annotated training sample files, along with their corresponding country information, are publicly available (see the “data and code availability” section).

Preprocessing and data augmentation

All images were normalized to a pixel value range of [0, 1] prior to training. Large-scale satellite images were divided into non-overlapping tiles of size 512 × 512 pixels to facilitate efficient processing. To improve model robustness and reduce overfitting, data augmentation techniques were applied during training, including random rotations, horizontal and vertical flipping, and brightness adjustments.

Model architecture and training settings

A U-Net architecture with a ResNet101 backbone was adopted for semantic segmentation of rooftop PV systems. While more advanced architectures have been proposed in recent studies,24,50,51,52 our primary objective is to establish a consistent and reproducible workflow optimized for large-scale mapping, rather than pursuing marginal gains in detection performance. This choice reflects a deliberate trade-off among model complexity, computational efficiency, and operational feasibility at the continental scale.48,50

The model was trained from scratch without using pretrained weights (Figure S5). Training was conducted for 50 epochs with a batch size of 8, using input images resized to 512 × 512 pixels. The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−3, which was gradually reduced to 1 × 10−6 using a dynamic decay strategy.53 A combination of binary cross-entropy and Dice loss was used to optimize segmentation performance.54

Post-processing and vectorization

The model produces pixel-wise probability maps indicating the likelihood of photovoltaic presence. These maps are binarized using a threshold of 0.5 to generate segmentation masks. Connected component analysis was then applied to identify individual photovoltaic regions. To reduce noise and remove spurious detections, small isolated regions below a predefined area threshold are filtered out. The remaining segments are subsequently converted into vector polygons for further spatial analysis.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics, including Intersection over Union (IoU), F1-score, Precision, and Recall. These metrics quantify the model’s ability to delineate rooftop photovoltaic areas at the pixel level. To compute these indicators, a confusion matrix was first constructed to quantify true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN), from which the standard metrics were calculated as follows:(Equation 1)IoU=TPTP+FP+FN(Equation 2)Precision=TPTP+FP(Equation 3)Recall=TPTP+FN(Equation 4)F1−Score=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall

Notably, because the primary objective of this study is to quantify the total area and installed capacity of rooftop photovoltaics, and given the significant difficulty in determining whether adjacent photovoltaic polygons belong to the same photovoltaic system or household, an object- or system-level performance assessment was not performed.

The results on the test set demonstrate reliable segmentation performance (Table S1).

Independent case study for generalization assessment

To further evaluate the generalization capability of the model, an independent case study was conducted in the Isipingo Beach area in Africa. This region served as an external test site, as it was entirely unseen during the training and validation phases. The area was manually annotated to provide a ground-truth reference exclusively for performance evaluation.

The results demonstrate that the model maintains consistent and reliable performance in this independent setting, achieving a Precision of 94.50%, Recall of 86.63%, F1-score of 90.40%, and IoU of 82.48%. These metrics confirm the model’s robustness and generalizability across diverse geographic contexts. Furthermore, a comparative analysis shows that our model’s performance is comparable to current state-of-the-art benchmarks (Figure S2).

Large-scale application

A total of 3,300 residential areas across Africa were selected based on the GUB dataset, which identifies residential regions larger than 1 km2 globally. High-resolution imagery corresponding to these geographic polygons was retrieved and processed using the trained model. As a result, 183,014 photovoltaic polygons were identified across the study area.

To focus on rooftop photovoltaic systems, the analysis is restricted to build-up areas, and the training data consist of manually labeled rooftop PV samples. Following model inference, large contiguous PV patches are manually inspected to exclude potential utility-scale solar farms. However, some non-rooftop photovoltaic installations located within or adjacent to build-up areas cannot be completely excluded.

Calculation of photovoltaic power generation

Based on the identified rooftop photovoltaic area, we estimate installed capacity and electricity generation using parameters adopted from a previous study.19 Specifically, we assume a photovoltaic conversion efficiency of 20%, and a system efficiency of 0.8. The installed capacity Pinstalled is calculated as follows:(Equation 5)Pinstalled=Prate×SRPV

where SRPV is the area of the photovoltaic panel (in m2), and Prate is the rated power density of photovoltaic panels, set to 200 W/m2 in this study. It is worth noting that the use of a fixed power density introduces uncertainty, because different photovoltaic technologies have different power densities. Therefore, the installed capacity should be interpreted as an approximate value.

The photovoltaic generation Ppower is calculated as follows:(Equation 6)Ppower=Pinstalled×GHI×Cpv×Kwhere GHI is the solar irradiance data received by the surface, Cpv is the conversion efficiency of the photovoltaic panel, and K is the overall efficiency of the rooftop photovoltaic system. It should be noted that this simplified formulation does not explicitly account for panel tilt angle or azimuth orientation. For rooftop photovoltaic systems, these factors are typically constrained by roof geometry and building orientation rather than optimized for solar exposure.19 As a result, the estimated generation represents an approximation based on average installation conditions, and may introduce uncertainty at the system level.

Rooftop photovoltaics potential development ratio

To assess the current rooftop photovoltaics development status in Africa, we defined the rooftop photovoltaics development ratio as the ratio of installed rooftop photovoltaics area to the technically exploitable rooftop area. We first aggregated the rooftop photovoltaics map at a 10-km resolution, then integrated rooftop area data from a study by Zhang et al.19 onto the same grid for analysis. Considering the relevant constraints of rooftop photovoltaics construction, and using a maximum rooftop development coefficient of 0.3 as our basis for calculating developable potential, we obtained the current development progress and future developable situation of Africa. Based on the calculation of installed capacity and power generation, to quantify the development level of rooftop photovoltaics, the formula for photovoltaics development rate is as follows:(Equation 7)RRPV=SRPVSroof×K

where RRPV is the utilization ratio of the grid, SRPV is the rooftop photovoltaics area, Sroof is the regional roof area, and K is the maximum rooftop utilization coefficient.

Spatial coupling analysis of solar resources and electricity demand in Africa

This study utilized average monthly GHI for Africa in 2024 from ERA5 data and 2023 VIIRS nighttime light (NTL) intensity data.33 On the resource side, annual average GHI was calculated from the ERA5 dataset and resampled to a 10 km × 10 km grid using an area-weighted approach. On the demand side, NTL intensity was log-transformed to reduce distribution skewness and similarly aggregated to a 10 km × 10 km grid. Both datasets were uniformly processed and integrated to characterize the spatial coupling of solar resources and electricity demand.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Spatial data processing, image downloading, and geospatial analyses were conducted using ArcGIS Pro 3.6 and QGIS 3.40. Deep learning model development, inference, and statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.10 using the PyTorch 2.0 framework. Model performance was evaluated at the pixel level using standard segmentation metrics, including Precision, Recall, F1-score, and IoU. These metrics were derived from confusion matrices based on manually annotated ground-truth labels. The annotated dataset consisted of n = 2,957 image tiles, which were divided into training (84%), validation (8%), and test (8%) subsets. An independent external validation was additionally conducted in the Isipingo Beach region using manually annotated reference data. To characterize the spatial coupling between electricity demand and solar resources, nighttime light (NTL) intensity and global horizontal irradiance (GHI) were classified into tercile-based categories (high, medium, and low), and rooftop PV deployment patterns were compared across the resulting regional categories. Detailed model evaluation metrics are reported in Method and Figure S2 and Tables S1–S5. All mapping, visualization, and plotting were performed using Origin 2021 and Microsoft PowerPoint 2024.
