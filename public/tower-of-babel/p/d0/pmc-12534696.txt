Genetic history and adaptation of Eurasian wild boars inform livestock breeding
Authors: Ji Yang, Wen-Tian Wei, Meng-Hua Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Historical expansions of wild boars (Sus scrofa) across Eurasia have shaped phenotypic variation, genetic diversity, and local adaptation of their populations. The study by Wang et al.1 investigates the demographic history and genetic adaptation of Eurasian wild boars based on 96 whole-genome sequences, informing a critical role of Central Asian populations in their expansions and identifying key genes and variants associated with their local adaptation. Also, the adaptive variants are potentially useful for domestic pig breeding in future.

Main text

Different from small ruminant livestock like sheep and goats, whose wild ancestors are limited in the Near Eastern domestication center,2 wild boar (Sus scrofa), the wild ancestor of swine, thrive across an impressive range of environments, from the humid tropics (e.g., Southeast Asia) to the temperate forests and arid steppes (e.g., Central Asia) of Eurasia.3 Originating from Southeast Asia ca. 3–4 million years ago, wild boars have demonstrated exceptional ecological flexibility, driven by their omnivorous diet, behaviors, and physiological resilience.4 Their wide geographic distribution has resulted in rich phenotypic and genetic diversity, making the wild boars an important source for investigating the demographic history, local adaptation, and impact of domestication on wild ancestors. Previous studies have documented deep phylogenetic splits between Asian and European wild boar populations and have characterized the genetic richness of Southeast Asian lineages5,6 (Figure 1). However, the Central Asian populations—geographically situated at the junction of East and West Asia—have remained largely underrepresented, despite their potential importance as a conduit for intercontinental gene flows and a reservoir of unique genetic variants. In this issue of Cell Genomics, Wang et al.1 leverage high-coverage whole-genome sequencing of wild boars from Central Asia and surrounding regions to reconstruct the species’ demographic history and identify candidate genes for local adaptation. Their study not only clarifies long-debated questions about wild boar dispersal across Eurasia but also highlights genetic pathways and variants that may inform the improvement of domestic animals (e.g., pigs, sheep, and goats) adapted to diverse climatic and ecological contexts.

As also shown in an early study of global wild and domestic sheep,7 what makes this study particularly impactful is its integration of demographic history with signals of natural selection. By tracing divergence times between regional populations, the authors reveal a branching structure shaped by geography, climate, and time, highlighting Central Asia not merely as a passageway but as a zone of unique genetic mixing and adaptation. Earlier studies have relied heavily on mitochondrial and Y chromosome data8; alternatively, this genome-wide perspective brings resolution and depth, confirming that the evolutionary relationships among populations are more complex than previously recognized. This provides an important and particular foundation for de novo domestication.

The identification of genes under selection—such as ADAMTS20, PDPK1, LPIN1, and ALPK2—points to the physiological systems most affected by environmental pressures: pigmentation, metabolism, and muscle development. Many of these adaptive changes appear to be driven by regulatory variants in non-coding regions rather than changes in protein-coding sequences. This aligns with a growing body of evidence across species that suggests that fine-tuned gene regulation is often the substrate of adaptation.9

The close phylogenetic relationship and genomic similarity between wild ancestors and domestic species mean that insights into natural adaptation—especially to cold climates—may inform breeding programs aimed at enhancing resilience and performance in different agricultural settings. Genes such as LPIN1 and ALPK2, which show clear functional links to fat deposition and muscle growth, are already of interest in commercial breeding, and natural variants identified in this study could offer novel alleles for incorporation into domestic lines of pigs and other livestock. The adaptive genes and variants are promising tools for future adaptation-targeted breeding in various ecological and production systems.

Nonetheless, the study also reveals several gaps that remain to be filled. The underrepresentation of wild boars from South Asia, particularly the Indian subcontinent, limits a full reconstruction of migration routes, especially those originating from Island Southeast Asia and passing westward. This region may have served as a major conduit or barrier in the species’ spread, and sampling it more comprehensively will be key to completing the phylogeographic puzzle. Also, additional genomes of other wild pigs, particularly those in Island Southeast Asia (e.g., Sus celebensis and Sus barbatus), would provide new insights into the genomic evolution among species of the Sus genus.

Additionally, while the authors present strong associations between genetic variants and plausible adaptive functions, functional validation remains an outstanding challenge. Demonstrating causal relationships between genotype and phenotype—particularly for regulatory variants—will require further integrative efforts combining experimental, ecological, evolutionary approaches and updated gapless telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genomes.10 Studies in controlled environments or in wild populations exposed to different climatic conditions may help confirm the adaptive roles of the candidate variants identified here.

Finally, the interplay between local adaptation and historical demography remains a complex area. Many signatures of selection can be confounded by population bottlenecks, expansions, or gene flow, especially in species with long-range migrations and broad habitats like wild boars. Future studies employing forward simulations or more refined models of demography and selection in other wild relatives of livestock (e.g., the argali Ovis ammon in Central Asia as well) may help disentangle these factors.

Overall, the study advances the field by providing a genomic roadmap of wild boar evolution and a resource for dissecting adaptation on a continental scale. Their work not only enriches our understanding of a keystone species but also lays a strong foundation for comparative studies of adaptation, domestication, and the evolutionary impact of geography and climate. As sequencing efforts continue to expand across underrepresented regions, and as functional genomics tools become more accessible in non-model species, the field is well poised to uncover how evolution shapes genomes in the wilds.
