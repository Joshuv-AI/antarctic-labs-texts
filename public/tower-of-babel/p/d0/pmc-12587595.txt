Vitamin E-valproate co-therapy attenuated oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, related cognitive deficits and neuronal damage in Cypermethrin exacerbated seizure
Authors: Aminu Imam, Abdulraheem Mudathir Tunde, Abdulbasit Amin, Wahab Imam Abdulmajeed, Alhassan Godwin Attai, Aalimah Akinosho Akorede, Omamuyovwi Meashack Ijomone, Moyosore Salihu Ajao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy, characterized by recurrent seizures and associated cognitive impairments, is often exacerbated by environmental neurotoxins such as Cypermethrin. This study investigates the effects of Cypermethrin (CPM) on Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ)-induced seizures and the potential neuroprotective properties of Vitamin E (VIT E) and valproate (VAP), both alone and in combination.MethodsBehavioral tests, biochemical assays, and immunohistochemical analyses were conducted to assess seizure severity, cognitive performance, neuronal integrity, and markers of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress.ResultsPTZ-induced seizures significantly increased seizure severity and impaired cognitive performance in the T-maze, Y-maze, and Morris’ water maze tests, with these deficits being exacerbated by CPM. Immunohistochemical staining revealed substantial neuronal and interneuron loss in the hippocampus, accompanied by heightened microglial activation. Biochemical analyses showed elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α) and oxidative stress markers (MDA), alongside reduced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD). Therapeutic intervention with VAP or VIT E mitigated these effects to varying degrees, demonstrating improvements in seizure scores, cognitive performance, neuronal integrity, and reductions in neuroinflammation and oxidative stress markers. These findings underscore the potential synergistic benefits of combining VAP and VIT E in managing seizures and associated neuropathologies.ConclusionThis study highlights the exacerbating effects of environmental toxins on seizure-related cognitive and neuronal impairments while providing evidence for antioxidant-based combination therapies as a promising approach to mitigate these effects.Keywords: Seizure, Antioxidants, Hippocampus, Glia, GABA, Cognition

Backgrounds

Epilepsy, one of the most prevalent neurological disorders, is characterized by recurrent seizures resulting from abnormal neuronal activity [1]. Understanding the mechanisms that underlie seizure onset, and progression is crucial for developing effective therapeutic interventions [2]. Experimental seizure models, such as the Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) model, have long been employed to mimic epileptic conditions in laboratory settings [3]. PTZ acts as a GABA_A receptor antagonist, thereby lowering the seizure threshold and inducing convulsions in experimental animals [4]. This model provides a valuable platform for assessing both the pathophysiology of seizures and the efficacy of potential therapeutic compounds.

In addition to chemical convulsants, growing evidence suggests that environmental toxins such as insecticides may exacerbate or even induce seizure-like activity. Cypermethrin (CPM), a widely used pyrethroid insecticide, has been linked to neurotoxicity, manifesting as motor deficits, tremors, and in some cases, seizures [5]. Despite its widespread use, the interactions between CPM exposure and chemically induced seizures, such as those induced by PTZ, are not well understood. Addressing this gap is vital, particularly given the increasing human exposure to pyrethroids in both agricultural and domestic environments.

Also, there exist a mechanistic cross point between the pathophysiology of seizure and the mechanism of action of cypermethrin, especially in the propagation of action potential. While CPM keep Na + gated channel open to allow continuous influx of Na+, PTZ blocks Cl- influx, leading to hyper depolarization, which is essential in seizure propagation and epilepto-genesis. Coincidental with the increasing incidence of epilepsy is the increasing use of insecticides including cypermethrin, thus the objective of this study.

In response to these concerns, antioxidant therapies have gained attention for their potential neuroprotective effects. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol), a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant, is capable of mitigating oxidative stress, a critical factor in the development and exacerbation of seizures [6]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are believed to contribute to neuronal damage during seizures [7], making antioxidants a promising therapeutic avenue. However, the efficacy of Vitamin E in seizure models, particularly in mitigating the combined effects of convulsants and neurotoxic agents, requires further investigation.

Beyond the investigation of seizure activity, understanding how these agents affect cognition and neuroinflammation is critical. Behavioral assessments, such as the Y maze [8] and T maze tests [9], offer insights into memory and spatial learning impairments, which are commonly affected in epileptic disorders [10, 11]. Furthermore, biochemical assays for proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α12, a proinflammatory cytokine that mediate glia activation signalling, as well as markers of oxidative stress such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biochemical pathways involved in seizure activity [13].

Immunohistochemical staining for key neuronal and glial markers, including IBA1 (a marker of microglial activation) [14], NEUN (a marker of neuronal integrity), and parvalbumin, fast-spiking calcium-binding GABAergic interneurons, allows for a detailed examination of cellular changes in specific brain regions affected by seizures. These markers can provide critical insights into both neuronal survival and the inflammatory response during seizure activity, offering a deeper understanding of how convulsant and neurotoxic agents impact brain pathology [15, 16].

This study explored the complex interplay between PTZ-induced seizures, Cypermethrin exposure, and the neuroprotective effects of Vitamin E. Due to the limited effectiveness and side effects associated with many of the available antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), finding alternative therapeutic approaches is highly important. Vitamin E, is a naturally occurring antioxidant that has garnered interest for its potential neuroprotective effects in epilepsy. In animal models and human clinical trial, Vitamin E supplementation has shown promising results in reducing seizure frequency and severity, decrease in oxidative stress markers, and improve seizure control in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy.

Here, we specifically, it focused on the mechanistic effects of cypermethrin neurotoxicity in progressing hyperdepolarization in epileptogenesis and the consequential neurological effects. Repeated cypermethrin exposures exacerbates PTZ-induced seizures, and neuro-behavioural consequences, Vitamin E supplementation’s therapeutic advantages are more related to the neurological consequences. Also, vitamin E was found to be comparable to vaproic acid’s activities in most parameters, including seizure score. Its supplementation therapy effectively improved spatial function in T-maze as observed in Correct alternation. They preserved the hippocampal neurons and GABAergic interneuron integrities, significantly in the CA1 and DG, maintained antioxidant defence and reduced lipid peroxidation and inflammation. These provide mechanistic view of the neurobiological changes associated with the combination therapy, offering new insights into the potential for antioxidant therapies in managing seizure disorders.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted at the Animal House, College of Health Sciences, University of Ilorin, in accordance with the guidelines and procedures of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Ethical approval was granted by the University of Ilorin ethical review committee (UERC) (UIL/UERC/21/68LD001) in September 2024, following the recommendation of the College of Health Sciences ethical review committee.

Experimental animals

Seventy-two (72) Male Wistar rats weighing between 90 and 100 g (about 4–5 weeks old) were used for the study. The rats were obtained from the breeding facility of the University of Ilorin Central Research Laboratory and housed in the Animal Holding Facility under standard conditions. They were randomized based on weight to 9 rats per group, kept in wire-gauzed plastic cages with sawdust bedding, maintained under a natural light/dark cycle, and fed with a standard laboratory diet and water ad libitum. The animals were acclimatized for at least one week before the experiment.

Treatments

The experimental design involved administering various compounds to assess their effects on seizure activity and neurotoxicity. Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) was administered intraperitoneally to induce seizures, while Cypermethrin was administered to examine its potential neurotoxic effects. Vitamin E, known for its antioxidant properties, was administered to explore its neuroprotective potential. Valproate, a standard antiepileptic drug, was also evaluated both alone and in combination with Vitamin E to assess any synergistic effects on seizure amelioration.

PTZ was administered either alone or in combination with Cypermethrin, and the effects of Vitamin E or Valproate on PTZ- and Cypermethrin-induced seizures were assessed. Cypermethrin was given daily for ten days, while PTZ was administered every 48 h during the experiment. Valproate and Vitamin E were administered 30 min before Cypermethrin or PTZ.

Experimental design

This research followed a two-phase design approach. For the First phase; Thirty-Six male Wistar rats were divided into 4 groups of 9 rats each, comprised the control group and 3 experimental groups which received the following treatments:

Group 1: (control) which received 1 ml/kg /day of Corn oil, Group 2: which received 6.25 mg/kg/body weight of Cypermethrin orally, Group 3: which received 35 mg/kg/day of PTZ intraperitoneally every 48 h, Group 4: which received 6.25 mg/kg/ body weight of Cypermethrin and 35 mg/kg bodyweight of PTZ.

For the second phase, guided by the results of the first phase

Thirty-Six male Wistar rats were divided into 4 groups of 9 rats each, comprised the control group and 3 experimental groups which received the following treatments:

Group 1: (Control) which received 6.25 mg/kg body weight of Cypermethrin and 35 mg/kg body weight of PTZ, Group 2: which received 100 mg/kg body weight of Valporic acid, 6.25 mg/kg body weight of Cypermethrin and 35 mg/kg body weight of PTZ, Group 3: which received 100 mg/kg body weight of Vitamin E, 6.25 mg/kg body weight of Cypermethrin and 35 mg/kg body weight of PTZ, Group 4: which received 100 mg/kg body weight of Valporic acid, 100 mg/kg body weight of Vitamin E, 6.25 mg/kg body weight of Cypermethrin and 35 mg/kg body weight of PTZ. Oral administration was done once daily per oral gavage for 10 consecutive days while intraperitoneal administration was done every 48 h (1, 3, 7 and 9) days.

Seizure scoring

To assess seizure activity, the PTZ-induced seizure model was employed. After the administration of PTZ, the rats were placed in plastic cages, and their behaviors were videotaped for 30 min. Seizure severity was determined using Racine’s scoring system, ranging from no response to death (stage 6). The stages included facial movements, myoclonic jerks, forelimb clonus, rearing, and tonic-clonic seizures.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral tests, including the Y-maze and T-maze, were used to assess hippocampal-dependent spatial memory and learning. The number of arm entries and alternations in both mazes were recorded. In the Morris water maze, spatial learning and long-term memory were evaluated by measuring the time it took for rats to find a hidden escape platform submerged in opaque water.

Animal euthanasia

24 h after the last exposure, the rats were euthanized with intramuscular ketamine (10 mg/kg). The brains of rats designated for biochemical evaluations were removed, weighed and immersed in cold sucrose solution. Hippocampal tissues (from Bregma − 10 mm to − 15 mm) of five brains from each group was grossed out, dipped in 0.2 M cold sucrose solution for further processing.

Rats designated for immunohistochemistry study were perfused transcardially with normal saline followed by 10% buffered formal saline. The brains were excised and stored in specimen bottles containing 10% buffered formal saline.

Biochemical analysis

Following euthanasia, the hippocampus was dissected and homogenized in cold sucrose for biochemical assays. Oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). The levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) were also measured to evaluate the antioxidant defense system.

A high sensitivity and specificity Cusabio Rat TNF-α ELISA Kit (Catalog Number. CSB-E11987r) was used for quantitative analysis of TNF-α. In brief, 100 µl of standard or sample was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, then 100 µl of Biotin-antibody (1x) was added for an hour incubation at 37 °C, followed by two washes. Next, the samples were incubated in 100 µl of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-avidin (1x) for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by five washes, and a 15–30 min incubation in 90 µl of TMB substrate at 37 °C. Lastly, 50 µl of stop solution was added, mixed, and then the optical density was determined within 5 min, using a microplate reader set to 450 nm.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to investigate microglial activation and neuronal integrity. Ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA1) was used as a marker for microglial activation, while Neuronal Nuclear Antigen (NEUN) was used to detect mature neurons. Parvalbumin staining was performed to examine calcium-binding interneurons. Brain tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained using standard immunohistochemistry protocols. The density of positive cells in hippocampal regions (CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus) was quantified.

To demonstrate the immunostaining of glia marker and inhibitory GABAergic interneurons in the hippocampal sections following the exposures, induction and interventions, rats from each group were perfused with 10% buffered formalin, their brains removed and processed for immunohistochemical staining with anti-NeuN, anti-Iba1, and anti-PV in the hippocampus.

Paraffin embedded hippocampal tissues from each of the brains were serially sectioned at 8 μm thickness, to achieve three sections for each stain, from each brain. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through descending grades of ethanol (100%, 95%, 70% ethanol), and treated for protein un-masking in citrate-based solution (heat-mediated antigen retrieval), pH 6.0 (Vector®, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 30 min in a steamer. Sections were washed in PBS for 2 min, treated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide solution in PBS for 10 min to achieve endogenous peroxidase blocking, washed again in PBS for 2 min and then incubated in 2.5% normal horse serum for 20 min for protein blocking. The sections were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature in primary antibodies: Goat polyclonal Iba-1 (Abcam, USA, ab5076) at 1:250, Mouse monoclonal NeuN-HRP conjugated (Santa Cruz, USA, 2E1: sc-33673) at 1:150 or Rabbit polyclonal PV antibody (Novus Biologicals, USA, NB120-11427) at 1:1000, followed by 5 min wash in PBS. Sections were incubated in ImmPRESS™ HRP Anti-Rabbit, Anti-Goat or Anti-Mouse IgG (Peroxidase) Polymer Reagent, raised in horse (Vector® Labs, USA), for 30 min. They were washed in PBS for 5 min twice, 3, 3 -diaminobenzidine (DAB) Peroxidase Substrate Kit (Vector® Labs, USA) was added to develop colour, rinsed well in tap water, and then counter-stained in haematoxylin. Lastly, the sections were dehydrated through ascending grades of ethanol (70%, 95%, 100%), cleared in Xylene, mount with Permount (Fischer Scientific, USA), and reaction sites were visualized as a brown staining.

Microscopy, photomicrography and counting

Three hippocampal sections from each of the three brains prepared for histochemistry stains were finally examined under an AmScope 40X-2500X LED Lab Compound Microscope and photographed using the AmScope 5.0 MP USB Still Photo & Live Video Microscope Imager Digital Camera 5MP, manufactured by iSCOPE corp., USA. Approximately 165 randomly selected images (27 per group) were captured at 400X magnifications, from 9 sections per group per stain as indicated in micrographs and exported to JPEG image format.

Image analysis

The images were analysed using image J software. Manual cell counting and staining intensity were done using this software.

Cell counting

Using image J, the images were opened and the grid size (30000–50000 pixel2) was chosen for comfortable counting of the 165 × 400 magnified images. The multipoint tool in the tool bar is selected. Counting was done manually by identifying and clicking on the specific cell types to count. The total number of cells count from each image was exported to excel for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All quantitative data were analysed using the GraphPad Prism® software (Version 8.4.2). Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc Turkey’s test to compare the means between the groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Co-administration of Cypermethrin exacerbates PTZ-induced seizures and impairs cognitive performance in rats across seizure scoring, T-maze, Y-maze, and Morris’ water maze tests

The procedures and results of this study are essential for understanding how environmental toxins like Cypermethrin can worsen seizure severity and cognitive impairments. By assessing seizure activity and cognitive functions through behavioral tests such as the T-maze, Y-maze, and Morris’ water maze, we can evaluate the interaction between PTZ-induced seizures and Cypermethrin exposure, shedding light on the broader neurotoxic effects and potential risks posed by these toxins in seizure disorders.

The results demonstrated that Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) and the combination of PTZ with Cypermethrin (CPM) significantly impacted seizure severity and cognitive performance across various behavioural tests (Fig. 1A-C). The seizure scores were markedly higher in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups compared to the control group, with the highest scores observed in the PTZ + CPM group, indicating that Cypermethrin exacerbated seizure severity (Fig. 1A). In the T-maze test, both PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups exhibited a significant reduction in arm entries, total alternations, and the percentage of correct alternations, with the PTZ + CPM group showing the most severe deficits, suggesting impaired spatial memory and exploratory behaviour (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the Y-maze results revealed a significant decline in the number of arm entries, total alternations, and correct alternations in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups compared to the control, with the combined treatment group showing the greatest impairment in short-term memory and spatial recognition (Fig. 1C). In the Morris water maze test, the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups displayed significantly longer escape latencies and spent less time in the platform quadrant than the control group, with the PTZ + CPM group again showing the most pronounced impairments in spatial learning and memory (Fig. 1D). Overall, the findings suggest that PTZ-induced seizures impair cognitive function, and the co-administration of Cypermethrin worsens these deficits, highlighting the detrimental effects of Cypermethrin on seizure-related cognitive dysfunction.

Co-administration of Cypermethrin with PTZ exacerbates neuronal and interneuron loss in the hippocampus, correlating with cognitive deficits

To further understand the impact of Cypermethrin (CPM) on PTZ-induced seizures and its influence on cognitive deficits observed in behavioral tests (Fig. 1), we conducted immunohistochemical staining using NEUN as a marker for neurons and parvalbumin for interneurons (Fig. 2). These staining methods allow for the visualization and quantification of neuronal integrity and the density of specific interneuron populations within key regions of the hippocampus: the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG). By linking these findings with the behavioral outcomes previously observed, we aim to explore how the structural integrity and neuronal populations in these regions correlate with cognitive impairments and seizure severity.

Representative photomicrographs of NeuN + neurons in the CA1 (A), CA3 (B), DG (C) and Parvalbumin + interneurons in the CA1 (D), CA3 (E), (F); and the respective graphs of the estimated immunoreactive cells in rats exposed to corn-oil (CTR), Pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), Cypermethrin (CPM) and PTZ plus CPM respectively. Asterisks (*, **, ***, ****) indicate statistically significant differences at P = 0.05, One-Way ANOVA

The results revealed a significant reduction in NEUN-positive cells in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups across all examined regions (CA1, CA3, and DG) compared to the control group. The most pronounced decrease in neuronal count was observed in the PTZ + CPM group, indicating a compounded neurotoxic effect when Cypermethrin is co-administered with PTZ (Fig. 2A-C). Specifically, in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions, NEUN-positive cells were significantly lower in the PTZ + CPM group, confirming the extensive neuronal damage (Fig. 2A-C).

Additionally, the staining for parvalbumin, which marks calcium-binding interneurons, showed a significant reduction in parvalbumin-positive cells in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups across the CA1, CA3, and DG regions compared to controls (Fig. 2D-F). The PTZ + CPM group exhibited the most severe loss of parvalbumin-positive interneurons, emphasizing that Cypermethrin exacerbates the depletion of this crucial neuronal population. This reduction in parvalbumin-positive cells aligns with the behavioral deficits noted earlier, as these interneurons are essential for maintaining inhibitory control and proper cognitive function within the hippocampus.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the co-administration of Cypermethrin with PTZ not only increases seizure severity but also results in significant neuronal and interneuron loss, particularly in key hippocampal regions linked to memory and learning. The immunohistochemical data corroborate the behavioral deficits observed in Fig. 1, suggesting that the cognitive impairments and increased seizure activity are associated with structural and functional disruptions in the hippocampal network.

Co-administration of Cypermethrin with PTZ intensifies neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, correlating with increased microglial activation and neuronal damage

To further elucidate the neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms underlying the cognitive deficits and neuronal damage observed in Figs. 1 and 2, immunohistochemical staining for microglia (IBA1) and biochemical assays for pro-inflammatory (TNF-α) and oxidative stress markers (SOD and MDA) were conducted. The aim was to assess how PTZ-induced seizures and Cypermethrin exposure influence neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which are critical factors contributing to neuronal damage and cognitive impairments.

The results showed a significant increase in IBA1-positive cells, indicating microglial activation, in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups across the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG) regions compared to the control (Fig. 3A-C). The highest levels of microglial activation were observed in the PTZ + CPM group in all three hippocampal regions, suggesting that Cypermethrin co-administration with PTZ exacerbates neuroinflammation (Fig. 3A-C).

Representative photomicrographs of Iba1 + microglia cells in the CA1 (A), CA3 (B), DG (C) and the respective graphs of the estimated immunoreactive cells; and the biochemical quantification of the brain concentrations of TNF alpha (D), SOD (E) and MDA (F) in rats exposed to corn-oil (CTR), Pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), Cypermethrin (CPM) and PTZ plus CPM respectively. Asterisks (*, **, ***, ****) indicate statistically significant differences at P = 0.05, One-Way ANOVA

Biochemical assays revealed that TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups compared to the control, with the highest TNF-α concentration detected in the PTZ + CPM group, reinforcing the finding of heightened neuroinflammatory response (Fig. 3D). Additionally, the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), an antioxidant enzyme, were reduced in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups compared to the control, indicating a compromised antioxidant defense mechanism (Fig. 3E). In contrast, malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, was significantly elevated in the PTZ and PTZ + CPM groups, with the PTZ + CPM group showing the highest MDA levels, highlighting the increased oxidative damage associated with Cypermethrin and PTZ co-administration (Fig. 3F).

Overall, these findings confirm that PTZ-induced seizures, particularly when combined with Cypermethrin exposure, significantly enhance neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress responses, which correlate with the neuronal and cognitive impairments observed in earlier figures. The results underscore the detrimental impact of Cypermethrin on seizure-induced neuroinflammation and oxidative damage, potentially aggravating the cognitive and neuronal deficits in seizure disorders.

Valproate and Vitamin E partially ameliorate PTZ + CPM-induced cognitive deficits despite limited impact on seizure severity

To assess the potential therapeutic effects of the antiseizure drug valproate (VAP), Vitamin E (VIT E), and their combination on the behavioral deficits induced by PTZ + CPM, we focused on evaluating seizure severity and T-maze performance. Given that PTZ + CPM treatment had no significant impact on performance in the Y-maze or Morris water maze in previous assessments (Fig. 1), only seizure score and T-maze performance were considered here. This approach allowed us to determine if these treatments could mitigate the heightened seizure scores and cognitive impairments linked to PTZ + CPM exposure, as observed in previous figures.

The results indicated a trend toward reduced seizure scores in the PTZ + CPM group treated with valproate, Vitamin E, or their combination; however, these reductions were not statistically significant (Fig. 4A). In the T-maze test, PTZ + CPM treatment led to a significant reduction in arm entries, total alternations, and correct alternations compared to the control group, reflecting cognitive deficits (Fig. 1). Treatments with either valproate and the combination treatment significantly improved performances in the T-maze, as shown by increases in arm entries, total alternation and correct alternations, an evidence of improved cognitive function (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that, while valproate and Vitamin E do not significantly reduce seizure severity in comorbid rats, ANOVA and Turkey’s posthoc test for multiple comparison revealed that the combination may provide substantial improvement in PTZ + CPM-induced cognitive impairments, with the combination improving the correct alternation better (Fig. 4B).

Plus (+) and Minus (-) signs indicate presence and absence respectively. Asterisk (*, **, ****) indicates statistically significant at P = 0.05, One-Way ANOVA

Combination therapy with valproate and Vitamin E restores neuronal and interneuron integrity in the hippocampus following PTZ + CPM-induced damage

To further explore the neuroprotective potential of valproate (VAP), Vitamin E (VIT E), and their combination in mitigating the neuronal damage induced by PTZ + CPM, immunohistochemical staining for NEUN (a neuronal marker) and parvalbumin (an interneuron marker) was conducted across the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG) regions of the hippocampus. These assessments aimed to correlate the treatments’ effects on neuronal integrity and interneuron populations with the observed cognitive and behavioral outcomes from previous figures.

The results demonstrated a significant reduction in NEUN-positive cells in the PTZ + CPM group across all hippocampal regions (CA1, CA3, and DG), indicating extensive neuronal damage (Fig. 5A-C). Treatment with valproate or Vitamin E independently increased the number of NEUN-positive cells in these regions, with the combination therapy (PTZ + CPM + VAP + VIT E) showing the most pronounced restoration of neuronal counts, approaching levels similar to normal conditions (Fig. 5A-C).

Plus (+) and Minus (-) signs indicate presence and absence respectively. Asterisks (*, **, ***, ****) indicate statistically significant differences at P = 0.05, One-Way ANOVA

Similarly, parvalbumin-positive interneurons were significantly reduced in the PTZ + CPM group in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions, reflecting a loss of inhibitory interneurons critical for proper hippocampal function (Fig. 5D-F). Treatment with valproate or Vitamin E individually improved the number of parvalbumin-positive cells, but the combination therapy yielded the highest restoration of these interneurons across all regions (Fig. 5D-F).

These findings highlight the potential of valproate and Vitamin E, particularly in combination, to mitigate PTZ + CPM-induced neuronal and interneuron loss in the hippocampus. The results provide a structural basis for the cognitive improvements observed in earlier behavioral tests and underscore the neuroprotective synergy between valproate and Vitamin E.

Combination therapy with valproate and Vitamin E mitigates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in PTZ + CPM-induced brain damage

To assess the neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress-modulating effects of valproate (VAP), Vitamin E (VIT E), and their combination on PTZ + CPM-induced damage, we analyzed microglial activation using IBA1 staining and biochemical markers of oxidative stress (SOD and MDA) and inflammation (TNF-α). These analyses aimed to correlate the treatments’ effects on cellular and biochemical processes with the observed neuroprotective and behavioral outcomes from previous figures.

The results revealed a significant increase in IBA1-positive microglia in the PTZ + CPM group across all hippocampal regions (CA1, CA3, and DG), indicating heightened neuroinflammation (Fig. 6A-C). Treatment with valproate or Vitamin E individually reduced microglial activation, as evidenced by a decrease in IBA1-positive cells, with the combination therapy (PTZ + CPM + VAP + VIT E) achieving the most substantial reduction, nearly restoring IBA1 levels to normal (Fig. 6A-C).

Representative photomicrographs of Iba1 + microglia cells in the CA1 (A), CA3 (B), DG (C) and the respective graphs of the estimated immunoreactive cells; and the biochemical quantification of the brain concentrations of SOD (D), MDA (E) and TNF alpha (F), in rats exposed to Pentylenetetrazol and Cypermethrin without intervention (CPM + PTZ), and with interventions, Valproic Acid (VAP), Vitamin E (VIT E) and the combination. Plus (+) and Minus (-) signs indicate presence and absence respectively. Asterisks (*, **, ***, ****) indicate statistically significant differences at P = 0.05, One-Way ANOVA

Biochemical analysis showed that brain SOD levels, which were significantly reduced in the PTZ + CPM group, were restored with both valproate and Vitamin E treatments (Fig. 6D). The combination therapy resulted in the most pronounced recovery of SOD levels, indicating enhanced antioxidant defense (Fig. 6D). Similarly, brain MDA levels, which were significantly elevated in the PTZ + CPM group, were reduced following treatment, with the combination therapy providing the greatest reduction, highlighting its ability to mitigate oxidative damage (Fig. 6E).

TNF-α levels, a marker of neuroinflammation, were significantly elevated in the PTZ + CPM group. Treatment with valproate or Vitamin E independently reduced TNF-α levels, but the combination therapy yielded the most significant reduction, aligning with the observed decrease in microglial activation (Fig. 6F).

These findings indicate that valproate and Vitamin E, particularly in combination, effectively reduce neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in PTZ + CPM-induced brain damage. The observed reductions in microglial activation, oxidative markers, and pro-inflammatory cytokines provide a mechanistic explanation for the neuroprotective effects and behavioral improvements seen in previous results.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that environmental toxins such as Cypermethrin (CPM) can exacerbate seizure severity and cognitive impairments induced by Pentylenetetrazole (PTZ), while antioxidant and anticonvulsant therapies show promise in mitigating these effects. The findings align with and expand upon previous studies, highlighting the multifaceted nature of seizure disorders and their modulation by external and internal factors.

The exacerbation of PTZ-induced seizures by CPM observed in this study corroborates prior research on the neurotoxic effects of pyrethroids. Pyrethroids are known to modulate voltage-gated sodium channels, prolonging depolarization and leading to hyperexcitability of neurons [17]. When combined with PTZ, which antagonizes GABA_A receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission [3], the resulting excitatory-inhibitory imbalance likely explains the heightened seizure severity and cognitive deficits. This dual impact aligns with studies showing that simultaneous exposure to neurotoxic agents can have additive or synergistic effects on neuronal hyperexcitability and excitotoxicity [18].

Behavioral impairments observed in the T-maze, Y-maze, and Morris’ water maze tests indicate significant hippocampal dysfunction, a hallmark of epilepsy [19]. Structural damage observed in hippocampal regions, including reduced NEUN-positive and parvalbumin-positive cells, supports the notion that inhibitory interneurons are particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress and excitotoxicity [20]. Parvalbumin-positive interneurons play a critical role in maintaining network oscillations and synchrony, which are disrupted during seizures. Loss of these interneurons has been linked to impaired gamma oscillations and memory deficits in rodent models of epilepsy [21].

Our findings of heightened IBA1-positive microglial activation in PTZ + CPM-treated animals align with previous research identifying neuroinflammation as a key driver of seizure propagation and neuronal damage [14, 22]. Microglial activation, triggered by excitotoxicity and oxidative damage, can release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, further exacerbating neuronal injury. TNF-α has been shown to modulate glutamate receptors, increasing excitatory neurotransmission and lowering the seizure threshold [12]. The observed increase in MDA and reduction in SOD levels highlights the role of oxidative stress in this cascade, consistent with studies showing that lipid peroxidation products and reduced antioxidant defenses exacerbate neuronal vulnerability during seizures [23].

Mechanistically, the neuroprotective effects of valproate (VAP) and Vitamin E (VIT E) observed in this study may be explained by their complementary actions on these pathways. Valproate’s ability to modulate sodium and calcium channels, enhance GABAergic neurotransmission, and reduce glutamatergic activity is well-documented [24]. Additionally, valproate has been shown to attenuate microglial activation and TNF-α release, providing anti-inflammatory effects [25]. Vitamin E, on the other hand, acts as a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant, scavenging free radicals and reducing lipid peroxidation [26]. Its protective effects on mitochondrial integrity and function may also contribute to the observed improvements in oxidative stress markers [27].

The combination therapy of valproate and Vitamin E was particularly effective, likely due to their synergistic targeting of both excitotoxicity and oxidative stress. This combination may also prevent the secondary inflammatory responses mediated by microglial activation. Interestingly, some studies suggest that antioxidants like Vitamin E can enhance the efficacy of traditional antiepileptic drugs by improving mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative stress, which may enhance synaptic stability and neuronal survival [28].

However, our study also highlights certain limitations. While seizure scores showed a trend toward reduction with treatment, the changes were not statistically significant. This could be due to variability in seizure susceptibility among animals or limitations in sample size. Additionally, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of valproate and Vitamin E warrant further exploration, particularly in terms of their impact on mitochondrial dynamics, calcium homeostasis, and redox signaling.

Conclusion

The study revealed that our mechanistic hypothesis of a severe seizure and neuro-behavioural sequel in the co-morbidity is correct, as repeated cypermethrin severed PTZ-induced seizures, and neuro-behavioural consequences. Although, valproic acid lone treatment is comparably better than vitamin E treatment, a combination treatment improved most parameters, including spatial working memory in T-maze, hippocampal neurons and PV + GABAergic interneurons integrity, antioxidant capacities, and reduced lipid peroxidation and inflammation. These provide a broad mechanistic view of the therapeutic advantages of the combination therapy, offering new insights into the potential for antioxidant therapies in managing neurological disorders.

Our findings emphasize the role of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in exacerbating seizure-related cognitive deficits and neuronal damage. The results underscore the importance of combination therapies that target multiple pathological pathways, particularly in contexts involving exposure to environmental neurotoxins. Future studies should aim to explore the long-term efficacy and safety of such therapies, as well as their potential applications in human seizure disorders complicated by environmental exposures.
