The Qur'anic heart and converging evidence from contemporary cardiac science: From qalb to cardioception
Authors: Ahmed S. BaHammam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For most of recorded history, the heart was considered far more than a pump. The Qur'an and Sunnah (ahadith) describe it as the seat of reasoning, faith, emotion, and moral accountability, employing a vocabulary of at least four distinct cardiac terms (qalb, fu'ad, lubb, and sadr) that appear over 200 times in contexts extending well beyond circulation. Arab–Islamic physicians and philosophers, including al-Balkhi, al-Razi, Ibn Sina, and al-Ghazali, built this into a systematic framework linking cardiac physiology to emotion, cognition, and spiritual life, centuries before the Cartesian biomedical model first treated the organ primarily as a hemodynamic pump. This integrative narrative review asks whether contemporary science has moved back toward the earlier view. Evidence is drawn from neurocardiology, cardiac endocrinology, cardioception research, psychophysiology, and studies of interpersonal synchrony. The heart has an intrinsic nervous system with ∼14,000–94,000 neurons, which are capable of autonomous information processing and local reflex processing that operates within central autonomic regulation. It synthesizes oxytocin and releases atrial natriuretic peptide, and these molecules are implicated in social bonding and anxiolysis. Approximately 80% of vagal cardiac fibers carry signals from the heart to the brain, contributing to emotional and perceptual processing alongside descending central regulation. Heartbeat-evoked potentials gate access to emotional stimuli across the cardiac cycle, and heart rate variability indexes the brain's capacity for emotional regulation and sound judgment. In a recent group study, cardiac synchrony between individuals in group settings was associated with collective decision-making performance. It is not claimed that science proves scripture, but instead it is shown that independent scientific disciplines, working without reference to religious texts, have arrived at a portrait of the heart that stands in analogical consonance with descriptions in Islamic tradition over fourteen centuries ago. Limitations are noted where evidence is weak, particularly regarding transplant personality-change reports and cardiac electromagnetic field claims. Future directions include characterizing intrinsic cardiac neuron subtypes in humans, examining the cardiac effects of practices such as dhikr, and designing prospective transplant protocols.

Introduction

For most of recorded history, human civilizations viewed the heart as far more than a blood-circulating organ. Ancient Egyptians preserved the heart during mummification while discarding the brain, viewing it as the seat of intelligence and personality.1,2 In China, the character “xin” denoted both “heart” and “mind”,3 and Aristotle held that the heart governed sensation and thought whereas the brain merely cooled the blood.4,5 These views persisted across cultures for millennia, although a uniquely detailed account of the heart's non-physical roles developed in Islamic tradition, and it remains a living part of Muslim belief, practice, and self-understanding for over 2 billion people worldwide.6

The Qur'an presents a detailed account of the heart and its faculties that classical scholars of Ahl al-Sunnah addressed at length, including Ibn Taymiyyah in Majmu' al-Fatawa and Ibn al-Qayyim in Ighathat al-Lahfan and Madarij al-Salikin.7, 8, 9 The Qur'an employs at least four distinct cardiac terms, i.e., qalb, fu'ad, lubb, and sadr, which together appear over 200 times, mainly in contexts of cognition, emotion, moral agency, and spiritual perception.10 In Section “The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition” of this review, we examine these terms and their functions in detail. This deep-rooted spiritual psychology shapes daily devotion, ethical reasoning, and conceptions of moral selfhood across Muslim communities, and it has done so for over fourteen centuries.

The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him [PBUH]) stated: “Indeed, in the body there is a piece of flesh, which if it is sound, the entire body will be sound, and if it is corrupt, the entire body will be corrupt; indeed, it is the heart” (Bukhari 52; Muslim 1599).11,12 He also frequently prayed: “O Turner of Hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion” (Tirmidhi 2140),13 affirming that the state of the heart shifts in response to human conduct, intention, and orientation toward divine guidance, rather than through any autonomous transformation of the organ itself.

Building on an earlier description of pulmonary circulation by the Arab physician Ibn al-Nafis in the thirteenth century,14 in 1628, William Harvey demonstrated that the heart functions as a circulatory pump, and his achievement gradually reduced the organ in the biomedical imagination to a mechanical device. Harvey himself never challenged the heart's emotional and metaphysical role,15 and it was actually the mechanistic philosophy of Descartes and the broader Enlightenment that stripped the heart of its emotional and spiritual associations.5,15 However, has modern science truly settled the question? Over the past four decades, converging discoveries from neurocardiology, cardiac endocrinology, interoceptive neuroscience, and transplant medicine have begun to reopen this question in unexpected ways.

Despite growing interest in neurocardiology and the Islamic psychology of the heart, previous work connecting the two16 has not engaged with the primary neuroscience literature in depth, graded the strength of evidence across disciplines, or incorporated recent advances in cardioception, heart rate variability (HRV) biofeedback, and interpersonal cardiac synchrony. Several of these advances are recent, including evidence from a randomized trial that training the heart's rhythm can alter the brain's emotion-regulating circuits.17 This review aims to address this gap.

In this review, we do not claim that modern science “proves” Qur'anic or Sunnah teachings about the heart as that approach would commit a category error, subordinating revelation to the shifting conclusions of empirical research. The Qur'anic and scientific accounts are better read as parallel discourses at different levels of description, one phenomenological and ethical, and the other mechanistic and measurable. This review examines converging evidence from multiple scientific disciplines to understand the extent to which contemporary findings are consonant with the Islamic spiritual psychology of the heart. Apparent convergences are noted as analogical consonance, not proof, and preliminary, contested, or animal-only evidence is flagged when cited. Where consonance is found, we propose testable hypotheses for future research and identify questions that remain open. The aim is not to prove but to explore, to map the resonances between a tradition that has shaped the inner life of over 2 billion people and a body of science that is only now beginning to appreciate what the heart may be. This framing keeps the reader alert to the risk of retrospective harmonization while permitting substantive resonances to be examined on their own terms.

Materials and Methods

The design of this narrative review is suited to interdisciplinary topics that bridge scientific and humanities literature.18,19 We synthesize evidence from neurocardiology, cardiac endocrinology, interoceptive neuroscience, and psychophysiology alongside primary Islamic textual sources, examining the extent to which contemporary findings about the heart's non-mechanical functions resonate with the Qur'anic-Prophetic descriptions of the heart.

Literature searches were performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar until February 2026. For the scientific component (Sections “The heart’s intrinsic nervous system: The “Little Brain” on the heart; Bidirectional heart–brain communication: The heart speaks to the brain; The heart as an endocrine organ: Hormones of bonding and tranquility; Cardiac interoception: The heart shapes consciousness and emotion; HRV, emotion regulation, and decision making; Heart transplant phenomena: Does the heart carry memory and personality?; The heart’s electromagnetic field: Information beyond neural pathways”), the inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed original research, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses in indexed journals, but preferably studies from the past decade while retaining seminal earlier work. Search terms for the scientific component comprised combinations of: heart, cardiac, intrinsic cardiac nervous system, neurocardiology, heart–brain axis, cardioception, interoception, heartbeat-evoked potential, HRV, cardiac oxytocin, atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), cardiac synchrony, and heart transplant personality. For the Islamic textual component, the searches combined the following terms: qalb, fu'ad, lubb, sadr, Qur'an heart, Islamic cardiac psychology, and heart in hadith. Primary sources for Sections “The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition” and “The heart in contemporary science” were limited to the Qur'an, authenticated hadith collections, and classical scholarly works in critical editions or authoritative translations. Reference lists in all of the retrieved articles were also screened to identify additional relevant studies.

Eligibility for the scientific component was defined as follows. We included peer-reviewed original research articles, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses indexed in the databases listed above, with no lower date limit and a cutoff of February 2026. Preprints were considered when no peer-reviewed equivalent was available and when they were identified as such at the point of citation. Searches were restricted to articles in English and Arabic. Reference lists in the retrieved articles and recent reviews in the field were screened by hand. Editorials, letters, opinion pieces, popular science commentaries, and non-indexed conference abstracts were excluded. Animal studies were retained only when they addressed a mechanism not yet examined in humans, with the species and model flagged at the point of citation.

In the lexicological analysis of Qur'anic cardiac vocabulary (Section “The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition”), Arabic root meanings were verified against Lisan al-'Arab by Ibn Manzur and Muʿjam Maqāyīs al-Lugha by Ibn Faris.20,21 Each cardiac term (qalb, fu'ad, lubb, sadr, and nafs) was traced to its triliteral root, and its semantic field was reconstructed before Qur'anic occurrences were examined. Frequency and distributional patterns (Makkan versus Madinan placement, verbal versus nominal forms, and recurrent collocations) were drawn from established concordances and cross-checked against the Tanzil corpus. Verse interpretation was based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir, and al-Tabari and al-Razi were consulted where the exegetical tradition diverged.22 Hadith material was taken only from the authenticated Sunni collections, and the grading of each report was verified against al-Nawawi for Sahih Muslim (SM) and Ibn Hajar for Sahih al-Bukhari (SB). A textual unit was accepted when its lexical and exegetical reading was stable across the consulted authorities, and readings that rested on a single late commentator were retained only as secondary supporting interpretive material.

For citations from the Qur'an, we used the English translation approved by King Fahd Complex for the Printing of the Holy Qur'an in Madinah. Qur'anic verses are cited by sura and verse number (sura:verse, e.g., 22:46). For citations from hadith, we refer to the compiled book and hadith number, where the primary authenticated collections used are SB, SM, and Sunan al-Tirmidhi. Classical Islamic works consulted include al-Ghazali's Ihya’ ‘Ulum al-Din and Ibn Sina's Kitab al-Adwiyat al-Qalbiyya’. This citation approach follows the method adopted in previous publications, integrating Qur'anic and hadith material with biomedical evidence.23,24

The article is organized thematically rather than chronologically. Islamic primary sources appear first (Sections “The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition” and “The heart in contemporary science”), followed by contemporary scientific evidence grouped by discipline (Sections “The heart’s intrinsic nervous system: The “Little Brain” on the heart; Bidirectional heart–brain communication: The heart speaks to the brain; The heart as an endocrine organ: Hormones of bonding and tranquility; Cardiac interoception: The heart shapes consciousness and emotion; HRV, emotion regulation, and decision making; Heart transplant phenomena: Does the heart carry memory and personality?; The heart’s electromagnetic field: Information beyond neural pathways”), and concluding with a convergence analysis that maps the two bodies of knowledge onto each other (Section “Convergence and discussion: When science meets scripture”). Synthesis was achieved by identifying points of consonance and limits of correspondence between Qur'anic-Prophetic descriptions and the empirical findings from each scientific domain. This organizational pattern follows the recommendation that narrative reviews should adopt the structure that best serves the argument.18,19

A point of consonance was recorded when an Islamic textual description of the heart referred to a non-circulatory function, such as perception, emotion, social bonding, or moral orientation; when contemporary empirical research documented a measurable cardiac process in the same functional domain; and when the two could be paired without distortion of either, meaning that the textual statement was not stripped of its phenomenological or ethical sense to fit a physiological referent, and the empirical finding was not extended beyond its primary source. The qalb as the seat of tranquility (Q 13:28), read alongside research on cardiac oxytocin, ANP, and parasympathetically mediated HRV during contemplative practice, illustrates this pairing. “Limits of correspondence” was applied where the textual and empirical descriptions operate at different levels and cannot be paired without category confusion, as found with the Qur'anic reference to “disease in the hearts” (Q 2:10), which is moral and spiritual in classical exegesis and has no counterpart in cardiovascular pathology. The procedure remains interpretive, as acknowledged in Section “Convergence and discussion: When science meets scripture”, but the criteria defined above are intended to make the interpretive choices visible and open to critique.

The inherent limitations of this approach, including the subjectivity of narrative synthesis and interpretive choices in mapping Islamic concepts to scientific findings, are acknowledged later in this section.

The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition

The Qur'anic vocabulary of the heart and its inner dimensions

In the Introduction, we noted that the Qur'an employs a layered vocabulary for the heart, with over 200 combined occurrences of its principal cardiac terms. Not all of these terms are synonyms for the heart in the narrow anatomical sense, but instead they describe distinct dimensions of the inner life that the Qur'an locates in the cardiac center, forming what amounts to a layered spiritual psychology of the heart. In this section, we examine each of these terms in greater depth, drawing on classical Arabic lexicography, Qur'anic concordance data, and contemporary semantic scholarship, to show that the Qur'an presents not one heart concept but an articulated cardiac psychology where each term maps a distinct dimension of human inner life.

Al-qalb (القلب): the dynamic spiritual center

The primary and most frequent term is al-qalb (القلب), derived from the root q-l-b, whose basic semantic cluster includes turning, flipping, changing, and transforming.25,26 Classical lexicographers recorded that the organ was called qalb precisely because of its constant fluctuation: “it was not named qalb except because it is changing”.21 Ibn Faris defined qalb as khalis al-shai’ wa sharafuhu (خالص الشيء وشرفه), “the core of everything and the most noble part,” indicating that the word carries a dual sense of both instability and centrality.20 The root q-l-b appears 168 times in the Quran, where 36 are verb forms and 132 are noun forms, distributed across 53 Mekkan (in Makkah) and 115 Medinan (in Madinah) verses.10,25 It has been observed that while it may seem paradoxical for a single root to mean both “center” and “change,” the paradox resolves itself when one considers the heart's own nature: the physical heart never stops moving, but it remains the body's central organ. In the same manner, the spiritual heart is in constant fluctuation between states, but it remains the core of the human person.7 Within the Qur'an, qalb carries the widest semantic range of all the cardiac terms. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with” (7:179).•Faith: “The bedouins say, We have believed. Al-fu'ad (الفؤاد): the emotional–perceptual conscience

The second term is al-fu'ad (الفؤاد), from the root f-’-d (ف-أ-د). Ibn Manzur defined al-fu'ad as al-qalb li-tafa'wudihi wa tawaqqudihi (القلب لتفأوده وتوقده), “the heart, so called because of its burning and blazing”.21 He added that some authorities held al-fu'ad to be ghisha’ al-qalb (غشاء القلب), “the covering of the heart,” whereas the qalb itself is its core and seed.21 Ibn Faris concurred, placing the root under a single semantic principle: homma wa shiddat harara (حمى وشدة حرارة), “fever and intensity of heat,” and concluded: “the fu'ad was named so because of its heat” (summiya bi-dhalika li-hararatihi). (سُمِّيَ بِذَلِكَ لِحَرَارَتِهِ).20 The word fu'ad (فؤاد) and its plural af'ida (أفئدة) appear 16 times in the Quran, all in Mekkan suras.10,25 In eight of these occurrences, fu'ad appears alongside al-sam’ (السمع, hearing) and al-basar (البصر, sight) as the third member of a perceptual triad, as in: “And Allah has extracted you from the wombs of your mothers not knowing a thing, and He made for you hearing and vision and af'ida (الأفئدة) that perhaps you would be grateful” (16:78). This consistent pairing suggests that fu'ad functions as an internal perceptual faculty as the organ of conscience and deep feeling, which processes what the ear and eye perceive.25 When the Qur'an singles out fu'ad (فؤاد) as a source of moral accountability, “Indeed, the hearing, the sight and the heart (fu'ad, الفؤاد) – about all those [one] will be questioned” (17:36), it positions this faculty as something that can verify or distort reality. A striking illustration occurs in the story of Prophet Moses' mother: “And the heart (fu'ad, الفؤاد) of Moses' mother became empty [of all else]. She was about to disclose [the matter concerning] him had We not bound fast her heart (qalbiha, قلبها) that she would be of the believers” (28:10). This deliberate lexical shift between fu'ad (فؤاد) and qalb (قلب) within a single verse suggests that the Qur'an treats them as related but not identical: fu'ad captures the burning, emotionally volatile dimension of the heart, whereas qalb denotes the broader spiritual center that can be steadied through faith. The emptiness of Moses' mother's fu'ad describes a state of overwhelming emotional intensity so acute that the heart could hold nothing else, and classical commentators explain that she could not think of any matter in the world except Moses.22,27 The Prophet (PBUH) usage confirms this distinction. In a hadith of the Prophet (PBUH), the people of Yemen are described as “araqqu af'idatan wa alyanu quluban” (أرقُّ أفئدةً وألينُ قلوبًا) (softer in their af'ida and more tender in their qulub) (SB 4388; SM 52). Al-Khattabi explained that the fu'ad is the covering of the qalb, where when it is thin (raqiq), words penetrate to the core, and when the qalb within is soft (layyin), it absorbs and retains what reaches it.28 The pairing of two distinct attributes with two distinct terms in a single phrase supports the reading that fu'ad and qalb are closely related but denote different dimensions of the cardiac center. In a further consistent grammatical distinction, fu'ad never appears in verb form in the Qur'an whereas qalb does, and fu'ad is never paired with terms for thinking (’aql, عقل; tadabbur, تدبر; tafaqquh, تفقه), whereas that is frequently the case for qalb.25 This pattern supports the reading that fu'ad conveys the same meaning as qalb in the domain of conscience and feelings, but not in the domain of rational thought.

Al-lubb (اللب): the purified intellect

The third term, al-lubb (اللب), from the root l-b-b (ل-ب-ب), appears 16 times in the Qur'an,10 exclusively in its irregular plural form al-albab (الألباب) and always within the compound ulu al-albab (أولو الألباب), “people of understanding” or “people of pure intellect”25 Ibn Manzur defined lubb in two senses: al-’aql (العقل), “the intellect,” and khulasat al-shai’ wa haqiqatuhu (خلاصة الشيء وحقيقته), “the essence and reality of a thing”.21 Ibn Faris traced the root to two principles: luzum wa thabat (لزوم وثبات), “persistence and firmness,” and khulus wa jawda (خلوص وجودة), “purity and quality,” adding that: “the intellect was called lubb for that reason”.20 Classical lexicographers further defined lubb as al-’aql al-khalis min al-shawa'ib (العقل الخالص من الشوائب), “a mind clear and clean from all defilement”,29 adding that: “every lubb is a form of ’aql (عقل), but not every ’aql is a lubb; only pure minds achieve lubb.” In other words, lubb represents not just any reasoning capacity but the highest, most purified form of cardiac intelligence. The primary semantic field of lubb in classical Arabic lexicography centers on the kernel, pith, or pure essence of a thing, and by extension, the purified core of intellect (khalis al-ʿaql) as distinguished from its surface or admixed forms. Why does it appear only in the plural? It has been argued that the irregular plural (jama’ taksir, تكسير جمع) conveys a combination of dynamism and consistency.25 The form itself changes from its singular pattern. However, the meaning stays constant, mirroring the quality of a mind that adapts to circumstances while remaining rooted in principle. What makes lubb especially interesting for this review is the accompanying vocabulary. This is a vocabulary of spiritual-intellectual integration rather than abstract reasoning alone, as in: "[This is] a blessed Book which We have revealed to you, [O Muhammad], that they might reflect upon its verses and that those of understanding (ulu al-albab, أولو الألباب) would be reminded” (38:29). Thus, the Qur'anic corpus presents lubb as a faculty in which thought, remembrance of God, piety, and reflective contemplation operate together rather than as separate cognitive acts. Classical exegetes, including al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, and al-Razi, gloss ulu al-albab as those whose intellects are purified of caprice and oriented toward right understanding.

The Qur'anic text consistently locates the faculty of understanding (ya'qiluna) in the qalb (Q 22:46; Q 7:179), and the mainstream classical Muslim (Sunni) tradition, including al-Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyyah, and Ibn al-Qayyim, accordingly identifying the heart as the seat of intellect. The minority view associating cognition primarily with the brain came from the Islamicate philosophical–medical tradition following Galen and Ibn Sina, and was contested by scholars working from the Qur'anic text. The present review follows the Qur'anic localization without entering the philosophical–medical debate.

The Qur'anic cardiac vocabulary, particularly lubb, was elaborated in the writings of classical Muslim scholars. Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim treated the heart's faculties in works such as Majmu' al-Fatawa, Ighathat al-Lahfan, and Madarij al-Salikin, presenting the purified intellect as operating through the qalb in concert with dhikr, taqwa, and adherence to revelation.9,30 These readings extend, rather than directly derive from, the primary Qur'anic and lexicographic material, and are cited here as scholarly elaborations within the tradition.

Al-sadr (الصدر): the container of the heart

The fourth major term is al-sadr (الصدر), from the root s-d-r (ص-د-ر), literally “the chest” or “the breast,” which appears ∼44 times in the Qur'an in forms referencing the human inner state.10,25 Ibn Faris identified two original senses for this root: khilaf al-wird (خلاف الورد), “the opposite of arriving at water,” i.e., returning or issuing forth, and sadr al-insan (صدر الإنسان), “the human chest”.20 Ibn Manzur elaborated that sadr means a'la muqaddam kulli shai’ wa awwaluhu (أعلى مقدم كل شيء وأوله), “the highest front part of everything and its beginning”,21 and noted regarding the Qur'anic phrase “the hearts in the chests” (22:46) that al-qalb la yakun illa fi al-sadr (القلب لا يكون إلا في الصدر), “the heart is only located in the sadr”.21,25

The Qur'an uses sadr in two distinct grammatical patterns, and the distinction matters. In 16 occurrences, sadr (صدر) appears as a subject (fa'il, فاعل) or object (maf'ul, مفعول), as in: “So whoever Allah wants to guide – He expands his breast (sadrahu, صدره) to [contain] Islam” (6:125) and “Did We not expand for you, [O Muhammad], your breast (sadraka, صدرك)?” (94:1). In these cases, sadr functions metonymically, the container standing for the contained: what is expanded, constricted, or healed is ultimately the heart within the breast. In another 28 occurrences, sadr follows a preposition (fi al-sudur, الصدور في, “in the breasts”) or serves as mudhaf ilayh (مضاف إليه) (dhat al-sudur, ذات الصدور, “that which is in the breasts”), as in: “For indeed, it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts. (fi al-sudur, في الصدور)” (22:46).25 This construction is particularly important: by placing qulub (قلوب, hearts) explicitly inside sudur (صدور, breasts), the Qur'an anchors its cardiac psychology in the physical chest, not the cranium.

The distinction between sadr and qalb can be understood through a spatial metaphor: sadr is the outer container, the threshold between the visible body and the invisible heart, whereas qalb is the inner reality residing within it. When the Qur'an says that Satan “whispers [evil] into the breasts (sudur, صدور) of mankind” (114:5), the implication is that temptation operates at the periphery of the heart, in the sadr, rather than penetrating directly to the qalb itself. God alone knows what the sadr conceals: “Indeed, He (Allah) is Knowing of that within the breasts” (11:5) and “He is Knowing of that within the breasts” (35:38), because the sadr conceals motives, intentions, and secrets that may not yet have reached the heart's settled conviction.

Al-nafs (النفس): the motivating self

The four terms analyzed above constitute the Qur'an's core cardiac vocabulary. A fifth term, al-nafs (النفس), from the root n-f-s (ن-ف-س), is not itself a cardiac synonym, but no account of the Qur'anic heart can be complete without it: nafs designates the motivating self whose moral orientation determines whether the qalb remains sound or falls into disease. It is included here not as a cardiac term per se but as the animating force without which the cardiac system described above would lack its driving mechanism. Ibn Manzur recorded that al-nafs carries two primary senses: al-ruh (الروح), “the spirit,” and jumlat al-shai’ wa haqiqatuhu (جملة الشيء وحقيقته), “the entirety and reality of a thing”.21 He also preserved a further distinction: “the ruh is that by which there is life, and the nafs is that by which there is intellect” (al-ruh alladhi bihi al-hayah, wa al-nafs allathi biha al-’aql, الروح الذي به الحياة، والنفس التي بها العقل).21 Ibn Faris placed the root under a single principle: khuruj al-nasim (خروج النسيم), “the issuing forth of breath,” whether of wind or of something else.20 Classical scholars identified al-nafs as the force that drives the qalb (قلب) toward good or evil.25 Al-Ghazali distinguished two meanings: nafs (نفس) as the seat of anger and desire that pushes toward reprehensible acts, and nafs as the subtle spiritual essence that is the true self. In its second meaning, when controlled by reason and the light of faith, it becomes al-nafs al-mutma'inna (النفس المطمئنة, the tranquil soul); when still in conflict with desire, it remains al-nafs al-lawwama (النفس اللوامة, the self-blaming soul).25,31,32

What matters in the present discussion is the dynamic between nafs and qalb: the nafs exerts a pull, and the qalb either yields or resists. If the qalb inclines toward obedience (taqwa, تقوى), the whole person is sound; if it follows the appetites of nafs, corruption spreads through body and conduct alike, a dynamic captured in the Hadith: “Indeed, in the body there is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Truly, it is the heart” (SB 52; SM 1599).11,12

Synthesis of the Qur'anic cardiac vocabulary

Taken together, these five terms compose what amounts to a Qur'anic cardiac anthropology. The qalb (قلب) is the dynamic spiritual center that turns between states, fu'ad (فؤاد) is the intense emotional–perceptual conscience, lubb (لب) is the purified intellect operating through remembrance and reflection, sadr (صدر) is the bodily container that mediates between the visible and the invisible, and nafs (نفس) is the motivating force that pushes the qalb in one direction or the other. No single English word, “heart,” captures this architecture. Thus, the Quran does not treat the heart as a metaphor for feelings in a vaguely poetic sense, but instead it presents a differentiated model where distinct cardiac faculties serve distinct functions, ranging from emotion to intellect to moral agency to spiritual perception, and all housed not in the brain but in the chest.

The heart's functions in the Qur'an

This section examines how the Qur'an assigns specific functions to the heart across six thematic domains. Many of the verses cited below use the terms analyzed in Section “The Qur'anic vocabulary of the heart and its inner dimensions”, and our aim is not to revisit vocabulary but to map the functional roles that the Qur'an attributes to the cardiac center.

The heart as the seat of reason and understanding

We noted above that the qalb is the locus of reasoning (22:46) and that those who fail to use it are likened in the Qur'an to cattle (al-an'am الأنعام), or even worse: “They have hearts with which they do not understand … Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray” (7:179). The Qur'an extends this further by making the heart's reasoning conditional on attentiveness: “Indeed in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart or who listens while he is present [in mind]" (50:37). According to al-Razi's reading in Mafatih al-Ghayb, the word qalb in verse 7:179 denotes not the physical organ but the spiritual and intellectual faculty capable of understanding, thinking, and reflecting.33 The Qur'an, as we understand, treats the heart as the organ of comprehension, but only when it is active and receptive. When it is not, the Qur'an warns that: “They have hearts with which they do not understand … ” (7:179). In other words, the heart's intellectual function is not automatic, but instead it must be consciously engaged, for a sealed or heedless heart loses its capacity to grasp divine guidance.34

The heart as the locus of faith and tranquility

Faith, in Qur'anic usage, does not reside in verbal profession but in the heart. The Qur'an states that faith “has not yet entered your hearts” among those who merely claim belief (49:14). Beyond initial faith, the Qur'an describes how God strengthens the hearts of believers: “It is He who sent down tranquility into the hearts of the believers that they would increase in faith along with their [present] faith” (48:4). Similarly, after the Battle of Badr: “And Allah made it not but good tidings and so that your hearts would be assured thereby” (8:10). The Qur'an also describes God as inscribing faith directly into hearts: “Those, He has decreed within their hearts faith and supported them with spirit from Him” (58:22). Remembrance of God produces settled calm, as noted in verse 13:28. This pattern suggests that the heart is not simply a passive container for belief but an organ whose capacity for faith can grow, deepen, and be fortified through divine action and adherence to revelation, as developed at length by Ibn Taymiyyah in Majmu' al-Fatawa and Ibn al-Qayyim in Madarij al-Salikin.9,30

The heart as the seat of emotion

The Qur'an locates a range of emotional states in the heart. Fear and terror are cast into it: “We will cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve” (3:151). The Prophet's heart did not misperceive during the Night Journey and Ascension (al-Isra’ and al-Mi'raj الإسراء والمعراج): “The heart did not lie [about] what it saw” (53:11), indicating that the heart's perceptual–emotional faculty may reach a degree of certainty beyond ordinary cognition. The story of Prophet Moses' mother (28:10, analyzed in Section “The Qur'anic vocabulary of the heart and its inner dimensions”) illustrates how the fu'ad can become “empty” with emotional intensity. These verses collectively portray the heart as the organ where emotions are not merely felt but carry epistemic weight, functioning as modes of perception rather than irrational reactions.25,34

Disease, sealing, and hardening of the heart

The Qur'an repeatedly describes hearts that become spiritually dysfunctional. Disease (marad مرض) and the condition worsen through persistent disbelief: “But as for those in whose hearts is disease, it has [only] increased them in evil [in addition] to their evil” (9:125). A distinct pathology is “ran,” a stain or rust that accumulates from sin: “No! Rather, the stain has covered their hearts of that which they were earning” (83:14). Hardening follows prolonged spiritual neglect: “Has the time not come for those who have believed that their hearts should become humbly submissive at the remembrance of Allah and what has come down of the truth? And let them not be like those who were given the Scripture before, and a long period passed over them, so their hearts hardened” (57:16; cf. 2:74 in Section “Al-Qalb (القلب): The dynamic spiritual center”). The most severe condition is sealing, expressed through two near-synonymous roots: khatm (ختم), to place a seal so that nothing enters or exits, and tab' (طبع), to stamp or imprint permanently. God “seals over every heart [belonging to] an arrogant tyrant” (40:35). The same act is described using tab' for those who inherit the earth but fail to learn from their predecessors' fate: “But We seal over their hearts so they do not hear” (7:100). Elsewhere, the two roots converge in a single portrait of spiritual ruin: “Have you seen he who has taken as his god his own desire, and Allah has sent him astray due to knowledge and has set a seal upon his hearing and his heart and put over his vision a veil?” (45:23). The foundational instance, “Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and over their sight is a veil” (2:7), is noted in Section “Al-Qalb (القلب): The dynamic spiritual center”.

Moral accountability and the sound heart

The Qur'an establishes the heart's condition as the criterion for salvation on the Day of Judgment. Prophet Ibrahim's prayer includes: “And do not disgrace me on the Day they are [all] resurrected, the Day when there will not benefit [anyone] wealth or children, but only one who comes to Allah with a sound heart” (26:87–89). The term qalb salim (قلب سليم, sound heart) has been interpreted by classical Qur'anic commentators as a heart free of polytheism (shirk), hypocrite (nifaq), and doubt in God's oneness and the resurrection.35,36 Thus, the Qur'an makes the heart's moral state the ultimate measure of a person's worth, not wealth, lineage, or social standing.

Divine sovereignty over the heart

Perhaps the most distinctive Qur'anic teaching about the heart is that God intervenes directly in its states. The Qur'an declares that: “And know that Allah intervenes between a man and his heart” (8:24). This divine sovereignty operates in multiple directions. God may guide: “Our Lord, let not our hearts deviate after You have guided us” (3:8), a supplication that presupposes the heart can turn away even after receiving guidance. God may also cause deviation as a consequence of willful disobedience: “And when they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate” (61:5). This framework positions the heart as the theater of an ongoing interaction between divine will and human moral agency.26

The heart in Sahih hadith

The Prophetic traditions reinforce and extend the Qur'anic framework by providing descriptions of how the heart functions, deteriorates, and is restored. The Qur'an establishes principles and the authenticated narrations provide what amounts to a practical theology of the heart.

The foundational hadith, quoted in the Introduction, identifies the heart as a physical piece of flesh (mudgha, مضغة) whose condition governs the entire body (SB 52; SM 1599).11,12 The word mudgha, literally “a morsel of chewed flesh,” is the same as the term used in the Qur'an for the embryo at an early developmental stage (23:14). By specifying a physical tissue and then declaring it the organ on which everything depends, the Prophet collapses the distance between the biological organ and the spiritual center.26,34,37

A second cluster of narrations establishes divine sovereignty over the heart. The Prophet's most frequent supplication was: “O Changer of the hearts (ya Muqallib al-qulub), make my heart firm upon Your religion” (Tirmidhi 2140).13 When his companions expressed surprise that even he needed such a prayer, he replied: “Indeed the hearts are between Two Fingers of the fingers of Allah; He turns them as He wills” (SM 2655).12 These texts present the heart not as a stable, self-governing organ but as one subject to constant divine action. Classical Sunni scholarship has interpreted these narrations through the principle of affirming the textual meaning while consigning the modality to divine knowledge (ithbat bi-la kayf), so that the expression is understood as a statement of continuous divine disposal over the human heart, not as an inference about anatomical or physical form.9,38

A third theme concerns God's criterion of judgment. The Prophet said: “Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth, but He looks to your hearts and to your deeds” (SM 2564c).12 This hadith inverts the social values of outward appearance and material success, relocating the locus of divine evaluation to the interior, which aligns with the Qur'anic concept of qalb salim (the sound/healthy heart) as the sole currency of salvation (26:89). The Prophet warned that: “No one who has the weight of a mustard seed of arrogance in his heart shall enter Paradise” (Muslim 91a).12 In this case, arrogance is not defined as self-confidence but as “disdaining the truth and contempt for the people” (SM 91a), a definition that locates the vice squarely in the heart's orientation rather than in external behavior.

The narration uses concrete sensory imagery to describe a spiritual and moral process: sin marks the heart with a black spot (nuktah sawda'), repentance polishes the heart clean, and persistent sin accumulates into the ran of Qur'an 83:14: “If he refrains, seeks forgiveness, and repents, his heart is polished clean. But if he returns, it increases until it covers his entire heart. And that is the ‘ran’ which Allah mentioned: ‘Nay, but on their hearts is the ran which they used to earn'" (Tirmidhi 3334; cf. Qur'an 83:14).13 The language is strikingly physical: sin produces a visible stain (nuktah sawda' نُكْتَةٌ سَوْدَاءُ), and repentance polishes it away.

A further narration defines Islam itself through the heart: “A Muslim is he from whose hand and tongue the Muslims are safe” (SM 41).12 Here, submission (Islam) is not reduced to ritual observance, but instead its primary locus is the heart's posture of surrender, from which safe conduct toward others then flows outward.

Taken together, these Prophetic narrations present a coherent account. The heart is a physical organ whose spiritual condition governs the health of the entire person. It is subject to divine turning, evaluated by God according to its inner states rather than outward appearances, susceptible to progressive staining through sin, and restorable through repentance and remembrance. This account provides the theological foundation upon which later Islamic scholars and, as argued in the remaining sections of this review, modern neurocardiology have built their respective understandings of the heart's significance.

The heart in Arab-Islamic medical and philosophical thought

Between the ninth and thirteenth centuries, Arab-Islamic physicians and philosophers explored the heart as both an anatomical organ and a center of cognition, emotion, and spiritual life. What distinguished their work was a refusal to separate the physical heart from its spiritual counterpart, where understanding one required understanding the other.

An early figure in this tradition was Abu Zayd al-Balkhi (849–934 CE), whose treatise (Masalih al-Abdan wa al-Anfus مصالح الأبدان والأنفس) (Sustenance of the Body and the Soul) systematically linked emotional disturbances to somatic illness. Al-Balkhi classified psychological conditions into categories that bear a striking resemblance to modern diagnostic frameworks, identifying states analogous to depression (al-huzn الحزن), anxiety (al-jaza’ الجزع), obsessive rumination (al-waswasah الوسوسة), and phobia (al-faza’ الفزع). He argued that physical and psychological ailments require parallel treatment, positioning the heart as the meeting point of body and soul, and anticipating the core principles of cognitive behavioral therapy by over a millennium.39 His work laid the groundwork for later scholars who would locate the body–soul interaction specifically in the heart.40

Abu Bakr al-Razi (865–925 CE), known in the West as Rhazes, made the heart's role in this interaction more explicit. In his al-Tibb al-Ruhani الطب الروحاني (The Spiritual Physick), al-Razi proposed that the rational soul governs the body through the intellect and that moral discipline is essential to both spiritual and bodily well-being.41,42 He advocated a therapeutic model in which reason governs the passions and moral discipline restores the harmony of soul and body. Clinically, he linked cardiac function directly to emotional states, noting that sudden death occurs when the heart contracts but fails to relax, an early recognition of what we now understand as stress-induced lethal arrhythmia.43,44

Ibn Sina (980–1037 CE), known as Avicenna, made foundational contributions to cardiology through both his al-Qanun fi al-Tibb القانون في الطب (The Canon of Medicine) and a dedicated treatise, Kitab al-Adwiyya al-Qalbiyya كتاب الأدوية القلبية (The Book on Drugs for Cardiac Diseases).45 Ibn Sina explicitly stated that “the heart is the origin of vital spirit” and “itself is the source of emotional states such as happiness, grief, fear, and anger”.45 He observed that emotional conditions cause alterations in the pulse, noting irregularity “especially after an outburst of violent emotion,” making him a pioneer in psychosomatic medicine.46,47 In his eleventh century framework, Ibn Sina further argued that cardiac remedies improve memory and mental function, and that this effect reflected a direct role for the heart in cognition.45 From a contemporary biomedical standpoint, any cognitive benefit of cardiac therapeutics is commonly attributed to improved cardiovascular efficiency and cerebral perfusion, although the possibility of additional cardiac contributions to cognition remains an open question explored later in this review; and the citation here is offered as a historical position rather than a current mechanistic claim. Beyond pharmacology, Ibn Sina's clinical judgment extended to psychosomatic cardiology: he famously diagnosed lovesickness by observing changes in pulse rate when the names of the beloved and associated locations were recited to the patient, a method that anticipated the word-association test later attributed to Carl Jung.46, 47, 48

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111 CE) offered the most comprehensive philosophical and spiritual account of the heart in the Islamic scholarly tradition. In Book 21 of his Ihya’ ’Ulum al-Din, titled Kitab Sharh ’Aja'ib al-Qalb عجائب القلب(The Marvels of the Heart), al-Ghazali distinguished between two meanings of the word qalb: the physical organ that pumps blood, belonging to the visible world; and a “subtle spiritual substance” (latifa rabbaniyya ruhaniyya لطيفة ربّانية روحانية) that is connected to the physical organ yet constitutes the true essence of the human being, the entity that “perceives and knows and experiences; it is addressed, punished, rebuked, and held responsible”.31 Central to al-Ghazali's account is the metaphor of the heart as a mirror. Just as a polished mirror reflects objects clearly, when purified through obedience and moral discipline, the heart reflects divine knowledge, whereas sins act as rust that obscures its reflective capacity. He identified five obstacles that can prevent this reflection: immaturity, disobedience that tarnishes the mirror, failure to turn the heart toward truth, uncritical acceptance of dogma without understanding, and ignorance of where to direct one's attention.31 Al-Ghazali also proposed that the heart receives knowledge through two doors: an “outer door” of sense perception through which intellectual knowledge enters via study and rational investigation, and an “inner door” of inspiration. He compared intellectual knowledge to food and inspired knowledge to medicine, arguing that neither suffices alone.31 His classification of the heart's faculties comprising appetite, anger, impulse, apprehension, intellect, and will, provided a comprehensive taxonomy of human motivation and moral psychology that continues to inform Islamic counseling and therapy.31,40

These philosophical and spiritual investigations dominated much of Islamic scholarly attention to the heart, but the tradition also produced major empirical advances. Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288 CE) correctly described the pulmonary circulation, demonstrating that blood passes from the right ventricle to the lungs for aeration and then returns to the left ventricle, directly contradicting Galen's long-held theory that blood passes through invisible pores in the interventricular septum.14,49 In his physiology, the heart was the organ responsible for continuously generating the spirit (ruh), a subtle substance that served as the material carrier of the soul's faculties throughout the body. The spirit received what Ibn al-Nafis called the “faculty of animation” (quwwat al-hayah قوة الحياة) from the soul while still in the heart, and could only carry the higher faculties of sensation, perception, and cognition to the rest of the body after traveling to the brain.49 In other words, every psychic act, from feeling to reasoning, depended on a spirit that originated in the heart. Ibn al-Nafis did not view his physiological work as incompatible with the heart's broader significance, and in his theological writings, he maintained that the heart served as the seat of the soul and the organ through which divine knowledge could be received.49

Thus, the Arab-Islamic scholarly tradition provided a uniquely integrated understanding of the heart as a site of physiological inquiry and the seat of the soul, a vital organ, and a spiritual organ, subject simultaneously to the laws of anatomy and the laws of God. This integration stands in marked contrast to the post-Cartesian Western separation of body and soul, where the heart was progressively stripped of its cognitive and emotional associations, and reduced to a mechanical pump by the nineteenth century.50 Whether this earlier, more integrated view has anything to contribute to contemporary science is the question addressed in the remainder of this review.

The heart in contemporary science

In the following sections, we survey seven lines of contemporary scientific evidence, each revealing a non-mechanical function of the heart that resonates with one or more of those descriptions. The heart's intrinsic nervous system: the “little brain” on the heart

The Qur'an ascribes perception and discernment to the heart: “it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts” (22:46), and the Prophet (PBUH) placed the condition of the entire body under the condition of the heart (SB 52; SM 1599). For most of the modern period, such statements were read as purely metaphorical. However, neurocardiology research has revealed that the heart does actually possess its own neural processing system, and one that is sufficiently complex to be called “the little brain on the heart”.51

The human heart contains an intrinsic cardiac nervous system (ICNS) of roughly 14,000–94,000 neurons, grouped in ganglionated plexuses on the heart's surface.52, 53, 54 This network includes sensory neurons, processing interneurons, and motor neurons, which are all the elements needed for local information processing.51 The ICNS coordinates beat-to-beat cardiac function through rapid reflexes, with little reliance on the central nervous system.51,55,56

Can this cardiac network function without central neural input? The supporting evidence is striking. Transplanted hearts, entirely denervated, still exhibit autonomous neural regulation through their intrinsic ganglia.57 The ICNS “is even capable of reflex control of regional cardiac function in the absence of all higher elements”.58 Rather than merely relaying signals from the brain, this network actively processes, integrates, and responds to cardiac information on its own, exhibiting what has been termed “neuronal plasticity and memory capacity”.57 The terms processing, integration, and memory capacity are used here in the neurocardiological sense established by Giannino et al.57 to denote local neural plasticity and pattern retention within cardiac ganglia, and the capacity of the ICNS to modulate its output in response to recurrent inputs, rather than higher-order cognitive memory or executive cognition, which remain functions of the central nervous system57

Recent molecular research has shown that these cardiac neurons are not a uniform population. In a preprint that has not yet undergone peer review, Xu et al.59 used single-cell RNA sequencing in mice to identify two distinct neuron subtypes within the ICNS: one essential for baseline heart function and another that appears to protect against stress-induced sudden death. When the investigators selectively ablated the stress-protective subtype, 63% of the animals died of sudden cardiac arrest within minutes of stress exposure, whereas activating these neurons prevented such deaths.59 If confirmed in a peer-reviewed publication and extended to human cardiac tissue, these findings would have significant implications for sudden death prevention. The data obtained by Xu et al.59 document a survival-protective neuronal subtype within the ICNS that modulates the cardiac stress response in mice, and these findings suggest functional specialization among cardiac neurons rather than a cognitive capacity of the heart, and we cite this study as evidence of structural heterogeneity within the ICNS rather than for cardiac cognition. This division of labor within the ICNS resembles the specialized circuits found in the brain.

However, is the heart's nervous system as versatile as the brain's? It is not. Brain neurons employ synaptic plasticity extensively for learning and memory, whereas ICNS neurons prioritize stability and direct cardiac control.60,61 Nevertheless, the parallel is closer than anyone anticipated a generation ago. The heart contains a structured neural network capable of sensing, integrating, and acting on local cardiac information without central neural input.57,58 This contemporary description of the organ is consonant with, rather than derived from, the Qur'anic statement that the hearts within the breasts may “reason” or be “blinded” (22:46), and the Prophetic statement that the soundness of the body follows the soundness of the heart (SB 52; SM 1599); the consonance is offered as an observation and not as a textual prediction of modern neuroanatomy.

Bidirectional heart–brain communication: the heart speaks to the brain

The Qur'an states: “O you who believe, respond to Allah and to the Messenger when he calls you to that which gives you life, and know that Allah intervenes between a person and his heart” (8:24). This verse presents the heart not as a passive recipient of external commands but as a center with its own activity and inclinations, one in which God Himself intervenes. The heart possesses its own tendencies that may require redirecting. Modern neurocardiology offers what may be a physiological correlate of this idea: the heart communicates with the brain at least as much as the brain communicates with the heart in specific contexts that have been experimentally examined, and cardiac signals contribute to perception, emotion, and cognition before conscious awareness, where the precise directional balance varies by state and condition.

How much of the neural traffic between the heart and brain actually runs upward? The majority of fibers traveling in the cervical vagus nerve are afferent, carrying information from the viscera to the brain. In cats, approximately 80% of vagal fibers are sensory,62 and studies in other species suggest a similar predominance, with 60–80% of cervical vagal fibers estimated as afferent C fibers.61,63 Thus, the heart sends far more signals to the brain than it receives.61 These afferent signals ascend through brainstem relay nuclei to the insular cortex, which is now recognized as the central hub of the heart–brain axis.67,68 The specific pathways are discussed further in Section “Cardiac interoception: The heart shapes consciousness and emotion”.

The idea that bodily and cardiac changes precede and shape felt emotion is not new. William James (1884) and Carl Lange (1885) independently proposed what became known as the James–Lange theory of emotion, where the perception of bodily changes, including changes in the heart and the viscera, constitutes the felt emotion itself rather than being its consequence.64,65 Cannon65 challenged this theory based on animal and clinical evidence, arguing that emotional feeling could persist after visceral input was severed, and Cannon's65 critique displaced the theory throughout much of the twentieth century. Contemporary neurocardiology based on non-invasive recordings of cardiac and cortical signals and computational modeling of directed coupling now allows the original claim to be tested with methods that were not available to either James64 or Cannon,65 and recent findings have reopened the question on substantially firmer empirical ground.

What happens once cardiac signals reach the cortex? Using a computational model of brain-heart interplay applied to electroencephalogram (EEG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals from 62 healthy subjects undergoing emotional stimulation via video, the cardiac sympathovagal activity was found to “play a leading and causal role in initiating the emotional response,” with ascending vagal modulations preceding neural dynamics and correlating with the reported level of emotional arousal.66 The heart's physiological response occurred first and then shaped the brain's processing of the emotional experience. This is not the classical model where the brain decides an emotion and then instructs the heart to race, but instead the heart's own rhythmic activity initiates and shapes the emotional cascade, which then unfolds through ongoing heart–brain coupling rather than through the heart in isolation. This field has been described under the concepts of “interoception and cardioception,” where the brain continuously monitors cardiac signals as a basis for emotional and cognitive regulation.67

The clinical consequences of this bidirectional axis can be dramatic. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, sometimes called “broken heart syndrome,” illustrates how emotional processing in the brain can physically damage the heart. An acute emotional shock, such as grief, terror, or neurological catastrophe, triggers a catecholamine surge that stuns the left ventricular apex, producing a characteristic ballooning pattern on echocardiography.67,68 Takotsubo syndrome has been described explicitly as “a disorder of the heart–brain axis”.68

Catecholamine biology adds a further layer to this dialogue. A distinct population of intrinsic cardiac adrenergic (ICA) cells resides within the cardiac parenchyma of rodent and human hearts, and carries the full enzymatic machinery required for catecholamine biosynthesis. ICA cells constitutively release norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine, which act locally on the cardiomyocyte rate and contractility, independent of extrinsic sympathetic input, and the system is dynamically regulated by the hormonal milieu of psychological stress.69, 70, 71 Thus, the heart does not only receive catecholamine signals from the brain and adrenal medulla during stress, but instead it also generates them locally, reinforcing the idea of the heart as an active participant in the physiology of arousal and emotion rather than a downstream effector.

The reverse direction is equally consequential. Heart failure elevates the risk of Alzheimer's disease by 53–84%, and atrial fibrillation increases the risk of stroke more than fivefold.72 Up to one-third of Alzheimer-related dementias may be attributable to cardiovascular risk profiles.72 The mechanism appears to involve cerebral hypoperfusion, with reductions in blood flow preceding brain atrophy.72

In Parkinson's disease, a “body-first” subtype has been described where pathology propagates from peripheral organs via the vagus nerve to the brainstem, suggesting that heart-to-brain communication can serve as a route for disease transmission.73,74 Depression and cardiovascular disease also share a bidirectional causal relationship: sympathetic overactivation drives both conditions through shared inflammatory pathways, including elevated levels of C-reactive protein, TNF-alpha, and interleukin-6. In addition, vagus nerve stimulation has been shown to have therapeutic benefits for both heart failure and depression.67,75

This emerging picture challenges the modern assumption that the heart is merely a pump awaiting instructions from the brain, but instead it is a participant in a continuous bidirectional dialogue, where cardiac signals shape how we feel, what we perceive, and even whether we develop neurodegenerative disease. The Qur'an positions the heart at the center of this dialogue as the organ where God intervenes in the human person (8:24). Whether this positioning reflects a physiological insight, a theological claim operating at a different level of description, or both, remains an open question. The convergences described above invite further interdisciplinary research into how classical descriptions of the heart and contemporary findings on heart–brain communication are related, while leaving the level of correspondence between the two open to continued inquiry.

The heart as an endocrine organ: hormones of bonding and tranquility

The Qur'an describes the heart as the locus of tranquility and love. “Those who believe, and whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of Allah, Verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest” (13:28). Elsewhere, God declares that He “sent down As-Sakinah (calmness and tranquility) into the hearts of the believers, that they may grow more in Faith along with their (present) Faith” (48:4). The Arabic term sakina (السكينة) (from the root s-k-n, meaning to be still or to dwell) denotes a deep, settled peace that God places directly into the cardiac center. These are not incidental references because they position the heart as the organ through which the human being experiences rest, bonding with the divine, and the quieting of distress. Modern cardiac endocrinology has shown that the heart contributes to the production and release of biochemical mediators that participate in the regulation of these states as part of a broader brain–heart–endocrine network.

In the late 1990s, Gutkowska et al.76 demonstrated the presence of functional oxytocin receptors in cardiac tissue. Jankowski et al.77 then showed that the rat heart itself synthesizes oxytocin by detecting oxytocin mRNA in every chamber, with the highest peptide concentrations in the right atrium. The cardiac oxytocin/oxytocin receptor system was subsequently characterized in human tissue. Thibonnier et al.78 demonstrated that human vascular endothelial cells from the umbilical vein, aorta, and pulmonary artery express functional oxytocin receptors that are structurally identical to those in the uterus and mammary gland. Across a series of subsequent studies, Gutkowska et al.79 and Jankowski et al.80 extended their findings to the human heart, where the oxytocin system, including the peptide and its receptor, were characterized in cardiac tissue and reviewed in detail. Therefore, the heart does not merely respond to oxytocin delivered from the brain, but instead cardiac and cardiovascular tissues contribute to local oxytocin availability.

Why does this matter? Oxytocin is widely recognized as the hormone of social bonding, surging during childbirth, breastfeeding, and close human contact. Cardiac oxytocin slows the heart rate, lowers blood pressure, and promotes parasympathetic tone,81 while also exerting cardioprotective effects such as reducing infarct size after ischemia and activating anti-inflammatory pathways.82 Thus, the heart contributes to the local availability of a hormone implicated in social bonding, parasympathetic activation, and cardioprotection, alongside the better characterized hypothalamic source.

The cardiac oxytocin system does not operate in isolation. When oxytocin activates its receptors in the atria, a primary downstream effect is the release of ANP.76 ANP is far more than a natriuretic peptide that regulates salt and water balance. ANP receptors are found in brain regions governing emotional states, including the amygdala and locus coeruleus, the brain's central norepinephrine hub. Intravenous ANP administration blocked experimentally induced panic attacks in patients with both panic disorder and healthy controls.83 ANP inhibited the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) stress axis, reducing cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone, while simultaneously dampening sympathetic activation. Patients with panic disorder had consistently lower basal ANP levels, and higher ANP concentrations in the nucleus tractus solitarius reduce sympathetic tone. Thus, a feedback loop emerges, where social bonding raises oxytocin, which triggers cardiac ANP release that acts on the brain to produce anxiolysis and suppress the stress response. Within this loop, the heart functions as an active endocrine node, releasing peptides that modulate central autonomic and stress-regulatory circuits, rather than serving as a passive downstream target.

The heart's endocrine repertoire extends beyond oxytocin and ANP. Cardiac myocytes possess the complete secretory apparatus of an endocrine cell,84 and the heart produces additional hormones, including growth differentiation factor-15, which signal to the brain and distant organs.85 By any standard definition, the heart functions as an endocrine organ.

The link between cardiac hormones and emotional life becomes clearer when considering the cardiovascular effects of social bonding. Higher plasma oxytocin levels are associated with lower blood pressure and reduced cardiovascular stress reactivity,86 and the frequency of partner hugs partially mediates this relationship.87 The connection between cardiac oxytocin, vagal tone, and emotional well-being is discussed further in Section “HRV, emotion regulation, and decision making”.

What emerges from this research is a picture of the heart as an organ that does not merely pump blood but that also actively produces hormones linked to calm, bonding, and stress relief, and distributes them to the brain, kidneys, and vasculature. When the Qur'an states that hearts find rest in the remembrance of God (13:28) and that tranquility is sent down into hearts (48:4), the physiological resonance is difficult to ignore. At a biochemical level, the heart participates in pathways closely associated with the affective states attributed to it in the Qur'anic text. This observation does not settle the theological question or assign exclusive causal primacy to the heart because the central nervous system remains the principal site of emotional integration. However, it does suggest that the Qur'anic language need not be read as mere poetic convention since modern cardiac endocrinology has now identified physiological pathways relevant to the states that the text attributes to the heart.

A small but growing clinical literature has examined whether the practices that the Qur'anic text links to cardiac rest, particularly dhikr and recitation or listening to the Qur'an, produce measurable effects on autonomic and affective markers. A systematic review from 2022 of 20 studies and 2566 participants reported reductions in anxiety, depression, and stress scores, improvements in sleep quality, and changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and salivary cortisol after listening to or memorizing Qur'anic passages, with effect sizes that varied widely across studies and outcome measures.88 At the mechanistic level, Surah Al-Rahman recitation in distressed diabetic patients increased right prefrontal alpha power, modulated heartbeat-evoked potentials, lowered systolic blood pressure, and shifted HRV, indicating integrated neurocardiac regulation.89 In addition, a multimodal EEG–ECG study of Salat found increased parasympathetic HRV indices and decreased sympathetic activity, where alpha power was positively correlated with high-frequency HRV.90 At the population level, a cross-sectional study of 400 older Saudi men found that higher levels of Qur'an memorization were associated with substantially lower adjusted prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and depression,91 and a systematic review of older adults from 2024 reported associations between regular Qur'an reading and preserved cognitive function.92 These studies were concentrated in Muslim-majority settings, varied in terms of design and sample size, and rarely included long-term follow-up, so their findings should be treated as preliminary support for an autonomic and affective effect rather than as established therapeutic claims. Within these limits, the data are consistent with the Qur'anic linkage of dhikr to cardiac rest (13:28) and suggest a candidate physiological pathway, i.e., modulation of autonomic and neuroendocrine tone, through which such practices may exert their reported effects.

Cardiac interoception: the heart shapes consciousness and emotion

The Qur'an presents the heart as a perceptive organ. “Verily, therein is indeed a reminder for him who has a heart or gives ear while he is heedful” (50:37), placing the heart alongside the ear as an organ that receives and comprehends. More strikingly, regarding the Prophet's experience during the Night Journey, the Qur'an states that: “The heart (fu'ad) did not lie [about] what it saw” (53:11), attributing to the heart a perceptive capacity so reliable that it could serve as a truthful witness.

Neuroscience research now suggests that the heart shapes what the brain perceives, feels, and knows through a process called “cardioception.” Every heartbeat sends a signal to the brain. When the ventricles contract during systole, baroreceptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinus fire sending ascending signals through the vagus nerve to the brainstem, and from there to the thalamus, insula, and cortex. The cortical imprint of this process is a measurable electrical response time-locked to each heartbeat, known as the heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP). Source localization and intracranial recordings have traced the HEP to the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal regions93,94 Direct intracranial EEG in patients confirmed that heartbeat-evoked responses arose primarily from the insular and opercular cortices, where the HEP amplitude was modulated during experimental manipulations of bodily self-consciousness.94 These findings establish the insula as a principal cortical site where cardiac signals are integrated into the experience of a unified, embodied self. People who perceive their heartbeats more accurately have higher HEP amplitudes in these regions, indicating that the brain's monitoring of cardiac activity varies considerably across individuals.95

Does this cardiac monitoring affect what we consciously experience? The evidence is increasingly affirmative. Fearful faces presented during cardiac systole (when baroreceptors fire) are detected more accurately, rated as more intense, and elicit stronger amygdala responses than those presented during diastole.96 Using continuous flash suppression, which renders stimuli invisible to conscious awareness, fearful faces timed to cortical systole broke through to consciousness faster than those presented during diastole, with no similar effect for neutral faces.97 Thus, the cardiac cycle selectively gates the brain's access to emotionally significant stimuli before conscious awareness. This gating extends to non-emotional perception. In magnetoencephalographic recordings, spontaneous pre-stimulus fluctuations in neural responses to heartbeats localized to the right inferior parietal lobule and ventral anterior cingulate cortex predicted whether a faint visual grating reached conscious detection.98 Thus, neural events locked to the heartbeat shape ordinary visual awareness, and not only emotional perception.

These findings have led to the proposal that cardiac sensing constitutes a distinct sensory modality called cardioception.99 With its own receptors, dedicated neural pathways, and behavioral consequences for arousal and decision making, cardioception may be as specific as vision or hearing. The heart, through its rhythmic contractions, generates a patterned sensory signal that the brain uses to calibrate emotional and cognitive processing moment by moment.99

The link between cardiac interoception and emotional experience is direct. Individuals with higher cardiac interoceptive accuracy report more intense emotional responses to both pleasant and unpleasant stimuli, with enhanced neural activation in the insula and anterior cingulate cortex during emotional processing.100 Higher interoceptive awareness also supports more effective emotion regulation, including greater downregulation of arousal during cognitive reappraisal.101 Thus, the heart's signal to the brain does not merely accompany emotion, but instead it shapes how intensely we feel and how effectively we regulate those feelings.

The Qur'an may offer the most vivid ancient account of this interaction with cardiac threat. Describing the Battle of the Trench, the verse states: “[Remember] when they came at you from above you and from below you, and when eyes shifted [in fear], and hearts reached the throats” (33:10). The Arabic phrase بَلَغَتِ القُلُوبُ الحَناجِرَ (balaghat al-qulub al-hanajir) situates the fear response in the cardiac organ itself and in its perceived upward displacement during acute distress. The Qur'anic phrasing describes the experienced sensation of intense fear, not a literal anatomical displacement of the heart; modern physiology, in turn, helps account for why human beings feel this way during acute threat. Modern physiology accounts for the mechanism: sympathetic activation surges catecholamines, sharply increasing the heart rate and contractile force, producing the pounding sensation people describe as the heart “rising” to the throat.102 Simultaneously, the sympathetic drive contracts the cricopharyngeal muscle, producing globus pharyngeus (a lump in the throat), the sensation that accompanies acute fear.103 These patients show reduced baroreflex sensitivity and decreased vagal modulation, suggesting a shared autonomic pathway linking cardiac pounding and throat constriction.103 Causal evidence that the heart can actively generate fear, rather than merely reflect it, comes from optogenetic pacing in mice, where artificially induced tachycardia alone produced anxiety-like behavior, but only in risky contexts, and this was mediated through the posterior insular cortex, which is the same region identified in cardioception research.104 What the Qur'an captures in a single phrase, “hearts reached the throats,” maps onto a documented physiological cascade where the heart participates in producing the actual experience of terror.

What happens when this cardiac interoceptive system breaks down? In major depression, patients show attenuated HEP amplitudes traced to reduced anterior cingulate cortex activity, and this weakened cardiac signal predicts a negative emotional bias toward sad facial expressions.105 In generalized anxiety disorder, normal modulation of the HEP amplitude between internally and externally directed attention is absent, and the severity of this deficit correlates with symptom severity.106

Across anxiety, depression, and psychosis, multiple dimensions of interoception have meaningful associations with psychiatric disturbance, although simple cardiac accuracy measures do not always correlate linearly with symptom severity in psychosis.107

Cardioception research suggests that the heart is not merely responding to the brain's emotional commands. It gates conscious access to emotional stimuli, shapes how intensely we feel, and, when its signals are disrupted, contributes to depression and anxiety. The Qur'an's assertion that revelation is “a reminder for whoever has a heart” (50:37) and that the Prophet's heart “lied not about what it saw” (53:11) may have seemed purely spiritual in earlier centuries. In light of this research, these statements resonate with a physiological reality: in a measurable sense, the heart is an organ of perception whose signals determine the quality and accuracy of inner experience.

HRV, emotion regulation, and decision making

The heart does not beat with mechanical regularity. Beat-to-beat intervals fluctuate continuously, and the pattern of these fluctuations, known as HRV, has emerged as one of the most informative biomarkers in psychophysiology.108,109 HRV reveals the heart as a physiologically dynamic organ whose rhythm is never truly fixed. This resonates with the Qur'anic and Prophetic portrayal of the heart as inherently changeable: the Prophet's most frequent supplication was “O Turner of hearts (ya Muqallib al-qulub), make my heart firm upon Your religion” (Tirmidhi 2140),13 reflecting an understanding that the heart's state shifts continually. In this section, we examine what contemporary science has learned about the relationship between the heart's rhythmic variability and the brain's capacity for emotional regulation, cognitive control, and decision making.

According to the neurovisceral integration model, vagally mediated HRV indexes the prefrontal cortex's tonic inhibitory control over subcortical threat-detection structures, particularly the amygdala, transmitted to the heart via the vagus nerve.110,111 Higher resting HRV reflects a greater prefrontal regulatory capacity and flexible autonomic system, whereas lower resting HRV suggests a system locked in defensive mode with a reduced capacity to modulate emotional responses.110,112 However, HRV is not specific to emotional or cognitive regulation. Advancing age is independently associated with reduced HRV across all standard time- and frequency-domain measures,113,114 and HRV also reflects primary cardiovascular disease, autonomic dysfunction, and medication effects, where antihypertensive drug classes produce differential changes in HRV indices.115 These confounders should be considered when interpreting HRV as a marker of regulatory capacity.

This framework has substantial empirical support. A systematic review of 15 studies involving 1051 healthy adults aged between 18 and 65 years found that higher vagally mediated HRV was consistently associated with better decision making, particularly under risk and uncertainty.116 More recently, Forte and Casagrande117 reported that higher resting vagal tone in healthy young adults was associated with superior performance in executive tasks, including cognitive control, motor and cognitive inhibition, cognitive flexibility, and working memory.

How might cardiorespiratory rhythmic activity influence brain function? High-amplitude heart rate oscillations at approximately 0.1 Hz, typically achieved by slow breathing at roughly six breaths per minute, may strengthen coupling between the left amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex, which is the circuit most consistently implicated in top-down emotional control.118 This hypothesis predicts that deliberately amplifying heart rate oscillations through biofeedback should produce lasting improvements in the brain's capacity to regulate emotion. This was confirmed by a randomized controlled trial of 106 young adults, where five weeks of daily HRV biofeedback designed to increase heart rate oscillations significantly increased amygdala–medial prefrontal cortex functional connectivity during rest, with no change in the control group.17 In the same cohort, this strengthened connectivity mediated a shift toward positive emotional memory, providing the first causal evidence that training the heart's rhythm can rewire the brain's emotional circuitry.119

A subsequent analysis of the same trial demonstrated that increases in resting HRV mediated reductions in negative emotions, and this mediation was specific to the group with trained heart rate oscillations.120 These findings extend the causal chain from heart rhythm training to brain network change to emotional well-being. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials further confirmed that HRV biofeedback improves both emotional and physical health outcomes across diverse populations.121

The Qur'an distinguishes between the qalb salim (sound heart) and the diseased heart (2:10), between hearts that are guided and hearts that deviate (3:8), between hearts open to understanding and hearts that have locks (47:24). HRV research offers one bodily window through which such descriptions may be partially appreciated, without claiming equivalence with their moral and spiritual meanings. In psychophysiological terms, higher resting variability is associated with greater autonomic flexibility and with capacities relevant to emotional regulation and adaptive cognition, whereas reduced variability is associated with autonomic rigidity and greater vulnerability to anxiety, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation. The science does not prove these Qur'anic categories, but it helps the reader appreciate their depth: the heart's physical state genuinely shapes the capacity to feel, think, and decide.

Heart transplant phenomena: does the heart carry memory and personality?

In the same context, few questions about the heart generate as much public curiosity as whether transplant recipients acquire personality traits from their donors. It should be stated at the outset that the evidence reviewed in this section is considerably weaker than that presented in Sections “The heart’s intrinsic nervous system: The “Little Brain” on the heart; Bidirectional heart–brain communication: The heart speaks to the brain; The heart as an endocrine organ: Hormones of bonding and tranquility; Cardiac interoception: The heart shapes consciousness and emotion; HRV, emotion regulation, and decision making”, and this section is included primarily to acknowledge a question of public and theological interest rather than to advance a scientific claim. Patients and families frequently raise this concern, and it has become a recurring theme in media and popular literature. The question also carries particular weight in light of the Qur'anic treatment of the heart as the seat of personality, moral character, and understanding. The Qur'an asks: “Do they not then reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?” (47:24), implying that each heart possesses its own capacity for comprehension, one that may be opened or sealed. The Sunnah (alhadith) similarly treats each heart as a unique, mutable entity. If the heart bears something of its owner's identity, what happens when it is placed in another person's chest?

The scientific evidence addressing this question remains limited and methodologically uneven, but the overall picture is worth presenting. In the only early consecutive clinical sample, 79% of 47 heart transplant recipients reported no personality change, 15% attributed changes to the life-threatening experience of surgery, and only 6% described shifts they linked to their new hearts, which the authors interpreted as incorporation fantasies.122 A later study of 10 recipients selected for openness to sharing personal changes reported 2–5 parallels per case between post-transplant behavior and donor history, proposing “cellular systemic memory” as an explanation.123 Despite its limitations, the study prompted an ongoing line of inquiry. A more recent survey comparing heart and non-heart organ recipients found similar rates of reported personality changes across both groups, suggesting that whatever drives these reports may not be unique to the heart.124 Biological mechanisms for cellular memory transfer have been proposed, including epigenetic and RNA-mediated pathways,125 but none have been demonstrated in human cardiac transplant recipients. In addition, well-established factors such as immunosuppressant drug effects,126 post-traumatic psychological growth, and the cultural symbolism of the heart may provide plausible alternative explanations. A scoping review of 21 studies found that mental representations about the donor occur in 6–52% of recipients and are associated with increased anxiety rather than accurate donor matching.127 According to a recent scoping review, the overall conclusion is that evidence on memory transfer remains “inconclusive” and calls for “rigorous interdisciplinary inquiry”.128

What can be stated with confidence is that heart transplantation does alter self-perception through well-understood physiological pathways. A longitudinal study that followed 13 heart transplant patients before surgery, at 4 months, and at 1 year found measurable deficits in interoceptive accuracy and reduced HEVs after transplantation, where the behavioral accuracy recovered by 1 year but neural markers remained diminished.129 The explanation is straightforward: transplantation interrupts the vagal afferent pathways that convey heart rate information to the brain, and recovery occurs through neuroplastic adaptation. It is also relevant that the heart's ICNS, described in Section “The heart in contemporary science”, survives transplantation and retains intrinsic function even after decentralization.57 Whether this preserved neural tissue carries experiential information from the donor is unknown, but it provides a biological substrate that keeps the question open.

Where does this leave the Islamic perspective? The Qur'an does not discuss organ transplantation, but its consistent portrayal of the heart as the bearer of personality, faith, and moral character suggests that the heart is more than interchangeable biological machinery. The Islamic tradition's insistence that each heart carries its owner's unique spiritual signature, and that the sound heart (qalb salim) is the one possession of ultimate value (26:89), resonates with the intuition behind these transplant reports, even as the scientific evidence remains far from settled. What science has confirmed is that replacing a heart measurably changes how a person perceives their own body, and that the transplanted heart brings with it a functioning neural network. These findings suggest that the heart's contribution to human identity may extend beyond mechanical pumping.

The heart's electromagnetic field: information beyond neural pathways

Can one person's heart influence another's? The question may sound fanciful, but it touches something deeply familiar. In everyday language, we speak of hearts “being in tune” or “beating as one.” The Qur'an also presents the heart as more than a private organ, where it describes hearts that are “brought together” (Qur'an 3:103). A well-known Prophetic hadith captures this relational quality with striking imagery: “Souls are troops collected together; those that recognized each other come together, and those that did not recognize each other differ” (SM 2638a; SB 3109).11,12 Classical scholars such as al-Nawawi and Ibn Hajar understood this as an innate affinity between the souls of similar natures, one that manifests only when people meet in person.38,130 It should be noted that the hadith refers to souls (arwah), not hearts (qulub). However, al-Ghazali held in the Ihya that ruh (soul) and qalb (heart), in their spiritual sense, denote the same subtle divine essence (latifah rabbaniyyah لطيفة ربّانيةّ), differing only in name.31 Ibn al-Qayyim, siding with the majority (jumhur), similarly held that such terms differ in attribute, not in essence.30 According to this reading, what is said of the soul's affinity applies equally to the heart. Over the past two decades, physiological research has begun to document something remarkably parallel: the synchronization of cardiac rhythms between interacting individuals.

The heart generates the strongest electromagnetic field in the body. Magnetocardiography recordings measure the cardiac magnetic field at roughly 50–100 picotesla at the chest surface, ∼100 times stronger than the brain's magnetic field detected by magnetoencephalography.131 It is sometimes claimed that this field “can be detected several feet away.” Technically, this is true, but only inside magnetically shielded rooms using superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs). At 1–2 m in an ordinary environment, the cardiac signal drops to the femtotesla range and far below ambient electromagnetic noise.131 The field is real and powerful, but its direct reach is limited.

However, the heart's capacity to synchronize with other hearts through identifiable physiological channels is not limited. A substantial body of research has shown that cardiac rhythms align between people during social interaction. Mothers and infants coordinate their heart rates within sub-second lags during face-to-face engagement, with increasing synchrony during vocal and affective exchanges.132 Romantic couples exhibit cross-partner heart rate coupling even when sitting together in silence, with no physical contact, and this coupling disappears entirely when individuals are randomly re-paired.133 During co-sleeping, adults synchronize their heart rhythms at roughly twice the rate observed while sleeping alone, probably through mechanical vibrations transmitted via the shared mattres.134 In group settings, cardiac synchrony among 204 participants predicted correct collective decisions with over 70% accuracy, outperforming self-reported measures.135 Shared cognitive processing of a common stimulus can synchronize hearts, brains, and eye movements simultaneously, modulated by attention and predictive of later memory recall.136 The mechanisms that underlie these findings are conventional: shared sensory input, respiratory entrainment, autonomic co-regulation, and mechanical coupling. However, no studies have demonstrated direct electromagnetic transmission between people. Nevertheless, the phenomenon where hearts fall into rhythm together is well replicated and functionally meaningful.

The hadith of “souls as troops collected together” speaks of an unseen recognition between souls that becomes apparent when people meet in the world. Cardiac synchronization research may suggest one physiological channel through which this affinity operates: a shared bodily resonance that emerges through proximity, attention, and interaction. What can be stated with confidence is that the heart generates a powerful field, hearts synchronize through identifiable mechanisms in social proximity, and this synchronization facilitates emotional connection and cognitive alignment. These are themes that resonate with the Qur'anic vision of hearts “brought together” by a wisdom beyond their own.

The pattern that emerges is not one of exact correspondence, but instead it is a pattern of consonance: independent disciplines converge on a portrait of the heart that is far more consistent with the Qur'anic-Prophetic account than with the pump model that has dominated biomedicine for the past three centuries.

How should this convergence be understood? Three interpretive errors must be avoided. The first is the claim, common in popular apologetics, that modern science “proves” the Qur'an, which commits a category error, subordinating revelation to the shifting conclusions of empirical research.137 The second is the opposite extreme: dismissing the Islamic view as “merely metaphorical.” The evidence reviewed here makes it difficult to sustain such a dismissal. The Qur'an does not locate the heart's functions in some abstract psychological space, but instead it places the heart “in the breasts (chests)” (22:46), and the Prophetic narration identifies it as a physical piece of flesh whose condition governs the entire body (SB 52; SM 1599). The Qur'an and Sunnah do not treat the heart as a dead metaphor; the physical organ is presented as a genuine participant in perception, understanding, and faith, a point developed at length by Ibn Taymiyyah in Majmu' al-Fatawa9 and by Ibn al-Qayyim in Ighathat al-Lahfan.7 The third error is to claim a one-to-one mapping between Qur'anic descriptions and specific scientific mechanisms. The Qur'an's account is irreducibly multi-dimensional, encompassing cognitive, emotional, moral, and spiritual functions within a theistic framework that includes divine sovereignty over the heart's states (Q 8:24; 61:5). Modern science, by methodological commitment, brackets questions of divine action. The two frameworks operate at different levels of description, but what they share is the recognition that the heart is far more than a pump.

A related objection deserves attention. Lakoff and Johnson138 argued that expressions linking emotion to bodily organs are conceptual metaphors rooted in repeated physical experience; saying that the heart “understands” or “fears” reflects how bodily experience shapes thought not actual cardiac function. The objection has force, but it rests on an assumption that may actually support the present argument: embodied metaphor theory assumes that such language tracks real bodily states. The heart does race during fear and slows during calm, and that is precisely why the metaphor works. The evidence reviewed in Section “The heart in contemporary science” suggests that this physiological basis extends deeper than the theory anticipates. The heart does not merely accompany emotional states but actively participates in generating them through autonomic, endocrine, and interoceptive pathways. Therefore, the Qur'anic language may occupy a space that the metaphor-versus-literal dichotomy fails to capture, one where bodily reference is at once symbolic in rhetorical function and accurate in physiological fact.

The concept that best captures this relationship may be consilience, a term coined by William Whewell139 in 1840 to describe cases where independent lines of evidence, derived from different classes of phenomena, converge unexpectedly on the same conclusion. In the present case, the Qur'anic-Prophetic description was not formulated as a scientific hypothesis. However, the discovery, through entirely independent investigation, that the heart possesses neural processing capacity, synthesizes hormones of bonding and calm, sends ascending signals that shape perception and emotion, generates rhythms that index cognitive health, and synchronizes with other hearts in social proximity, constitutes a consilience of sorts, and findings from unrelated disciplines converge on a picture that is strikingly consonant with that described by scripture over fourteen centuries ago. This is not the reductive consilience proposed by Wilson140 where all branches of knowledge will ultimately be unified under the natural sciences, but instead it is closer to what Barbour141 called “dialogue” between science and religion, a relationship where the two domains maintain their methodological independence but find areas of mutual illumination.

Al-Ghazali's framework from the Marvels of the Heart may offer the most fitting epistemological model. As discussed in Section “The heart in the Quran and Prophetic tradition”, al-Ghazali identified two kinds of knowledge entering the heart: intellectual knowledge acquired through study and rational investigation, and religious knowledge acquired through divine inspiration. He compared the first to food and the second to medicine, insisting that both are necessary and neither is sufficient alone.31 The scientific evidence presented in this review shows that the heart processes information, produces hormones, sends signals to the brain, and synchronizes with other hearts.

This review is best characterized as an interdisciplinary integrative analysis that brings Islamic textual scholarship into conversation with neurocardiology, psychophysiology, and interoceptive neuroscience, rather than a conventional narrative review or systematic synthesis. This scope has several limitations. The selection of studies reflects authorial judgment rather than a predetermined protocol, and the mapping between Islamic concepts and scientific findings involves interpretive choices that other scholars might contest. The scientific evidence varies in strength, where the existence of the ICNS, the heart's endocrine function, and cardiac interoception are based on robust, replicated research, whereas the transplant personality-change literature remains weak and preliminary. The literature search was confined to English and Arabic language sources, which are the languages that the author reads fluently, and in which the major biomedical and Islamic studies databases are most fully indexed. Therefore, studies in other languages, such as French and German contributions to the phenomenology of the heart, and Persian and Urdu work on Islamic cardiac psychology, may be underrepresented, and a fuller comparative synthesis remains a task for future collaborative work.

Future research directions

The process of reading Qur'anic verses and Prophetic hadiths about the heart alongside contemporary scientific evidence has itself been generative, raising research questions that might not emerge from within either tradition alone. The following directions may prove especially productive.

The scientific evidence in this review, while increasingly convergent, also reveals significant gaps that suggest dedicated investigations are needed. First, the functional roles of the newly identified intrinsic cardiac neuron subtypes, particularly the stress-resilience Ddah1-positive neurons described by Xu et al.,59 need to be characterized in human hearts because it is not known whether the human ICNS contains analogous specialized subpopulations with implications for sudden cardiac death prevention. Second, the precise mechanisms that allow HEVs to modulate conscious perception and emotional regulation require experimental designs that can disentangle cardiac from respiratory and broader interoceptive contributions. Third, the cardiac oxytocin system has been studied primarily in animal models, and thus human studies to measure local cardiac oxytocin synthesis and its relationship to affective states and spiritual practices, such as meditative prayer and dhikr, would be particularly informative. Fourth, heart transplant research would benefit from prospective, blinded, longitudinal protocols that systematically assess personality, interoceptive accuracy, and ICNS integrity before and after surgery, moving beyond the current dominance of retrospective case reports. Fifth, HRV biofeedback has shown promise in rewiring emotion-related brain networks.17 However, whether this training produces lasting structural changes and whether specific breathing frequencies correspond to optimal outcomes for different populations need to be determined. Finally, and perhaps most ambitiously, interdisciplinary research that brings together neurocardiology, psychology, and Islamic studies could develop empirical frameworks to examine the relationship between spiritual practices centered on the heart, such as dhikr and contemplative prayer, and measurable cardiac-neurological outcomes, including cardiac interoceptive accuracy, HRV coherence, and amygdala–prefrontal connectivity. This research would not seek to “prove” theological claims but could test whether practices designed to “polish” the heart produce the physiological states that contemporary science associates with cardiac-emotional health.31

Conclusions

The post-Cartesian (post-Descartes mind–body split) reduction of the heart to a mechanical pump left little room for the Qur'anic account of the heart as an organ of cognition, emotion, and moral agency. The evidence presented in this review does not aim to restore that account on scientific grounds and nor should that be expected. The Qur'an addresses dimensions of reality, including the heart's relationship to God and its capacity for spiritual blindness and illumination, which empirical investigations cannot reach. What the evidence does show is that multiple independent disciplines, such as neurocardiology, endocrinology, interoceptive neuroscience, and psychophysiology, have arrived at a portrait of the heart that is far more consonant with the Islamic tradition than the pump model that displaced it. The heart contains its own nervous system, produces hormones that shape bonding and tranquility, sends ascending signals that gate perception and color emotional life, and beats with a variability that, through central autonomic and neurovisceral regulation, indexes the brain's capacity for emotional control and sound judgment. To call the Qur'anic language about the heart “merely metaphorical” is to ignore what the body itself has been telling us.

Perhaps the most productive direction this consonance points toward is empirical. Practices that the Islamic tradition designed to “polish” the heart, such as dhikr, contemplative prayer, and moral self-discipline, can now be studied with tools that did not exist a generation ago: HEVs, HRV biofeedback, and amygdala–prefrontal connectivity mapping. Investigating whether these practices produce the cardiac-neurological states that contemporary science associates with emotional health and clear judgment would not constitute proof of theological claims, but it would test whether a fourteen century old tradition of cardiac cultivation has physiological meaning and implications worth examining in contemporary medicine.
