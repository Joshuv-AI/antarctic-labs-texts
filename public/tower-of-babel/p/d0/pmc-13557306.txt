Dermoscopy of Capillary Malformations in Capillary Malformation-Arteriovenous Malformation Syndrome Type 2
Authors: Inês Pereira Amaral, Ivânia Soares, Inês Tribolet Abreu, Madalena Pupo Correia, Filipe Monteiro, Joana Antunes
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old woman presented with multiple, asymptomatic flat lesions on the face, arms and right leg since birth. Family history was unremarkable, and systemic examination was normal. Dermatological assessment identified eight erythematous- to-brownish macules and patches with well-defined, geographic borders, measuring 0.5–5 cm (Figure 1A). The lesions increased in number over time but were neither warm nor pulsatile. Additional findings included telangiectasias on the lips (Figure 1B), as well as scattered whitish macules on the limbs (Figure 1C). Dermoscopy demonstrated branched linear vessels over a light brown homogeneous background with visible follicular openings (Figure 1D and E). Genetic testing identified a heterozygous stop mutation in the EPHB4 (c.1180 C>T; p.Arg394), confirming capillary malformation-arteriovenous malformation (CM-AVM) syndrome type 2. Brain and spine magnetic resonance imaging was unremarkable.

Teaching Point

CM-AVM syndrome is rare autosomal dominant genodermatosis caused by heterozygous mutations in RASA1 (type 1) or EPHB4 (type 2) genes. It manifests as multifocal congenital or early-onset capillary malformations (CMs) – reddish-brown macules or patches, occasionally surrounded by a whitish halo suggesting microshunting. CM-AVM2 carries a lower risk of arteriovenous malformations or fistulas, and may feature Bier spots, telangiectasias, and recurrent epistaxis.[ 1] Dermoscopy reveals a mixed vascular and pigmentary pattern, with branched linear vessels over an underlying light brown reticular background. Compared with common port-wine stains, CMs in CM-AVM may have a whitish halo, while the brownish pigment resembles café-au-lait spots but, unlike them, presents a clear vascular network. [2] Neuroimaging is recommended at diagnosis and repeated if symptoms arise. Genetic testing and counselling is recommended.
