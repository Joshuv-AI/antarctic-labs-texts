Metformin dose up-titration and glycemic control in type 2 diabetes in a resource-limited setting, northern Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gebrehiwot Teklay, Halefom Tafere, Yirga Legesse Niriayo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetformin is the preferred initial therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and gradual upward dose titration is recommended to reach target blood glucose levels. The aim of this study was to assess the pattern of metformin dose up-titration and its effect on glycemic control among T2DM patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of people with T2DM was conducted at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from January to March 2020. Participants with fasting plasma glucose above 140 mg/dl after one month of initiating metformin were followed over the course of treatment. Multivariable binary logistic regression models were used to evaluate independent variables associated with metformin dose up-titration and glycemic control. The output of the logistic regression was expressed as adjusted odds ratios at 95% confidence intervals. Test results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. A total of 284 T2DM patients on metformin were included in the study.ResultsThe mean age and SD of the study participants was 54.0 ± 10.5. Metformin dose was up-titrated at least once in 61.6% of the study participants. Almost half of the study participants had achieved glycemic control (HbA1c ≤ 7). Having a duration of treatment less than five years, initiating metformin at a daily dose of 500 mg and the addition of hypoglycemic medications were independent predictors of metformin dose up-titration. T2DM patients whose metformin dose was up-titrated had two times higher odds of having controlled blood glucose levels compared to those not up-titrated (P-value: 0.015; OR: 1.823; 95% CI: 1.121–2.963). A gradual metformin dose titration at later than the 4th month was associated with better glycemic control. T2DM patients whose metformin dose was titrated only once were less likely to achieve glycemic control (P-value: 0.038; OR: 0.482; 95% CI: 0.242–0.962).ConclusionsIn this resource-constrained setting, metformin dose up-titration was less commonly practiced than adding another hypoglycemic agent in the management of T2DM. However, dose up-titration was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving glycemic control. These findings suggest that optimizing metformin dosing may improve glycemic outcomes before intensifying therapy with additional agents, although prospective studies are needed to confirm this association.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disorder characterized by impaired metabolism of carbohydrates, fats and proteins resulting in elevated blood glucose levels [1]. Diabetes represents a growing worldwide epidemic and is a major global health and economic concern. In the past three decades the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has risen dramatically in countries of all income levels. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that there were 422 million adults with diabetes worldwide in 2014. The majority of these people live in low-and middle-income countries and 1.5 million deaths are directly attributed to diabetes each year. Both the number of cases and the prevalence of diabetes have been steadily increasing over the past few decades [2].

The diagnosis of diabetes is based on values of fasting plasma glucose (FPG) (126 mg/dL or higher) or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of above 6.5% [1, 3]. Metformin is the preferred initial pharmacologic agent for the treatment of T2DM. Metformin should be started at the time T2DM is diagnosed unless there are contraindications. It can be started at a daily dose of 500 mg, and gradually increased [3, 4]. Metformin is effective, safe, inexpensive, and reduces the risk of cardiovascular events and death [4–6].

HbA1c is the monitoring test of choice for people with diabetes and measures the level of blood glucose control over the previous 3 months. The recommended HbA1c target for diabetes control is ≤ 7.0% (≤ 53 mmol/mol) [3, 7]. Maintaining blood glucose values within the normal range significantly reduces the risk of microvascular complications [8]. In the management of T2DM, treatment should be reevaluated regularly every 3 to 6 months and intensified initially by increasing metformin dose and later by adding oral glucose-lowering drugs until treatment goals are met [3, 4, 9]. Furthermore, the likelihood of success with any antidiabetic medication in the management of T2DM is dependent on factors such as patient lifestyle modifications, adherence to treatment recommendations, physician adherence to guidelines and stepped prescribing patterns. Recent evidence indicates the potential benefit of early treatment intensification [10] and metformin titrated to a high dose was clinically effective and tolerable [11, 12]. In practice, the pattern of metformin up-titration is variable across different settings, ranging from the lowest 6.7% [13] to the highest 96.8% [14].

Poor glycemic control is common in resource constrained settings of Ethiopia ranging between 62% and 74% [15–17]. The contribution of low treatment adherence and insufficient physical activity to suboptimal glycemic control has been well investigated in these settings. However, the contribution of clinicians’ failure to up-titrate dose, initiate or intensify glucose-lowering therapy for suboptimal glycemic control is not well studied in these settings. This study aimed to assess the pattern of metformin dose up-titration and its association with glycemic control among T2DM ambulatory patients attending Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The findings of this study fill the knowledge gap in the field of treatment intensification in resource-limited settings. Moreover, the results of this study could aid in clinical decision-making processes and provide a basis for further research in diabetes management in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH) located in Mekelle, the capital city of Tigray Regional State, 783 km away from Addis Ababa. ACSH was established in 2008 under Mekelle University College of Health Sciences and is a 500-bed tertiary hospital in the nation serving as a teaching hospital and referral center for about 10 million people in northern Ethiopia. It provides a broad range of medical services to both inpatients and outpatients of all age groups with a daily rising patient flow [18]. The study was conducted in the diabetic ambulatory clinic of ACSH. In 2020, 792 T2DM patients had attended the ambulatory clinic of ACSH. The study was conducted from January to March 2020.

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted among patients with T2DM at ACSH. Patients with T2DM who initiated metformin therapy and continued treatment for more than three months, either as monotherapy or in combination with other glucose-lowering agents, were included. Participants with FPG remained above 140 mg/dL one month after treatment initiation, indicating suboptimal response to initial metformin therapy and potential need for dose escalation, were followed for evaluation of metformin dose up-titration and glycemic outcomes. Patients with incomplete medical records or evidence of non-adherence were excluded. Additionally, patients who were transferred into or out of ACSH, discontinued or reduced their metformin dose, or switched to another glucose-lowering agent were also excluded.

Study outcomes

The initial daily dose of metformin was reviewed from the medical records and categorized as low dose (500 mg), medium dose (850 to 1000 mg), and high dose (1500 to 1700 mg). The primary outcomes variables of this study were patterns of treatment intensification (up-titration of metformin dose and/or addition of other oral glucose-lowering drugs, and glycemic control measured by HbA1c. Up-titration was defined as progression to a higher metformin dose than the one initiated. The time to initial up-titration and the number of up-titrations during the treatment were recorded. FPG measurement at baseline and each visit were also recorded. The last recorded HbA1c measure was used to evaluate the overall glycemic control.

The dependent variables in this study were metformin dose up-titration, glycemic control (HbA1c) and metformin response. The independent variables included age, sex, duration of treatment, initial daily dose and the presence of other glucose lowering agents.

Sampling size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size for this study was determined using the single population proportion formula, assuming a standard normal deviation of Z = 1.96, a proportion of 50% in the target population, and a margin of error of 5%. Based on these assumptions, the initial calculated sample size was 384. However, since the total population of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was less than 10,000 (approximately 792), a finite population correction formula was applied. After adjustment, the final sample size required for the study was 285 T2DM patients.

The selection of study participants to be included as a sample was done conveniently. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria stated above, all medical records of participants visiting the diabetes clinic during the data collection period (January to March 2020) were included in this study.

Data collection

Data were collected using a pre-tested, semi-structured data abstraction format prepared by retrieving various literature sources and considering the study’s objectives. The first part of the format was used to gather data on socio- demographic variables such as age and sex, while the second part focused on clinical and treatment-related data including baseline FPG, initial metformin dose, consecutive doses of metformin, other glucose lowering agent added, HbA1C, FPG values at each visit and duration of treatment. Data collection was carried out by pharmacists trained in abstracting data from patient records and maintaining confidentiality. A pretest was done in 10 medical records (not included in this study) to ensure the feasibility of data collection tools and procedures.

Data analysis

The completed form was checked for the completeness of all necessary information. Records with missing values for key study variables were excluded from the analyses. Therefore, analyses were conducted using complete-case data. The data were cleaned before entry in to Epi Info™ version 3.5.3. To minimize data-entry errors, data were double entered and checked for discrepancies. Data analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) program version 20 (SPSS; Chicago, IL, U.S.A). Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation (SD) were used to summarize participants characteristics. Multivariable binary logistic regression models were utilized to assess the demographic and clinical factors associated with metformin dose up-titration and glycemic control. Independent variables were selected based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous literature. For the analysis of metformin dose up-titration, the variables included age, sex, duration of treatment, initial daily dose, and the addition of glibenclamide and/or insulin. For the analysis of glycemic control, the variables included age, sex, duration of treatment, initial daily dose, metformin dose up-titration, time to first dose titration, frequency of titration, addition of glibenclamide and/or insulin, and metformin dose at the end of the cohort follow-up. The results of the logistic regression was expressed as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Test results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Operational definitions

Metformin dose up-titration

Increasing the dose of metformin to a higher level than initially prescribed if FPG remains above 140 mg/dl after at least 1 month of treatment initiation.

Metformin response

Metformin response was defined as improvement in glycemic control following initiation or dose escalation of metformin therapy. Participants whose FPG remained above 140 mg/dl after one month of treatment initiation were considered as having suboptimal response to initial metformin therapy prompting dose up-titration.

Treatment intensification

Up-titrating the metformin dose and/or adding other glucose lowering agents such as glibenclamide(glyburide) and/or insulin.

Glycemic control

Glycemic control was defined as an HbA1c level ≤ 7%, measured at least three months after initiation or adjustment of metformin therapy. In this study, the last HbA1c measurement recorded in the patient’s medical record during follow-up was used as the indicator of glycemic control.

Results

A total of 284 T2DM patients on metformin were included in the study. The mean age of the study participants at diagnosis was 54.0 ± 10.5. More than half (58.1%) of the study participants were female. About 62% of the T2DM patients had duration of treatment between one to five years. The initial metformin daily dose was between 850 and 1000 in about half (53.5%) of the participants, while the remaining 31% and 15.5% had initial daily doses of 500, and between 1500 and 1700, respectively (Table 1).

The dose of metformin was up-titrated at least once in more than half of the study participants (61.6%). In the majority of participants, metformin dose titration was done once and early within the first three months of initiating treatment. About half (48.6%) of the participants had achieved glycemic control (HbA1c ≤ 7). The majority (71.8%) of the study participants had additional treatments such as glibenclamide and/or insulin in addition to metformin (Table 1).

In the multivariable regression analysis, initiating metformin at a low daily dose and adding other glucose lowering agents were independent predictors of metformin dose up-titration. Participants who started metformin at a low daily dose (500 mg) were two times more likely to be up-titrated compared to those who started at a higher dose (P-value: 0.014; OR: 2.066; 95% CI: 1.161–3.676). Patients with T2DM who were prescribed other glucose lowering agents in addition to metformin were two times more likely to be up-titrated compared to those without oral glucose-lowering drug(s) (P-value: 0.025; OR: 1.821; 95% CI: 1.077–3.078). Additionally, participants with a treatment duration of less than five years were less likely to be up-titrated compared to those with duration of more than five years (P-value:0.01; OR: 0 0.491, 95% CI: 0.286–0.845) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, metformin doses were up-titrated in over half of the participants (61.6%). metformin dose titrations were mostly done once and early in the first three months of initiating treatment. The pattern of metformin up-titration seems to vary across different settings. Less frequent up-titration of metformin ranging between 10% and 35% was reported in studies from both the developing [19] and the developed world [13, 20]. The rate of metformin dose up-titration reported in the current study was higher than most previous reports. However, it was lower than the finding by Das et al. [14] among Indian adults with T2DM receiving high doses of metformin (1500–2500 mg/day) which was reported at 96.8%. However, the Indian study was done in a selected patient population focusing only on those receiving high dose metformin. This may not show the true pattern of up-titration practice across all patient groups as most of the T2DM patients initiate treatment at lower doses. Participants who were initiated metformin at a low daily dose (500 mg/day) were two times more likely to be up-titrated compared to those who started at a higher dose. This is consistent with the guideline recommendation [3, 4].

In our study, half of the study participants had poor glycemic control (HbA1c > 7.0%) which is slightly lower than previous studies done in similar study settings [15–17]. T2DM patients whose metformin dose was up-titrated were two times more likely to have controlled blood glucose. Metformin has a dose-dependent effect on glycemic control and metformin dose up-titration was one of the predictor variables for glycemic control. For optimal achievement of glycemic control, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends increasing the metformin dose by 500–850 mg every 7 days after initiating therapy with a daily dose of 500 or 850 mg [21]. In contrast to this recommendation, clinicians in the present study titrated metformin more slowly, with dose adjustments occurring over several months. This slower rate of dose titration may be related to concerns about adverse effects. Metformin is commonly associated with gastrointestinal side effects, which are dose-dependent, and clinicians may therefore prioritize tolerability over the rapid achievement of glycemic control in order to prevent poor adherence and early discontinuation of therapy. Gastrointestinal intolerance has been reported by both patients and healthcare providers as the primary barrier to metformin use [22–24]. Furthermore, the slower titration observed in the current study may reflect the gap between evidence-based guideline recommendations and real-world clinical practice.

Although clinical guidelines recommend metformin dose escalation every one to two weeks, the present study observed that slower titration, or titration occurring after the fourth month, was associated with better glycemic control. Gradual dose escalation may enhance tolerability, medication adherence and persistence, thereby contributing to better long-term glycemic outcomes. However, this observation may also reflect clinical decision-making patterns rather than a true therapeutic advantage of delayed titration. For instance, patients who achieve adequate glycemic response with the initial metformin dose may not require early dose escalation, whereas those with poorer response may undergo earlier titration. Additionally, follow-up schedules and routine clinical practice patterns may influence the timing of dose adjustments, as most participants in this study were followed at approximately two-month intervals.

Up-titration of metformin dose was associated with approximately twofold higher odds of achieving glycemic control among patients with T2DM compared with those whose dose was not up-titrated. The effect of optimizing metformin dose on glycemia was well demonstrated in the GRADE Cohort [12] and other studies [11, 25, 26]. In a prospective study conducted in Japan, a significant dose-dependent improvement in daily profiles of blood glucose was observed when metformin dosages were titrated up to 1,500 mg/day, with a trend towards further improvement observed at 2,250 mg/day [25]. Likewise, Hirst et al. [26] also demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in HbA1c using higher doses of metformin compared with lower doses of metformin with no significant increase in side effects.

According to ADA and WHO diabetes treatment guidelines [3, 4], inadequate glycaemic response should prompt the addition of another glucose-lowering agent. In the current study, T2DM patients taking another glucose lowering agent along with metformin were twice more likely to have their metformin dose up-titrated. However, adding another glucose-lowering agent to metformin was not a predictor of glycemic control. In the current study, clinicians more frequently added another glucose-lowering agent to metformin rather than up-titrating the metformin dose (Table 1). However, the findings of this study suggest that metformin dose up-titration may be more strongly associated with achieving glycemic control than the addition of another glucose-lowering agent. The Ethiopian Standard Treatment Guidelines recommend metformin as the first-line pharmacologic therapy for T2DM, with dose titration every two weeks based on fasting blood glucose levels. If the glycemic target is not achieved with metformin monotherapy after three months, the guidelines recommend adding either a sulfonylurea or basal insulin [27]. Studies are conflicting with regard to the preferable initial intensification methods whether to add another glucose lowering agent or up-titrate metformin monotherapy. Two randomized controlled trials showed significant improvements in key parameters of glycemic control (HbA1c and FPG) in patients taking combination glucose-lowering drugs (rosiglitazone and metformin) as compared with up-titrated metformin alone [28, 29]. In contrast, in another randomized study, metformin dose up-titration significantly but modestly improved glycemic control as compared to patients treated with other oral glucose-lowering drug (vildagliptin) and a low-dose metformin (500 mg or 750 mg/d) (change in HbA1c: 0.22 ± 0.57 vs. -0.15 ± 0.58%, group comparison, P < 0.05) [30]. Likewise, a retrospective cohort study also demonstrated that metformin dosage up-titration was as effective as adding another T2DM medication with the probability of not achieving glycemic control (P = 0.599) and rate of glycemic control (adjusted hazard ratio = 1.28, 95% confidence interval = 0.98–1.68) within 6 months of intensification [31]. As per the WHO diabetes treatment recommendation [3] and the evidence from the above studies, metformin dose up-titration could be a preferable initial intensification strategy in patients with suboptimal glycemic control unless there are concerns for adverse effects.

In our study, metformin titration to a daily dose of 2000 mg or higher was associated with better glycemic control. This finding may be explained by the dose–response relationship of metformin, whereby higher doses produce greater inhibition of hepatic gluconeogenesis and improved insulin sensitivity. In routine clinical practice, metformin is often under-titrated due to concerns about adverse effects, which may result in suboptimal glycemic outcomes. Patients who reached higher doses in this study may therefore have benefited from more adequate pharmacologic therapy. Although most clinical guidelines recommend a maximum daily dose of 2000 mg [3, 4, 27], studies have reported additional glycemic benefits with acceptable safety at doses up to 2500 mg [14, 25]. These findings emphasize the importance of appropriate metformin dose titration before initiating additional glucose-lowering therapies. However, dose escalation should be individualized and carefully monitored, particularly in patients at risk for gastrointestinal intolerance or renal impairment.

This study has some limitations. We assumed optimal adherence in all participants when dealing with the outcome variables of glycemic control level and metformin response. However, being a retrospective study based only on documented information in the medical records, patient adherence to pharmacologic treatments and lifestyle modifications were not fully documented, and not measured. Baseline HbA1c as marker of baseline glycemic severity were not available in most of the study participants’ record. This study was conducted in a single center, tertiary hospital, limiting the generalizability of its findings. A potential future research avenue is to further compare different diabetes treatment regimens and identify the best treatment intensification method that fits for the diabetes population in a resource limited setting using multicenter, prospective studies.

Conclusion

In this resource-constrained setting, up-titration of metformin dose was less commonly practiced compared with the addition of another hypoglycemic agent in the management of T2DM. However, metformin dose up-titration was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving glycemic control. These findings suggest that optimizing metformin dosing may play an important role in improving glycemic outcomes before treatment intensification with additional glucose-lowering agents. Further prospective studies are warranted to better evaluate the impact of metformin dose optimization on glycemic control.
