Introduction

Ethics is the application of values and moral principles to human activities. Bioethics is a subset of ethics that provides rational and defensible answers that combine ethical principles for actual or projected moral quandaries confronting professionals in medicine and biology [1].

An ethical dilemma is a circumstance in which you must choose between two or more options. An agent is in an uncomfortable and difficult situation since he/she frequently needs to select between ethical and immoral alternatives, and when it comes to ethical alternatives, he/she must choose the best one [2]. In the context of decision-making, ethical competence can be defined as the ability to interpret relevant information optimally. This encourages a focus on autonomy. It is believed that autonomy is a crucial basis for achieving full ethical competence. The concept of ethics defines this competence: Not as the ability to constantly follow guidelines or established principles; not as the ability to act in a way that is consistent with the greatest number of philosophies; but as the ability to utilize the appropriate ethical problem-solving and decision-making process when dealing with moral issues [3].

Ethical dilemmas are part of everyday clinical practice, and clinicians all over the world must make value-driven judgments. Promoting ethical awareness within the healthcare workforce entails creating an environment that fosters critical thinking, empathy, and reflection on ethical elements in everyday practice. Healthcare organizations that prioritize education enable professionals to handle ethical challenges with competence and compassion [4].

In hospital-based treatment, emergency medical care is an essential component. It differs from other fields of medical care in that it requires quick reactions, does not have time to think through the medical and ethical aspects of the case or circumstance, and does not have prior knowledge of the patients. Analysing every facet of the scenario in a short period of time is really challenging [5]. Emergency doctors must hear the worries of family, friends, and medical decision-makers. He or she needs to disclose the nature of the situation with the family or patient and provide the choice to withdraw medical interventions. Simple language that is suitable for the patient, family, and caregivers must be used. Additionally, the emergency physician should prepare the people present for what to anticipate when the care is discontinued [6].

Physicians may be better able to handle ethical dilemmas if they are aware of these potentially competing objectives. This knowledge ought to help guide the practice of ethics consultation as well, since doctors encounter moral dilemmas on a daily basis but nevertheless seek ethics advice on a regular basis. The public is gradually becoming more aware of global trends in medico-legal issues, and complaints against physicians appear to be rising in developing nations [7]. This has highlighted how important it is for healthcare professionals to have a strong sense of professionalism. The breadth of medical professionals’ knowledge and their ability to apply medical ethics in their daily work are critical components of this professionalism [8].

Some of the ethical and decision-making challenges in medical practices have been recorded in western literature; the frequency of the biggest ethical challenges varied by country, as did the kind of problem deemed most challenging, according to research conducted among European physicians. Uncertain or impaired decision-making ability (94.8%), disagreement among caregivers (81.2%), and treatment limitation toward the end of life (79.3%) were the most frequently mentioned ethical challenges [9]. Understanding these issues and decision-making strategies is essential when it comes to emergency care in low-income countries.

Healthcare workers and policymakers in low-income countries with insufficient resources face ethical problems [10]. Health workers’ decisions and challenges are described in a multitude of empirical research [9, 11]. Nevertheless, a large number of studies focus on high-income countries; few present findings from low- and middle-income nations (LMICs), and the bulk of available studies are small qualitative studies [12, 13]. According to studies, health care consumers and providers in Sub-Saharan Africa have a low level of awareness about various ethical issues [13–16].

A survey from Ethiopian physicians showed that they have to cope with many ethical problems in their clinical work, with resource distribution being the most frequently mentioned difficulty, 93% of respondents agreed that they often or sometimes had to make tough decisions because of resources limitation [17]. But to the best of our knowledge, there was no research on this issue in our setup, and our research could provide key information. Although emergency treatment is a fundamental component of medicine, doctors frequently face moral conundrums that compromise patient care, put patient families in a difficult situation, and even cause them emotional pain. The focus of this study was on medical ethical dilemmas that physicians face in the emergency room and decision making process employed to resolve them.

Methods and materials

Study area

Sidama Regional State is the newly established state of the Southern Nation and Nationality People Regional State (SNNPR), which previously was one among 15 zones in the SNNPR regional state. Its capital city Hawassa is 275 km. from Addis Ababa. The region is located in the southern part of the country and bounded by Oromiya in the North, East, and South East, with the Gedieo Zone in the South, and the Wolayta Zone in the West. There are 12 primaries, four general hospitals, and one referral hospital in the region. Among these HUCSH is a teaching comprehensive specialized hospital in Hawassa town, Adare Hospital is a general hospital in capital city of Sidama region Hawassa, Tula Hospital is a primary hospital 10.9 Km from Hawassa, Leku Hospital is a general hospital 23.1 Km from Hawassa city, Aleta Wondo Hospital is primary hospital 62.5 km from Hawassa city, Bensa Daye Hospital is a general hospital 127 km from Hawassa city, and Yirgalem Hospital is a general teaching institution 38 Km away from Hawassa town were the seven facilities that took part of the study. All of these hospitals provide emergency services through their emergency departments.

Study period

A study was conducted from Oct 2023 –May 2024, in Sidama region public hospitals, in Southern Ethiopia.

Study design

An Institutional cross-sectional study was conducted in Sidama region public hospitals, in Southern Ethiopia.

Population

Source population and study population

The source population was all physicians working in Sidama region public hospitals, in Southern Ethiopia. And study population was all physicians; regardless of their specialty working in emergency departments of seven selected public hospitals were study participants, in the Sidama region, Southern Ethiopia.

Eligibility criteria

All physicians, regardless of their specialty, working in emergency departments during the data collection period and physicians who had more than six months work experience were included in the study.

Operational definitions

Ethical difficulties (dilemmas): Ethical difficulties in determining treatment actions and the obstacles of selecting among multiple possibilities hold equal ethical significance in medical practices for various reasons [2].

Resources: Any human and material resources used in medical practices such as staff, beds, mechanical ventilation, oxygen, medications, etc. [18].

Often: The issues that physicians deal with on a regular basis or frequently.

Sometimes: The things that physicians occasionally had to deal with.

Sample size determination

All physicians working in emergency departments of seven public hospitals during the data collection period were the sample size of the study.

Sampling procedure

Convenient sampling was conducted. We have chosen seven hospitals that provide emergency services based on their physician capacity and proximity to Hawassa University (mostly general and specialized hospitals). A total of 274 physicians participated in this study, including 87 from Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, 56 from Yirgalem General Hospital, 38 from Adare General Hospital, 30 from Leku General Hospital, 26 from Tula Primary Hospital, 19 from Aleta Wondo Primary Hospital, and 18 from Bensa Daye General Hospital.

Variables

Dependent variables were physician ethical dilemmas and decision-making process, and socio-demographic variables, medical ethical courses, and hospital resources were independent variables.

Data collection tool and data collection procedure

A self-administered prepared questionnaire adapted and interview [19] were used for data collection from physicians working in emergency departments of Sidama region public hospitals, Southern Ethiopia. In order to include all potential variables that address the study’s goal, a structured interview questionnaire with two sections of open-ended and closed-ended questions was created. It was adapted from a similar questionnaire on literature that was pertinent to the issue being studied. Socio-demographic data including age, sex, and rank, title of current job, types of ethical difficulties encountered and how frequently they occurred, methods for resolving them, and help with ethics and ethical training were all included in the structured questionnaires. Data was collected by trained BSC nurses using the Kobo data collection tool, and the primary and co-investigators monitored it on a daily basis.

Data quality control

The pre-test was done on 5% of the sample to identify impending problems with data collection instruments and to check the consistency of the questionnaires, and the Cronbach’s α value was 0.74. Supervision was held during the data collection process by seven health professionals and each questionnaire was checked for completeness by the supervisor daily.

Data processing and analysis

The entire filled checklist was checked visually by the principal investigator. Data was coded, entered, and cleaned using Epi Data version 3.3.1 software. Errors related to inconsistency were verified using the data cleansing method and finally exported into SPSS version 24 software for analysis. During analysis frequencies of the different variables were determined and the result was presented in texts, tables, and graphs, using descriptive statistics such as Frequency distributions, to describe the variables of the study.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Health Research Ethical Review Board of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Hawassa University (Ref.No: IRB/386/15) before data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before collecting data for the study. No names were recorded on the data collection tools.

Discussion

This study assessed physician ethical dilemmas and decision making process in emergency department in selected seven public hospitals Sidama region. Data were collected from a total of 274 physicians working at different departments and hospitals working at emergency. The results showed almost all physicians have encountered ethical difficulties while they were working at emergency department.

In our study most physicians have encountered resource limitation for clinical decision at emergency department, 90.8% faced it often or sometimes. This is in line with a national survey among physicians in Ethiopia the most frequently reported challenge concerned resource distribution: 93% of 565 agreed that they often or sometimes had to make difficult choices due to resource limitation [19], and study done in Ghana which found out resource constraints (inadequate resource) as cause of ethical dilemma [20]. This similarity might be attributed to a resemblance in the study setting and economic status of the communities. And a research on ethical concerns among Slovenian family doctors revealed decision-making about the utilization of limited resources was regarded as the most frequent ethical challenge (23.9% very frequent vs. 2.8% unusual), followed by patient attempts to abuse health care services (20.4% very frequent vs. 0.7% unusual) [21]. This supports our study, which found that limitation of resources frequently led to ethical dilemmas.

All physicians have faced difficulty to pursued the preferred treatment because of a patient’s (patient’s family) inability to pay (100%) and 90.8% often or sometimes have encountered ethical dilemma due to this. Like a national survey done in Ethiopia, 83% of 565 physicians often or sometimes encountered difficulties because patients were unable to pay for the preferred course of treatment [19]. Unlike our study studies done in European doctors the ethical difficulties most often reported as being encountered were uncertain or impaired decision-making capacity (94.8%), disagreement among caregivers (81.2%) and limitation of treatment at the end of life (79.3%) [9]. Additionally, other study done in America Internists most commonly reported dilemmas regarding end-of-life decision making, patient autonomy, justice, and conflict resolution [22] and in western countries, one of the most prevalent ethical issues that physicians encounter in healthcare is whether to inform terminally sick patients of their entire diagnosis. In other words, physicians frequently struggle with breaking bad news to their patients [23]. May this difference explained by variation in economic status of the nation and difference in infrastructure of health care setting.

Regarding to medical ethics courses 8% of physicians who worked in emergency rooms did not complete any medical ethics training, while 82.0% of them did. Over 60% of physicians did not receive courses on how to prioritize scare resources, abortion, and related issues. Among physicians, more than half (50%) did not receive training on disclosure information, end-of-life decision-making, or legal regulation and misconduct. Study done in Addis Ababa Ethiopia from total study participants of 500, 91.8% (459) took medical ethics course which has its own curriculum during their medical education. Majority, 417 (83.4%) of the respondents have not taken any training on code of ethics/ medical ethics after medical school/since qualification and those who took training on code of ethics/ medical ethics, the median year since they took the training was two years [24]. Unlike our study result study in Ghana discovered that all doctors are mandated by law to attend ethical program every year before their licences are renewed [20]. This difference could be attributed to variations in medical education curriculum and due to recall bias.

When faced with an ethical difficulty, the most common strategy employed by 140 physicians (51.1%) was to discuss the problem with their colleagues in order to find a solution. Alternatively, 65 physicians (23.7%) would speak with the patient’s family and letting them to find a solution. This finding is in line with study done in Ghana and Nigeria that showed physicians consulting their colleagues regarding their opinion on a particular decision [20, 25]. This may be the result of similarities in medial hierarchical consultation and decision-making processes.

Strength and limitation of the study

The strength of this study was done in multiple hospitals and the first of its kind in the study area. The study was limited only to physicians who worked at emergency room during data collection. Furthermore, it was a cross-sectional study which might not have addressed all the specific problems that a qualitative study might have. Recall bias may be present in some of the responses that called for long-term memory.

Conclusion and recommendations

Physicians are routinely challenged with ethical dilemmas and face difficulty in decision-making processes at emergency departments during their medical practices. They often encounter limitations of resources, and a patient’s (patient’s family) inability to pay that result in difficulty in treating patients at emergency department. As per the study there was no uniform and enough ethical training how to handle different ethical dilemmas. Majority of physicians used communication to their colleagues as a strategy to solve ethical dilemmas they faced at emergency department, but there were no uniform strategies used by all physicians that may worsen their challenge. We recommend the following based on our study findings about ethical dilemmas and decision-making processes in public hospital emergency departments. Hospitals should endeavour to tackle hospital resource constraint, which is the primary cause of ethical dilemmas, and develop guidelines to handle ethical issues. Furthermore, we urge that physicians and medical schools provide consistent and well-addressed medical ethics courses.
