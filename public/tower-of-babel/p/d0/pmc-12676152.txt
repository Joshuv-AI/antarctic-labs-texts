Protecting the cardiac catheterization laboratory team from radiation exposure is a critical safety priority, yet it must not come at the expense of timely reperfusion for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Door-to-balloon (D2B) times ≤90 minutes remain a cornerstone quality metric, requiring flawless coordination under urgent conditions.1 Radiation exposure remains a significant occupational risk for the catheterization laboratory team, contributing to orthopedic strain from lead apron use as well as potential long-term health consequences such as cancer diagnoses.2 Emerging leadless radiation protection systems offer the potential to markedly reduce operator and staff exposure without the ergonomic burden of heavy protective garments.3 Leadless radiation protection systems offer substantial occupational benefits, but their impact on emergent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) efficiency is unknown. In this quality improvement, noninferiority study, we evaluated whether implementing a leadless radiation protection system will preserve guideline-concordant D2B times while enhancing operator and staff safety.

Project Rationale

This initiative was designed to balance operator safety and procedural efficiency. The primary aim was to determine whether introducing the Rampart M1128 enhanced radiation protection system could improve occupational safety for catheterization laboratory personnel without delaying D2B times, ensuring continued delivery of high-quality care for STEMI patients.

Project Description

This QI project included 174 patients with STEMI who were undergoing PCI at a single high-volume tertiary-care cardiac catheterization laboratory. A total of 87 patients were randomly selected from 298 historical cases in which staff used lead aprons over a 3-year period. In addition, data were prospectively collected from 87 consecutive patients treated after implementation of the Rampart M1128 system over 11 months. D2B times were recorded in HH:MM:SS format and converted to total minutes. Patient demographics, procedural factors, and complications were also documented.

With 87 patients per group, the study had >90% power to demonstrate noninferiority within a 5-minute margin, considered clinically acceptable given the substantial occupational safety benefits of the enhanced radiation protection system.

Project Deliverables

Key deliverables of this QI initiative included successful implementation of the Rampart M1128 system across all STEMI procedures and comprehensive staff training to ensure proper setup and use. The project also established a standardized process for collecting and analyzing D2B times, patient demographics, procedural factors, and complications before and after system adoption. Finally, the initiative generated actionable insights demonstrating that enhanced radiation protection can be integrated into high-acuity workflows without compromising patient care, providing a foundation for broader adoption, workflow optimization, and ongoing monitoring of occupational safety outcomes.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions/Next Steps

Rampart M1128 implementation was noninferior to standard lead aprons in terms of D2B times (mean difference: 1.51 minutes; 95% CI: −0.86 to 3.87 minutes; P = 0.21). No delays were observed in high-acuity subgroups, including patients in cardiogenic shock or requiring intubation or mechanical support, and no significant learning curve effect was detected.

Implementation of the Rampart M1128 system enhanced occupational safety for catheterization laboratory staff by reducing radiation exposure and musculoskeletal strain without delaying D2B times, even in high-acuity STEMI cases. This finding demonstrates that provider safety interventions can coexist with, and support, high-quality, time-sensitive patient care. The project establishes a framework for broader adoption of ergonomic radiation protection, ongoing monitoring of procedural efficiency, and evaluation of long-term staff outcomes.

Future efforts will focus on expanding use across all catheterization laboratory procedures, collecting multicenter data, and optimizing workflow to minimize setup time, particularly in lower volume centers, ensuring sustainable improvements in both staff safety and patient care.

Discussion

In our QI initiative, the findings demonstrated that the implementation of this system did not result in a statistically significant prolongation of D2B time. Maintaining rapid reperfusion is critical, and any workflow modification in the catheterization laboratory must be carefully assessed to ensure it does not delay patient care. This finding supports the feasibility of integrating advanced operator safety measures without compromising a key performance metric for emergent PCI.

Orthopedic strain is one of the most common and burdensome occupational hazards faced by the cardiac catheterization laboratory team. The combination of prolonged standing and the use of heavy lead aprons for radiation protection places a significant physical toll on operators. Surveys have shown that more than half of interventional cardiologists have required treatment for neck or back pain, with many experiencing cervical disc disease, multiple spinal level involvement, and periods of missed work.4 Traditional lead aprons can weigh up to 10 pounds and may generate intervertebral disc pressures approaching 300 pounds per square inch, which over years of practice can lead to chronic injury. Beyond the musculoskeletal burden, studies have also reported a 6-fold higher rate of anxiety and depression among exposed staff,5 suggesting that both the psychological stress of the work and possible direct effects of chronic low-dose cranial radiation may contribute to long-term psychiatric morbidity.

In addition to musculoskeletal problems, chronic exposure to ionizing radiation remains a concern. Even with protective measures, operators have demonstrated higher rates of DNA damage and chromosomal abnormalities than their nonexposed colleagues. Studies have linked such exposures to increased risks of leukemia, skin, breast, and brain cancers, and reports of left-sided brain tumors in operators are consistent with the pattern of cranial exposure during procedures.5 The Rampart system and other leadless systems substantially reduces overall body radiation exposure when compared with conventional lead apron shielding.3

Unlike conventional radiation protection strategies that require pregnant women to double the thickness of lead aprons or use additional fetal dosimeters,6 the leadless radiation system eliminates these concerns entirely. With its design, fetal radiation exposure is minimized at the source,7 meaning pregnant staff do not need to worry about extra shielding or added monitoring. This ensures both safety and comfort in the cardiac catheterization laboratory while maintaining compliance with federal workplace protections for pregnant women.

Many factors have been studied in reducing D2B time, all with the goal of enhancing patient care and improving outcomes for those experiencing critical cardiac events.8 Leadless radiation protection systems may also contribute indirectly to improved D2B times by streamlining workflow and reducing the need for time-consuming equipment adjustments. Efficient catheterization laboratory processes are known to be critical determinants of STEMI performance, and minimizing procedural interruptions can help maintain or even improve reperfusion metrics.

This study has some limitations. It was a single-center retrospective analysis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Procedures were performed by different operators, introducing potential variability in technique and workflow. Additionally, outcomes were focused on D2B times and staff safety, and longer term clinical or occupational endpoints were not assessed. Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable real-world insight into the feasibility of implementing enhanced radiation protection without compromising emergent PCI performance.

The Rampart M1128 provided substantial ergonomic and radiation safety benefits for the catheterization laboratory team, aligning with broader efforts to reduce occupational hazards.

Conclusions

The implementation of the Rampart M1128 enhanced radiation protection system in a high-volume STEMI program demonstrated noninferiority in D2B times while providing significant ergonomic and radiation safety benefits for the catheterization laboratory team. These findings indicate that advanced operator protection can be integrated into emergent PCI workflows without compromising procedural efficiency or patient outcomes. This QI initiative supports broader adoption of leadless protection systems as a means to safeguard staff health while maintaining high standards of timely, lifesaving cardiac care.
