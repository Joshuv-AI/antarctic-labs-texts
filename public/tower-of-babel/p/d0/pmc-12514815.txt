Spontaneous hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme alleviates osteoarthritis via oxidative stress reduction and mitochondrial dysfunction reversal
Authors: Qi Wang, Minyi Zhang, Weihao Jiang, Dejian Li, Beijie Qi, Shi Shi, Shuo Shi, Chengqing Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA), a chronic and degenerative joint disease, has become increasingly prevalent due to the aging population, posing a significant societal burden. However, despite progress, effective therapeutic options for osteoarthritis remain limited. In OA, chronic inflammation mediates a hypoxic microenvironment, leading to increased cellular energy demands. Over time, this causes mitochondrial dysfunction, favoring the accumulation of ROS, thereby perpetuating inflammation. Furthermore, reduced autophagy in aging chondrocytes hinder the clearance of damaged mitochondria, exacerbating oxidative stress. Herein, we have developed a simple and environmentally friendly strategy to fabricate hydrogen-releasing nanozymes (Se-HMPB@AB@COS) that spontaneously release hydrogen gas, effectively treating osteoarthritis through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mitochondrial dysfunction reversal mechanisms. During the process of hydrogen therapy, small hydrogen molecules can readily penetrate mitochondria, specifically reducing the levels of •OH thereby protecting mitochondrial function. Our research further unveils that hydrogen therapy can effectively enhance mitophagy and delay chondrocyte senescence. In vivo, Se-HMPB@AB@COS encapsulated with chondroitin sulfate significantly promotes the synthesis of collagen II, inhibits the degradation of extracellular matrix, and reduces inflammatory factors. Overall, this study innovatively synthesized a hydrogen-releasing nanozyme, demonstrating its effectiveness in inhibiting oxidative stress, inflammation, promoting mitophagy and extracellular matrix synthesis, thereby reducing cartilage and mitochondrial damage, and delaying OA progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03716-0.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, progressive, degenerative disease affecting the whole joint, including articular cartilage, subchondral bone, ligaments, articular capsule, and synovium [1]. Currently, osteoarthritis of the knee impacts over 250 million people worldwide, making it one of the fastest-growing health concerns globally [2]. The progression of OA is intricately linked to factors such as oxidative stress, chronic low-grade inflammation, reduced chondrocyte proliferation and extracellular matrix synthesis, chondrocyte apoptosis, and extracellular matrix degradation [3]. Joint injuries trigger an increase in intracellular free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS), with mitochondria being one of the prime targets of free radical attack [4]. Mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation further fuels ROS production, creating a vicious cycle. Elevated ROS levels contribute to low-grade inflammation in cartilage and synovium, leading to the secretion of extracellular matrix degrading enzymes that enhance cartilage degradation. This, in turn, downregulates the expression of cartilage-specific anabolic genes like aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen alpha 1 (COL2A1), ultimately compromising chondrocyte function and joint structure [5, 6]. Currently, there is no curative treatment for OA, and due to the complexity of the disease, no known treatment can prevent structural damage, and interventions can only produce moderate symptomatic improvement at best [7].

Within cells, mitochondria serve as crucial organelles, playing a pivotal role in catabolic and anabolic processes, apoptosis, and signal transduction. Notably, mitochondrial-mediated aerobic respiration is crucial for chondrocyte energy metabolism, particularly in a relatively hypoxic microenvironment [8]. As osteoarthritis progresses, oxidative stress leads to mitochondrial damage and reduced mitochondrial respiratory capacity. This mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation promotes ROS production, which further fuels inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, ultimately forming a vicious cycle that accelerates cartilage degeneration [9]. Mitophagy, the primary process for mitochondrial quality control, selectively eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory levels in chondrocytes [10]. Consequently, developing novel mitochondrial-targeted interventions for osteoarthritis holds promise, as they could potentially prevent cartilage degeneration at the subcellular level.

Hydrogen therapy emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy, harnessing molecular hydrogen (H2) as a safe antioxidant. It specifically consumes hydroxyl radicals in reactive oxygen species to exert anti-oxidative stress, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects [11, 12]. Unlike NO, CO, and H2S, hydrogen does not interfere with the oxygen-carrying capacity of red blood cells, nor poses a risk of blood poisoning even at high concentrations [13]. Furthermore, hydrogen can easily penetrate cell membranes and quickly transfer to the nucleus and mitochondria, effectively eliminating excess reactive oxygen species in mitochondria. Studies have demonstrated that hydrogen safeguards against diseases by protecting mitochondria and regulating mitochondrial autophagy [14]. Currently, molecular hydrogen is primarily administered in the form of H2-enriched water or inhaled H2 gas for therapeutic purposes, and it has been confirmed to protect against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury through selective antioxidant mechanisms [15]. However, the significant low solubility and high diffusibility of H2 pose significant challenges to its translational application. Ammonia borane (AB) with a hydrogen content of 19.6 wt% is widely recognized as a safe and effective hydrogen storage and release medium, capable of sustaining hydrogen supply through gradual reaction in acidic environments [16]. In the acidic microenvironment of osteoarthritis, ammonia borane can serve as a continuous hydrogen supplier and help improve the pH value within joints [17].

In recent years, nanozyme systems have emerged as promising candidates for traditional enzymes in treating various inflammatory diseases, owing to their exceptional ability to scavenge ROS [18]. Prussian blue, composed of Fe2+ and Fe3+ to form iron hexacyanoferrate, possesses diverse enzymatic activities that allow it to efficiently eliminate ROS. Crucially, Prussian blue exhibits excellent biosafety and has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as an anticoagulant for the clinical treatment of thallium poisoning [19, 20]. Multiple studies have confirmed that PB nanozymes can reduce intracellular ROS and exhibit significant anti-inflammatory effects [21, 22]. Selenium, an essential nutrient and trace element crucial for redox homeostasis, has been proven in numerous studies to be a promising therapeutic strategy for improving osteoarthritis development through selenium supplementation [23].

Herein, we present a novel multifunctional nanozyme based on zero-valence selenium-enriched Prussian blue (Se-HMPB) with the ability to spontaneously release hydrogen, for the treatment of osteoarthritis (Scheme 1). Following the synthesis of an HMPB structure with hollow cavity characteristics, a selenite (Na2SeO3) layer is deposited onto the HMPB surface and reduced to zero-valent selenium via ascorbic acid, fabricating the specialized Se-HMPB nanozyme particles. Subsequently, ammonia borane, a component capable of sustained hydrogen release, is loaded onto the particles. The Se-HMPB@AB exhibits remarkable anti-oxidative stress properties, effectively reducing mitochondrial ROS accumulation and promoting mitophagy. Ultimately, the entire nanozyme is encapsulated with chondroitin sulfate (COS), which inhibits extracellular proteases involved in connective tissue metabolism while stimulating the production of proteoglycans in chondrocytes, thereby alleviating osteoarthritis [24]. The multifunctional Se-HMPB@AB@COS demonstrates exceptional mitochondrial protection and anti-oxidative stress properties, safeguarding chondrocytes and inhibiting the degradation of the extracellular matrix, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of osteoarthritis.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

Poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP K30), ascorbic acid (VC), sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), ammonia borane (AB), chondroitin sulfate (COS) and H2O2 were acquired from Aladdin Corporation (Shanghai, China). Ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]·3H2O) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent CO. Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium were purchased from MeilunBio Biotechnology Co.Ltd (Dalian, China). 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA and penicillin-streptomycin purchased from New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co.Ltd (Suzhou, China). Fetal bovine serum purchased from VivaCell Biosciences (Shanghai, China). Anti-Collagen II rabbit polyclonal antibody (pAb) and anti-MMP13 rabbit pAb were from Affinity Biosciences LTD. Anti-Pink1 rabbit pAb, Anti-Parkin mouse monoclonal antibody (mAb), anti-Lamp2 mouse mAb, Anti-Tomm20 rabbit pAb, Anti-LC3B rabbit pAb and anti-COX-2 mouse mAb were from Proteintech Group Co., Ltd. Anti-P53 rabbit pAb were from Cell Signaling Technology. Mouse IL-1β and TNF-α elisa kit (U96-1494E, U96-3112E) were purchased from YOBlBlO (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanocomposites

HMPB was prepared with a few modifications [25]. 198 mg of K3[Fe(CN)6] and 4.5 g of PVP K30 were added to 60 ml with HCl (0.01 M) to obtain the clear solution and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. Then heated at 80 ℃ for 20 h without stirring. After that, the PBs were obtained by centrifugation (12000 rpm, 10 min) and washed with ethanol and deionized (DI) water. Then, PB (30 mg) and PVP (300 mg) added into 30 ml of HCl solution (1 M) under magnetic stirring. After stirring for 4 h at room temperature, the solution was transferred into a Teflon via and heated at 140 ℃ for 3 h. The HMPB were collected by centrifugation (12000 rpm, 10 min) and washed with ethanol three times.

After adding an equal volume Na2SeO3 (6 mg/ml) to HMPB (2 mg/ml) and stirring at room temperature for 30 min, an equal volume of VC (12 mg/ml) was slowly added. The reaction was carried out at room temperature for 3 h. Then, the centrifugation was performed to collect Se-HMPB nanocomposites and wash with deionized water, dialysis (Mw = 3500 Da).

Ammonia borane (AB, 160 mg) was dissolved into Se-HMPB (10 mg/ml, 1 ml) under stirring for 24 h at room temperature. Then, Se-HMPB@AB nanocomposites were collected by centrifugation and washed with deionized water.

Chondroitin sulfate (COS) (2 mg/ml, 1 ml) was added to Se-HMPB (1 mg/ml, 1 ml) and then the solution was stirred for 12 h. Se-HMPB@AB@COS was acquired after centrifugation and washed deionized water three times.

Characterizations

The crystal phase of the specimens was analyzed using X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) employing a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer, and Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) images were acquired using a JEM-2100 instrument from JEOL, Japan, operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 KV. UV-Vis absorption spectra were captured using a Hitachi U-2900 Spectrophotometer. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) and ζ-potential of the samples were determined using a Litesizer TM 500 instrument by Anton Paar. X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were obtained using a Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi with Al Kα (1486.6 eV) serving as the excitation source.

Detection of the AB loading capacity in Se-HMPB nanocomposites

The determination of AB loading capacity in Se-HMPB was carried out using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Briefly, 10 mg of Se-HMPB@AB, or Se-HMPB nanocomposites were dried via lyophilizer overnight. TGA was were subjected to overnight drying using a lyophilizer. TGA was then conducted with a temperature ramp from 298 to 1073 K at a heating rate of 283 K/min under a nitrogen atmosphere.

H2 and ion release from Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanocomposites

In detail, Gas Chromatography (GC) was utilized to establish a standard curve for various volumes of H2. Firstly, phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with different pH values (pH 6.5, and pH 7.4) were prepared and introduced into a 100 ml reactor equipped with stirring under an argon gas environment. Subsequently, 30 mg of Se-HMPB@AB@COS was added into the reactor. At different time intervals, 0.1 ml of gas was extracted from the container, and the release of H2 was quantified using the (Trace GC Ultra) system.

To investigate the release profiles of Se and Fe ions under different pH conditions, we dispersed 4 mg of freeze-dried Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanomaterials into 2 ml of PBS solutions with varying pH values (pH 7.4 and 5.6). The mixtures were then oscillated at 37 ℃. At predetermined time points, samples of the solutions were taken out, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant solutions were collected. Subsequently, the contents of Se and Fe ions in the supernatants were determined using ICP-OES. Finally, time-dependent ion release curves were plotted using the cumulative addition method.

A solution was made by diluting a fresh 5 mM MB stock (Solarbio, China) with platinum nanoparticles (Sigma, Germany) at 1:4 to get a final MB concentration of 1 mM. This solution then dissolved dried Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozyme for intra-articular injection to detect in-vivo hydrogen. MB shows a characteristic absorption peak at 664 nm, and absorbance drop is linked to hydrogen release [26, 27]. We quantified hydrogen from the nanozyme by measuring the peak intensity reduction.

The detection of GSH comsumed

Se-HMPB@AB@CS nanocomposites were evaluated using 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) as the chromogenic substrate. The experimental setup involved groups: (1) Negative control: H2O + 1 mM GSH; (2) Se-HMPB@AB@CS group: 400 µg/ml Se-HMPB@AB@COS + 1 mM GSH; (3) Positive control: 1 mM H2O2 + 2 mM GSH; (4) Se-HMPB@AB: 400 µg/ml Se-HMPB@AB + 1 mM GSH; (5) Se-HMPB: 400 µg/ml Se-HMPB + 1 mM GSH; (6) HMPB: 400 µg/ml HMPB + 1 mM GSH Following 6 h incubation in darkness, DTNB (100 µl, 1 mM) was added to the supernatant, and incubation for another 5 min, the absorbance at 450 nm was finally assessed.

The detection of superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity

Superoxide Dismutase (Water-Soluble Tetrazolium salt-8) Assay Kit was used to detected the different treatments at 325 nm utilizing UV-Vis spectroscopy.

The Total SOD Assay Kit (WST-8 method) was used to validate O2•− scavenging capacity, demonstrating the SOD activity of the nanozyme and enabling enzyme-kinetic curve construction. The experiment followed the manufacturer’s instructions. After 30-min incubation, samples were centrifuged to obtain supernatant, which was measured for absorbance at 450 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Free-radical-scavenging capability of Se-HMPB@AB@COS

The 2-phenyl-4,4,5,5-tetramethylimidazoline-1-oxyl 3-oxide radical (PTIO•) is an oxygen-centered species that can reflect the free radical scavenging performance of nanozymes. First, a PTIO stock solution was prepared in PBS buffer, and its concentration was adjusted to approximately 0.18 mg/ml. Next, 0.5 ml aliquots of the Se-HMPB@AB@COS solution (with concentrations varying as required) were each mixed with 0.5 ml aliquots of the prepared PTIO solution. Subsequently, each mixture was incubated at ambient temperature for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance value of PTIO in each mixture was measured at the characteristic wavelength of 557 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Animals

This research program has been approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Pudong Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University (approval number: 20230920-2). All animal procedures involving C57 mice were purchased from Charles River (Beijing) and fully compliant with the ARRIVE guidelines [28]. Every effort was made to minimize the pain and suffering of the C57 mice.

Male wild-type C57BL/6 mice aged 10 weeks underwent ACLT surgery on the right knee to induce mechanical instability and create an experimental OA model. The mice were randomly divided into five groups: sham surgery with saline treatment, ACLT surgery with saline treatment, ACLT surgery with Se-HMPB nanoparticle treatment, ACLT surgery with Se-HMPB@AB treatment, and ACLT surgery with Se-HMPB@AB@COS treatment. Nanomaterials were injected into the joints of the mice using a 33-gauge needle (Hamilton Company) and a 10 µl CASTIGHT syringe (Hamilton Company). The accumulation and metabolism of in-vivo nanozymes within the knee joint were monitored by detecting fluorescence intensity via in-vivo imaging and measuring the contents of Fe and Se ions in tissues using ICP-OES at 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection.

Histological evaluation

After evaluating the macroscopic changes, the samples were decalcified with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for at least two weeks. Then, the samples were dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sliced to 4 μm. The sections were stained with Safranin O-fast green and Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) to assess cell morphology and matrix content.

Tissue Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were dried in a 60 ℃ oven for 2 h, and then treated with 3% H2O2 and 0.5% Triton X-100. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with specific antibodies overnight at 4 ℃. Finally, the sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488/594). Finally, the sections were examined under a microscope, and suitable areas were selected for imaging.

Micro-CT

The right knee joint of the mouse was taken, fixed, and then scanned using Micro-CT with a scan resolution of 6.534165 micrometers and an exposure time of 350 milliseconds. The reconstructed images were selected and reconstructed using the three-dimensional reconstruction software NRecon (software version V1.7.4.2, Bruker Corporation, Germany). The region of interest (ROI) was analyzed using CTAnalyser (software version 1.20.3.0, Bruker Corporation, Germany). With unified parameters set, the software calculated parameters such as osteophyte size and maturity, total volume (TV), bone volume (BV), volume ratio (BV/TV) and subchondral bone plate thickness of the tissue.

Combining Micro-CT and histological evaluation, three blinded reviewers used the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system to grade the samples.

ELISA assay

After the completion of the animal experiments, mouse serum was collected. The levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the mouse serum were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture and cell viability

The ATDC5 cell line was purchased from CellVerse (Shanghai, China) and authenticated by Shanghai BIOWING Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cell culture was performed according to ATCC instructions.

Cell toxicity was detected using the cell counting kit-8 (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). ATDC5 cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at 37 ℃ with 1 * 104 cells per well for 24 h. After treatment with H2O2, H2O2 + Se-HMPB, H2O2 + Se-HMPB@AB, or H2O2 + Se-HMPB@AB@COS, the cells were incubated under normal growth conditions for another 24 h. Then, 10 µl/well of CCK8 was added and incubated for half an hour, followed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm)

ATDC5 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and incubated overnight before undergoing different treatments. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution in the dark at 37 ℃ for 20 min and analyzed using flow cytometry. Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) was determined using the JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Cell apoptosis

In brief, ATDC5 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and incubated for 24 h. After undergoing different treatments, the cells were tested for apoptosis using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime) and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA).

Detection of intracellular ROS

2,7-Dichlorodi-hydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was used to measure the level of intracellular ROS. Cells were incubated with 10 nmol/l of DCFH-DA in the dark at 37 ℃ for 0.5 h, washed with PBS, and then detected using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA).

MDA, SOD and GSH assay

Cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and, after undergoing different treatments, cell lysates from the designated groups were collected. GSH, MDA, and SOD levels were measured using the corresponding kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of ATP level

The intracellular ATP level of chondrocytes was measured using the Enhanced ATP Detection Kit (Beyotime). Cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and treated differently after 24 h. Following another 24 h of incubation, the cell contents were lysed using ATP release reagent. The supernatant was transferred to an opaque 96-well plate, and 100 µl of ATP detection solution was added to each well. The luminescence intensity was measured using a multi-functional enzyme standard instrument. Finally, the errors due to protein quantity differences during sample preparation were eliminated based on the concentration of cellular protein.

Cellular Immunofluorescence staining

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, the treated cells were washed with PBS and permeabilized with Triton X-100 for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with blocking buffer for 1 h. The primary antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. After washing three times with PBST, the cells were co-incubated with goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488/594) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. Upon completion of the PBST wash, an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added, and the cells were then analyzed under a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Senescence β-Galactosidase staining

The detection of cellular senescence was performed separately using the SPiDER-βGal senescence detection reagent (DOJINDO) and the senescence β-Galactosidase staining kit (Beyotime). After undergoing various treatments, experiments were conducted following the experimental protocols provided by different manufacturers, and finally, observations were made under a microscope.

Detection of mitophagy flux

The pCNP-mTagRFP-mWasabi-LC3 lentivirus was purchased from GeneChem Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and subsequently co-incubated with the lentiviral working solution for a duration of 24 h. After 24 h, puromycin was used for cell selection. Subsequently, following different treatments, the cells were observed using a confocal microscope.

Western blot analysis

After treating the ATDC5 cells in the same manner, protein lysates were obtained using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Subsequently, SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis was performed, and the proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore). After blocking in 5% BSA for 2 h, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃. Then, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody for another 2 h at room temperature before being developed. The enhanced chemiluminescent images were visualized and analyzed using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SEM. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test and unpaired t-test were employed to assess any significant differences among groups at different time points. Each group comprised at least three independent samples for each statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9 (San Diego, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant at *, # P < 0.05, **, ## P < 0.001, and ***, ### P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structural characterization of hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme

In this study, we have rationally and effectively designed and synthesized hydrogen-releasing nanozymes loaded with AB and encapsulated with chondroitin sulfate, aiming to enhance matrix synthesis and anti-inflammatory activities in OA treatment. Following previous procedures [29], we synthesized Prussian blue nanoparticles (PB NPs) and then etched them at 140 ℃ for 4 h to create a hollow mesoporous structure. Afterward, the nanoparticles underwent reduction with selenite (Na2SeO3), loading of AB, and encapsulation of COS, ultimately resulting in our ideal nanozyme, Se-HMPB@AB@COS (Fig. 1 A). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images revealed that PB displayed an average size of 93 nm, displaying a well-defined cubelike structure (Fig. 1B). After etching, PB transformed into HMPB structures, which featured a hollow cavity. The coating of the HMPB surface with a layer of selenite (Na2SeO3) was performed. Subsequently, the reduction of this selenite layer to zero-valence selenium was accomplished through ascorbic acid. Then, AB was loaded to prepare Se-HMPB@AB (Fig S1A). Finally, Se-HMPB@AB was successfully synthesized by coating COS. Additionally, elemental mappings revealed the distributions of Fe, C, O, N, Se, B, and S elements in Se-HMPB@AB@COS (Fig. 1 C).

As shown in Fig S1B, the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of Se-HMPB@AB@COS showing characteristic peaks at 1224 and 1381 cm−1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of S = O in COS and NH2 in AB, respectively.

Elemental concentration was measured through inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), indicating the mass percentage of Se in Se-HMPB@AB@COS was 16.05%. The retention time (1.271 min) and a standard curve of H2 were obtained in GC (Fig. 3H). As revealed in Fig. 3I, a few H2 release was measured in 7.4. In contrast, amount of H2 was detected in 6.5 after 60 min, indicating that Se-HMPB@AB@COS was pH correlation. Meanwhile, we utilized ICP-MS analysis to monitor the ion release from nanozymes across a range of pH values. The findings consistently revealed that the rate of ion release was accelerated under low-pH conditions, thereby endowing the nanozymes with enhanced antioxidant capabilities (Fig S2). Then, DTNB was used to detect the consumption of GSH (Fig. 3 J, S1H). The Se-HMPB@AB@COS group showed the highest UV-Vis absorption spectra compared to other groups and demonstrated the lowest scavenging effect on the GSH level. In an acidic environment. The phenomenon could be explained that under acidic conditions, the group of the Se-HMPB@AB@COS could produce H2, leading to the effective reduce oxidized glutathione (GSSG) back to GSH and facilitate the reaction between DTNB and GSH. Considering that O2•− is a principal component of ROS, the enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD), which is essential for antioxidative defense in cells, facilitates the transformation of toxic O2•− into H2O2 and O2 through the dismutation process. The Se-HMPB@AB@COS demonstrated markedly improved O2•− and PTIO• scavenging capabilities (Fig. 3 K and L, S3), indicating that H2 and COS potentially bolster the SOD-mimetic properties of Se-HMPB@AB@COS.

Cytotoxicity of hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme and its protective effect on oxidative stress

The CCK8 assay was employed to assess the cytotoxicity of the Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes. As depicted in Fig. 4 A, the nanozymes, including Se-HMPB, Se-HMPB@AB, and Se-HMPB@AB@COS, all exhibited remarkable biocompatibility and exceedingly low cytotoxicity. At low concentrations, the nanozymes displayed negligible cytotoxicity, and their robust antioxidant stress capability enhanced cell proliferation, resulting in higher cell viability than the untreated cell group. However, at high concentrations (above 100 µg/ml), the nanozymes exhibited minimal cytotoxicity, far below the IC50 value. Notably, compared to other nanozymes, the chondroitin sulfate-encapsulated nanozyme maintained significantly lower cytotoxicity at high concentrations, significantly lower than the other two groups. Even at a concentration of 200 µg/ml, cell viability remained at 82.9%. Moreover, we employed flow cytometry to investigate the rescuing effect of the chondroitin sulfate-encapsulated nanozyme on ROS induced by H2O2 at different concentrations (Fig S4). At low concentrations, as the concentration of the nanozyme increased, its inhibitory effect on cellular ROS enhanced. Furthermore, within the concentration range of 20–40 µg/ml, the nanozyme exhibited the optimal inhibitory effect on cellular ROS. Based on these findings, we ultimately selected a concentration of 20 µg/ml as the therapeutic concentration for subsequent experiments.

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes on chondrocytes, an inflammatory microenvironment induced by high-level oxidative stress was created by adding H2O2. As shown in Fig. 4 A, the cell viability of the H2O2 -treated group decreased significantly, indicating severe damage to the cells caused by the high ROS levels induced by H2O2. However, upon the addition of the nanozymes, the cell viability increased significantly, and the release of hydrogen gas exhibited a more pronounced cytoprotective effect. DCFH-DA is a fluorescent probe used for the detection of ROS. DCFH-DA itself is non-fluorescent and can freely pass through the cell membrane to enter the cell. Under the action of ROS within the cell, the non-fluorescent DCFH is oxidized to generate fluorescent DCF [30]. As shown in Fig. 4B and S5, Se-HMPB@AB@COS exhibited a stronger intracellular ROS scavenging ability compared to Se-HMPB and Se-HMPB@AB at the same concentration. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a key by-product of oxidation reactions, has been identified as an effective marker of oxidative stress [31]. The change in MDA levels can effectively reflect the overall ROS level within the cell over a period of time. As depicted in Fig. 4 C, after 24 h of H2O2 treatment, the MDA level within the cells significantly increased, but the addition of nanozymes reversed this change, indicating that the high ROS level induced by H2O2 was effectively suppressed, reducing the by-products of oxidation reactions and preventing further damage to the cells. Glutathione (GSH) is an important member of the cellular antioxidant system. In the cell, GSH levels decrease significantly during the process of scavenging excess ROS and detoxifying exogenous substances. As shown in Fig. 4 C, the Se-HMPB nanozyme effectively reversed the decline in GSH levels caused by H2O2 oxidation. Furthermore, compared to Se-HMPB and Se-HMPB@AB at the same concentration, Se-HMPB@AB@COS significantly increased the GSH level that was oxidized by H2O2. Se-HMPB itself possesses rich redox potentials and exhibits various enzyme-like activities, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, which can catalyze the conversion of superoxide into O2•− (Superoxide radical) and hydrogen peroxide [32]. We used an OD detection kit to assess the ability of nanozymes to scavenge O2•−. The Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozyme demonstrated better O2•− scavenging ability than the other two materials. The SOD-like activity of nanozymes is directly related to the elimination of O2•−, suggesting that the SOD-like activity of Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes is significantly enhanced due to the therapeutic effect of hydrogen gas.

ROS mediate lipid peroxidation, resulting in the production of 4-hydroxynonenal and other related products, which lead to various forms of cell death, including apoptosis [33]. We used Annexin V and PI co-staining to detect the apoptotic stages of cells and further evaluate the protective effect of Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes. As shown in Fig. 4D, the apoptotic cell population, especially in the early stage, significantly increased in the H2O2 model group and significantly decreased after incubation with nanozymes. Among them, the Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozyme showed the strongest protective effect on cells, reducing the early apoptosis rate from 31.3% to 7.05% and the late apoptosis rate from 24.9% to 5.59% compared to the H2O2 model group, indicating the significant cytoprotective effect of the hydrogen-releasing nanozyme.

Protection of mitochondrial function by hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme

Mitochondria serve as a pivotal center for numerous vital cellular processes, encompassing yet not limited to electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, fatty acid β-oxidation, amino acid synthesis, calcium homeostasis, and iron metabolism, specifically the biosynthesis of heme and iron-sulfur clusters [34]. Inside cells, mitochondria are responsible for the generation of the vast majority of ROS, an inevitable by-product of the electron transport process. Tragically, mitochondria themselves are particularly susceptible to ROS-mediated oxidative damage, often leading to mitochondrial dysfunction [35]. Notably, the loss or impairment of mitochondrial key functions is predominantly manifested in mitochondrial membrane depolarization or the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, abnormal ROS production, and the depletion of ATP generation [36].

In Fig. 5 A, we initially assessed mitochondrial function through mitochondrial membrane potential detection. The MMP has long been a valuable indicator of cellular functional status. Imbalances in redox homeostasis can disrupt mitochondrial membrane stability, resulting in a reduced MMP [37]. When ATDC5 cells were treated with H2O2, a high concentration of monomers (51%) and the lowest number of aggregates were observed. However, Se-HMPB exhibited protective effects on mitochondria by inhibiting oxidative stress, revealing a higher proportion of aggregates and fewer monomers (36%). Hydrogen gas can readily penetrate mitochondrial membranes within cells, scavenging excessive ROS within mitochondria. Studies have shown that the protective effects of H2 may primarily depend on its unique ability to penetrate various cellular aspects, regulating mitochondrial homeostasis through the activation of the Keap1-Nrf2 phase II antioxidant system [38]. In a study by Xie et al., hydrogen therapy improved 7-day survival rates, cognitive function, and significantly enhanced mitochondrial function (MMP, ATP levels, Complex I activity) in mice [39]. Both Se-HMPB@AB and Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes displayed superior mitochondrial protective functions, exhibiting fewer monomers (26.9% and 18.1%) in the JC-1 experiment compared to pure Se-HMPB. Mitochondria’s most crucial function is energy production through respiration, and an increase in ROS levels can inactivate key enzymes in mitochondrial metabolism (such as α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and aconitase), leading to reduced ATP production [40]. The application of nanozymes to H2O2 -damaged chondrocytes effectively restored ATP concentrations, with the Se-HMPB@AB@COS group achieving higher ATP levels, even approaching those of the ctrl group, indicating that Se-HMPB@AB@COS can effectively protect mitochondrial function (Fig. 5B).

Hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme modulates cellular senescence via mitophagy

The development of osteoarthritis (OA) is associated with an increase in the number of senescent cells within joint tissues. Senescence represents a specific cellular state characterized by the permanent arrest of the cell cycle and the chronic release of harmful proinflammatory molecules into the surrounding microenvironment, a phenomenon known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). This SASP contributes to reduced cell proliferation, impaired tissue regeneration, and dysfunction [41]. In joints, senescence-induced metabolic reprogramming of cells triggers OA pathogenesis and, over time, exacerbates the severity of OA.

Firstly, we utilized P53 immunofluorescence staining and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) detection to assess cellular senescence. In ATDC5 cells, we induced senescence by using a low concentration of H2O2 (5 μm), which led to a conspicuous increase in P53 expression within the cell nuclei (Fig. 6 A, S6A). Similarly, the proportion of senescent cells stained with SA-β-Gal was also notably higher (Fig. 6B). However, following treatment with Se-HMPB, Se-HMPB@AB, and Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozymes, the expression of P53 was notably reduced. Similarly, the hydrogen-releasing nanozymes effectively mitigated the increase in SA-β-Gal staining induced by H2O2 incubation, further indicating their efficacy in alleviating cellular senescence. To gain a more intuitive and detailed understanding of cellular senescence, we employed a novel SPiDER-βGal reagent that was capable of analyzing senescence in individual cells. Upon reviewing Fig. 6 C, it became apparent that the SA-β-gal levels of cells in the H2O2 group had undergone a significant increase. However, subsequent to the addition of nanozymes, a substantial decrease in cellular senescence levels was observed. Notably, hydrogen therapy had exerted an even more pronounced effect, significantly reversing cellular aging.

Mitophagy is a process that selectively eliminates damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through autophagy mechanisms, serving a vital role in maintaining mitochondrial quality control and homeostasis [42]. Mitophagy plays a crucial role in the progression of osteoarthritis (OA). During the development of OA, mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, leading to increased ROS burden and decreased mitochondrial autophagy. This reduction in mitochondrial autophagy fails to effectively clear damaged mitochondria, resulting in increased tissue damage and further progression of OA [43]. Additionally, during the aging process, mitochondrial function declines, manifesting as increased ROS production, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations, protein oxidation, abnormal energy metabolism, and reduced mitophagy [44]. The direct consequence of reduced mitophagy is the failure of mitochondrial quality control. Due to the breakdown of mitochondrial quality control and the expansion of mitochondrial defects, the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria ultimately leads to cellular senescence and death [45].

In Fig. 7 A, we conducted dual-fluorescence staining in cells using the mitochondrial marker protein Tomm20 and the lysosomal marker protein Lamp2. Notably, the fluorescence expression of Tomm20 in the H2O2 group significantly increased, indicating the accumulation of damaged mitochondria. LAMP-2, a highly conserved and ubiquitous protein crucial for mitophagy, showed a marked decrease in fluorescence in the H2O2 group, reflecting a reduction in mitophagy [46]. After treatment with the Se-HMPB nanozyme, Tomm20 fluorescence weakened, while LAMP-2 fluorescence intensified. Notably, Se-HMPB@AB produced even more pronounced changes in fluorescence intensity (decreased Tomm20 fluorescence and increased LAMP-2 fluorescence), suggesting that hydrogen therapy effectively enhances mitochondrial autophagy and restores cellular homeostasis. The Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozyme exhibited an even more significant enhancement in mitochphagy.

The PINK1-Parkin-LC3 pathway is a critical pathway in mitochondrial autophagy. In damaged mitochondria, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, recruiting parkin for phosphorylation, further mediating binding to LC3, and ultimately degrading damaged mitochondria [47]. To further substantiate the occurrence of mitophagy within cells, we performed a dual-fluorescence colocalization analysis of Parkin and LC3B, two pivotal proteins intimately involved in this process (Fig. 7B, D). Upon exposure to H2O2, we observed a pronounced decrease in the expression levels of both Parkin and LC3B, accompanied by a significant reduction in their colocalization coefficient, indicative of suppressed mitophagy activity. Remarkably, nanozymes emerged as effective agents in enhancing the expression of these two mitophagy-related proteins, leading to a notable increase in their colocalization. Furthermore, hydrogen therapy exhibited a profound stimulatory effect on mitophagy, as evidenced by the even more pronounced colocalization of the proteins, underscoring its potential as a potent enhancer of this cellular process. Monitoring autophagic flux is crucial for analyzing autophagy. A handy method for monitoring autophagic flux is the use of tandem fluorescent - labeled LC3 (mTagRFP-mWasabi-LC3) [48]. This method relies on the different pH-stability characteristics of the Wasabi and mRFP fluorescent proteins. Wasabi is sensitive to acid, whereas mRFP is a stable group for fluorescent expression. When autophagosomes enter the second stage, they combine with lysosomes to create autolysosomes. The acidic environment inside autolysosomes causes Wasabi to quench. Based on this, we can detect mitophagy flux. The results revealed that following cellular damage, the occurrence of mitophagy exhibited a significant decline (Fig. 7 C). Both the red and green puncta demonstrated a marked decrease, with the reduction in green puncta being more pronounced, indicating a substantial decrease in the level of early-stage mitophagy. However, upon the addition of nanozymes, there was a notable increase in the number of green puncta, signifying the restoration of early-stage mitophagy. Furthermore, hydrogen therapy led to a significant rebound in both red and green puncta, clearly promoting the recovery of mitophagy.

Then, protein level verification through Western blot analysis revealed that nanozyme treatment significantly enhanced the expression levels of Pink1 and Parkin, with hydrogen therapy exhibiting a more pronounced effect, thus significantly enhancing mitophagy (Fig. 7E). Finally, we evaluated the changes in Collagen II (COLII) and matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) during the treatment of chondrocyte injury, revealing that the Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanozyme exerted the most significant effect in reversing H2O2 -induced cartilage damage (Fig. 7E). These findings suggest that hydrogen-releasing nanozymes significantly promote mitochondrial autophagy, aid in mitigating cellular senescence, and delay the progression of osteoarthritis.

Hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme alleviates osteoarthritis progression in C57BL/6 mice

To further investigate the mitigating effects of hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme on osteoarthritis in vivo, we established a mouse model of osteoarthritis using anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) and further treated them with different nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 7 A, the nanoenzyme (5 mg/ml, 10 µl) was administered via intra-articular injection two weeks post-surgery, once every two days for a duration of two weeks. Control rats that underwent ACLT surgery as OA knee joints and sham-operated rats representing normal knee joints were treated with saline at the same time points. The mice were sacrificed eight weeks after ACLT surgery. The HE staining of the five vital organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) provided compelling evidence that the nanozyme exhibited no notable in vivo toxicity (Fig S6B). By conducting in-vivo imaging of the knee joint and detecting the contents of Se and Fe within it, we have found that the nanozyme is capable of exerting long-lasting effects in the articular cavity (Fig. 7B, C). Meanwhile, we also successfully detected hydrogen release from nanozymes within the joint cavity by employing the methylene blue reduction method (Figure S8).

Visualization of the joints in each group using micro-CT image reconstruction revealed that the ACLT group exhibited narrowed joint spaces, significant cartilage degeneration, and increased osteophyte formation (Fig. 8D). However, the addition of nanoenzymes significantly improved the situation, with hydrogen therapy bringing the bone morphology closer to the natural group, exhibiting less osteophyte formation and lower maturity. The adjuvant effect of chondroitin sulfate in Se-HMPB@AB@COS further alleviated cartilage degeneration. Osteophyte scoring was primarily reflected in the size and maturity of the osteophytes. We observed that the ACLT group developed prominent osteophytes with high size and maturity, indicating the success of our model. After nanoenzyme treatment, a significant reduction in osteophyte size and maturity was observed, but the addition of hydrogen therapy showed a more pronounced therapeutic effect (Fig. 8 F). Moreover, the measurement trends of subchondral bone plate thickness (SBP.Th) in all five groups were consistent with the osteophyte scoring results (Fig. 8 F). In the mouse model of osteoarthritis, BV/TV is an essential parameter to quantify the ratio of bone tissue volume to total volume. It is typically expressed as a percentage, calculated by dividing the bone volume by the total volume [49]. Quantitative results showed that the BV/TV values for the ACLT and Sham groups were 32.1% and 62.9%, respectively. The BV/TV values for the Se-HMPB and Se-HMPB@AB groups were 36.7% and 42.6%, respectively. Notably, the Se-HMPB@AB@COS nanoenzyme group had a BV/TV value of 48.7%, closest to the Sham group (Fig. 8 F).

Subsequently, we performed fixation, embedding, and slicing on the knee joint specimens collected eight weeks post-surgery, followed by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Safranin-O/Fast Green (SO-FG) staining for histological observation (Fig. 7E). The H&E staining revealed cracks and deformations on the articular surface in the ACLT group, indicating two of the most severe forms of articular cartilage erosion. While the Se-HMPB and Se-HMPB@AB groups also exhibited some erosion, the Se-HMPB@AB group showed less damage to the cartilage surface, and the Se-HMPB@AB@COS group exhibited even better morphological integrity, resembling the Sham group. In the SO-FG staining, the ACLT group displayed a significant decrease in glycosaminoglycan and proteoglycan content. However, after the application of nanoenzyme treatment, a remarkable recovery was observed. Combining the results from 3D CT scans and histological staining, we assessed the severity of OA using the OARSI scale. Compared to the ACLT group, the OARSI scores in the Se-HMPB and Se-HMPB@AB groups decreased by 13.4% and 41.5%, respectively. The addition of hydrogen therapy yielded an extremely significant therapeutic effect on osteoarthritis. Notably, the Se-HMPB@AB@COS group exhibited the most significant reduction, with an OARSI score decrease of 59.7%.

Inflammation and degenerative changes play pivotal roles in the development of osteoarthritis (OA). In a chronic pro-inflammatory environment, chondrocytes express catabolic and proinflammatory phenotypes, resulting in excessive production of proteolytic enzymes and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. These cytokines reduce the chondrocytes’ ability to produce ECM while significantly enhancing their catabolic activities, ultimately leading to ECM degradation and OA progression [50]. We employed ELISA kits to detect the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in mouse knee joints. The ACLT group exhibited significantly elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α, indicating an increased inflammatory response. Immunofluorescence staining also revealed a notable increase in the expression of the inflammatory protein COX-2 in the ACLT group. Concurrently, we observed an elevation in the expression of the ECM-degrading protein MMP13 and a decrease in the expression of the synthetic protein COLII (Fig. 9 A, B). MMP13, not only represents a key matrix-degrading enzyme in OA, but its expression level is often used as an indicator of OA severity. Moreover, MMP13 effectively degrades the primary collagen component, type II collagen, in articular cartilage [51]. Treatment with nanoenzymes effectively reduced the inflammatory levels in the tissues. We observed a decrease in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and an increase in the expression of the inflammatory protein COX-2. The therapeutic effects of Hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme (Se-HMPB@AB and Se-HMPB@AB@COS) were even more pronounced, suggesting that hydrogen therapy can effectively alleviate inflammation during OA treatment. Additionally, hydrogen therapy significantly reduced MMP13 protein secretion and enhanced COLII protein synthesis. The Se-HMPB@AB@COS group induced the optimal type II collagen secretion, approaching levels similar to the natural group. We also examined the expression levels of mitochondrial autophagy proteins Pink1 and parkin in the tissues, and the results were consistent with the COLII expression levels, indicating that nanoenzymes can effectively protect mitochondria, and hydrogen therapy can effectively prevent mitochondrial dysfunction and promote autophagy.

Conclusion

This study innovatively employed a nanoenzyme drug delivery system to achieve sustained hydrogen therapy in the treatment of osteoarthritis. The zero-valence selenium-riched Prussian blue (Se-HMPB) nanoenzyme achieves the role of anti-oxidative stress. Additionally, it can deliver hydrogen therapy through drug loading, enhancing anti-oxidative stress capabilities while also protecting mitochondrial function. In our research, we further discussed and verified that hydrogen therapy can promote mitophagy and inhibit the senescence of chondrocytes in osteoarthritis. For practical applications, we encapsulated the nanomaterials with chondroitin sulfate, enhancing the nanoenzyme’s capacity to promote extracellular matrix synthesis while inhibiting degradation, aligning with clinical needs. Based on the above, we have successfully synthesized the Se-HMPB@AB@COS hydrogen-releasing nanoenzyme, which exhibits robust antioxidant properties and significantly inhibits inflammation in osteoarthritis. Moreover, it safeguards mitochondrial function, eliminating damaged mitochondria through mitophagy, notably suppressing senescence phenotypes, and delaying the progression of osteoarthritis. These findings underscore the promising potential of nanotherapy and hydrogen therapy as novel treatment modalities for osteoarthritis.
