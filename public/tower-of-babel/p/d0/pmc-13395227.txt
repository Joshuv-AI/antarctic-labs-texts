Transient, dose-dependent QTc prolongation with lurasidone in an older woman with schizophrenia: A case report
Authors: Natarajan Varadharajan, Manjil Bharati, Shubam Shree Patyal, Ravi Philip Rajkumar, Vikas Menon
Published: 2026

Sir,

QTc prolongation in susceptible individuals is linked to torsade de pointes (TdP), which can cause ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.[1] A recent meta-analysis showed a pooled prevalence of 4% for QTc prolongation in antipsychotic-treated patients with schizophrenia; female sex and older age were important risk factors.[2] Among second-generation antipsychotics, lurasidone, paliperidone, and brexipiprazole are understood to have a low propensity for QTc prolongation, with lurasidone associated with the lowest risk.[3] We describe an older woman with schizophrenia who experienced a transient, self-limiting, dose-dependent QTc prolongation with oral lurasidone.

A 59-year-old woman presented with a 30-year episodic illness. The current episode, lasting 18 months, was characterized by persistent delusions, auditory hallucinations, disorganized behavior, and significant socio-occupational dysfunction. She was diagnosed with schizophrenia, multiple episodes, as per the International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11).[4] Her baseline Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score was 103. Her six previous episodes had responded to combined treatment with oral haloperidol (dose details unavailable) and electroconvulsive therapy. During the current episode, she developed significant persistent QTc prolongation (518 ms) and arrhythmias with oral aripiprazole alone (10 mg), necessitating its discontinuation. No contributory past, personal, or family/medical history was elicited.

We sought a cardiology consultation before initiating antipsychotics for the current episode. The baseline QTc was 441 ms. A trial of oral risperidone 2 mg raised her QTc to 511 ms without any clinical symptoms. We discontinued the medication, and after 4 days, the QTc was 411 ms. The electrocardiogram (ECG), 2D echocardiogram, serum electrolytes, and liver and renal function tests were normal. The serum calcium level was borderline at 8.8 mEq/L, so we provided calcium supplementation. After 1 week, it rose to 9.5 mEq/L. Given her elevated body mass index (27 kg/m2), we initiated her on oral lurasidone 20 mg due to its relatively favorable metabolic and cardiac safety profile. The QTc interval increased in a dose-dependent manner to 479 ms and 490 ms with lurasidone 40 and 60 mg, respectively. In the absence of clinical symptoms related to QTc prolongation and need for symptom control, and following consultation with the cardiology team, we cautiously escalated lurasidone to 80 mg with serial ECG monitoring.

The transient QTc elevation resolved after 2 weeks, with QTc decreasing to 449 ms. Hence, lurasidone was hiked to 100 mg; the QTc again rose to 498 ms and subsequently reduced to 423 ms. The Naranjo nomogram score was 8, indicating a probable drug adverse reaction.[5] We discharged her on oral lurasidone 100 mg. At discharge, her PANSS score had reduced to 36, reflecting marked symptomatic and functional improvement. The patient has remained under regular follow-up with periodic QTc monitoring, serum calcium, and electrolytes. At 3 months postdischarge, the QTc measured 441 ms. We report this case for two reasons. First, lurasidone is generally regarded as an antipsychotic with a low risk of QTc prolongation.[3] However, the index patient experienced a probable, dose-dependent, asymptomatic, and self-limiting QTc prolongation with lurasidone in the absence of identifiable personal, familial, or metabolic risk factors. Notably, the incidental finding of borderline calcium and its subsequent correction did not prevent further QTc prolongation with dose escalation.

Second, although QTc prolongation was significant, it was transient and did not necessitate a dose reduction or change in medication, unlike before, when QTc was persistently elevated with risperidone and aripiprazole. Our approach was consistent with prior reports that have advocated for a careful risk–benefit analysis when measurable QTc prolongation is encountered with an antipsychotic.[6] In the index case, lurasidone was selected specifically for its relatively favorable metabolic and cardiovascular (QTc) safety profile; discontinuation would have further narrowed already limited treatment options. Close QTc monitoring, together with continuous cardiology liaison, allowed us to recognize the fluctuating and transient nature of QTc changes, enabling continuation of treatment.

To our knowledge, only one prior report of lurasidone-associated QTc prolongation is available.[7] In that report, an adolescent male with schizophrenia developed symptomatic, dose-dependent, QTc prolongation with 80 mg of lurasidone, necessitating its discontinuation. Our report adds to the limited evidence base by documenting dose-dependent QTc changes at lower doses of lurasidone and, importantly, an asymptomatic clinical course that permitted continuation of treatment with close monitoring. Notably, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) evaluation of lurasidone studies also did not show a dose-dependent increase in QTc.[8]

Lurasidone may be associated with transient, dose-dependent QTc prolongation in vulnerable individuals. Close ECG monitoring, cardiology liaison, and judicious dose adjustments may help avert unnecessary discontinuation or switch of antipsychotic medications. As a research implication of our findings, registry-based studies examining patterns and predictors of cardiac conduction abnormalities associated with lurasidone, and, more broadly, other antipsychotics, may inform risk stratification, clinical decision-making, and the prevention of such treatment-limiting adverse effects.
