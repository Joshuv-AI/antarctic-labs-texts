A theoretical approach to the remediation of saline streamflow conditions downstream of the Nwanedi catchment reservoir
Authors: Anesu Dion Gumbo, Evison Kapangaziwiri, Ephias Mugari, Simbarashe Kativhu, Marizvikuru Manjoro, Sagwati Eugene Maswanganye, Lebogang Machimana, Nthaduleni Nethengwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Nwanedi River Catchment in Limpopo Province, South Africa, experiences seasonal salinity accumulation that constrains downstream water use and undermines agricultural productivity. This study develops a hydrology-based framework to define environmental flow requirements (EFRs) to mitigate salinity through flow-driven dilution and flushing. A desktop methodology integrating the Revised Desktop Reserve Model (RDRM), GIS-based catchment characterisation, stakeholder insights, and empirical water quality analysis was implemented. Results reveal a distinct downstream increase in salinity, with electrical conductivity (EC) reaching approximately 146.8 mS/m during the dry season. Modelled EFRs indicate that, under Environmental Management Class D, minimum low-flow allocations correspond to 18.7% and 21.0% of mean annual runoff for sub-catchments A80H and A80J, respectively. Monthly dry-season flow releases range between 0.35 and 1.05 Mm3, while maintaining an average residual volume of approximately 1 Mm3 to support agricultural demand. These environmental flows are derived from naturalised flow regimes and are assumed to re-establish the critical hydrological conditions necessary to dilute and mobilise saline baseflows. The findings, therefore, do not quantify direct reductions in salinity but demonstrate that maintaining these flow thresholds can recreate the hydraulic conditions required for salt flushing, particularly in downstream reaches where salinity accumulation is most pronounced. This study provides a preliminary, process-based foundation for developing adaptive water allocation protocols. It establishes a testable framework for future cause-and-effect analyses, in which controlled dam releases and systematic water-quality monitoring can be used to quantify the extent to which EFRs reduce salinity in semi-arid, data-scarce catchments.Keywords: Agricultural sustainability, Environmental flows, Irrigation efficiency, Saline baseflow, Sustainable practices, Water resources managementSubject terms: Environmental impact, Hydrology

Introduction

Balancing water resources to meet competing demands is a formidable challenge, especially in data-scarce, semi-arid regions. Many southern African countries face chronic water scarcity and require sustainable management of their water resources1. Achieving sustainable water resource management in these settings is a serious challenge. Limited resources (funding, observed data, internet infrastructure, access to high-resolution imagery, skilled personnel, etc.) directly affect sustainable water resources management in the region2. South Africa’s semi-arid climate exacerbates this challenge. Extreme precipitation variability and high evaporative losses exacerbate water scarcity in the country.

The constraints imposed by limited water resources are particularly acute in catchments dominated by water-intensive agricultural activities, where water availability underpins the primary source of livelihood. Due to the country’s semi-arid climate, reservoirs have been constructed to store water during the rainy season for use during the dry season3. However, reservoir construction alters the river’s downstream characteristics and can introduce detrimental conditions by diminishing flow variability4–7. In the Nwanedi catchment, two reservoirs (Luphephe and Nwanedi) were constructed primarily to provide irrigation water to downstream farmers. These two reservoirs are commonly referred to as the Nwanedi reservoir.

In a study by Nell8, streamflow salinity was measured along the river, with downstream regions of the smaller reservoir exhibiting high salinity, as indicated by electrical conductivity (EC) values. In a recent investigation by Kativhu et al.9, their engagement with farmers in the catchment re-highlighted the occurrence of saltwater. Qualitative views from farmers who participated in the study revealed concerns about the salinity levels of the river water and its potential impact on crops. Given these challenges, this study aimed to develop a hydrology-based intervention to mitigate saltwater intrusion in the Nwanedi Irrigation Catchment. The current research, based on Gumbo et al.10, suggests that maintaining the river’s environmental flow requirements (EFRs) can help mitigate saltwater intrusion. This understanding should begin a conversation about good water resource management in the catchment.

Two objectives guided the study:

The assumption is that, if these EFRs are met through strategic dam releases, particularly during the dry season when baseflow contributes more to streamflow, saline baseflow can be diluted and flushed from the system. This will improve water quality and agricultural yields for the many downstream farmers. The theoretical approach applied here is an initial step toward addressing saline streamflow conditions in the catchment. Longitudinal research will be conducted to provide practical releases and monitor salinity levels in the field. As such, this article serves as the baseline for ongoing assessment of the river to reduce salinity in the Nwanedi catchment.

Saline water occurrence in the Nwanedi river basin

Natural and human activities contribute to saline streamflow conditions in inland catchments. Saltwater intrusion is typically recorded in estuaries, but it can also occur in inland catchments11. In natural systems, saline baseflows result from interactions between groundwater and the soil and rock formations beneath the ground12. Anthropogenic activities, particularly irrigated agriculture, significantly amplify salt yields13. Irrigation activities increase river salinity by dissolving soil salts and returning them to rivers via return flows14. Evaporation in hot, dry regions, such as the Nwanedi catchment, leaves salts in the soil, and subsequent irrigation washes them into streams.

Fertilisers increase dissolved ion concentrations, which also enter rivers. These processes raise the ion concentration in river water. Studies indicate that irrigated landscapes can nearly double salinity levels compared to non-irrigated areas15. The geology, land cover, land use, and precipitation dynamics influence how salts are mobilised and transported through river systems16. Knowledge of these characteristics should inform effective water resource management in saline rivers. A critical mechanism driving salinity accumulation in river systems is baseflow17. In catchments with prolonged dry seasons, baseflows account for most of the streamflow present in a river during this period.

The underlying geology often determines the quality and mineral composition of groundwater that contributes to the river. In regions where the geological formations contain high salt content, groundwater inflows can introduce dissolved salts into the river system18. Research in the Upper Colorado River Basin in the United States of America (USA) indicates that up to 89% of river salinity may originate from baseflow19. The interaction between groundwater and high-salinity geological formations resulted in significant salt loading in the catchment, with substantial economic consequences, as annual damages exceeded $300 million19.

However, between 1986 and 2011, the US government undertook regular monitoring of salinity levels in the catchment and implemented remedial strategies. The Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Programme invested substantial funds in management projects that limit salt accumulation in the river system19. The programme supported actions, including the adoption of improved irrigation technologies, the recovery of natural vegetation, and other measures to reduce salinity levels. Rumsey et al.20 reported that approximately 63% of affected catchments experienced a reduction in saltwater levels.

The reduction in salinity levels indicates that the river monitoring provided valuable insights into saltwater accumulation, informing management strategies. In the Nwanedi catchment, similar conditions are evident, with Nell8 reporting saline river flow downstream. It has been more than 10 years since this assessment was conducted, and this study represents the first attempt to address this issue. The current study was driven by stakeholder consultations that provided a qualitative evaluation of the phenomenon. The evidence from these consultations reinforces the urgent need for expanded research and consistent water-quality monitoring to better quantify the scale and severity of salinity.

Study site description

The Nwanedi River Catchment in the Limpopo Province, South Africa (between 30° 20′ 0″ E and 30° 40′ 0″ E; and 22° 10′ 0″ S and 22° 40′ 0″ S), consists of two sub-catchments, identified locally as A80H and A80J, forming part of the greater Limpopo River Catchment (Fig. 1). A80H forms the headwater catchment, with A80J forming the downstream catchment that joins with the Limpopo River. The Nwanedi and Luphephe Rivers, which originate in the Soutpansberg Mountains, converge to form the Nwanedi River (Fig. 1). At the outlets of these rivers, just upstream of their natural confluence, there are the Luphephe Dam (with a capacity of 14.8 Mm3) and the Nwanedi Dam (5.31 Mm3)8. During the wet season, the two reservoirs merge to form Nwanedi Dam. Downstream of the main dam, a smaller impoundment, Cross Dam, further regulates water distribution for irrigation purposes. The river flows from the south towards the northwest and into the Limpopo River.

Map of the Nwanedi River Catchment, showing constituent sub-catchments A80H and A80J. The inset shows the catchment’s location in South Africa, including long-term summary climate data (1964–2023).

The catchment spans a semi-arid climate zone with distinct wet and dry seasons. Rainfall occurs between November and March, with annual fluctuations ranging from 400 to 600 mm in most years. Much of the rainfall is concentrated in December and February, further reducing the rainfall period. The dry season is the more extended period, characterised by extreme evapotranspiration rates exceeding 1400 mm per year21. These high evaporation rates significantly impact crop performance. Crop irrigation is essential in this catchment. The imbalance between precipitation and water loss makes irrigation a crucial component of agricultural sustainability. The Nwanedi River flows northward into the Limpopo River. The reservoirs within it primarily control water resource availability in the catchment. The reservoir was completed in 1964 with a height of 36 m.

These reservoirs ensure that irrigation needs are met despite seasonal variations in river flow8. The Nwanedi Catchment supports extensive agricultural activities, with farmers cultivating crops of national importance, such as maize, tomatoes, vegetables, and fruits22. Many livelihoods in the catchment depend on agricultural activities. Farming is both subsistence and commercial, with many farms drawing irrigation water from the river, thereby supporting both subsistence and commercial production. Due to irrigation, farmers in this catchment can cultivate crops year-round. As such, the catchment is crucial to the country’s economy and food security, and addressing salinity issues within it will improve agro-based livelihoods.

Methods and methodology

Documenting stakeholder insights on saltwater occurrence

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to select five key stakeholders. The selection looked for participants who were actively involved in water management and use within the Nwanedi catchment. The participants included reservoir operators, agricultural researchers with prior experience in local crop value chains, an extension worker familiar with farming constraints across the catchment, an upstream farmer who had benefited from controlled dam outflows, and a downstream farmer located in the salt-affected zone. Co-production of information was a key aspect of the study, as explained by Mugari et al.23. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with the selected stakeholders.

A semi-structured interview guide, covering themes such as water availability, salinity, infrastructure functionality, and irrigation sustainability, was designed and pre-tested to improve clarity and consistency of meaning. All interviews were audio-recorded with informed consent, later transcribed verbatim, and anonymised. A thematic descriptive approach was employed for data analysis. It involved repeated reviews of transcripts to identify recurring patterns and concepts. Verbatim responses were preserved to capture participants’ views. Findings were triangulated with hydrological records and prior assessments to substantiate emerging narratives.

Participatory catchment characterisation and mapping

The participants selected in Sect. 4.1 were provided with maps of the catchment and asked to identify areas where salinity issues have been observed. The maps were later digitised in Google Earth Pro and then visualised in ArcGIS Pro, following the methodology described by Mbendana et al.24. Google Earth Pro (version 7.3.6, Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) was used in accordance with its terms of service, which permit use for academic and non-commercial research purposes. In their study, Mbendana et al.24 demonstrated the utility of applying simple GIS techniques in data-scarce, small-area studies for environmental assessments. The methodology employs participatory mapping to assess changes in land use and land cover. This approach was adopted for this study and proved crucial in identifying the affected portions of the river.

GIS-based catchment characterisation

The physical attributes of the catchment were characterised to understand the current conditions, particularly concerning the occurrence of salinity. Soil and geological data for assessing saltwater ingress from underground sources were obtained from the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform, and Rural Development (DALRRD) in South Africa. These data were processed and analysed using GIS software (ArcGIS Pro) to classify soil texture, permeability, and organic matter content, which were subsequently used to identify areas with high salinity-generating potential.

RDRM model setup

The RDRM was implemented using a structured approach (Gumbo et al.25) that integrates hydrological, hydraulic, ecological, and flood sub-models. Gumbo et al.25 provide a detailed description of the RDRM, including its setup, input parameters, and governing equations for each submodel. In this article, the process is summarised using a conceptual diagram that illustrates the overall functioning of the RDRM (Fig. 2) and a table summarising the input and output variables (Table 1). These sub-models collectively yield a hydrological estimate of probable EFRs by combining simulations of natural flow conditions, hydraulic characteristics, ecological requirements, and flood dynamics. The hydrological sub-model of the RDRM is the foundation that uses naturalised and observed streamflow data. Naturalised streamflow data, representing river flow without human interference, were sourced from the Water Resources 2012 (WR2012) database and cover 90 hydro-years (1920–2009) at monthly intervals.

Historical streamflow data were obtained from flow-gauging stations managed by South Africa’s Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) to assess deviations from natural flows. Both A80H and A80J had datasets from 1964 to 2023 (at the time of this study). These datasets were crucial for simulating the stream’s hydrological behaviour and for understanding long-term water availability in the catchment. The hydraulic sub-model was driven by catchment parameters derived from Google Earth Pro, GIS, and remote-sensing tools26. Key parameters included the geomorphological zone, valley slope fraction, catchment area, and maximum river width. These attributes define the river’s hydraulic characteristics, thereby influencing water flow through the catchment.

The model provided insights into how flow interacts with the physical landscape by incorporating spatial data. This is essential for simulating hydrological responses. The ecological sub-model was run after the hydraulic sub-model. This sub-model is informed by the outputs of the hydrological and hydraulic sub-models, which provide flow and channel characteristics27. When available, ecological data can be incorporated to refine the model’s accuracy in assessing the impact of flow variations on aquatic ecosystems. However, default ecological parameters are predefined and can be automatically generated by the model based on field surveys conducted across South Africa during the model’s development.

Consequently, as demonstrated in this study, its application within South African catchments can adopt a purely desktop approach (utilising datasets within the model surveyed across the country) or employ field surveys to populate the ecological parameter values28. Finally, the flood sub-model simulated flood events across the sub-catchments. This model utilised synchronised inputs from hydrological, hydraulic, and ecological sub-models to estimate flood magnitude, duration, and frequency. Understanding floods is crucial to ensuring that e-flow recommendations account for extreme hydrological events. The RDRM offers a comprehensive assessment of e-flows within the catchment by integrating these four sub-models. A summary of the inputs, parameters, and outputs for each sub-model is presented in Table 1.

This approach facilitated the evaluation of water availability, hydraulic interactions, ecological requirements, and flood risks, ensuring that decisions regarding water resource management were informed by comprehensive scientific analysis. The ultimate step of the model is the sensitivity analysis, which was initiated after several runs of the modelling process. The key sensitive parameters identified were baseflow separation BFα (0.95–0.98) and BFβ (0.42–0.44), the Flow Duration Curve (FDC) percentage points used for maximum baseflows (5, 10, 15, 20), and the width/depth scaling parameter (0.45, 0.5, 0.55). These values were carefully selected, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted to validate the study’s results. Table 2 presents the values used in this study for the two sub-catchments.

The sensitivity analysis targeted these parameters because they exert primary control over baseflow partitioning, ecological benchmark flows, and hydraulic geometry, which collectively determine environmental flow outcomes. Small variations in these inputs propagate across hydrological, hydraulic, and ecological components, producing measurable changes in stress indices and habitat simulations. Their selection, therefore, ensured that the analysis captured the most influential sources of uncertainty within the RDRM framework.

Model performance assessment

The evaluation of model performance followed a structured, multi-layered approach that prioritised process representation over conventional calibration, given the limited availability of observed ecological datasets within the study area. This approach recognises the inherent constraints of data-scarce environments and positions the RDRM as a process-based assessment tool rather than a strictly data-driven predictive model. The hydrology sub-model was assessed by examining the extent to which simulated naturalised flow regimes reproduced regional hydrological signatures. Attention was paid to seasonality and interannual variability, as these characteristics govern ecological functioning and water availability. Consistency with expected flow patterns indicated that the model adequately captured basin-scale hydrological dynamics.

The hydraulic sub-model evaluation focused on the realism of derived channel geometry and associated roughness parameters. These outputs were compared against expected geomorphic conditions for the designated zones, ensuring that simulated cross-sections and resistance factors aligned with established regional channel forms. This step ensured that the physical template underpinning habitat simulation remained credible. The ecological sub-model was evaluated through the plausibility of simulated stress–flow relationships. The resulting habitat response patterns were compared with established findings reported in the literature, thereby confirming that the model produced ecologically meaningful outputs despite the absence of detailed species-level data.

The flood sub-model assessment examined whether peak discharge estimates and scaling relationships corresponded with known flood behaviour in comparable hydro-climatic regions. This evaluation ensured that high-flow processes and flood magnitudes remained within realistic bounds. At the integrated level, overall model performance was assessed using sensitivity analysis and scenario. Parameter perturbations were systematically introduced to evaluate model responsiveness, with particular focus on the stability of environmental flow requirement outputs. Consistent responses across simulations, combined with ecologically plausible results, were interpreted as evidence of model robustness. This evaluation framework emphasises internal coherence and process fidelity rather than strict statistical validation, aligning with the intended application of the RDRM to support rapid EFRs in contexts where high-resolution field data remain unavailable.

Empirical evidence

Water quality data were obtained from the South African Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) for six monitoring stations located along the Nwanedi River. These stations, identified by their site codes 90386, 90381, 194543, 194544, 194545, and 194546, were selected based on data availability and their spatial distribution along the river continuum. The data acquisition process involved obtaining departmental approval and emailing the files, from which historical measurements were downloaded in tabular format.

Electrical conductivity (EC), measured in microSiemens per metre (mS/m), was used as the primary variable to assess the presence and variability of saltwater along the river29. EC is a widely accepted proxy for salinity in freshwater systems because it is sensitive to dissolved ionic concentrations. Data were compiled for the period ranging from 1968 to 2025, depending on the availability of records for each site. The EC values were then statistically summarised to determine the minimum, maximum, mean, and median conductivity at each location. These metrics were used to infer spatial and temporal patterns of saltwater occurrence within the catchment.

Remedial water requirements

The e-flows generated from the modelling process provided monthly (especially for the affected dry months from April to November) remedial water requirements based on Environmental Management Classes (EMCs). An EMC is a classification system used in e-flow assessments to determine the level of ecological protection required for a river system or to define its current state of degradation30. EMC category A is pristine, and EMC category D has undergone critical modifications in the case of the RDRM. Other e-flow calculators, such as the Global Environmental Flow Calculator (GEFC), have EMCs ranging from category A to F. EMCs help balance water resource development with environmental sustainability by categorising rivers based on their current condition and desired future state. They guide flow allocations, conservation efforts, and long-term monitoring of river health by defining acceptable levels of river modification30.

Results

Stakeholder insights on saltwater occurrence

The perspectives of five strategically selected stakeholders provided spatially differentiated insights into the sources, causes, and implications of salinity in the Nwanedi River, particularly during the dry season. The dam operator confirmed that upstream water quality originates from the Soutpansberg Mountains and remains generally good. Controlled releases follow a seasonal routine, with water discharged to supply farmers in the A80J irrigation scheme once the rainy season ends. He acknowledged, however, that upstream abstraction remains entirely unregulated:

“Upstream users can take as much water as they want. There are no strict rules on water allocation, and most of the farmers do not have water meters.”

This uncontrolled extraction means that downstream farmers in A80J frequently struggle to access adequate water during the dry period.

A researcher from the University of Venda offered a retrospective account rooted in a 2020 crop value chain study. Although the original research focused on enhancing produce value across both the Nwanedi and Luvhada irrigation schemes, field interactions redirected attention to water quality. He noted:

“While we were talking to farmers in Nwanedi, they kept bringing up the issue of saltwater in the river during the dry season.”

Critically, this concern was entirely absent from interactions in Luvhada, establishing salinity as a problem specific to the Nwanedi context.

The agricultural extension worker contextualised the agronomic pressures shaping irrigation behaviour. Limited rainfall and high evapotranspiration rates concentrate farming activity along riverbanks, while inefficient drip irrigation systems and leaking infrastructure compound water losses. He stressed a fundamental capacity gap:

“Most of the farmers don’t know the actual water requirements of their crops. Once the dam releases water into the river, they just start pumping, even overnight.”

The upstream farmer described an uncoordinated irrigation regime driven by uncertainty over release schedules. Water is extracted opportunistically whenever flow appears in the river, with no metering to track volumes consumed. She reported no direct experience of salinity and confirmed adequate dry-season water availability, though her account implicitly reinforces the unregulated abstraction dynamic identified by the dam operator.

The downstream farmer presented the most direct account of saline intrusion. Saltwater occurrence during the dry season, by his account, is a long-standing and recurring problem, exacerbated by insufficient flows reaching the lower scheme after upstream abstraction. Borehole water offered no viable alternative, as groundwater quality deteriorates markedly in the dry season. His sensory observations were unambiguous:

“You can taste it. The water is salty during that period. And when the river dries up, you can literally see salt on the riverbed. It collects there, visible with the naked eye.”

Participatory mapping results

The Nwanedi irrigation scheme comprises numerous farmers who independently determined their crop choices, irrigation plans, and marketing strategies. The respondents expressed that water quality assessments were inconsistent. They noted that University of Venda students conducted most of the water quality assessments. However, they explained that the results of these studies are not shared with stakeholders for decision-making purposes. Participants also raised concerns about the growing human population and inadequate sanitation infrastructure, which they believed contributed to increased contamination of water sources. The irrigation scheme was reportedly threatened by declining groundwater levels, unpredictable rainfall, and rising temperatures, all of which pose risks to food and water security in the area.

Water scarcity was a recurring challenge, especially during the dry season (March to November), as uncoordinated water use affected downstream farmers. The stakeholders stated that climate change poses a real threat to water availability in this semi-arid catchment. Based on stakeholder insights, the assessment of saline water occurrence along the Nwanedi River revealed distinct spatial variations in its impact on agricultural lands. The farmlands unaffected by saline water are marked green on the map (Fig. 3). These areas were suitable for agricultural activities and had a low risk of salinisation. The farmlands experiencing saline water occurrence during the dry period are highlighted in red (Fig. 3). GIS-based catchment characterisation

The croplands were located along the Nwanedi River, near the water source (Fig. 4a). The geological assessment indicates that in sub-catchment A80H, the Wylliespoort rock formation is dominant, characterised by permeable sedimentary and fractured volcanic rocks, providing good groundwater potential and quality (Fig. 4b). In contrast, sub-catchment A80J is underlain by basalt rock formations containing minerals such as olivine, pyroxene, and feldspar. The soil classification shows variations across the sub-catchments. Class 2 soils, which are humic and found in high-rainfall areas, are present in A80H (Fig. 4c). In A80J, soils are classified as Class 3 and Class 4. Class 3 soils are sandy and have moderate limitations for agriculture, whereas Class 4 soils are shallow and impose harsh conditions for farming.

The geological framework of the Nwanedi Catchment is simplified into broader lithological classes to improve interpretability while retaining hydrological relevance (Table 3). Individual geological units (Fig. 4b) were grouped by dominant hydrogeological properties into five principal classes: unconsolidated deposits, sedimentary formations, intrusive rocks, basaltic formations, and crystalline basement complexes. Unconsolidated deposits are characterised by high permeability and strong groundwater interaction, which enhances baseflow contributions. Sedimentary formations exhibit moderate permeability and support both infiltration and surface runoff processes. Intrusive and basaltic rocks generally exhibit lower permeability, limiting subsurface storage and promoting rapid runoff. Crystalline basement formations exhibit very low permeability, resulting in minimal infiltration and a dominance of quickflow responses.

The streamflow results for A80H and A80J show significant variations between naturalised and present-day flows (at A80H), highlighting the effects of dam regulation on hydrological regimes (Fig. 5). For both stations, baseflow contributions to streamflow were higher during the dry period (May to September), as indicated by the grey dashed line. The higher present-day flows indicate that the dam supplied additional streamflow to meet downstream demands. This regulation sustains streamflow levels during the dry season, ensuring a consistent water supply for downstream users. Water quality and salinity

The participatory and GIS catchment characterisation results suggest a potential for saline groundwater entering the Nwanedi river system. Empirical evidence from historical electrical conductivity (EC) data from legacy monitoring points along the Nwanedi River also confirmed the qualitative assessments carried out. EC became the basis of analysis with 46 samples collected at site 194,546 between 2016 and 2025. EC values at this site were the lowest among all contemporary sites, ranging from 0.21 µS/ to 8.76 mS/m. The mean EC value was 3.27 mS/m, and the median was 2.9 mS/m, suggesting limited salinity influence in this catchment section. At site 90,386, spanning 1968 to 2012 and comprising 350 samples, EC values ranged from 4.5 to 42.3 mS/m, with a mean of 10.8 mS/m and a median of 10.2 mS/m (Fig. 6).

The sampling points (green triangles) from the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) at different locations along the river and their respective Electrical Conductivity (EC) measured in mS/m. Extreme EC values are highlighted in red.

Site 90,381, which had 391 samples from 1988 to 2017, exhibited EC values ranging from 5.45 to 23.5 mS/m, with a mean of 10.6 mS/m and a median of 10.2 mS/m, indicating relatively stable historical salinity conditions. At site 194,543, 46 samples collected from 2016 to 2025 exhibited lower EC values, ranging from 0.8 to 14 mS/m. The mean EC value was 9.37 mS/m, and the median was 9.65 mS/m, showing a stable salinity regime with minimal variability. Further downstream, site 194,545 recorded EC values from 43 samples collected between 2016 and 2025.

Minimum and maximum EC values were 1.04 mS/m and 100.3 mS/m, respectively. Although the mean EC was relatively low at 24.2 mS/m, the median was high at 66.55 mS/m, indicating a right-skewed distribution with frequent high-salinity events that could influence the river’s ecological health during dry periods. At the stream site 194,544, 43 samples collected between 2017 and 2025 revealed considerable variability, with EC values ranging from 2.94 to 146.8 mS/m. The mean EC value at this location was 68.8 mS/m, while the median was 64.5 mS/m, indicating a generally elevated salinity profile with potential episodes of higher salt loading.

The EC values demonstrate a clear spatial gradient in salinity along the Nwanedi River, with downstream sections exhibiting higher and more variable conductivity. In contrast, upstream sections exhibit lower and more stable salinity. Historical records suggest that while salinity has fluctuated over time, recent years have seen higher peaks at some locations, potentially indicating that changes in land use, water abstraction, or climatic factors are influencing salinity in the river system.

It should be noted that EFRs were determined using naturalised flows, which reconstruct the flow regime as it would exist in the absence of human disturbance, providing an ecologically meaningful baseline for flow prescription. The premise is that maintaining flows that approximate natural conditions will restore physical and chemical processes, including salinity dynamics, that sustain ecosystem function. Actual flow releases and salinity monitoring will allow real-time adjustments in response to observed salinity conditions, making explicit salinity representation a managed outcome rather than a modelling prerequisite.

Discussion

Stakeholder testimonies provided a foundational understanding of saltwater occurrences and the broader water quality situation in the Nwanedi River Catchment. Participants’ initial remarks on salinity issues from another study conducted in the catchment informed the design of this study. These accounts exposed critical governance and monitoring deficiencies. Fragmented hydrological data and erratic water quality assessments have compromised institutional oversight. This is common in many regions of the Global South that lack the necessary infrastructure to monitor their water resources. Farmers, extension officers, and dam operators reported increasing salinity levels during the dry season, which was particularly detrimental to downstream users.

Their experiences indicated that poor water management was a primary driver of deteriorating water quality. These observations mirror broader trends documented across semi-arid southern Africa31. Rural communities in the region often rely on experiential assessments of water suitability because of the absence of consistent, real-time monitoring infrastructure32–34. Although qualitative, stakeholder insights in this study were not treated as speculative or peripheral. Instead, they were systematically validated using participatory mapping, GIS-based catchment characterisation, and empirical evidence on water quality35. Through simple mapping exercises, farmers identified downstream reaches associated with increased salt exposure.

These claims were then spatially analysed and confirmed using geological and hydrological overlays. Sub-catchment A80H, situated at a higher elevation and underlain by sandstone formations, showed greater groundwater recharge potential and better drainage capacity. In contrast, sub-catchment A80J is predominantly composed of basaltic rocks. This rock formation exhibits poor drainage and a higher risk of salt accumulation, findings consistent with those of Muavhi et al.36 and Raiber et al.37. These lithological and drainage characteristics, long understood to modulate salinity risks in semi-arid regions38–40, further strengthened the credibility of the stakeholder insights.

Empirical water quality data obtained from the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) added another layer of confirmation. A spatial salinity gradient was observed based on EC measurements at six sampling points along the river. The points in the catchment’s headwater regions exhibited low conductivity values. This suggests that the headwaters of the Soutpansberg Mountains contribute high-quality water41, consistent with the geological assessment. However, salinity levels increased progressively downstream of the dam. At the most downstream sampling site, EC values were elevated and aligned closely with areas that both stakeholders and GIS mapping had independently identified as salt-affected.

Poor-quality irrigation water affects crop yields, alters soil physical properties, increases fertiliser requirements, and reduces the performance and longevity of irrigation systems42. Among the various water quality concerns, salinity hazard, measured as EC, is the most influential determinant of crop productivity. When EC rises, plants experience “physiological drought.” Physiological drought occurs when high salt concentrations in the soil solution outcompete roots for water. This leaves less usable water available even when soils appear moist43. This phenomenon is particularly detrimental for crops sensitive to salinity, such as tomatoes, a significant cash crop grown in the Nwanedi Catchment.

In this study, recorded EC levels were around 146.8 mS/m. These values fall within the range that is considered detrimental. At these levels, tomato plants exhibit visible signs of stress, including leaf burn, marginal necrosis, and reduced vegetative growth, all of which impair photosynthesis and fruit development44. The cumulative effect is a decline in fruit size, which threatens food security and farmers’ incomes. These findings indicate that sustainable tomato production relies on effective agronomic practices and the monitoring and management of irrigation water quality, as salinity hazards ultimately determine the long-term productivity of both soils and crops45.

The consistency between lived experiences, spatial mapping, and DWS empirical data presents a compelling case for the reliability of participatory methods in data-scarce regions46. Nonetheless, the DWS data, while helpful, exhibited significant data gaps and inconsistent sampling frequencies, a common issue in water resources management in southern Africa. The role of local stakeholders in diagnosing water quality issues cannot be overstated. Their knowledge, although qualitative, offers a valid foundation for action, especially when triangulated with geospatial and geological data.

These findings support the view that integrated, multi-method approaches can provide meaningful insights even without high-frequency empirical datasets. A deeper examination of the system revealed that the core problem is not necessarily physical water scarcity, but rather mismanagement of water resources. While dam release volumes between April and November exceeded 6.07 million cubic metres, downstream degradation persisted. This disconnect between the water released and its actual impact highlights systemic inefficiencies in water distribution and use47. Stakeholders frequently cited limited knowledge of crop water requirements and poor irrigation scheduling, both of which contributed to water wastage.

Similar patterns have been reported in other semi-arid, irrigation-dependent regions48, where low awareness and outdated practices contribute to unsustainable water use. The study modelled environmental flow requirements using the RDRM and naturalised streamflow data for both sub-catchments to address these challenges. The mean annual runoff (MAR) was estimated at 25.09 Mm3 for A80H and 27.88 Mm3 for A80J. Notably, A80J, the lower sub-catchment, contributes approximately 2.8 Mm3 to the total natural flows at the outlet. This can be explained by its low precipitation, high evapotranspiration rates, and limited groundwater yield (as described in the study site description).

Environmental Management Classes (EMCs) were used to set monthly e-flow requirements, with the catchment classified as Class D due to evident anthropogenic pressures. While higher ecological classes (e.g., C or B) would improve environmental outcomes, they would also constrain water availability for agriculture unless trade-offs are actively negotiated49. The desktop modelling approach used here cannot fully capture system dynamics but remains a valuable decision-support tool. The derived values can serve as a basis for guiding water-allocation trade-offs and for structuring water-sharing arrangements. These targets also highlight seasonal shifts in demand; for instance, irrigation needs decline in April due to recession flows and spike in October–November, when natural flows are at their lowest.

The study recommends introducing crop-specific water foot-printing to guide dam release schedules. This will ensure that the irrigation supply aligns with crop calendars and field-level requirements50. The absence of actual water requirements makes the catchment vulnerable to water wastage. Anticipatory dam operations can significantly enhance water-use efficiency and reduce dependence on marginal water sources51. Equally important is the installation of gauging stations in A80J and the routine sampling of EC or ion concentrations. Such empirical monitoring would improve model calibration and enhance the robustness of stakeholder-informed diagnostics.

Complementary socioeconomic studies should explore the obstacles to adopting water-efficient technologies, promoting locally adapted solutions, such as low-cost drip systems, and targeted farmer training52. These environmental flow requirements must be urgently tested by releasing the recommended volumes and evaluating the ecological responses. However, this is usually difficult to achieve in a water-scarce, resource-limited catchment such as the Nwanedi. As such, these results and recommendations remain theoretical until they can be implemented in the catchment. Ultimately, the salinity challenge in the Nwanedi River Catchment reflects a broader governance challenge.

For instance, these issues remain invisible on national agendas, potentially because they affect marginalised rural communities with limited political influence. However, the persistence of saline water threatens agricultural productivity and rural livelihoods. This study, thus, offers a scalable approach for identifying and addressing salinity risks in data-poor river systems. It deployed a low-cost, community-informed methodology validated by GIS and empirical data. The path forward must now focus on translating these findings into institutional reforms that are grounded in science, guided by local experience, and aimed at long-term sustainability.

Study limitations

This study operates within constraints arising from its reliance on limited empirical datasets and the absence of continuous, high-resolution monitoring across the Nwanedi River Catchment. The analysis draws on available water quality records, stakeholder knowledge, and spatial datasets, which, although systematically integrated, do not fully capture temporal variability or short-term hydrological dynamics. The application of the RDRM and the derivation of environmental flow requirements therefore remain theoretical, as no field-based validation through controlled flow releases or ecological response monitoring has yet been undertaken. In addition, uncertainties associated with parameter estimation, particularly in the hydrological and ecological sub-models, may influence the precision of simulated outputs. Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the analytical value of combining participatory knowledge with geospatial and limited empirical evidence in data-scarce environments. This approach establishes an initial, evidence-informed basis for dialogue on sustainable water allocation, while also identifying critical gaps in monitoring and governance that require targeted intervention.

Conclusion

This study provides a theoretical foundation for understanding the occurrence of saline water and the challenges associated with water resource management in the Nwanedi Catchment. The study identified inefficiencies in water use and the significant role of unregulated extractions in exacerbating downstream salinity issues by reducing river flows. The study used hydrological modelling, stakeholder engagement, and GIS-based assessments. While dam releases currently sustain agricultural activities, their effectiveness is undermined by inefficient irrigation practices such as flooding and over-abstraction upstream, necessitating improved regulatory and management strategies. The study highlights the need to adopt an integrated approach to water management that aligns agricultural demands with e-flow requirements.

Implementing functional flow strategies, as outlined by Gumbo et al.25, will design environmental flow releases that mimic the timing, magnitude, and variability of natural flow regimes, thereby promoting periodic flushing of accumulated salts and preventing their concentration in downstream reaches. Such managed flow pulses enhance dilution and sustain the channel, thereby collectively regulating salinity levels and stabilising water quality in the catchment. These targeted releases would facilitate flushing of saline water from downstream areas, thereby ensuring the sustainability of both agricultural productivity and riverine ecosystems. Water managers can optimise release volumes and timing by incorporating stakeholder input to balance agricultural water needs with ecological health, thus safeguarding livelihoods. Encouraging participatory decision-making fosters a cooperative approach to water governance, promoting sustainable and equitable water allocation.

Improving irrigation efficiency is a key water management intervention for mitigating the occurrence of saline water. The continued reliance on outdated and inefficient flood irrigation systems contributes significantly to downstream water scarcity. Continuous water-quality monitoring, particularly of salinity downstream of the Cross Dam, is essential for informing adaptive water management. Regular salinity assessments would provide empirical data to guide timely adjustments in water release strategies and abstraction controls. This real-time monitoring framework would enable proactive interventions to prevent salinity levels from reaching critical thresholds that could jeopardise crop production and river health. Further studies should investigate socioeconomic barriers to the adoption of improved irrigation technologies and explore policy frameworks to enforce sustainable water-use practices. Strengthening collaborative water management among policymakers, researchers, and local stakeholders will ensure long-term water security and agricultural resilience in the Nwanedi Catchment.
