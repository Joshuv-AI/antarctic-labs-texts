Pulmonary metastasectomy for malignant melanoma prolongs survival significantly compared to non-surgical therapy
Authors: Georg Schlachtenberger, Abraham Goitom, Andres Amorin, Alberto Lopez Pastorini, Julika Merres, Christopher Gaisendrees, Matthias B Heldwein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary metastasectomy (PM) is an established treatment for selected patients with pulmonary metastases. While systemic therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, and stereotactic radiation have become standard for unresectable or metastasized melanoma patients, no randomized controlled trials directly assess the benefit of PM by itself in oligometastatic melanoma patients with lung metastases. This retrospective study compares long-term survival outcomes in patients undergoing PM versus those receiving non-surgical therapy for pulmonary metastatic melanoma.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed melanoma patients from the skin cancer center Cologne who were treated with curative intent for their primary tumor and later developed isolated pulmonary metastases. Patients in the non-surgical group had biopsy-confirmed lung metastases. Survival outcomes between patients undergoing PM and those receiving non-surgical therapies were compared.ResultsBetween 2012 and 2020, 87 patients met the inclusion criteria: 49 underwent PM and 38 received non-surgical therapy. The number of pulmonary metastases differed significantly between groups (1.27 ± 0.57 vs. 3.2 ± 1.1; p < 0.001). BRAF-mutations were more common in the non-surgical group (35.4% vs. 55.3%; p = 0.04).Patients undergoing PM showed significantly improved 3-year (88.0% vs. 65.7%; p = 0.04) and 5-year survival rates (81.3% vs. 30.8%; p = 0.002) compared to the non-surgical group. Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed prolonged overall survival in the PM group (log-rank p < 0.02). However, among patients with BRAF mutations, survival did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.09).ConclusionsPM is associated with favorable overall survival. These findings support considering PM as a treatment option in selected patients to improve long-term outcomes.Graphical abstract Keywords: Pulmonary metastasectomy, Isolated pulmonary metastases, Melanoma, Long-term survival

Introduction

Malignant melanoma is a cancer originating from melanocytes in the epidermis, with increasing incidence rates, particularly among Caucasians. Cutaneous melanoma is primarily associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure from natural sunlight and indoor tanning; however, several melanoma subtypes arise independently of UV exposure [1]. Early-stage disease, such as stage I melanoma, carries a favorable prognosis, with 5-year overall survival (OS) rates exceeding 90% [2]. The lungs represent the most frequent site of distant metastasis, being affected by over 30% of patients with stage IV melanoma [3, 4]. Historically, metastatic melanoma was associated with poor outcomes due to the limited effectiveness of chemotherapy, resulting in 5-year survival rates of less than 10% [5, 6]. Pulmonary metastasectomy (PM), a surgical procedure performed with curative intent in carefully selected patients with limited disease burden and resectable metastases, has been shown to improve survival outcomes. [7–9]. In these cases, median survival times of up to 18.3 months and 5-year survival rates approaching 35% have been reported [3, 7, 10]. In recent years, systemic therapies have markedly evolved, particularly with the introduction of targeted therapies (e.g., BRAF and MEK inhibitors), incorporated into European guidelines since 2012, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors), included since 2011, significantly improving survival outcomes in patients with metastatic melanoma. Contemporary 5-year survival rates with these agents now reach approximately 34% [4, 11, 12]. Given these advances, the role of PM in the management of pulmonary metastatic melanoma is being reconsidered. Despite its potential benefits, PM remains underutilized and is largely absent from current melanoma treatment guidelines. This may be due in part to limited awareness among dermatologists and the absence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing PM to non-surgical treatments [13, 14]. Furthermore, no retrospective studies have previously compared survival outcomes between these two treatment strategies.

This study aimed to evaluate overall survival in patients with isolated pulmonary metastatic malignant melanoma by treatment modality. Specifically, we retrospectively compared long-term survival outcomes between patients who underwent pulmonary metastasectomy as first treatment for isolated melanoma lung metastases and those who received non-surgical therapies.

Methods

Ethic statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University Hospital of Cologne and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the analysis, the requirement for written informed consent was waived on September 15, 2022.

All patients diagnosed with metastatic melanoma and treated at the skin cancer center of the University Hospital of Cologne between January 2012, and December 2020 were included in this retrospective analysis. Medical records were reviewed, and relevant clinical, therapeutic, and follow-up data were extracted from the institutional patient database. Collected variables included perioperative details, systemic therapy administration (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and checkpoint inhibitors), and long-term survival outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion only if they had only pulmonary metastases and no affection of other organ structures. Additionally, the primary melanoma must have been treated with curative intent. Patients with 1–5 pulmonary metastases were included. In the non-surgical therapy group, inclusion required histological confirmation that radiologically suspected metastases originated from the corresponding melanoma, typically obtained through CT-guided biopsy. The interval from diagnosis—established by CT-guided biopsy and subsequent histological examination—to treatment initiation in the non-surgical group was minimal, though a short delay was inevitably present.

These stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria were established to ensure a well-matched patient population across both treatment arms, thereby minimizing confounding variables and allowing for a more accurate comparison of long-term survival outcomes.

Diagnosis and treatment

The attending dermatologist initially evaluated patients with melanoma and suspected newly emerged pulmonary metastases. Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET-CT) was performed in patients with suspected distant metastases [14].

Surgical resection was considered only for patients with adequate cardiopulmonary fitness and when an R0 resection was highly achievable.

In cases where patients declined surgery or when metastases were located in positions requiring extensive lung resections (e.g., bilobectomy or pneumonectomy) to achieve R0 resection, alternative treatments such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiation therapy, or checkpoint inhibitors were administered.

The most common surgical approach for pulmonary metastases was wedge resection performed via uniportal video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). Lymphadenectomy was performed only in cases where CT/PET-CT imaging indicated suspected mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

In cases of bilateral pulmonary metastases, surgical management was consistently performed in two stages to minimize perioperative risk and allow adequate recovery between procedures.

In the non-surgical group, all patients were treated in accordance with melanoma guidelines. As targeted therapies (BRAF and MEK inhibitors) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors) were incorporated into European guidelines in 2012 and 2011, respectively, both were available throughout the study period [13–15].

Statistical analysis

Follow-up data were obtained from our institutional patient database. One-year, three-year, and five-year survival rates were calculated for the pulmonary metastasis (PM) group, measured from the time of surgery to the date of death. In the non-surgical therapy group, survival rates were measured from the time of definitive pathological diagnosis of pulmonary metastases to the initiation of non-surgical therapy, and from that point until death. The mean overall survival following PM surgery or the diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary metastases in the non-surgical therapy group was assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. Cox regression model was assessed for multivariable survival analysis described by hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The disease-free interval (DFI) was also measured, defined as the duration between the surgery for the primary tumor and the onset of pulmonary metastases. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Additionally, a multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed, including age at the time of metastasis diagnosis and the number of metastases as covariates. Differences were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean follow-up duration for the cohort was 40.8 ± 27.2 months. Figure 1 outlines the patients excluded from further analysis based on the applied inclusion and exclusion criteria. Pulmonary metastases therapy

Table 3 presents data on the therapy of pulmonary metastases. Wedge resection was the most common approach among patients undergoing surgery, performed in 84.0% of cases. Segmentectomy was performed in 4.0%, and lobectomy in 12.0% of patients. R0 resection was achieved in 100% of surgical patients. Postoperative complications included thoracotomy for pleural fistula in 1.2% and pneumonia requiring intravenous antibiotics in 4.1% of patients. The mean postoperative in-hospital stay was 4.5 ± 1.7 days, with no hospital readmissions reported within 90 days. The 30-day mortality rate was 0%. Notably, 36% of patients in the surgical group received perioperative immunotherapy as part of an oligometastatic treatment strategy. In the non-surgical therapy group, 18.4% of patients received radiation therapy, while 81.6% underwent immune or checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Of these, 39.5% received pembrolizumab, 32.4% received nivolumab, 10.5% received ipilimumab, and 7.9% received ipilimumab + nivolumab. Additionally, 15.8% were treated with encorafenib + binimetinib, and 5.3% with dabrafenib + trametinib. Therapy was interrupted or discontinued in 18.4% of patients due to adverse effects. In the multinomial logistic regression analysis comparing pulmonary metastasectomy (PM) and non-surgical therapy (Table 4), no significant associations were found for age at the time of metastasis diagnosis (OR 1.04, 95% CI: 0.98–1.01; p = 0.28), BRAF status (OR 2.28, 95% CI: 0.38–13.54; p = 0.37), or ECOG status (OR 1.85, 95% CI: 0.26–12.22; p = 0.51). However, the number of metastases was significantly higher in the non-surgical therapy group (OR 8.66, 95% CI: 3.08–24.32; p < 0.001).

At three years, 88% of PM patients were alive, compared to 67.6% of non-surgical therapy patients (p = 0.04). The five-year survival rate was significantly higher for PM patients at 53.4%, compared to 20.0% for non-surgical therapy patients (p = 0.02).

Discussion

This retrospective two-arm study on the treatment of patients with malignant melanoma and isolated pulmonary metastases demonstrated significantly better long-term survival rates for those undergoing pulmonary metastasectomy (PM) compared to patients receiving non-surgical therapy. To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective parallel-arm study to compare these.

Patients with a BRAF mutation showed similar survival rates between PM and non-surgical therapy. The most common surgical approach was VATS wedge resection, performed in 76.2% of patients, with an R0 resection achieved in 100% of cases. Conversely, 18.4% of patients in the non-surgical therapy group had to interrupt or discontinue treatment due to severe side effects.

The average hospital stay for patients undergoing PM was 4.5 ± 1.7 days. The 30-day mortality rate was 0%, and no hospital readmissions occurred within 90 days post-discharge, suggesting that PM is a safe procedure.

Are RCTs to be expected?

Although PM is widely accepted as potentially curative in selected cases of colorectal carcinoma, sarcoma, and malignant melanoma, this acceptance persists despite the absence of robust randomized controlled trial (RCT) data [16]. The only RCT conducted to date, by Treasure et al., was terminated prematurely in 2019 due to insufficient patient enrollment. The study aimed to compare active surveillance with PM in patients with pulmonary metastatic colorectal carcinoma. Initially, the authors planned to enroll 1500 patients, aiming for 600 participants to undergo 1:1 randomization. Only 155 patients were registered, with just 65 randomized [17]. Notably, many patients declined randomization due to a personal preference for undergoing PM. Given that this was a multicenter RCT involving 24 centers and failed to meet recruitment goals, the feasibility of conducting similar trials in other primary tumor types, such as malignant melanoma, is highly unlikely.

Pulmonary metastasectomy or non-surgical therapy?

Metastatic melanoma had a grim prognosis before the advent of effective targeted immunotherapies. For decades, the only treatment option was dacarbazine alone or in combination, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 5 to 26% [18, 19]. The recent resurgence in effective treatments for stage IV melanoma has been driven by the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1, as well as novel targeted therapies aimed at BRAF and MEK mutations. Nivolumab and pembrolizumab are the two approved anti-PD-1 drugs for use in metastatic melanoma and many other malignancies, such as lung cancer [20, 21]. Ipilimumab is the only approved anti-CTLA-4 drug.

Combining Ipilimumab with nivolumab yielded the best results, with 5-year survival rates of up to 60% [11, 22]. The combination is associated with more side effects, which can range from mild skin manifestations to life-threatening complications like colitis, neutropenia, agranulocytosis, and cardiac dysfunction [4, 23]. In our cohort, about 18.4% of patients stopped their therapy due to side effects, 21.5% of those were receiving Ipilimumab + Nivolumab.

Finally, patients with BRAF or MEK mutations who received targeted therapy showed a five-year survival rate of 71% [22, 23]. In our cohort, we performed a subgroup analysis of BRAF status, revealing that 55.3% of the non-surgical therapy group had a BRAF mutation. In contrast, only 35.4% of patients in the surgical therapy group had a BRAF mutation. The two groups showed similar survival rates with a log-rank of 0.48 (Fig. 3a). However, targeted therapy induces resistance through several pathways, including BRAF gene amplifications and MEK mutations [4, 24].

Patients with wild-type BRAF or those who have developed resistance have limited treatment options [25, 26]. We demonstrated that patients undergoing PM with wild-type BRAF showed significantly better survival rates than those receiving non-surgical therapy, with a log-rank of 0.02 (Fig. 3b). Therefore, patients with BRAF wild-type tumors should be considered for pulmonary metastasectomy, while those with BRAF-mutant disease may derive greater benefit from less invasive targeted systemic therapy. Most studies dealing with metastasectomy show worse survival rates than our cohort. He et al. included spinal and cerebral metastases and showed survival rates of < 1 year [27]. Hanna et al. included 99 patients with melanoma and pulmonary metastases. They showed 5-year survival rates of 21%. 23% of those received a lobectomy and > 60% a thoracotomy. Furthermore, in more than 12% of operations, the surgeons did not achieve an R0 resection. Lastly, they do not provide information on whether patients received surgery alone or as part of a multimodal approach [6]. We did not perform an extended pulmonary resection because there is no survival advantage to anatomical resection as PM. The majority received a VATS wedge resection. R0 resection was achieved in all cases. And 36% of patients received perioperative immunotherapy. Viehoff et al. showed that the patients benefit particularly from PM if nodules are < 20 mm. With a mean follow-up of 30.8 months, with a two-year survival of 54% [3].In our cohort, the mean lesion size in both groups was < 16 mm.

Our cohort's exceptionally high survival rates are likely due to improved therapy for the primary melanoma, more accurate diagnostics for pulmonary metastases, and more selective patient selection for PM compared to older studies [7, 8, 10].

Which therapy for which patient?

Patients undergoing pulmonary metastasectomy (PM) demonstrated significantly better survival rates compared to those receiving non-surgical therapy. However, it is important to note that these patients are carefully selected. PM should only be considered when a complete (R0) resection is achievable. Additional prerequisites include curative treatment of the primary melanoma, absence of other distant metastases, and adequate pulmonary function [3, 9].

In our cohort, patients with BRAF wild-type status particularly benefited from PM. On the other hand, given the comparable survival rates, patients with BRAF mutations should primarily receive targeted therapy with BRAF inhibitors. Pulmonary metastasectomy should probably be reserved for cases in which systemic therapy fails. We also observed that patients undergoing non-surgical therapy had significantly more pulmonary metastases than those treated with PM. This can be attributed, in part, to the fact that patients with more than three pulmonary metastases are often not referred to thoracic surgeons due to the extent of disease.

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) wedge resection is the gold standard for PM, whereas thoracotomy should be avoided due to its invasiveness. Patients with bilateral pulmonary metastases may be candidates for a two-stage surgical approach.

To ensure comparability, we only included patients with pulmonary metastases in both groups. Patients deemed unsuitable for surgery should be considered for non-surgical options such as radiotherapy or stereotactic body radiation therapy. In cases where PM is not feasible—particularly in patients with more than five pulmonary or distant metastases—treatment with checkpoint inhibitors, immunotherapy, or chemotherapy is recommended.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that patients with metastatic malignant melanoma selected for pulmonary metastasectomy exhibit favorable survival outcomes compared to those receiving non-surgical therapy. In subgroup analyses, patients with BRAF-mutant tumors showed survival rates comparable to those undergoing pulmonary metastasectomy. Based on these findings, patients with BRAF wild-type tumors should be considered for pulmonary metastasectomy, whereas those with BRAF-mutant disease may derive greater benefit from upfront, less invasive targeted systemic therapy. Furthermore, pulmonary metastasectomy should be considered only when complete (R0) resection is achievable and when extrathoracic metastases have been excluded. Given the limited feasibility of conducting randomized controlled trials in this setting, the present study may contribute to the further establishment of pulmonary metastasectomy as a treatment option.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective, single-center analysis and therefore entails the inherent limitations associated with retrospective designs, including the use of postoperative pathology data. Another limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefor more multicenter investigations are warranted to better elucidate the impact of pulmonary metastasectomy on the prognosis of patients with melanoma. By applying stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, we aimed to establish a highly comparable cohort. Nevertheless, our results may still be influenced by confounding factors, which are inevitable when patients undergo different therapeutic approaches. Another limitation is that the non-surgical group predominantly received immune and targeted therapies, representing a more intensive systemic treatment. Consequently, differences in treatment intensity between groups must be acknowledged.
