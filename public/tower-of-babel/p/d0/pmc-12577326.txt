Conditioned medium from stem cells of human exfoliated deciduous teeth ameliorates mouse ovalbumin-specific allergic dermatitis
Authors: Yang Xu, Noboru Hashimoto, Fumiya Kano, Linze Xia, Cheng Ding, Houjun Lao, Yifei Wang, Takahiro Arai, Tomonori Iwasaki, Akihito Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD), a chronic inflammatory skin disease, is characterized by intense itching and recurrent eczema-like lesions, requiring safer, more targeted therapies that modulate the immune response and restore skin barrier integrity. Conditioned medium derived from stem cells of human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED-CM) exhibits considerable immunomodulatory and regenerative effects. We evaluated the efficacy of SHED-CM in an AD-like mouse model and explored the underlying mechanisms.MethodsMice with ovalbumin (OVA)-specific allergic dermatitis (OSAD) were intravenously treated with SHED-CM, conditioned medium derived from fibroblast, or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, followed by antigen restimulation to induce AD-like dermatitis. Disease severity was assessed macroscopically and histologically. T cell phenotypes were analyzed using immunohistochemical staining, RT-qPCR, and flow cytometry. Total serum and OVA-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels were measured using ELISA. Naïve splenic CD4⁺ T cells were activated using CD3/CD28 beads in SHED-CM to evaluate their direct effects on T cell differentiation through flow cytometry. Highly expressed proteins in SHED-CM were identified using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, and neutralizing antibodies were used to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms.ResultsThe treatment with SHED-CM significantly ameliorated AD-like symptoms, reduced epidermal hyperplasia, and restored filaggrin expression. SHED-CM created an anti-inflammatory microenvironment in OSAD skin and draining lymph nodes by promoting Treg differentiation and inhibiting Th2 and Th17 populations. SHED-CM markedly suppressed B cell maturation, antigen-specific IgE production, and mast cell activation. Furthermore, we found that SHED-CM directly promoted Treg differentiation from naïve CD4⁺ T cells stimulated by CD3/CD28, which was mediated, in part, through TGFβ contained in SHED-CM. SHED-CM exhibited little to no effect on in vitro Th1, Th2, and Th17 differentiation.ConclusionsSHED-CM shows significant therapeutic potential for AD-like symptom, effectively modulating immune response and enhancing skin barrier restoration. Our findings offer a new approach for developing novel, targeted therapies for inflammatory skin disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04735-2.

Background

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease that affects approximately 15%–20% of children and 1%–3% of adults globally [1]. AD affects patients’ quality of life and can result in psychological problems, e.g., depression and anxiety [2]. The underlying causes of AD are complex and diverse, involving genetic and environmental factors, immune imbalance, and impaired skin barrier function [3]. The clinical manifestations of AD include chronic skin inflammation, severe itching, dry skin, and eczematous rash, generally presenting erythema, exudation, and crusting during the acute phase and lichenification in the chronic phase [4]. Immunologically, AD is characterized by a T helper (Th)2 cell-driven immune response, accompanied by the production of elevated levels of immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies and eosinophil infiltration into the skin [5]. Th17 and regulatory T (Treg) cells are also involved in the pathogenesis of AD, wherein Th17 cells aggravate skin inflammation by secreting inflammatory factors [6] and Tregs maintain a balance by inhibiting the immune response. Patients with AD often exhibit increased Th17 and weakened Treg function, which results in immune imbalance and drives disease progression [7].

Current treatments for AD are primarily focused on symptom management and controlling inflammation. Topically applied glucocorticoids and calcineurin inhibitors are widely used drugs that effectively reduce skin inflammation and itching [8]; however, these treatments have limitations. Long-term use of glucocorticoids may result in skin atrophy and immunosuppression, whereas calcineurin inhibitors have a potential risk of tumorigenesis [9]. Therefore, efforts to develop new AD treatments should focus on more targeted and safe therapies, particularly to control the disease through modulating the immune response and promoting skin barrier repair [10].

Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy represents a promising new approach for AD treatment owing to its multipotent differentiation potential and immunomodulatory properties [11]. Although there is no single animal model that fully recapitulates the pathogenesis of human AD, the OVA-specific allergic dermatitis (OSAD) model is relatively well characterized and exhibits several pathogenic features associated with the human disease [12]. MSCs reduce OSAD symptoms by secreting anti-inflammatory factors and modulating the activity of immune cells; thus, decreasing skin inflammation and itching [13]. Bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs) attenuate the severity of OSAD by reducing immune cell infiltration and Interleukin (IL)-4 expression [14]. Human umbilical cord blood-derived MSCs inhibit mast cell degranulation to exert significant protective and therapeutic effects on AD-like mice [15]. In addition, MSCs can improve skin barrier function, which is often compromised in patients with AD [16]. Despite these advances, the long-term efficacy and safety of MSC therapy are unclear, requiring additional clinical trials to establish its therapeutic potential and address potential treatment-associated risks [17].

Stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED), capable to self-renew and multilineage differentiate, are MSCs isolated from children’s deciduous teeth [18]. They can differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes as well as promote tissue repair and immunomodulation by secreting growth factors, cytokines, and exosomes. SHED exhibit a stronger proliferation ability and immunomodulatory function than MSC from other sources, imparting broad application prospects in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [19].

Conditioned medium derived from SHED (SHED-CM) is rich in trophic and immunomodulatory factors and may be effective for the treatment of various intractable diseases. In neurological diseases, SHED-CM promoted nerve cell regeneration and reduced inflammatory responses when applied to experimental spinal cord and brain injury models [20]. Moreover, SHED-CM has shown significant therapeutic effects in animal models of diseases, such as liver damage, cardiovascular disease, and diabetic metabolic disorders [21–23]. In the field of dermatology, SHED-CM accelerates wound healing, improves scar formation, and ameliorates chronic skin ulcers [24, 25]. In this study, we examined the therapeutic effects of SHED-CM in an OSAD mice model and the underlying mechanism for its therapeutic action.

Methods

Conditioned medium (CM) preparation

SHED was prepared as previously described [26]. Briefly, exfoliated deciduous teeth were collected from individuals aged 6–12 years at Tokushima University Hospital (Project name: Development of Regenerative Medicine Using Human Dental Pulp, Periodontal Ligament, and Oral Mucosal Stem Cells. Project Number: 3268. Approval Date: October 10, 2018). The dental pulp was gently collected and digested in a solution containing type I collagenase (3 mg/mL) (Fujifilm Wako, Tokyo, Japan) and dispase (4 mg/mL) (Fujifilm Wako) at 37 °C for 1 h. Single-cell suspensions were prepared and seeded into culture dishes at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich). Then, the cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. The SHED used in this study have been characterized as described previously [26]. To prepare CM derived from fibroblast (Fibro-CM), the human skin fibroblast cell line established from a 36-year-old donor was obtained from the Health Science Research Resource Bank (Osaka, Japan). SHED and fibroblasts at 70%–80% confluence were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and DMEM, followed by medium replacement with DMEM. After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h, CM was collected and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected, and the protein concentration of each CM was measured using Bradford ULTRA (Sigma-Aldrich) and normalized to 3 µg/mL with DMEM.

OSAD model and CM treatments

All animal experiments were performed in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Female 6-week-old BALB/c mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and housed in plastic cages with free access to food and water under specific pathogen-free conditions, ambient temperature (22–24 °C), and a fixed 12 h light-dark cycle. Potential confounders such as the order of treatments or cage location were not specifically controlled in this study.

OSAD was induced based on an established protocol [27]. The experimental unit in this study was a single mouse. The mice were randomly divided into four groups, which included one Sham control group, and three experimental OSAD groups, each group contains 3 to 9 mice based on previous OSAD studies. Each mouse was intraperitoneally injected (i.p) with 10 µg of chicken OVA (Sigma-Aldrich) mixed with 1 mg aluminum hydroxide (AlumVax Hydroxide; OZ Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) in 200 µL of sterilized PBS on days 0, 7, and 14.

On day 14, the mice were anesthetized with a combination of three anesthetics. The mixture consisted of 0.75 mg/kg of medetomidine (Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Koriyama, Japan), 2 mg/kg of midazolam (Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan), and 2.5 mg/kg of butorphanol (Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan). The back hair of the mice was shaved using an electric razor and depilatory cream and stripped six times with tape. Subsequently, the mice were sensitized with a 1 × 1 cm sterile gauze patch soaked with 100 µg OVA (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 µl sterilized PBS on the back skin. The patch was fixed using a transparent film dressing (Tegaderm, 3 M Company, Saint Paul, MN, USA) and changed each day for a week. The mice were sensitized twice every other week for one week each time. During the interval week, each mouse from three experimental groups were separately injected intravenously (i.v.) with 200 µL of DMEM, SHED-CM, or Fibro-CM through the tail vein. The mice were sacrificed on day 35 by overdose (150 mg/kg) i.p of sodium pentobarbital (Kyoritsu Seiyaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and absence of cardiac activity. Skin and lymph node tissues were obtained for subsequent examination (Fig. 1A). All procedures were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Tokushima University (Permit No: T2022-54; Date of Permit: August 10, 2022) and performed in accordance with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). All mice were included in the final analyses unless they died before the end of experiment endpoint.

Clinical severity assessment

The severity of back skin OSAD lesions was assessed based on six symptoms [28]: erythema/hemorrhage, oozing/crust, erosion/excoriation, swelling/edema, lichenification, and dryness. Specifically, erythema/hemorrhage refers to inflammatory redness of the skin accompanied by occasional bleeding spots due to scratching or tissue damage. Oozing/crust describes the presence of serous fluid exudation from the disrupted skin barrier and the subsequent formation of crusts when the fluid dries. Erosion/excoriation indicates superficial loss of the epidermis or scratch-induced lesions caused by repeated rubbing or clawing. Swelling/edema denotes inflammatory thickening of the skin and accumulation of interstitial fluid, leading to visible or palpable swelling. Lichenification is characterized by chronic skin thickening, with exaggerated skin markings resulting from long-term scratching or rubbing. Dryness represents reduced skin hydration, resulting in a rough texture, scaling, or even cracking of the skin surface. They were scored as 0, 1, 2, and 3 representing no, mild, moderate, and severe symptoms, respectively. Clinical score of skin lesions was evaluated by three independent investigators who were blinded to the experimental conditions. The total score reflects the sum of each symptom score and ranged from 0 to 18.

Histology and immunohistochemical analysis

The dissected dorsal skin of the mice was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-µm sections. Lymph nodes were directly embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek Japan, Tokyo, Japan), then cut into 8-µm sections, fixed with acetone and methanol at a 1:1 ratio at − 20 °C for 20 min before staining. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) (Sakura Finetek Japan) staining was used to detect epidermal thickness and infiltrating immune cells. Toluidine blue (TB) (Fujifilm Wako) staining was performed to evaluate mast cell infiltration. For immunohistochemical analysis, the antibody profiles are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1. The sections were blocked with Blocking One Histo (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with SignalStain Boost IHC Detection Reagent (HRP, Rabbit; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Color development was done using DAB Substrate (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin (Sakura Finetek Japan). Alexa Fluor 488- and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for tissue immunofluorescence staining and counterstaining was done with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma-Aldrich).

Five random locations in the tissue sections were selected and imaged using a general-purpose fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence, Osaka, Japan) and analyzed using the supporting software (BZ-X800 Analyzer; Keyence). Positive immunohistochemical and immunofluorescenct cells were counted using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using Isogen Ⅱ (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR were performed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), respectively. A StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for gene amplification and analysis. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 2: Table S2.

Flow cytometry analysis

The details of the antibodies used for flow cytometry are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1. Lymph nodes were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a cell strainer, washed with 1% bovine serum albumin (Fujifilm Wako) in PBS, and counted. TruStain FcX antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to block nonspecific binding to Fc receptors. T cells were stained with CD3 and CD4. To stain intracellular proteins, the cells were permeabilized with the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained. B cells were fixed in 10% neutral formalin solution (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 10 min, then permeabilized with − 20 °C pre-cooled methanol (Fujifilm Wako) on ice for 30 min, blocked with TruStain FcX antibody, and stained with B220, IgE, and immunoglobulin M (IgM). CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was used for flow cytometry and the data were analyzed using the accompanying analysis software (Kaluza; Beckman Coulter).

Measurement of serum total IgE, OVA-Specific IgE, IL-2 and IL-6

Fresh blood was collected from the maxillofacial venous plexus of mice at days 21, 24, 28, and 35. The blood was placed at room temperature for 60 min to clot and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The upper serum layer was carefully aspirated and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. The levels of total IgE, OVA-specific IgE, IL-2 and IL-6 in the serum were separately quantified using mouse IgE uncoated ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), LBIS Mouse OVA-IgE ELISA Kit (Fujifilm Wako), Mouse IL-2 ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (R&D Systems) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured using a BioTek Cytation 3 imaging reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) and analyzed using Gen5 software (BioTek Instruments).

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis

Secretome analysis of CM was carried out following established protocols. Briefly, SHED-CM and Fibro-CM were concentrated using Amicon Ultra 3 K filters (Sigma-Aldrich). The proteins were purified by methanol/chloroform precipitation, and the resulting pellets were dissolved in MS buffer (8 M urea and 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). The proteins were reduced with 5 mM DTT (Fujifilm Wako), alkylated with iodoacetamide (Fujifilm Wako) at room temperature for 30 min protected from light, and diluted 8-fold with 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, before overnight digestion at 37 °C with 50 ng Lys-C (Fujifilm Wako) and trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The peptides were purified using GL-Tips SDB (GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified using a Pierce colorimetric peptide assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Finally, the peptides (300 ng each) were analyzed by mass spectrometry using an EASY-nLC1200 system connected to a Q-Exactive PLUS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mass spectrometry system. Protein identification and label-free quantitation were achieved using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Treg differentiation and neutralizing antibody analysis

Naïve T cells were purified from female 6-week-old BALB/c mouse splenocytes using the EasySep Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada), based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified cells (1.5 × 105) in 100 µL of RPMI (Nacalai Tesque) were activated using 5 µL of Dynabeads Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per well in a 96-well U-bottom plate (Corning, Oneonta, NY, USA), and 100 µL of DMEM or a different concentration of SHED-CM or rhTGFβ1 (R&D Systems) with or without rmIL-2 (BioLegend) in DMEM was added to each well and cultured for 24 h for Treg induction. Anti-human TGFβ (R&D Systems), anti-human LIF (R&D Systems), and anti-human IGF2 (R&D Systems) were preincubated at a concentration of 1 µg/mL with SHED-CM overnight at 4 °C. Mouse IgG (R&D Systems) was used as isotype control antibody. The proportion of differentiated Treg cells were analyzed using flow cytometry.

Assessment of the inhibitory effect of SHED-CM on Th1, Th2, and Th17 differentiation in vitro

To assess Th1 differentiation, CD3/CD28-activated naïve T cells were incubated with 30 U/mL rmIL-2 (BioLegend), 10 ng/mL rmIL-12 (R&D Systems), and 10 µg/mL anti-mouse IL-4 (BioLegend) and cultured for 4 days in a 96-well U-bottom plate (Corning) containing 100 µL RPMI and 100 µL SHED-CM or DMEM. Similarly, the cells were incubated with 30 U/mL rmIL-2 (BioLegend), 10 ng/mL rmIL-4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 5 µg/mL anti-mouse IFN- (BioLegend) for Th2 differentiation and 20 ng/mL rmIL-6 (R&D Systems), 10 ng/mL rmIL-23 (R&D Systems), 5 ng/mL rhTGFβ1 (R&D Systems), 5 µg/mL anti-mouse IFN- (BioLegend), and 5 µg/mL anti-mouse IL-4 (BioLegend) for Th17 differentiation. T cells differentiation for each subtype was analyzed using flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed to compare differences between two groups. For more than three groups, an ordinary one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used (GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

SHED-CM ameliorates the symptoms of OSAD mice

OSAD mice were generated by i.p. injections of OVA mixed with aluminum hydroxide, followed by back skin restimulation with an OVA patch. On day 21, at the end of OVA patch stimulation, the OSAD mice exhibited severe erythema and oozing on the dorsal skin, accompanied by lichenification and excoriation (Additional file 3: Fig. S1A, B). These OSAD mice were divided equally into three groups based on the severity of skin lesions and treated with i.v injections of DMEM, SHED-CM, or Fibro-CM, followed by OVA patch restimulation (Fig. 1A). On day 35, at the end of the observation period, the DMEM group showed further exacerbation of the skin lesions, whereas the SHED-CM group exhibited marked improvement, which was not observed in the Fibro-CM group (Fig. 1B). The clinical scores for the SHED-CM group were significantly lower compared with those of the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0153) (Fig. 1C).

Histological analysis revealed that epidermal thickness and inflammatory cell infiltration were increased in the DMEM group, and both were significantly decreased in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D, E). TB staining revealed that the number of mast cells were higher in the DMEM group and significantly lower in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0139) (Fig. 1D, F). Immunofluorescence staining showed destruction of the Filaggrin-positive stratum corneum layer in the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups but not in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0059) (Fig. 1D, G). These results indicate that SHED-CM treatment establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment, ameliorates histological abnormalities, and maintains the epidermal integrity of OSAD mouse skin.

SHED-CM induces TGFβ-expressing Treg cells in OSAD mice skin

Immunohistological analysis revealed an increase in the number of CD3ε- and CD4-positive cells in the DMEM group and significant decreased of these cells in the SHED-CM group (CD3ε, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0011; CD4, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0045). Conversely, the number of Foxp3-expressing Tregs was significantly increased in the SHED-CM group compared with that in the DMEM or Fibro-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0249, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0183). The number of thymic-derived Helios+ Tregs was reduced in the DMEM and Fibro-CM group but maintained in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0012, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0007) at a level comparable to Sham control. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that > 80% of the Foxp3-positive cells in the SHED-CM group coexpressed TGFβ (Fig. 2A, B). In addition, SHED-CM treatment suppressed the infiltration of B220- and CD11c-positive cells in the OSAD mouse skin (B220, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.001; CD11c, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0053) (Additional file 3: Fig. S2A, B).

The expression of inflammatory immune cell markers was measured using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3). Increased expression of Cd3e and Cd4, Th2-related genes (Il4 and Il13), and Th17-related genes (Il17a and Rorc) was observed in the DMEM group, which was reduced by SHED-CM treatment (Cd3e, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0009; Cd4, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0041; Il4, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0213; Il13, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.024; Il17a, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0484; Rorc, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0212). SHED-CM treatment had no effect on the expression of tslp and Th1-related genes (Tbet and Ifng); however, it significantly increased the expression of Treg-specific molecules (Foxp3, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; Tgfb, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; Helios, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001). SHED-CM treatment also inhibited the expression of a series of inflammation-related factors, including Tnf, iNOS, CD206, and Arg1 (Tnf, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0013; iNOS, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; CD206, SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; Arg1, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0082). These results demonstrate that SHED-CM shifts the Th2, Th17-dominated proinflammatory environment of OSAD skin toward that of a Treg-dominated anti-inflammatory environment.

SHED-CM suppresses lymphadenopathy and induces Treg-dominant circumstances in OSAD mice lymph nodes

Draining lymph nodes in the SHED-CM group were significantly smaller compared with those in the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001, SHED vs. Fibro, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A, Additional file 3: Fig. S3A, B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the number of GATA3-positive cells was increased in the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups and decreased in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0012, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0018). In contrast, the number of Tbet was significantly higher in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0268, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.021). Notably, the number of Foxp3-expressing cells was significantly increased in the SHED-CM group but not in the DMEM or Fibro-CM groups (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; SHED vs. Fibro, p < 0.0001). In addition, the number of Helios-expressing cells was preserved in the SHED-CM group but was reduced in the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001; SHED vs. Fibro, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B, C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of Tbet+ and Foxp3+ cells was significantly increased in the SHED-CM group (Tbet+, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0292, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0069; Foxp3+, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0093, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0198), whereas that of GATA3+ and RORγt+ cells was decreased (GATA3+, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0111, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0398; RORγt+, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0467, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0255) (Fig. 4D, Additional file 3: Fig. S4A-C). These results indicate that SHED-CM improves the balance of Th1/Th2 and Th17/Treg in OSAD lymph nodes.

SHED-CM suppresses the production of IgE, IL-2 and IL-6 in OSAD mice

Immunohistological analysis revealed that the number of IgE-positive cells were increased in the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups and significantly decreased in the SHED-CM group (SHED vs. DMEM, p < 0.0001, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0011) (Fig. 5A, B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of IgE+ cells, not B220+ and IgM+ cells, was significantly decreased in the SHED-CM group compared with the DMEM and Fibro-CM groups (SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0027, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0209) (Fig. 5C, Additional file 3: Fig. S5A-D). ELISA analysis of mouse serum revealed that the SHED-CM group total IgE and OVA-specific IgE, compared with the DMEM and Fibro-CM group, were significantly reduced from day 24 (IgE, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0365, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.0256) and 28 (OVA-specific IgE, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0005, SHED vs. Fibro, p = 0.013), respectively (Fig. 5D, E). The results indicate that SHED-CM treatment effectively reduced antigen-specific IgE production in OSAD mice. The serum levels of key inflammatory cytokines, IL-2 and IL-6, were also examined. The ELISA analysis revealed that the levels of both were significantly reduced by SHED-CM treatment on day 28 (IL-2, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0142; IL-6, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0005) and 35 (IL-2, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0006; IL-6, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0002) (Additional file 3: Fig. S6A, B).

Secretome analysis of SHED-CM and fibro-CM

To identify the therapeutic factors responsible for improving OSAD skin lesions, we reanalyzed our previously reported proteomic data by comparing differentially expressed proteins in SHED-CM and Fibro-CM [29]. We identified 1,261 membrane-associated proteins in SHED-CM and Fibro-CM. Of these, 655 proteins had more than 2-fold statistically significance increase in abundance in SHED-CM (p < 0.05). Based on previous reports, we identified four proteins in the enriched SHED-CM that regulate Treg induction, including transforming growth factor beta-2 (TGFB2), leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), insulin-like growth factor-2 (IGF2), and transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGFB1) (Fig. 6A, B).

SHED-CM directly induces Treg differentiation, not Th1, Th2, or Th17

To determine the effects of SHED-CM on Treg differentiation, naïve CD4⁺ T cells were isolated from mouse spleens and stimulated with CD3/CD28 antibody-coated beads in a medium consisting of a 1:1 mixture of RPMI and DMEM or SHED-CM. Flowcytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of Foxp3⁺ cells and CD25⁺ cells was significantly higher in the SHED-CM group compared with that in the DMEM group (Foxp3+, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0003; CD25⁺, SHED vs. DMEM, p = 0.0008) (Fig. 7A, Additional file 3: Fig. S7A-E). In the SHED-CM dilution experiments, no significant difference in Treg-inducing capacity was observed between the 1:2 and 1:20 diluted CM conditions, whereas the Treg-inducing capacity of the 1:40 diluted CM was significantly reduced (p = 0.0074) (Additional file 3: Fig. S8A). Given the total blood volume of a mouse (approximately 2 mL), intravenous administration of 0.2 mL SHED-CM corresponds to an approximate 10-fold dilution in vivo. Thus, the in vitro 10-fold dilution condition reflects the in vivo environment. The dosage was adopted based on our previous studies [29].

To determine whether the Treg-inducing factors identified through LC-MS analysis are directly involved in SHED-CM-mediated Treg induction, we added neutralizing antibodies against TGFβ, IGF2, and LIF, or isotype control antibodies, to the Treg induction assay. Of these, only the neutralizing antibody against TGFβ significantly inhibited the induction of Foxp3⁺ cells by SHED-CM (p = 0.0205), whereas it had no effect on the induction of CD25⁺ cells (Fig. 7A, Additional file 3: Fig. S7A-E). The level of Treg induction by SHED-CM was compared with that induced by a previously reported standard protocol using 5 ng/mL exogenous TGFβ protein with or without IL-2 [30, 31]. No significant difference was detected between them. Under these experimental conditions, the induction of RORγt⁺ Th17 by SHED-CM was not detected (Additional file 3: Fig. S8B). Heat-treated SHED-CM (75 °C for 10 min or 95 °C for 5 min) showed no detectable ability to promote Treg differentiation, indicating that the bioactive components of SHED-CM are predominantly proteinaceous (Additional file 3: Fig. S8C).

Next, we determined whether SHED-CM inhibits differentiation into the Th1, Th2, and Th17 subsets. Naïve T cells were induced to differentiate into Th1, Th2, or Th17 cells in RPMI/DMEM or RPMI/SHED-CM medium. SHED-CM did not alter the proportion of Tbet⁺, GATA3⁺, or RORγt⁺ cells (Fig. 7B, Additional file 3: Fig. S9A-E). These results indicate that SHED-CM can directly induce Treg differentiation from naïve T cells, primarily through TGFβ; however, it does not directly influence differentiation into Th1, Th2, or Th17 cells.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that the systemic administration of SHED-CM to OSAD mice inhibited the development of AD-like skin symptoms triggered by local antigen restimulation. SHED-CM treatment decreased epidermal thickness, cellular infiltration, and mast cell number, while preserving the surface filaggrin layer. Notably, in the SHED-CM-treated group, Helios⁺ thymic-derived and Foxp3⁺ peripherally induced Tregs expressing TGFβ, which were increased in the OSAD skin and draining lymph nodes. The expression of Th2 cytokine genes associated with AD pathology, IL-4 and IL-13, was markedly decreased following SHED-CM treatment. Moreover, the serum levels of total IgE and OVA-specific IgE were significantly decreased following SHED-CM administration. Taken together, these results suggest that SHED-CM establishes an immunosuppressive environment in the skin and draining lymph nodes of OSAD mice, thereby protecting the skin from tissue-destructive inflammatory responses induced by antigen restimulation during OSAD.

This study is also the first to demonstrate that SHED-CM directly acts on naïve splenic CD4⁺ T cells to induce their differentiation into Treg cells. Previous studies have shown that secretory factors from dental pulp stem cells promote Treg induction. Matsumura-Kawashima et al. administered conditioned culture media from adult dental pulp stem cells (DPSC-CM) to a murine model of Sjögren’s syndrome and observed a significant increase in the number of CD4⁺Foxp3⁺ Treg cells in the spleen, along with a decrease in inflammatory Th17 cells, which resulted in clinical improvement [32]. The effect of DPSC-CM on T cells was assessed using whole splenic mononuclear cells, excluding red blood cells, which made it unclear whether the observed Treg induction resulted from a direct effect on T cells or was mediated through other immune cells. Because dendritic cells, M2 macrophages, and B cells promote Treg differentiation [33–36] and SHED-CM exhibits strong M2 macrophage-inducing properties [20, 21, 37–39], indirect mechanisms could not be ruled out. Conversely, we used highly purified splenic CD3⁺CD4⁺ naïve T cells (≥ 99% purity), which were stimulated in vitro with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies in the presence of SHED-CM. Under these conditions, robust induction of Treg cells was observed within 24 h. These findings demonstrate that SHED-CM directly acts on naïve T cells to induce their differentiation into Treg cells.

Th2 cells produce IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which promotes IgE production, disrupts skin barrier function, and recruits inflammatory cells, contributing to the acute phase of AD [5]. Th17 cells are involved in the chronic or severe stages of AD. They secrete IL-17 and IL-22, which disrupt the skin barrier, enhance inflammation, and recruit neutrophils [6]. The administration of SHED-CM to OSAD mice resulted in a significant decrease in the activity and/or presence of Th2 and Th17 cells; however, in vitro experiments demonstrated that SHED-CM did not affect the differentiation of splenic naïve T cells into Th2 or Th17 lineages. These results suggest that the Treg-inducing activity of SHED-CM is key to establishing an immunosuppressive environment in OSAD skin. Moreover, the suppression of Th2- and Th17-mediated inflammation may occur secondarily through these induced Treg cells. Further mechanistic studies are needed to characterize the phenotype and function of SHED-CM-induced Treg cells in OSAD and to evaluate their therapeutic potential.

The immunoregulatory effects of MSC-derived factors, particularly their capacity to enhance Treg populations, have garnered significant attention. In coculture experiments with bone marrow- or adipose tissue-derived MSC and effector T cells, MSC secrete immunosuppressive molecules, such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, TGFβ, and prostaglandin E2, promoting the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells [40, 41]. MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) also exhibit Treg-inducing properties. When MSC-EVs isolated from culture supernatants were added to T cell culture, naïve CD4⁺ T cells were polarized toward the CD25⁺Foxp3⁺ Treg phenotype [42]. In the present study, LC-MS analysis of SHED-CM and Fibro-CM revealed candidate molecules responsible for Treg induction. Four potential Treg-inducing factors were identified in SHED-CM. IGF2, in combination with T cell receptor signaling, induced robust Treg cell proliferation and TGFβ secretion, enhancing the immunosuppressive activity of Treg cells and suppressing intestinal allergic inflammation mediated by effector T cells [43]. LIF was also implicated in Treg induction, as its neutralization significantly decreased Foxp3⁺ Treg cell populations in cocultures of lymphocytes [44]. TGFβ signaling is a well-established pathway that promotes Foxp3 expression, the master transcription factor governing Treg differentiation. It is indispensable for their development and suppressive effects [45, 46]. Neutralizing antibody experiments revealed that TGFβ is essential for Treg induction by SHED-CM with CD3/CD28 costimulation. Although Treg induction typically requires stimulation with CD3/CD28, TGFβ, and IL-2, LC-MS analysis did not detect IL-2 in SHED-CM. During Treg differentiation, the IL-2/JAK-STAT5 and TGFβ signaling pathways synergistically induce Foxp3 expression [47]. Based on these findings, SHED-CM may contain factors that activate the JAK-STAT5 pathway independent of exogenous IL-2. Further elucidation of the underlying mechanisms through which SHED-CM induces Treg differentiation may provide new therapeutic strategies for immune modulation in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases.

In the lymph nodes of OSAD mice, B cells activated by Th2 cells undergo isotype switching and produce antigen-specific IgE, instead of IgM. This IgE binds to FcεRI receptors on the surface of mast cells. The antigen-IgE complex activates mast cells and trigger the release of inflammatory mediators, such as histamine, chemokines, and cytokines, exacerbating AD-like symptoms [48]. In the present study, the number and proportion of IgE-positive cells were decreased in the SHED-CM group, whereas the proportion of B220-positive B cells and IgM-positive cells remained unchanged. In the DMEM group, total and antigen-specific serum IgE levels were increased during the early phase of OSAD (days 0–21) and also during the late phase following antigen reactivation (days 28–35). Conversely, in the SHED-CM group, IgE levels began to decrease during the treatment phase (days 21–28), and the subsequent late-phase increase was absent. This inhibitory effect on IgE production may be attributed to Th2 cell inactivation mediated by SHED-CM-induced Treg cells; however, it is also possible that SHED-CM exerts direct effects on immature B cells, inhibiting isotype switching. Previous studies indicate that various types of MSCs reduce serum IgE levels in OSAD model mice [11]. Furthermore, in vitro coculture experiments have shown that MSCs suppress IgE production in B cells stimulated with lipopolysaccharide or anti-CD40 and inhibit B cell proliferation and differentiation [14, 49]. Park et al. reported that TGFβ plays an important role in the therapeutic effects of human umbilical cord-MSCs on OSAD by regulating TNF-α production in mast cells and controlling B cell maturation [50]. The results of the present study strongly suggest that SHED-CM may suppress the onset of OSAD in a multifaceted manner by directly controlling the induction of Tregs, B cell isotype switching, and mast cell activation through TGFβ. Future studies are warranted to clarify the direct effects of SHED-CM on B cell differentiation and proliferation, identify the responsible effector molecules, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of B cell regulation by SHED-CM.

The OSAD model used in this study reflects only part of the human disease [12]. OSAD is induced by a Th2-skewed immune response characterized by increased production of IL-4, IL-13, and IgE, together with activation of mast cells and eosinophils. The tape-stripping with the application of OVA patches disrupts the skin barrier and triggers cutaneous injury. Nevertheless, OSAD does not recapitulate many key aspects of human AD. (1) Antigen stimulation: Human AD arises from natural sensitization to diverse environmental antigens, whereas OSAD is provoked by a single, artificially applied strong antigenic stimulus. (2) Disease course: Human AD is chronic and relapsing, but OSAD represents a relatively simple, acute dermatitis. (3) Barrier and microbiome: Human AD is influenced by genetic skin-barrier defects and the skin microbiome, while OSAD is induced in a specific-pathogen-free environment through artificial barrier disruption. (4) Lesion distribution: Human AD typically presents with bilateral, symmetrical lesions, whereas OSAD lesions are confined to the sites of OVA patch contact. Thus, the OSAD model reproduces certain aspects of human AD, it does not adequately capture the disease’s complex and heterogeneous pathology. The therapeutic effects observed in this study should therefore be interpreted as benefits against only a subset of AD-like pathophysiology rather than against human AD as a whole.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated that intravenous administration of SHED-CM alleviates AD-like symptoms by establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment in the skin and draining lymph nodes of OSAD mice. Within this environment, Tregs were effectively induced, whereas the presence and activity of Th2 and Th17 cells were significantly suppressed. Furthermore, B cell maturation, antigen-specific IgE production, and mast cell activation were markedly inhibited. Notably, we demonstrated that SHED-CM directly promoted the differentiation of Tregs from naïve CD4⁺ T cells stimulated by CD3/CD28, which was mediated, in part, through TGFβ associated with SHED-CM. SHED-CM exhibited little to no effect on Th1, Th2, and Th17 differentiation in vitro. Thus, this study provides novel insights into the therapeutic potential of the MSC secretome for the treatment of AD-like symptom.
