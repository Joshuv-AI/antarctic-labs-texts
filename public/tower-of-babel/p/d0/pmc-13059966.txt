Fungal microbiota signatures anticipate neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy outcomes in esophageal cancer
Authors: Liping Liang, Shijie Mai, Gautam Sethi, Yingjie Luo, Zeheng Ma, Lele Wu, Di Lu, Jimin Han, Ruijun Cai, Yongjian Zhou, Xinying Wang, Le Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPredicting neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT) response remains a critical challenge in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) management. While the gut bacteriome’s role in immunotherapy has been established, the mycobiome’s predictive potential remains largely unexplored. This study investigated whether gut fungal signatures could serve as reliable biomarkers for NICT response prediction in patients with ESCC.MethodsWe performed internal transcribed spacer 2 sequencing on 155 fecal samples from 68 patients with ESCC (pre-NICT and post-NICT) and 19 healthy controls. Patients were stratified by tumor regression grade scores. We analyzed mycobiome-immune marker correlations and developed multilayer perceptron (MLP) models using Boruta feature selection. Performance was validated in 37 independent pretreatment patients. Functional causality was confirmed using Candida_boidinii in syngeneic mouse experiments with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) therapy.ResultsPatients with ESCC exhibited significant mycobiome dysbiosis compared with healthy controls, characterized by reduced alpha diversity and enrichment of pathogenic fungi including s_Rhodotorula_minuta, s_Actinomucor_elegans, and s_Candida_zeylanoides. Baseline mycobiome profiles distinguished treatment responders from non-responders before therapy initiation. Responders demonstrated higher fungal diversity, more stable co-occurrence networks, and enrichment of beneficial taxa (s_Candida_boidinii, g_Meyerozyma, s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis) that correlated with Th1-polarized immunity and elevated cytotoxic markers (interferon-γ, interleukin (IL)-12p70, IL-2). Non-responders harbored immunosuppressive fungi (s_Candida_albicans, s_Candida_parapsilosis, s_Candida_glabrata, g_Saccharomyces) associated with Th2 skewing and regulatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, IL-13). Functional analysis revealed responders exhibited enhanced catabolic pathways and phospholipase activities, while non-responders showed upregulated nucleotide biosynthesis. The MLP model achieved robust discriminative performance (genus-level: training area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) 98.0%, test AUC 82.9%; species-level: training AUC 87.1%, test AUC 87.4%). Candida_boidinii administration enhanced anti-PD-1 efficacy in mice, validating predicted metabolomic and immune changes.ConclusionsBaseline gut mycobiome signatures predict NICT response in ESCC with high accuracy. Experimental validation confirms functional causality, enabling precision medicine approaches for patient stratification and identifying therapeutic targets.Keywords: Esophageal Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is the sixth most common cancer in China and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death. Over 90% of these cases are esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).1 2 The standard treatment for locally advanced ESCC typically includes neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by minimally invasive esophagectomy,3 with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (nCT) being the preferred option in regions such as China and Japan.4 5 Although these therapies improve long-term survival, the high recurrence rates and limited overall survival gains highlight the need for better treatments.

Recent trials, such as the CheckMate 577 study, have show that postoperative adjuvant immunotherapy following nCRT can extend disease-free survival by up to 11 months.6 Similarly, the ATTRACTION-3 study found that nivolumab improved survival compared with chemotherapy in advanced, previously treated ESCC.7 Combining immunotherapy with nCT has shown promising results in locally advanced ESCC, yielding favorable pathological complete response (pCR) rates and manageable toxicity.8 9 These findings suggest that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT) could represent a significant advancement in ESCC treatment, potentially improving patient outcomes. Achieving pCR is increasingly seen as a key predictor of long-term survival,10 11 with non-responders to neoadjuvant therapy often facing poorer prognoses due to therapy-related complications.12 However, not all patients with ESCC respond to NICT, prompting the need for predictive biomarkers and insights into the underlying mechanisms of treatment response.

The human microbiome, including its fungal component, plays a critical role in health and disease. The mycobiome, composed of fungi residing in and on the body, has been relatively understudied compared with the bacteriome. Although fungi make up less than 0.1% of the microbiome, they possess unique metabolic features that contribute to host functions. Recent advances in genomic sequencing have enhanced our understanding of the mycobiome’s role in health and disease, including cancer. The most common fungal phyla are Ascomycota (eg, Candida, Cladosporium, Saccharomyces), followed by Basidiomycota and Zygomycota.13,15 In healthy individuals, fungi help regulate immune responses by interacting with antigen-presenting cells, leading to the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ.16

Fungal probiotics, such as Saccharomyces boulardii, show anti-neoplastic effects and may play a role in cancer prevention and therapy. Fungi also interact with the immune system in ways that can influence cancer development.17 For example, in pancreatic cancer, fungi promote interleukin (IL)-33 secretion, which activates T helper 2 (Th2) cells and aids in tumor progression.18 Recent research suggests that gut fungi may modulate immune responses to cancer immunotherapy by priming both innate and adaptive immune systems.16 19 Additionally, β-glucans, key components of the fungal cell wall, have shown anticancer properties by modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) and enhancing immunotherapy responses.20 Studies have also demonstrated that fungi like S. boulardii CNCM I-745 can inhibit cancer cell signaling and suppress tumor growth,21 while Schizophyllum commune exhibits anticancer activity in murine models.22

Despite these findings, the impact of gut mycobiome changes during NICT and their association with treatment effectiveness remains poorly understood. This study aims to analyze fecal samples from patients with ESCC before and after NICT to explore how mycobiome shifts correlate with pathological responses. By identifying fungal markers that predict therapeutic outcomes, we hope to provide insights into how the mycobiome may influence NICT efficacy. Ultimately, this research could pave the way for personalized treatment strategies based on individual mycobiome profiles and highlight fungi as potential predictors of NICT response in ESCC.

Materials and methods

Study design and selection of participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and performed at Nanfang Hospital, affiliated with Southern Medical University, following approval from the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee (NFEC-2022–383). Each participant gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part. The study population comprised 105 individuals with ESCC who underwent NICT between April 2020 and April 2025, including 68 patients in the training set and 37 patients in the testing set. Exclusion criteria encompassed recent usage of antibiotics, prebiotics, probiotics, steroids, or immunosuppressants within 4 weeks of fecal sample collection. For baseline comparisons, 19 healthy controls (HC) free of any malignancies, as verified by clinical assessments, were recruited. These controls were cohabitants of the patients with ESCC, helping to reduce lifestyle or dietary variation effects. NICT involved the administration of chemotherapy with platinum-based drugs like cisplatin or carboplatin combined with programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) inhibitors. Detailed demographic and clinicopathological data were collected from all participants. The study used the American Joint Committee on Cancer (eighth edition) tumor regression grade (TRG) system with four levels to measure NICT response. In the training set, 26 patients with TRG scores of 0–1 were categorized as responders (R) and 42 patients with TRG scores of 2–3 as non-responders (NR). In the external testing set, 20 patients with TRG scores of 0–1 were categorized as R and 17 patients with TRG scores of 2–3 as NR. Patients’ basic information is shown in online supplemental table 1.

Collection and DNA extraction from fecal samples

Fecal samples were meticulously collected following the standardized guidelines established by the Human Mycobiome Project’s Manual of Procedures Protocol. Each patient with ESCC provided two fecal samples: one collected prior to initiating NICT and another obtained shortly after completing the therapeutic regimen. In contrast, a single baseline fecal sample was collected from each HC participant at study enrollment. All fecal specimens were immediately processed and frozen at −80°C to preserve microbial DNA integrity until further analysis. Total genomic DNA extraction was performed using the E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA Kit (D4015, Omega Bio-tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were assessed to ensure consistent quality across all samples.

Amplicon library construction and sequencing

Internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) sequencing was performed to characterize gut mycobiome composition. Amplicon libraries targeting the ITS2 rRNA genes were prepared using fungal-specific PCR primers ITS1FI2 (5'-GTGARTCATCGAATCTTTG-3') and ITS2 (5'-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3') tagged with sample-specific barcodes. PCR amplification was performed in 25 µL reactions containing 25 ng of template DNA with the following thermocycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s; 32 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s; final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were purified using AMPure XT beads and quantified by Qubit. Library quality was assessed on Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and negative controls were included throughout the process. The libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq PE250 platform by LC-Bio Technology, Hangzhou, China.

Bioinformatic analysis

Paired-end reads were assigned to samples based on unique barcodes and merged using PEAR. Quality filtering was performed using fqtrim (V.0.94), and chimeric sequences were filtered using VSEARCH software (V.2.3.4). After dereplication using DADA2, feature tables and representative sequences were obtained to identify amplicon sequence variants. Taxonomic annotation was conducted using the QIIME2 plugin feature-classifier with alignment against RDP and UNITE databases. Data normalization was performed to account for sample size disparities. Alpha and beta diversity were calculated by QIIME2, with relative abundance (fungi count/total count) used for fungal taxonomy analysis. Using R (V.3.5.2), various diversity indices, including observed species, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, were calculated. Beta diversity analyses involved principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and heatmap visualization using Spearman’s correlation. Predictive functional profiling was performed using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States2 (PICRUSt2), with predicted functional genes categorized according to Clusters of Orthologous Groups and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology. Comparative analyses across patient groups were conducted using STAMP, and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differentially abundant taxa as potential biomarkers.

Development and interpretation of a predictive model using machine learning

To develop a robust classifier for predicting NICT therapeutic responses, we implemented a comprehensive machine learning pipeline with strict data separation protocols to prevent data leakage. The external testing cohort (n=37) was set aside at the outset of the study and remained completely isolated from all model development processes, including feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, and cross-validation procedures, to ensure unbiased performance evaluation. All model development was conducted exclusively on the training cohort (n=68). The Boruta algorithm was employed for feature selection by comparing importance scores of real fungal features against randomized “shadow features” generated through iterative random forest analysis. Only features consistently outperforming shadow features across multiple iterations were retained as biologically meaningful predictors. Following feature selection, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network was implemented for model development using exclusively the training data. Fivefold cross-validation was systematically applied within the training cohort for internal validation and overfitting prevention, with all hyperparameter tuning performed solely within this framework. Only after complete model development and optimization using the training set, the model’s generalizability was assessed using the independent external testing cohort for final validation. This strict separation ensured the testing set represented truly unseen data that never influenced model development. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To enhance model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was integrated to quantify individual feature contributions using game theory principles. SHAP provided both global feature importance rankings and local explanations for specific predictions, with force plots illustrating how individual fungal biomarkers influenced treatment response predictions. This approach, with rigorous data separation protocols, ensured high predictive accuracy, clinical interpretability, and reliable generalizability for mycobiome-guided prediction of NICT therapeutic responses in patients with ESCC.

Animal experiment

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University. A syngeneic tumor model was established using C57BL/6 male mice (5–6 weeks old) and the murine esophageal cancer cell line AKR. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions throughout the study. AKR cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For tumor inoculation, mice received subcutaneous injections in the right lateral abdomen with 5.0×10⁵ viable AKR cells suspended in 0.1 mL of a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and Matrigel. Approximately 1-week prior to tumor cell inoculation, mice were pretreated with Candida_boidinii (200 µL/mouse, 1×107 colony-forming units (CFU)) administered daily via oral gavage. Once tumors reached an average volume of approximately 100 mm³, mice received intraperitoneal injections of anti-PD-1 antibody (100 µg/mouse, Bio X Cell) every 2 days for a total of three injections. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: 0.5×tumor length×(tumor width)².

Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC/MS) non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Fecal metabolites were extracted using standard procedures and analyzed on a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS LC-Xevo G2-XS QTof MS system with a Waters HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1×100 mm). Mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) for positive and negative ionization modes. Data acquired via MassLynx V.4.2 underwent processing with Progenesis QI software for peak extraction, alignment, and metabolite identification using METLIN database with theoretical fragmentation. Differential metabolites were identified using Student’s t-test with fold change ≥2 and p<0.05. Functional enrichment analyses characterized metabolic changes between treatment groups.

Transcriptome sequencing of subcutaneous tumor tissue (RNA sequencing)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse subcutaneous tumors using TRIzol reagent and assessed for quality using NanoDrop ND-1000 and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (RNA Integrity Number (RIN) >7.0). Complementary DNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC Bio-Technology, Hangzhou, China) generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome, and gene expression levels were quantified. Differential expression analysis used DESeq2 (|log2FC|>1, p<0.05). ImmuCellAI quantified immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment. KEGG pathway enrichment and data visualization were performed using OmicStudio and R software.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as either mean±SD or median with IQR. For comparisons between independent groups, Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables, while categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test. Significance thresholds were denoted as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, and ****p<0.0001. To control for false positives in the analysis of differentially expressed bacteria, the false discovery rate adjustment was applied, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Missing clinical data, accounting for 20% of the dataset, were addressed through random forest imputation. This approach, implemented using the “mice” package in R, ensured the dataset’s completeness while preserving its structural complexity and analytical reliability.

Results

NICT treatment partially restores gut mycobiome diversity with residual differences from healthy controls

We assessed the fungal diversity and community composition among patients with ESCC post-NICT intervention (Post), pre-NICT intervention (Pre), and HC using Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and Goods coverage indices. The Shannon and Simpson indices revealed that both Post and Pre groups were significantly lower than the HC group, indicating that fungal diversity remained altered compared to healthy controls. The Chao1 index showed significantly lower richness in the Pre group compared with the HC groups. Goods coverage was high across all groups, confirming adequate sequencing depth. Overall, NICT treatment showed a trend toward improvement in fungal community diversity and richness metrics, but full recovery to healthy levels was not achieved (figure 1A). PCoA demonstrated distinct clustering patterns between pretreatment, post-treatment, and healthy control groups (Jaccard distance, PCoA1=11.75%, PCoA2=3.3%). Post-treatment samples exhibited tighter clustering compared with pretreatment samples, indicating treatment-induced convergence of microbial communities. Notably, paired pretreatment and post-treatment samples maintained greater within-patient similarity than between-patient similarity, preserving individual-specific microbial signatures despite therapeutic intervention. Household-matched controls showed partial but incomplete overlap with patient samples, reflecting shared environmental influences while maintaining distinct microbial profiles (online supplemental figure 1A,B). These findings collectively demonstrate measurable treatment effects on gut mycobiome composition within the context of persistent individual microbial fingerprints. The stacked bar plot of the top 30 fungal phyla showed that p_Ascomycota and p_Basidiomycota dominated across all groups, with notable differences in their relative abundances. The Pre group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of p_Ascomycota, while the Post group showed a reduction in p_Ascomycota and an increase in p_Basidiomycota, moving towards a composition more similar to the HC group. However, p_Basidiomycota was still less abundant in the Post group compared with the HC group, indicating that the microbial shift, while significant, did not completely match the HC. These observations further emphasize the partial but incomplete recovery of the fungal community post-NICT intervention (figure 1B). The stacked bar plot of the top 30 fungal genera revealed distinct differences in relative abundance between the groups. The Pre group had a higher abundance of genera such as g_Saccharomyces, g_Cladosporium, and g_Nakaseomyces. In contrast, the Post group showed a shift towards a community composition more similar to the HC group, with higher relative abundances of genera like g_Candida, g_Trichosporon, g_Meyerozyma and g_Cryptococcus. However, despite these shifts, significant differences remained between the Post and HC groups, suggesting that while NICT intervention contributed to restoring the fungal community towards a more healthy profile, complete recovery to the HC composition was not achieved (figure 1C). At the species level, s_Fusarium_annulatum, s_Rhinocladiella_atrovirens, s_Cryptococcus_laurentii, s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, s_Candida_boidini, and s_Fusarium_pseudonygamai were significantly enriched in the HC group, while s_Rhodotorula_minuta, s_Actinomucor_elegans, s_Rhodotorula_slooffiae, s_Ganoderma_sichuanense, s_Candida_zeylanoides, s_Phoma_macrostoma, s_Elaphomyces_leucosporus, s_Cladosporium_dominicanum, and s_Malassezia_globosa were significantly enriched in ESCC. After NICT intervention, the relative abundance of s_Torulaspora_delbrueckii, s_Alternaria brassicae, s_Alternaria_sp_BMP_2012, s_Colletotrichum_cliviae, s_Setophoma_chromolaenae, and s_Coprinellus_radians significantly increased in the gut (figure 1D). The Sankey diagram (figure 1E) provides a hierarchical view of fungal taxonomic distributions across the Pre, Post, and HC groups, transitioning from phylum down to genus level. Initially, the Pre group shows a strong predominance of p_Ascomycota, with a large portion of its relative abundance funneled through the c_Saccharomycetes class and o_Saccharomycetales order into genera such as g_Candida and g_Saccharomyces. In contrast, the HC group features a higher proportion of p_Basidiomycota, commonly passing through the c_Tremellomycetes class, o_Trichosporonales and o_Tremellales order, leading to genera like g_Cryptococcus, and g_Trichosporon. After NICT, the Post group’s fungal community shifts toward a composition more closely resembling the HC profile, as evidenced by an increased flow toward p_Basidiomycota and its associated classes, orders, and genera. Nevertheless, certain lineages, such as those ending in g_Rhodosporidium, remain less represented in Post compared with HC, emphasizing that the NICT-induced reconfiguration, while substantial, is still incomplete relative to the healthy baseline. Co-occurrence network analysis, which infers potential ecological interactions between microbial taxa based on correlation patterns across samples, was performed to understand community structure and stability. Genus-level co-occurrence networks (figure 1F) underscored variation in community interactions. The Pre group’s network was sparsely connected, suggesting fewer functional coordination associations among taxa. Post intervention, path distances shortened, indicating improved microbial coordination. Nevertheless, the Post network remained less complex than the HC group’s, wherein p_Basidiomycota and other fungal lineages formed intricate, well-integrated communities. These observations collectively suggest that while NICT facilitates partial restoration of the gut mycobiome in ESCC, additional or prolonged interventions may be required to fully match healthy fungal profiles.

Divergent mycobiome signatures and functional pathway shifts in patients with ESCC undergoing NICT

As shown in figure 2A, the bar plots of the top 15 fungal genera revealed marked differences in the gut mycobiome across Pre-NICT, Post-NICT, and HC groups. g_Meyerozyma and g_Cryptococcus were significantly more abundant in HC samples, suggesting these taxa may be indicative of a healthier fungal profile. By contrast, the Pre group harbored notably higher levels of g_Saccharomyces, g_Nakaseomyces, g_Atradidymella, g_Coriolopsis, g_Russula, g_Ganoderma, and g_Inocybe compared with the Post group, indicating only partial normalization of the fungal community following NICT therapy. Although several taxa trended closer to HC levels in the Post group, persistent differences in key genera (g_Candida, g_Trichosporon, g_Meyerozyma) underscored the incomplete restoration of a healthy-like mycobiome. At the species level (figure 2B), s_Candida_boidinii and s_Trichosporon_dermatis were less abundant in Pre samples but increased markedly after NICT, pointing to dynamic shifts attributable to therapy. LEfSe analysis (figure 2C,D) further underscored these disparities, identifying g_Saccharomyces, g_Nakaseomyces, s_Saccharomyces_unclassified, and s_Candida_albicans as more common in patients with ESCC, whereas g_Trichosporon, g_Candida, s_Candida_boidinii, and s_Trichosporon_dermatis predominated in HC samples. Comparing Pre and Post groups, LEfSe also revealed significantly higher abundances of s_Candida_boidinii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, and g_Ascotricha in the Post group, while g_Atradidymella, g_Coriolopsis, g_Ganoderma, s_Trametes_trogii, s_Ganoderma_sichuanense, and s_Peniophora_incarnata were reduced (figure 2E–F). Figure 2G illustrates a genus-level Spearman’s correlation network derived from LEfSe-identified fungal taxa that differ significantly between the Pre and HC groups. Notably, g_Gymnostellatospora, g_Leucoagaricus, g_Geomyces, g_Ramicandelaber, and g_Chaunopycnis exhibited strong positive correlations with other taxa, whereas g_Saccharomyces showed a pronounced negative correlation. These network patterns suggest potential cooperative or antagonistic ecological dynamics driving the broader fungal community structure. Indicator analysis at both genus and species levels revealed consistent patterns of mycobiome signatures associated with different disease states and treatment phases. At the genus level (figure 2H), g_Meyerozyma, g_Trichosporon, and g_Cryptococcus emerged as highly significant indicators for the healthy state, while g_Atradidymella demonstrated strong association with the pretreatment group. These broad taxonomic patterns were further refined at the species level (figure 2I), where s_Candida_boidinii, s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, and s_Rhodosporidium_toruloides showed high statistical significance as HC indicators, complementing their genus-level associations. The analysis also revealed a compelling pattern where fungi enriched following NICT treatment were almost characteristic of healthy mycobiome states, suggesting that successful therapy promotes restoration toward healthier fungal community profiles. s_Cryptococcus_laurentii and s_Penicillium_chrysogenum demonstrated this dual association pattern, serving as indicators for both HC and post-treatment states. Conversely, disease-associated taxa such as s_Phoma_macrostoma at the species level and g_Atradidymella at the genus level were specifically enriched in pretreatment patients, representing potential biomarkers for ESCC-associated mycobiome dysbiosis. The hierarchical consistency between genus and species-level findings, exemplified by the g_Trichosporon genus and its representative species s_Trichosporon_dermatis, validates the robustness of these mycobiome signatures and supports the concept that NICT treatment selectively promotes beneficial fungal taxa while reducing pathogenic species, ultimately facilitating mycobiome restoration toward healthier community structures.

PICRUSt2-based functional profiling revealed significant alterations in enzyme functions and metabolic pathways associated with ESCC pathogenesis and NICT treatment (figure 3A–D). Patients with ESCC demonstrated marked enrichment of nucleotide biosynthesis enzymes, including phosphoribosylformylglycinamidine synthase and asparaginyl-tRNA synthase, while HC showed superior metabolic capacity through acetolactate synthase, nitrate reductase, and dethiobiotin synthase (figure 3A). At the pathway level (figure 3B), patients with ESCC exhibited comprehensive enrichment of nucleotide biosynthesis superpathways, particularly superpathway of adenosine nucleotides de novo biosynthesis I and pyrimidine ribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis, whereas HC demonstrated enhanced biotin biosynthesis I, photorespiration, and glucose and glucose-1-phosphate degradation. Following NICT intervention, patients exhibited significant functional recovery with enhanced formate dehydrogenase, and galactarate dehydratase, indicating restored one-carbon metabolism and DNA synthesis capacity (figure 3C). Treatment-induced metabolic rebalancing through enrichment of superpathway of L-arginine and L-ornithine degradation, D-glucarate degradation I, and guanosine nucleotides degradation III, along with reduced vitamin biosynthesis pathways including flavin biosynthesis I and coenzyme A biosynthesis I (figure 3D). This functional analysis revealed that ESCC is characterized by mycobiome communities with enhanced anabolic processes supporting cellular proliferation, while healthy individuals maintain superior catabolic and vitamin biosynthetic capabilities. NICT treatment appears to normalize mycobiome function by reducing excessive biosynthetic activities and enhancing degradation pathways, suggesting that therapeutic success may involve restoration of metabolic balance rather than enhancement of biosynthetic capacity. The post-treatment shift toward enhanced amino acid and nucleotide degradation, coupled with reduced vitamin biosynthesis, indicates that NICT-induced mycobiome changes prioritize catabolic processes that may support tissue repair and immune function recovery while reducing metabolic support for proliferative processes. This metabolic rebalancing provides mechanistic insights into how successful NICT treatment modulates gut mycobiome function to create an environment less conducive to tumor growth and more supportive of host recovery.

Distinct mycobiome composition and network complexity underlie differential NICT responses in ESCC

Based on TRG scores, patients with ESCC were categorized as R or NR groups. At baseline, NR displayed significantly lower fungal diversity than R, as indicated by reductions in Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and observed species indices (figure 4A). This divergence in diversity was particularly pronounced at the pretreatment stage. NMDS analysis using Jaccard distance further supported these findings, showing a clear trend of separation between the NR and R groups (figure 4B,C). At the phylum level, p_Basidiomycota and p_Ascomycota dominated, accounting for approximately 92% of the fungal community. Notably, R consistently exhibited a higher proportion of p_Basidiomycota relative to p_Ascomycota (figure 4D). At the family level, f_Saccharomycetales_Incertae_sedis, f_Trichosporonaceae, f_Debaryomycetaceae, and f_Tremellaceae were more abundant in R, whereas f_Saccharomycetaceae and f_Mycosphaerellaceae were enriched in NR (figure 4E). The Sankey diagram (figure 4F) illustrates proportional shifts in fungal composition across NR_Pre, NR_Post, R_Pre, and R_Post groups, from phylum down to genus level. p_Ascomycota tended to be more prevalent in NR groups, while p_Basidiomycota was generally more abundant in R groups. At the class level, c_Saccharomycetes appeared more common in NR samples, whereas c_Tremellomycetes were relatively enriched in R samples. At the order level, o_Saccharomycetales was notably more abundant in NR samples, while o_Trichosporonales increased in R samples. At the genus level, g_Saccharomyces were more abundant in NR groups, whereas g_Trichosporon and g_Cryptococcus became more prominent in R groups. These patterns indicate dynamic shifts in fungal community structure associated with treatment and response status. A heatmap of the top 50 fungal species further underscored these compositional differences (figure 4G). Species such as s_Phaeoseptoria_musae, s_Candida_parapsilosis, s_Asterotremella_pseudolonga, s_Udeniomyces_pyricola, and s_Candida_albicans were more abundant in NR, while s_Penicillium_chrysogenum, s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii, s_Rhinocladiella_atrovirens, s_Cryptococcus_laurentii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, s_Candida_boidinii, s_Fusarium_annulatum, and s_Rhodosporidium_toruloides were enriched in R. Co-occurrence network analyses revealed a predominance of positive correlations in all groups (NR_Pre: 99.82%; R_Pre: 99.74%; NR_Post: 93.92%; R_Post: 99.83%). Notably, R networks (both pretreatment and post-treatment) exhibited higher density and average degree than those of NR (figure 4H). This suggests that R maintains more interconnected and potentially more functionally resilient fungal networks. In summary, NICT therapy influences the gut mycobiome composition and structure in patients with ESCC, with R displaying greater baseline diversity, distinct taxonomic enrichments favoring Basidiomycota-related taxa, and more tightly integrated fungal networks.

Baseline mycobiome signatures reveal distinct predictive indicators of NICT response in ESCC

To assess potential clinical confounders affecting mycobiome composition and its association with NICT response, redundancy analysis (RDA) incorporating baseline characteristics, including tumor location, clinical staging, age, gender, body mass index, smoking, and alcohol history, was performed. RDA showed no significant influence of these variables on fungal community distribution between R and NR, indicating that observed mycobiome differences reflect genuine biological signatures rather than demographic or clinical confounders (online supplemental figure S2; online supplemental table 2). Metastats analysis identified multiple taxa differing significantly between NR_Pre and R_Pre groups at genus and species levels. NR_Pre samples were enriched in genera such as g_Saccharomyces, g_Nakaseomyces, g_Atradidymella, g_Coriolopsis, and g_Actinomucor, whereas R_Pre samples were enriched in g_Gibberella, g_Eupenicillium, g_Russula, g_Rhodosporidium, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Cryptococcus, g_Candida, g_Trichosporon, and g_Meyerozyma (figure 5A). At the species level, 15 taxa differentiated the groups; for example, s_Candida_parapsilosis, s_Candida_albicans, s_Candida_glabrata, s_Phoma_macrostoma, s_Trametes_trogii, s_Actinomucor_elegans, and s_Rhodotorula_slooffiae predominated in NR_Pre, while s_Penicillium_chrysogenum, s_Rhodosporidium_toruloides, s_Rhinocladiella_atrovirens, s_Cryptococcus_laurentii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii, and s_Candida_boidinii were abundant in R_Pre (figure 5B). Indicator species analysis corroborated these findings: at genus level, g_Saccharomyces, g_Nakaseomyces, and g_Coriolopsis indicated NR_Pre status, while g_Trichosporon, g_Eupenicillium, g_Cryptococcus, g_Meyerozyma, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Rhodosporidium, g_Inocybe, g_Russula, g_Astraeus, and g_Elaphomyces marked R_Pre (figure 5C). At the species level, 13 taxa displayed significant indicator values (p<0.01), with s_Candida_parapsilosis, s_Candida_albicans, s_Candida_glabrata, s_Trametes_trogii, s_Candida_tropicalis, and s_Actinomucor_elegans as key NR_Pre indicators (figure 5D). LEfSe with LDA scores identified biomarkers across taxonomic ranks (figure 5E,F). At phylum level, p_Basidiomycota associated with R_Pre, while p_Zygomycota and p_Ascomycota linked with NR_Pre. At the class level, c_Tremellomycetes, c_Eurotiomycetes, c_Agaricomycetes, and c_Sordariomycetes characterized R_Pre; c_Mucoromycotina_Incertae_sec, c_Microbotryomycetes, and c_Saccharomycetes were enriched in NR_Pre. Genera like g_Meyerozyma, g_Candida, g_Cryptococcus, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Rhodosporidium, and g_Russula favored R_Pre, while g_Actinomucor, g_Coriolopsis, g_Atradidymella, g_Nakaseomyces, and g_Saccharomyces were linked to NR_Pre. These compositional and phylogenetic differences highlight the gut mycobiome’s potential as a predictive biomarker for NICT response in ESCC. Network analysis revealed complex ecological interactions among fungal genera (figure 6A). For instance, g_Cryptococcus positively correlated with g_Rhinocladiella, g_Rhodosporidium, g_Russula, g_Meyerozyma, and g_Trichosporon, while g_Saccharomyces negatively correlated with many of these, suggesting competitive or segregated niches.

Serum cytokine profiling distinguished immune landscapes prior to treatment (figure 6B). NR_Pre patients exhibited elevated immunosuppressive and Th2-polarizing cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, IL-13), alongside increased IL-5, indicating a Th2-skewed, immunosuppressive, and inflammatory environment. Conversely, R_Pre patients showed higher Th1 cytokines IL-12p70, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, with elevated granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and IL-2, suggesting a pro-immunogenic milieu conducive to effective antitumor immunity. Correlation analyses between fungal taxa and immune markers illuminated divergent patterns (figure 6C). R-associated genera g_Russula, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Rhodosporidium, g_Trichosporon, g_Meyerozyma, and g_Cryptococcus positively correlated with Th1 cytokines TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-12p70. NR-associated genera, including g_Nakaseomyces and g_Saccharomyces correlated with immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, IL-13) linked to poor immunotherapy outcomes. Species-level correlations (figure 6D) reinforced these insights. R-enriched species s_Candida_boidinii correlated strongly with TNF-α and IFN-γ. Moreover, s_Candida_boidinii, s_Rhinocladiella_atrovirens, s_Rhodosporidium_toruloides, s_Cryptococcus_laurentii, s_Trichosporon_dermatis, and s_Meyerozyma_guilliermondii also correlated positively with IL-2 and GM-CSF, essential for T-cell and dendritic cell activation. NR-associated species, including s_Candida_albicans, s_Candida_parapsilosis, s_Candida_glabrata, and s_Saccharomyces_unclassified, correlated positively with Th2 cytokines, reflecting an immunosuppressive, anti-inflammatory environment detrimental to NICT efficacy. Hierarchical clustering defined two functional clusters: a “pro-immunogenic cluster” comprising R-associated taxa linked to Th1 cytokines, T-cell activation, and favorable hematologic profiles, and an “immunosuppressive cluster” of NR taxa associated with Th2 polarization and regulatory markers. These consistent patterns indicate the gut mycobiome influences NICT response through modulation of systemic immune-inflammatory status in ESCC.

Functional profiling using PICRUSt2 revealed significant differences in predicted enzyme functions and metabolic pathways between R_Pre and NR_Pre groups (figure 6E,F). R demonstrated enhanced RNA processing capabilities, with multiple RNA methyltransferases showing significant enrichment, including 16S rRNA (guanine(1516)-N(2))-methyltransferase and tRNA pseudouridine(32) synthase. Most notably, phospholipase A(1) and phospholipase A(2) showed the highest enrichment in R, indicating enhanced phospholipid metabolism and membrane remodeling capacity. R also exhibited elevated activities of key reductases and nucleotide metabolism enzymes, suggesting enhanced cellular reduction processes and metabolic flexibility (figure 6E). At the pathway level (figure 6F), NRs were predominantly enriched in nucleotide biosynthesis pathways, particularly the superpathway of pyrimidine ribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis and related adenosine nucleotide biosynthesis pathways, indicating comprehensive upregulation of anabolic processes. Conversely, R demonstrated significant enrichment in catabolic pathways, most notably photorespiration, glucose and glucose-1-phosphate degradation, fatty acid β-oxidation I, and L-arginine degradation II. Additionally, R showed enhanced tRNA processing and biotin biosynthesis pathways, indicating superior RNA processing capabilities and vitamin metabolism. This functional analysis revealed a fundamental metabolic dichotomy between response groups, with R characterized by enhanced catabolic processes supporting energy production and cellular adaptation, while NR exhibited increased anabolic demands potentially interfering with effective antitumor immune responses. The enhanced phospholipase activities and catabolic pathways in R may facilitate membrane dynamics and energy metabolism conducive to effective immunotherapy responses.

Refining NICT treatment strategies in ESCC through mycobiome-derived biomarkers and advanced machine learning

By integrating advanced machine learning techniques with comprehensive fungal profiling, we identified key mycobiome members predictive of NICT non-response in ESCC. In the training cohort, Boruta-based feature selection singled out 11 fungal genera (figure 7A), including g_Trichosporon, g_Sebacina, g_Scleroderma, g_Saccharomyces, g_Russula, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Mortierella, g_Meyerozyma, g_Eupenicillium, g_Cryptococcus, g_Clavulina, as candidate predictors. The boxplots in figure 7A highlight the stability and consistency of these selected features across multiple iterations, reinforcing the reliability of the identified taxa rather than reflecting random selection artifacts. Subsequent MLP modeling with fivefold cross-validation refined these candidates into an optimal classifier, yielding robust discriminative performance at the genus level, with an AUC of 98.0% in training (figure 7B). When applied to an independent test set, the classifier maintained high discriminative accuracy (AUC 82.9%) (figure 7C).

Candida_boidinii enhanced anti-PD-1 therapeutic efficacy through validated metabolic and immune pathway modulation

To experimentally validate the functional insights derived from our clinical mycobiome analysis, we investigated the therapeutic potential of Candida_boidinii, a key beneficial fungus enriched in treatment R, using a syngeneic esophageal cancer mouse model. Mice were randomly allocated into four treatment groups: control (PBS), Candida_boidinii monotherapy, anti-PD-1 monotherapy, and combination therapy. Candida_boidinii was administered via oral gavage beginning approximately 1-week prior to tumor cell inoculation and continued until about 10 days after tumor cell inoculation. Anti-PD-1 antibody treatment commenced when tumors reached approximately 100 mm³, administered intraperitoneally every 2 days for three total injections (figure 9A). Tumor growth kinetics confirmed that both Candida_boidinii and anti-PD-1 monotherapies significantly inhibited tumor progression compared with controls, with combination treatment demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy and achieving the most pronounced tumor growth suppression (figure 9B). Critically, this enhanced antitumor effect was accompanied by metabolic reprogramming that directly validated our clinical PICRUSt2 functional predictions. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) analysis revealed distinct metabolomic separation between combination and anti-PD-1 monotherapy groups (figure 9C), with Candida_boidinii treatment specifically inducing metabolic changes consistent with the beneficial pathways predicted to be enriched in clinical R. Comprehensive metabolomic profiling demonstrated that Candida_boidinii exposure resulted in measurable activation of the exact metabolic pathways our clinical analysis identified as favorable for treatment response (figure 9D). Specifically, the combination therapy group exhibited elevated alpha-N-(3-hydroxyhexadecanoyl) L-ornithine and reduced arginine-related metabolites (D-pipecolic acid, D-arginine, leucyl-isoleucine), directly validating the L-arginine degradation II pathway and ribonucleotide biosynthesis enrichment predicted by PICRUSt2 in clinical R. Additionally, the observed variations of phospholipid derivatives, including phosphatidylcholine and lysophosphatidic acid species, provided direct experimental confirmation of the enhanced phospholipase A(1) and A(2) activities predicted in clinical R. The reduction in nucleotide precursors and enhanced catabolic metabolite profiles in Candida_boidinii-treated mice further corroborated our clinical findings that R exhibit enhanced catabolic capacity rather than the nucleotide biosynthesis pathway enrichment characteristic of NR. ImmuCellAI deconvolution analysis of tumor transcriptomes demonstrated that Candida_boidinii treatment significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration compared with monotherapy approaches, while modulating other immune cell populations including increased natural killer T (NKT) cells and altered macrophage polarization states (figure 9E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant upregulation of metabolic pathways including protein digestion and absorption, and lipid metabolism (arachidonic acid, retinol, linoleic acid), alongside immune-related pathways such as calcium signaling (online supplemental figure S2B). These transcriptomic changes aligned precisely with our clinical predictions and metabolomic validation. These controlled experimental findings provide direct mechanistic evidence that Candida_boidinii exposure leads to measurable activation of the specific metabolic pathways (L-arginine degradation, enhanced phospholipase activities, catabolic processes) and immune responses (enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration) that our clinical analysis predicted to be beneficial for NICT response. This experimental validation transforms our clinical correlative observations into mechanistically validated therapeutic insights, demonstrating that beneficial fungal signatures can be functionally manipulated to enhance treatment responses in patients with ESCC.

Discussion

Improved predictors for NICT response are essential for optimizing therapeutic strategies in ESCC. While tumor-intrinsic genomic features, such as tumor mutation burden, IFN signaling defects, and programmed death-ligand 1 expression, have been proposed as potential biomarkers for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), their predictive value varies considerably. This variability has drawn attention toward host-related factors within the gut environment. Although initial efforts centered on bacterial biomarkers, these approaches have not fully resolved the challenge of reliably forecasting NICT outcomes, prompting investigations into other components of the gut microbiome, particularly the gut mycobiome.

Our study highlights that gut fungal communities in patients with ESCC differ markedly from those of HC, in line with observations reported by Vitali et al.23 The ESCC-associated mycobiota exhibited enrichment in g_Saccharomyces, g_Nakaseomyces, and s_Candida_albicans, alongside a depletion of g_Trichosporon, certain Candida species (eg, s_Candida_boidinii), and s_Trichosporon_dermatis. Additionally, patients with ESCC showed significantly lower alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1) than healthy subjects, suggesting that a less diverse fungal ecosystem may foster tumor-promoting conditions. Importantly, we identified several fungal genera correlating with differences in NICT outcomes: g_Meyerozyma, g_Candida, g_Cryptococcus, g_Rhinocladiella, g_Rhodosporidium, and g_Russula were associated with positive responses, whereas g_Actinomucor, g_Coriolopsis, g_Atradidymella, g_Nakaseomyces, and g_Saccharomyces were enriched in NR. These findings suggest that specific fungal signatures may serve as key biomarkers, potentially improving the accuracy of NICT response prediction.

Technological advances have enabled more accurate fungal profiling.24 25 Fungi interact with host immunity through pattern recognition receptors (Dectin-1, toll-like receptors), influencing cytokine production (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, IFN-γ), and shaping T-helper (Th1, Th17) cell responses.26 27 Fungal dysbioses reminiscent of those observed in colorectal and pancreatic cancers indicate broad relevance to multiple malignancies.28 29 Within this context, specific fungal taxa may alter the TME by modulating T-cell infiltration, influencing myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) accumulation, and affecting immune checkpoint expression, all of which are central to NICT efficacy. Our comprehensive correlation analyses between fungal taxa and systemic immune-inflammatory markers revealed compelling evidence that specific fungal signatures are indeed associated with distinct tumor immune phenotypes. At both genus and species levels, we observed clear dichotomous patterns where responder-associated fungi correlated with “hot” tumor signatures characterized by elevated Th1-associated cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12p70) and T-cell activation markers, while NR-associated fungi correlated with “cold” tumor characteristics including elevated immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, IL-13) and systemic immunosuppressive markers. These correlation patterns suggest that beneficial fungi enriched in R may promote systemic Th1 polarization and maintain lymphocyte functionality, contributing to tumor phenotypes conducive to immunotherapy success, while detrimental fungi enriched in NR may drive Th2 skewing and systemic immunosuppression, potentially contributing to tumor environments resistant to ICIs. The strong correlations between mycobiome signatures and systemic immune markers that reflect tumor immune status provide compelling evidence that the gut mycobiome acts as a mediator of systemic immune-inflammatory balance, which in turn influences the tumor immune microenvironment and NICT efficacy.

Candida, especially Candida_albicans, is a standout fungal biomarker. Candida_albicans has been implicated in various diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), alcohol-associated liver disease, asthma, and COVID-19.30,33 Within the TME, Candida_albicans can promote pro-inflammatory gene expression, support metastatic potential, and associate with poor survival.34 Mechanistically, Candida_albicans secretes carcinogenic metabolites (eg, acetaldehyde) and can enhance immune checkpoint activation (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4, PD-1, T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT)), potentially suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activity and facilitating immunoevasive states.35,39 By inducing inflammatory cytokines and altering dendritic cell maturation, Candida_albicans may also promote MDSC recruitment and their immunosuppressive functions, thus impeding robust antitumor immunity. Our observation that high baseline Candida_albicans levels correlate with non-response and that its abundance declines during anti-PD-1 treatment highlights its dynamic involvement in shaping therapy outcomes. Therapeutic interventions that reduce Candida_albicans burden or mitigate its immunosuppressive effects could potentially improve NICT responsiveness.

Candida_tropicalis, similarly enriched in NR, has been linked to exacerbated intestinal inflammation and heightened colorectal cancer (CRC) risk in genetically susceptible hosts (eg, CARD9 deficiency).40,42 Its role in driving dysregulated Th17 responses or enhancing MDSC infiltration could further compromise effective antitumor T-cell activity. Together, these findings underscore the importance of Candida species as critical fungal biomarkers that may modulate key immunosuppressive pathways. While Saccharomyces species such as Saccharomyces_cerevisiae are generally considered commensal or even beneficial microbes, their dysregulated abundance in NR suggests a potential shift in their functional role. Under certain conditions, Saccharomyces may contribute to altered mucosal immunity, impacting epithelial barrier integrity and influencing local cytokine profiles that either dampen T-cell infiltration or favor immunosuppressive cell types. Nakaseomyces, closely related to Candida, may share immunomodulatory properties, promoting immune checkpoint expression or fostering conditions conducive to MDSC expansion and diminished cytotoxic responses. Understanding the precise mechanisms through which these fungi transition from benign symbionts to potential immunomodulators in ESCC is an important area for future research.

Malassezia, associated with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, and emerging as a potential player in other malignancies,28 43 44 can metabolize host lipids and produce immunomodulatory compounds that engage receptors like the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. By influencing epithelial and dendritic cell function, Malassezia may skew T-cell responses away from effective antitumor activity, and potentially enhance the recruitment of immunosuppressive cell populations. Such a shift could reduce the capacity of NICT to reinvigorate cytotoxic T cells, ultimately hindering tumor eradication. Delineating Malassezia’s role in ESCC, including whether it promotes MDSC infiltration or supports regulatory T cell (Treg) expansion, could inform targeted interventions to restore a more favorable immune landscape. The presence of Aspergillus in NR, known for producing carcinogenic aflatoxins,45 raises the question of whether fungal-driven genotoxic stress contributes to immune evasion. Persistent DNA damage and chronic inflammation may foster environments tolerant of tumor growth. Such conditions might hinder NICT-induced immunostimulatory processes, reducing effective T-cell infiltration and promoting the persistence of immunosuppressive cells.

To fully understand how these fungal biomarkers modulate immune cell infiltration, cytokine networks, and T-cell functionality, future studies must incorporate multiomics approaches (metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics) and detailed immunophenotyping. Mapping fungal metabolism and secreted molecules onto immune cell populations in the TME will provide valuable clues. For example, characterizing how fungal metabolites influence MDSC differentiation, or identifying fungal ligands that trigger immune checkpoints, could inform novel therapeutic strategies. Additionally, the interplay between fungal and bacterial communities is likely significant. Certain fungal species may outcompete beneficial bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, leading to compromised mucosal immunity and reduced regulatory T-cell function. Such imbalances could further encourage MDSC accumulation and restrict effective T-cell recruitment. Integrative fungal-bacterial analyses will be essential to elucidating these ecological and immunological interactions.

We also conducted controlled animal experiments using Candida_boidinii, one of the key beneficial fungi identified in our clinical analysis. Our syngeneic mouse model demonstrated that Candida_boidinii treatment significantly enhanced anti-PD-1 therapeutic efficacy, with corresponding metabolomic and immune changes that directly validated our clinical PICRUSt2 predictions. Specifically, Candida_boidinii exposure resulted in measurable activation of L-arginine degradation pathways, enhanced phospholipase activities, and elevated CD8+ T cell infiltration, precisely matching the metabolic and immune signatures we observed in clinical R. This experimental validation transforms our correlative clinical observations into mechanistically validated therapeutic insights, providing direct evidence that beneficial fungal signatures can be functionally manipulated to enhance treatment responses. However, we acknowledge that while our animal experiments establish biological plausibility and functional causality, they represent an initial step toward comprehensive mechanistic understanding. The detailed molecular mechanisms through which specific fungal strains modulate immune cell differentiation, metabolite production, and TME dynamics require further investigation.

Machine learning models, such as MLP, offer a powerful approach to integrate complex fungal-omics data with clinical and immunological parameters.46,47 By leveraging machine learning, we can identify nonlinear relationships and subtle patterns linking fungal biomarkers to NICT outcomes. These predictive frameworks can help clinicians stratify patients, pre-emptively adjusting treatment strategies for those predicted to be NR. Potential interventions include antifungal therapies, dietary modifications, probiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation to restore a mycobiome that supports effective antitumor immunity. Our ESCC mycobiome findings demonstrate both convergence with and divergence from existing cancer research. Consistent with pan-cancer patterns, our ESCC cohort shows dominant fungal phyla of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, with characteristic reduction in fungal alpha diversity compared with HC, reflecting universal signatures of fungal dysbiosis in malignancy.48 Similarly, pathogenic Candida species such as s_Candida_albicans and s_Candida_parapsilosis are enriched in NR, aligning with their documented protumor and immunosuppressive roles across multiple cancer types. However, our study reveals ESCC-specific associations not emphasized in prior works, including the enrichment of s_Candida_boidinii and s_Trichosporon_dermatis in treatment R, which positively correlate with Th1-type cytokines and T-cell activation markers, suggesting distinct immunostimulatory fungal signatures. Functionally, we identified fungal metabolic pathways such as L-arginine degradation enriched in R, mechanistically linked to T-cell activation and antitumor immunity. Furthermore, our characterization of fungal ecological network topology revealed higher network density and clustering coefficients in R, implying community stability tied to favorable outcomes, features not previously reported in ESCC or pan-cancer mycobiome studies. Our integration of longitudinal sampling, machine learning with SHAP interpretability, and independent validation demonstrates robust predictive accuracy, with our NICT response prediction models retaining strong capacity in temporally separated cohorts, supporting their clinical utility as non-invasive biomarkers for therapy stratification. These findings highlight that while fundamental fungal dysbiosis patterns are broadly conserved across cancers, the detailed species associations, functional pathways, and ecological structures mediating immune responses are tumor- and treatment-specific, emphasizing the necessity for dedicated cancer-specific mycobiome research.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Our findings are based on a single-institution cohort and require validation in larger, multicenter studies. Differences in immunotherapy regimens, comorbidities, and antibiotic use may influence the gut mycobiome and its effects on treatment responses. Additionally, while genus-level analyses provide stability, shotgun metagenomics and metatranscriptomics could unveil finer species-level or strain-level differences. Elucidating the mechanistic underpinnings of how specific fungal metabolites, cell wall components, and interaction with immune cells shape the NICT response will be the key to unlocking targeted interventions.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into gut mycobiome characterization in patients with ESCC, identifies fungal signatures associated with NICT responses, and highlights dynamic changes in fungal communities that correlate with therapeutic outcomes. By characterizing fungi as potential biomarkers in the immunotherapy landscape, we establish a foundation for refined predictive models and novel strategies to leverage mycobiome profiling for improved precision oncology. The integration of mycobiome signatures with machine learning approaches achieved high predictive accuracy, demonstrating the clinical potential for personalized treatment strategies based on individual fungal profiles. Our findings reveal that beneficial fungi promote Th1-polarized immune responses conducive to immunotherapy success, while pathogenic fungi drive immunosuppressive environments resistant to treatment. As research advances, integrating machine learning with mycobiome characterization will likely refine these predictive approaches, offering the potential to overcome current limitations and ultimately enhance clinical decision-making in ESCC management through precision medicine approaches.
