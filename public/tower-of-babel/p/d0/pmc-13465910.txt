Integrating Imaging Markers for Clinical Risk Stratification of Takotsubo Syndrome
Authors: Liya Dai, Yuyi Chen, Frank Seghatol, Han Tang, Crystal Licari, Sabrina Vaughn, Juan Lei, Amanda Chang, Silma Subah Raisa, Xiaodong Wu, Kan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The purpose of this study was to develop machine learning (ML) models that integrate clinical and echocardiographic markers to predict adverse cardiovascular events in patients with Takotsubo syndrome (TTS).Methods:ML-based prediction models were developed on the basis of 2 retrospective data sets comprising 450 TTS patients (252 in an internal cohort and 198 in an external cohort). Baseline clinical characteristics included demographic characteristics, medical history, triggering factors, symptoms, and vital signs. All echocardiograms were performed within 24 hours of hospital admission. Feature selection and model development were performed using the random forest algorithm for prediction of 3-month major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Model performance was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), calibration, sensitivity, and specificity. Model interpretability was evaluated using SHapley Additive exPlanations.Results:The established ML models, which integrated key clinical and echocardiographic variables, demonstrated good discrimination for 3-month MACE prediction. The machine learning-based clinical-echocardiographic-4 model, which incorporated mitral valve inflow (E wave) deceleration time, systolic blood pressure, right ventricular fractional area change, and heart rate showed an AUC of 0.80 (95% confidence interval, 0.73–0.86) in the internal cohort and an AUC of 0.76 (95% confidence interval, 0.69–0.84) in the external cohort. Calibration analyses showed good agreement between predicted and observed risks internally and acceptable calibration externally. SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis highlighted the dominant contributions of key features to MACE prediction. Restricted cubic spline analyses identified clinically relevant thresholds for elevated MACE risk: deceleration time < 129.9 ms, systolic blood pressure < 125 mm Hg, right ventricular fractional area change < 34.7%, and heart rate > 93 beats per minute.Conclusions:ML-based clinical-echocardiographic prediction models showed prognostic value in early risk stratification in hospitalized TTS patients.Keywords: Takotsubo syndrome, echocardiography, major adverse cardiac events, deceleration time, machine learning

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is an acute ventricular dysfunction in the absence of culprit coronary artery disorders.1,2 Mounting evidence indicates that TTS has heterogeneous courses and can cause serious acute complications.3–6 Although multiple clinical registries, including the International Takotsubo Registry,7 German-Italian-Spanish Takotsubo Registry,8 and Chinese Registry of TTS9 have provided a variety of outcome prediction scores, few imaging markers have been incorporated into these models, likely because of the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of imaging findings in patients with TTS.8,10,11

As a real-time cardiovascular imaging modality, echocardiography often aids in front-line physicians’ bedside diagnosis and management decisions in hospitalized TTS patients. Nonetheless, effective analytical approaches are lacking to identify intrinsic relationships and extract optimal feature combinations from multidomain clinical and imaging data sets. Therefore, we developed and externally validated machine learning (ML)-based clinical-echocardiographic models using data from 2 tertiary cardiovascular centres in the United States for early risk stratification and prediction of short-term adverse outcomes in hospitalized patients with TTS.

Methods

Study population

We used 2 data sets (450 TTS patients) for model reconstruction: the internal cohorts (252 TTS patients) were derived from Barnes-Jewish Hospital, Washington University in St Louis, between April 1, 2008, and July 31, 2024. The external validation cohort (198 TTS patients) was obtained from the University of Iowa between March 4, 2010, and September 6, 2022. All cases were retrospectively reviewed and adjudicated using standardized diagnostic criteria, with systematic review of clinical records, coronary angiography, and cardiac imaging to meet fulfilment of the International Expert Consensus Document on TTS12 (InterTAK Diagnostic Criteria). Patients were excluded if they had primary valvular disorders, significant pulmonary hypertension, or if segmental wall motion could not be accurately determined using either left ventriculography or transthoracic echocardiography. Standardized spreadsheets were used to collect patient data in the study, including baseline demographic and clinical characteristics on admission, medical history, triggering factors, and echocardiographic findings.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiograms were performed in all patients within 24 hours of hospital admission, using GE Vivid E9 machine (GE Healthcare, Vingmed Ultrasound A/S, Horten, Norway) with 2.5–3.5 MHz phased-array transducers. Standard comprehensive assessments included 2-dimensional, Doppler, and tissue Doppler, and speckle-tracking echocardiography. All images were digitally recorded and reviewed by 2 expert readers (L.D. and K.L.), who were blinded to the patients’ clinical and biochemical data.

Left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) and LV end-diastolic volume were calculated using the biplane Simpson method.13 LV diastolic function was evaluated according to the recommendations of American Society of Echocardiography.14 Mitral E and A velocities were obtained from pulsed wave Doppler in the apical 4-chamber view to calculate the E/A ratio. Septal and lateral e′ velocities were measured using tissue Doppler imaging, and the average E/e′ ratio was computed. E-wave deceleration time (DT) was measured as the time interval from peak E-wave along the slope of LV filling extrapolates to the zero-velocity baseline. LV Tei index, isovolumic relaxation time, and isovolumic contraction time were obtained using tissue Doppler imaging (TDI). Left atrial (LA) volume was measured using the biplane area-length method and indexed to body surface area. LV outflow tract (LVOT) velocity-time integral was obtained using pulsed-wave Doppler from apical 3- or 5-chamber views, and stroke volume (SV) was calculated as SV = π × (1/2 × LVOT diameter)2 × LVOT velocity-time integral. A dynamic intraventricular pressure gradient ≥ 30 mm Hg was defined as significant LVOT obstruction (LVOTO).15 Right ventricle (RV) fractional area change (RVFAC), TDI S′ velocity, and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) were measured according to American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.13 RV wall motion was evaluated with visual assessment for the detection of RV involvement. Mitral regurgitation was assessed using colour Doppler and graded semiquantitatively as absent, minimal, mild, moderate, or severe.16 Speckle-tracking analysis automatically traced the endocardial border at end-systole, with manual adjustments to ensure the region of interest covered the full myocardial thickness. Poorly tracked segments were excluded. Final strain analysis integrated peak systolic longitudinal strain values into the standardized 17-segment model (“bull’s-eye” plot) to quantify LV regional and LV global longitudinal strain (LVGLS).13 In this study, LVGLS values are reported in absolute terms.

Primary end point

Patients with TTS were stratified into 2 groups according to the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within 3 months of initial hospital admission. The 3-month timeframe was chosen a priori to capture adverse events occurring during the acute and early recovery phase of TTS. MACE was defined as a composite end point including all-cause mortality, cardiogenic shock, and rehospitalization for heart failure.

Feature selection and model-building

Candidate variables included clinical and echocardiographic parameters. All variables were first screened for data completeness, and those with > 20% missing data were excluded. No patients were excluded solely because of missing data for the primary models. Random forest classifiers were then trained on the remaining variables, and feature importance was determined using the mean decrease in Gini index. Predictor selection was guided by feature importance ranking in combination with clinical interpretability and avoidance of redundant or highly correlated variables. Reduced ML models were then developed by sequentially adding a limited number of top-ranked predictors, with model size constrained a priori to prioritize parsimony considering the modest number of outcome events.

Cross-validation and model performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using stratified fivefold cross-validation. In each iteration, 4 folds (80%) were used for training and the remaining fold (20%) for testing, ensuring that each sample served once as part of the test set. Model performance was assessed in the internal cohort and independent external cohort using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated. The optimal classification threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index, from which sensitivity and specificity were derived. Calibration was assessed with calibration curves comparisons of predicted probabilities with observed event rates. To assess the robustness of model performance, bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was performed to estimate the stability of these metrics. Model interpretability was evaluated using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify the contribution of each feature to model predictions. As a sensitivity analysis, a regression-guided feature selection workflow is additionally reported in Supplemental Appendix S1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using Student t test or Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Random forest algorithms were used for feature selection and importance ranking. The predictive performance of ML models for short-term MACE was according to receiver operating characteristic analysis, and pairwise comparisons of AUC values between models were performed using the DeLong test. Restricted cubic spline regression was used to explore potential nonlinear associations between selected continuous echocardiographic parameters and MACE risk. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R software (version 4.5.1; https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the internal cohort

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. During the 3-month follow-up, 55 patients (21.8%) experienced MACE. Compared with patients without MACE, those who experienced MACE had significantly higher heart rates (HRs) (102.0 [95% CI, 81.5–119.8] vs 92.0 [95% CI, 78.0–106.3] beats per minute; P = 0.008), lower systolic blood pressure (SBP; 112.67 ± 26.25 vs 129.28 ± 25.95 mm Hg; P < 0.001), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP; 67.07 ± 18.93 vs 79.45 ± 17.03 mm Hg; P < 0.001) at admission. Physical triggers were significantly more common than emotional ones (75% vs 9.9%; P < 0.001), and 15.1% of patients had no identifiable or unknow trigger. Chest pain was less frequently reported in the MACE group (18.2% vs 33.5%; P = 0.029). Patients who developed MACE were more likely to have a history of hypertension (76.4% vs 59.9%; P = 0.025) and diabetes mellitus (36.4% vs 22.8%; P = 0.043). No significant differences were observed between groups with respect to age, sex, body mass index (BMI), trigger distribution, presence of shortness of breath, or other medical history variables (all P > 0.05).

Echocardiographic findings at admission are summarized in Table 2. The apical form of TTS was the most prevalent, observed in 75.8% of patients, followed by the midventricular (17.5%), basal (5.2%), and focal (1.6%) variants. Among the subtypes, patients with midventricular TTS were less likely to develop MACE compared with those with other variants (7.3% vs 20.3%; P = 0.025). ML prediction of 3-month MACE

Among the 49 candidate predictors, 3 variables had more than 20% missing data and were excluded before model development (Supplemental Table S1). The remaining 46 variables were entered into the random forest model for feature importance ranking (Supplemental Fig. S1). No patients were excluded solely because of missing data for the primary models. The top 10 ranked features are: DT, DBP, SBP, RVFAC, HR, BMI, LVGLS, TAPSE, A peak velocity, and LV Tei. On the basis of the feature importance ranking and with consideration of model parsimony and clinical interpretability, a limited number of top-ranked predictors were selected for construction of reduced ML models. Because DBP and SBP were strongly correlated and convey overlapping hemodynamic information, they were not included simultaneously in the same model. SBP was retained in subsequent models because of its greater clinical relevance and more widespread use in routine clinical practice and previous risk stratification studies.

Model performance for 3-month MACE prediction in TTS on the basis of different feature sets is summarized in Table 3. Among established ML models, discrimination improved with the sequential inclusion of top-ranked clinical and echocardiographic features. The machine learning-based clinical-echocardiographic (MLCE)-3 model (DT, SBP, and RVFAC) achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.68–0.84), with a sensitivity of 0.69 and specificity of 0.74. The MLCE-4 model (including DT, SBP, RVFAC, and HR) achieved an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.73–0.86), sensitivity of 0.84, and specificity of 0.67. The MLCE-5 model (including DT, SBP, RVFAC, HR, and BMI) yielded an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.73–0.86), sensitivity of 0.80, and specificity of 0.70. The MLCE-6 model (including DT, SBP, RVFAC, HR, BMI, and LVGLS) achieved an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.74–0.87), sensitivity of 0.69, and specificity of 0.81. The MLCE-7 model (including DT, SBP, RVFAC, HR, BMI, LVGLS, and TAPSE) achieved an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.73–0.87), sensitivity of 0.71, and specificity of 0.81. Pairwise AUC comparisons indicated that no significant differences were observed among the MLCE models (all P > 0.05).

External validation

Baseline clinical features and echocardiographic findings of the external validation cohort are reported in Supplemental Tables S4 and S5. The incidence of 3-month MACE in this cohort was 32.8% (n = 65). Similar to the internal cohort, patients who developed MACE showed lower SBP and DBP, higher HR, impaired LVEF, lower SV index, lower A-wave peak velocity, shorter DT, reduced TAPSE and RVFAC, elevated E/A ratio, and a greater prevalence of RV involvement.

LVGLS was sparsely available in the external cohort and therefore excluded from external validation. Accordingly, MLCE-4 and MLCE-5 models were applied for external testing. The MLCE-4 model achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.69–0.84), with sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.61 (Fig. 1A). The MLCE-5 model demonstrated similar performance, with an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.71–0.85), sensitivity of 0.89 and specificity of 0.63 (Fig. 1B).

Calibration of MLCE models

Calibration of the MLCE models was evaluated using calibration curves in the internal and external cohorts (Fig. 2). MLCE-4 and MLCE-5 models demonstrated good calibration in the internal cohort, with predicted probabilities closely matching observed event rates across deciles of risk. In the external cohort, calibration remained acceptable but was less precise, with modest deviations from the ideal line.

Nonlinear association between key features and MACE

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed in the internal cohort to examine nonlinear relationships between key features and MACE risk, with all models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (Fig. 4). Shorter DT (< 129.9 ms) was associated with a markedly increased risk (Fig. 4A). For RVFAC, risk increased sharply below 34.7%, plateaued between 34.7% and 40%, and progressively declined beyond 40% (Fig. 4B). Lower SBP (< 125 mm Hg) and higher HR (> 93 beats per minute) were both associated with increased MACE risk (Fig. 4C and D).

Discussion

The main findings of the present study are: (1) parametric echocardiographic features reflecting LA pressure overload and RV dysfunction were associated with increased MACE among hospitalized TTS patients; (2) random forest analysis identified DT, blood pressure, RVFAC, and HR as top-ranking imaging and/or clinical features to predict adverse hemodynamic events; and (3) we have built and validated ML-based clinical-echocardiographic models for early risk stratification in hospitalized patients with TTS.

Although TTS has traditionally been considered a disorder of systolic dysfunction, its diastolic dysfunction has increasingly drawn attention over time.17–19 Because a disease-specific phenotype has not yet been characterized, the prognostic roles of TTS-associated diastolic function remain poorly defined. In the present study, we identified a spectral Doppler parameter (shortened DT of early diastolic filling) that was associated with MACE in hospitalized TTS patients. This result provides the evidence that LA pressures and/or volume overload might play an essential role in the development of TTS-associated hemodynamic events.14,20–22 Consistent with our23 and other groups’ previous investigations,24,25 the present study again showed that RV dysfunction in TTS patients was associated with increased MACE. Impaired RV function can contribute to hemodynamic instabilities by reducing LV preload and SV.25

Of note, the established ML-based models demonstrated comparable discrimination in the internal and external cohorts. Among them, the MLCE-4 model, incorporating DT, SBP, RVFAC, and HR, provides a clinical feasible framework for early risk stratification. A triad manifestation of “tachycardia, hypotension, and dyspnea” frequently occurred in TTS patients who developed hemodynamic complications. The established model in the present study delineates that increased LA pressure and RV dysfunction might play more important pathological roles in the adverse hemodynamic events in the setting of lower blood pressure and high HR. Although a high HR might reflect a more pronounced initial catecholamine surge in TTS patients, tachycardia itself can further deteriorate effective atrial-ventricular diastolic filling, ventricular preload and SV, worsening a vicious hemodynamic cycle.26 The inter-relations of elevated HR and worse inpatient outcomes have become a promising therapeutic target in TTS patients.11,27,28

Different from previous studies,7,10,29,30 our findings showed that an LV diastolic function parameter showed more prognostic values than LVEF or LVGLS in TTS patients. Significantly increased LA pressure might have resulted from chronic myocardial stiffness and acute catecholamine-induced myocardial injuries.14,20–22 Because LV diastolic dysfunction can persist beyond LV systolic function recovery,31 this temporal disparity explains why it outperformed LVEF and LVGLS in predicting 3-month outcome, and helped in monitoring the follow-up and to guide secondary prevention in TTS survivors.

In the present study, ML highlights an intrinsic linkage of clinical and echocardiographic features, providing insight into their complex correlations in TTS pathophysiology. Clinical and echocardiographic features in the established models are routinely available in clinical practice and through point-of-care echocardiograms. This supports broad clinical applicability and provides interpretable models that might facilitate individualized risk assessment in hospitalized TTS patients.

Limitations

There are several limitations in our study. First, systematic differences in echocardiographic acquisition protocols, measurement techniques, and interpretation among centres might affect model performance. To mitigate such variability, we limited our study to 2 major tertiary cardiovascular centres with complete clinical and comprehensive echocardiographic data. This resulted in a relatively small study size. Second, TTS is a dynamic process. Certain highly specific but less sensitive imaging features (such as LVOTO32) might be missed on single-time echocardiograms. Third, because this is a retrospective TTS study, some degree of diagnostic uncertainty with respect to myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries or myocarditis cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, the composition of the 3-month MACE differed between cohorts, which might affect the interpretation of model predictions. Finally, the present analysis focused on short-term (3-month) outcomes, and the prognostic value of these models for longer-term outcomes in TTS patients warrants further investigation. Future studies with extended follow-up, larger and more diverse cohorts, and detailed reclassification analyses are needed to further validate and refine the proposed risk stratification models.

Conclusion

MLCE prediction models showed prognostic value in early risk stratification in hospitalized TTS patients.
