Recollections of an open U.S.–China anti-hantaviral clinical trial in the 1980s
Authors: Zhi-Ming Zheng
Published: 2026

In mid-December 1985, the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China (PRC), the Department of Defense (DoD) of United States of America (U.S.), and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a proposed study entitled “Double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial of ribavirin therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of epidemic hemorrhagic fever (EHF).” The DoD awarded a contract to the U.S. Army Medical Research and Development Command and the Virus Research Institute of Hubei Medical College (VRIHMC), China. The project was led by John Huggins of the U.S. Army Medical Research Institute of Infectious Diseases (USAMRIID) and by Zhi-Ming Zheng (郑志明) and Chin-Min Hsiang (向近敏) of VRIHMC as co-principal investigators (Figure 1A). With around $1 million in funding, the collaboration quickly began clinical trials with about 300 EHF patients in Hubei province, China, over three years during epidemic seasons. According to Dr. Minzhang Chen (陈敏章), the late Minister of Health of the PRC, this project represented the largest medical research collaboration between China and the U.S. up to that time and the first double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial ever conducted in China. Dr. Chen conveyed this to me during a personal meeting when he authorized the approval of the study. Ribavirin was an investigational new drug in the U.S.; therefore, the proposed study required the use of good clinical practices conducted under FDA standards and with FDA approval. In the early 1980s, laboratories in multiple countries made significant progress in understanding similar diseases then known locally under different names: EHF, Korean hemorrhagic fever (KHF), nephropathia epidemica (a milder form), now known collectively as hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS). HFRS in China and the Republic of Korea is caused by hantavirus infection and has long been a life-threatening disease among farmers during the fall and winter in rural areas, with a mortality rate of up to 10% in Hubei province, China [1,2]. KHF was also a major health problem among troops in the early 1950s. The principal reservoir for hantavirus in China and the Republic of Korea is the striped field mouse, Apodemus agrarius, from whose lung tissues the Hantaan virus was first isolated in 1978 by Ho Wang Lee (李鎬汪) in the Republic of Korea and named in recognition of the Hantaan River [3]. Soon, Zhi-Yi Xu (徐志一) from Shanghai, China, and Chin-Min Hsiang from Wuhan, China, working at the National Institutes of Health in Carleton Gajdusek’s lab, showed that the Chinese EHF was caused by the same virus [4,5]. Subsequently, the Hantaan virus was renamed as Hantan virus or hantavirus and further identified as a member of Bunyavirus [6]. Humans become infected primarily through inhalation of aerosolized urine, droppings, or saliva from infected wild rodents or through dust contaminated by their nesting materials. Patients typically present with high fever, headache, back pain, and abdominal pain during the initial phase of infection. The illness may then progress to hypotension, hemorrhage, or vascular leakage, and acute renal failure characterized by oliguria followed by polyuria. In the early 1980s, supportive care was the only available treatment regimen for HFRS.

A new era in the U.S.–China relationship began with President Richard Nixon’s visit to Beijing in 1972. On January 1, 1979, under President Jimmy Carter, the U.S. formally established diplomatic relations with China. As relations between the two countries warmed up, new opportunities for academic exchange also emerged. I was a member of the 1978 graduate class at VRIHMC. In the spring of 1980, I was selected to pursue advanced training at the Yale University School of Medicine. After extensive training and with anticipation, I reached New Haven, Connecticut, U.S., on October 7, 1981, and began three years of study in clinical and experimental virology under the late Professor Edith Gueh-Djen (G.D.) Hsiung (熊菊贞) in the Department of Laboratory Medicine. My goal was to help establish a first-class national clinical virology program in China. As a Yale–China Association visiting scholar, I was warmly welcomed by Dr. Hsiung and many other faculty members at Yale by visiting their homes and spending weekends with their families.

Returning to China in September 1984 was both exciting and extremely challenging, particularly for scientific research. In January 1985, following the retirement of Professor Chin-Min Hsiang, the former Director of VRIHMC, I was appointed Acting (Deputy) Director. My first step as director was to reorganize and expand the institute as a major medical virology center in southern China. However, efforts to obtain financial support were unsuccessful. Applications to the State Scientific and Technological Commission, the Ministry of Education, and the Ministry of Health of the PRC, and relevant bureaus of Hubei province, China, yielded no funding. I therefore sought support from overseas, exploring scientific collaboration opportunities with virologists in the U.S. The National Natural Science Foundation of China had not yet been established, making international collaboration one of the viable paths to sustain and advance scientific research.

USAMRIID is a major U.S. DoD laboratory dedicated to developing vaccines, drugs, and diagnostics against high-hazard, emerging, or biological threat agents. Its mission has been to protect military personnel and the nation through research on highly dangerous pathogens such as the Ebola virus, hantavirus, and Bacillus anthracis (anthrax), with unique capabilities for biosafety level-4 studies. I met John Huggins at a virology meeting in the U.S. At that time, John was working at USAMRIID, specializing in antiviral research on viral hemorrhagic fevers. During the meeting, John presented an interesting poster on the use of ribavirin to treat hantavirus infection in suckling mice. Ribavirin holds broad-spectrum antiviral activity in cell cultures and animal studies, most likely through inhibition of viral RNA synthesis and host-cell inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase [7]. I spoke with John about HFRS in China, exploring the possibility of a future clinical trial based on his preliminary results. I was already familiar with the discovery of hantavirus in 1978 and, as a graduate student, had worked with lung and kidney tissues from captured field A. agrarius mice, identifying viral antigens in the tissues using convalescent sera from HFRS patients. I wrote to John to follow up on our previous discussion and explore possible collaboration, inviting John to visit Hubei Medical College (HMC) in Wuhan, China. His visit proved highly productive and, together with VRIHMC and HMC Teaching Hospitals (HMCTH), we developed a proposal to evaluate ribavirin therapy in HFRS patients who were receiving standard supportive treatment in Hubei province, China. In the mid-1980s, it was remarkable that we were able to establish a formal collaboration between VRIHMC and USAMRIID with support from both governments. At that time, Wuhan lacked modern clinical laboratory capacity. With USAMRIID’s support, VRIHMC imported advanced laboratory instrumentation and established two dedicated laboratories, one for clinical testing and another for virus detection, data collection, entry, and validation. In the meantime, VRIHMC sent Jianming Hu (胡建明) and Tianming Zhang (张天明) to the U.S. and USAMRIID to receive training on the HFRS clinical assays and, upon returning to Wuhan, helped establish those assays and train the clinical lab personnel. These facilities enabled scientists and physicians to perform essential diagnostics confirming patient infections by detection of hantavirus-specific IgM antibody, an assay developed by James Le Duc and James Meegan [8], and monitoring clinical parameters and pharmacodynamics during the clinical trial.

The clinical aspects of the trial were led by Mei-Ying Guan (管美英) and Zhen-Ou Wu (巫振欧) of the First HMCTH and Qi-Nan Wang (王启楠) and Xi-En Gui (桂希恩) of the Second HMCTH, together with VRIHMC staff and clinicians from nine rural hospitals in the prime epidemic zone with standardized patient care. An extensive regional public education campaign about HFRS recognition and the importance of seeking early hospital care resulted in enrollment of cases early in the disease course when antiviral therapy is most effective. Specimens were collected early each morning at the nine rural hospitals and transported to the central clinical and virology laboratories, and results were returned with the courier on the same day. Through the painstaking efforts of Chinese and American scientists, clinicians, and administrative officials on both sides, the collaboration progressed rapidly and successfully. With open access, visits, timely communication, and extensive discussions (Figure 1B–1C), the original three-year research plan was nearly completed within two years. By 1988, the study had reached a clear conclusion: ribavirin was effective in the treatment of patients with early-stage HFRS. During the epidemic seasons, we enrolled 293 patients with suspected HFRS. Of these, 242 patients with serologically confirmed HFRS by Shu-Yuan Xiao (肖书渊) and Bao-Lian Zhu (朱宝莲) at VRIHMC [8] were included in the final clinical trial analysis, following extensive validation of the collected data from this FDA-regulated study. When the study code was broken, statistical analysis using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) showed a significant reduction in mortality rate from 8.5% in the placebo control group (10/117) to 2.4% in the ribavirin-treated group (3/125). Ribavirin therapy also resulted in a significant reduction in the risk of patients entering the oliguric phase and experiencing hemorrhagic complications [9]. A clinical report adhering to U.S. FDA standards was completed and submitted to both the U.S. FDA and the Drug Regulatory Authority of the Ministry of Health of the PRC for scientific evaluation and approval (Figure 1D). This fruitful achievement in Sino–American collaborative research led to the organization of the 1988 International Symposium on HFRS in Wuhan, China (Figure 1E–1F). The success of the clinical trial also contributed to official approval by the then Drug Regulatory Authority of the Ministry of Health of the PRC and to the use of ribavirin by the U.S. Army as an investigational new drug in the treatment of hantavirus infection in U.S. and Republic of Korea soldiers. While supportive care in HFRS patient management has made significant advances, ribavirin treatment is recommended today in China for the early disease treatment [10].

I conclude this recollection by echoing the final paragraph of a deeply moving article, The power of we, published by James Le Duc, the former Director of the Galveston National Laboratory and one of the USAMRIID virologists who participated in our clinical trial [11]:Since the original isolation of Hantaan virus by Ho Wang Lee, “We” have all contributed to and indeed benefitted from the enormous power of collaborations. It’s better to collaborate than to compete and everyone wins in a collaboration. Through mutual respect and shared vision, “We” have accomplished much and seen the tremendous rewards of working together. “We” have built a knowledge base on an important group of viruses that has been the scourge of society for ages. “We” have worked together to make fundamental discoveries and to develop interventions to mitigate the impact of infections, and “We” have shared our findings with one another through informal conversations, joint meetings, and scientific publications. Our reputation as trusted collaborators will facilitate our continued success; our networks of collaborators are our most valuable assets—we must treasure them. “We” have invested in training future generations of hantavirologists, and “We” have worked to ensure their success. The investments made in nurturing junior colleagues will pay a lifetime of dividends.
