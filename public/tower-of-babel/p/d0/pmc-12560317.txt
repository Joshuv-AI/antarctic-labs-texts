Barriers and enablers for generative artificial intelligence in clinical psychology: a qualitative study based on the COM-B and theoretical domains framework (TDF) models
Authors: Daniel de la Fuente Tambo, Sara Iglesias Moreno, Manuel Armayones Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the perceptions of care psychologists regarding the adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in therapeutic practice. As AI continues to be integrated into various sectors, including healthcare, understanding how psychologists perceive its implementation in therapeutic settings is essential. The study explores the factors that act as barriers and facilitators to GenAI adoption and examines their impact on the future of therapeutic interventions.MethodsA qualitative study design was adopted, involving semistructured, in-depth interviews with 14 private care psychologists in Spanish cities. The study focused on urban private care settings. The interviews were designed based on TDF domains to identify barriers and enablers. All sessions were recorded and transcribed. Data were analysed using a content approach, with the identified topics mapped onto the TDF and COM-B components.ResultsEighteen factors were identified that influenced the decision to accept or reject GenAI in therapy, with 12 factors acting as barriers and 6 acting as facilitators. These factors are classified within the TDF domains. Highlighted barriers included a lack of understanding of AI and concerns about the confidentiality and privacy of information shared in therapy, while the main facilitators were training in AI skills and the possibility of having a digital assistant.ConclusionThis study reveals the need for greater understanding of training in AI among psychologists. The acceptance of AI varies depending on the training and experience of professionals; some show concern for the future of their profession, while others highlight that it is an opportunity to improve interventions. Information privacy concerns are significant and have been identified as key factors for enabling AI deployment.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI), Healthcare psychology, COM-B model, TDF, Barriers and facilitators

Background

Psychologists in clinical practice often face a significant workload that includes administrative tasks, extensive documentation, and time-consuming analyses of patient data. Several studies [1, 2] show that a large portion of their time is spent taking notes, analysing questionnaire responses, and preparing follow-up reports. Additionally, the growing complexity of cases and the need to stay up to date with evidence-based interventions add to their cognitive and logistical burden. These challenges can limit the time available for direct patient care, increasing stress and reducing efficiency in therapeutic processes.

Given these challenges, Generative AI (GenAI) has been proposed as a potential tool to assist psychologists by automating administrative work, helping data analysis, and even supporting clinical decision-making [3]. By reducing the time spent on non-clinical tasks, AI could allow psychologists to dedicate more attention to patient care, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes.

Value-Based Digital Health (Sociedad Digital Basada en Valores, SDBV) is a synergy between digital health and healthcare that focuses on the use of digital technologies to improve the quality of healthcare. Its goals include enhancing healthcare quality, increasing efficiency, and simultaneously improving the patient experience [4]. Within this paradigm, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a key driver for optimizing processes and personalizing care. However, despite the growing adoption of AI in digital health, its application in psychological care remains underexplored. While AI has been widely studied in areas such as medical diagnostics and administrative automation, there is still a lack of research on how digital health principles apply specifically to psychology and how AI-based solutions align with the professional needs of clinical psychologists.

In the medical field, AI is expected to become an essential tool for both practitioners and patients. In some cases, its presence is imperceptible, such as in electronic medical records and MRI analysis. However, there is another form of AI that directly interacts with professionals and patients: Generative AI (GenAI).

GenAI is an AI subfield that focuses on autonomously generating content or responses tailored to human conversations, mimicking human-like interactions through chatbots or virtual assistants. These systems use natural language generation and machine learning techniques to create meaningful dialogues [5]. In the medical or psychological care process, GenAI can take on various roles, offering a wide range of possibilities to improve care and treatment [6]. Recent studies have begun to explore the role of AI in mental health, particularly in diagnostic support, therapy chatbots, and administrative automation [7–9]. However, its integration into psychological practice remains limited, and existing research has not sufficiently addressed how clinical psychologists perceive its implementation. While some studies focus on the technical capabilities of AI, there is still limited understanding of the psychological, ethical, and professional barriers that may affect its adoption. Key questions remain regarding how psychologists perceive GenAI’s role in therapy, what factors facilitate or hinder its use, and whether it aligns with their professional frameworks and ethical considerations.

This research aims to address this gap by systematically analysing the perceptions of private practice psychologists about the introduction of GenAI in therapy. By identifying the factors influencing its acceptance or rejection, this study contributes to the broader discourse on AI integration in Value-Based Digital Health within psychological care.

Aims and objectives

The purpose of this study was to explore the perceptions and expectations of private care practice psychologists regarding GenAI. It aimed to identify the variables that may influence the acceptance (facilitators) or rejection (barriers) of the introduction of GenAI into therapeutic processes. This was intended to lay the foundation for future studies and the development of an intervention plan aimed at promoting the introduction of this technology in the field of care psychology.

This study was guided by the following research questions:

What are the main barriers that influence the rejection of GenAI among private care psychologists?

What are the main barriers that influence the rejection of GenAI among private care psychologists?

What are the key facilitators that contribute to the acceptance of GenAI in therapeutic practice?

How do psychologists perceive the role of GenAI in their professional field, and what expectations do they have regarding its implementation?

This study was based on the COM-B Model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior) [10] and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [11], which were used to identify barriers and enablers affecting the introduction of GenAI in private care practice. Although the COM-B and TDF have been used in other health settings [12, 13], their application in the study of GenAI adoption by healthcare psychology represents an innovation.

Methods

COM-B model and theory domain framework (TDF)

Michie et al. [10] developed the COM-B Model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior) to understand and design interventions for behavior change. This model asserts that behavior (B) is a result of an interaction between an individual’s capability (C), opportunity (O), and motivation (M). Capability involves having the necessary skills and knowledge; Opportunity means having the external conditions that make the behavior possible; and Motivation encompasses the brain processes that influence behavior, including habits, emotional responding, and analytical decision-making. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) is a comprehensive classification of behavior change determinants across 15 domains, which can be aligned with the COM-B components. For instance, TDF domains such as ‘Knowledge’ and ‘Skills’ directly correspond to the ‘Capability’ component of COM-B, while ‘Environmental Context and Resources’ relate to ‘Opportunity’, and domains like ‘Beliefs about Consequences’ and ‘Intentions’ link closely to ‘Motivation’.

Integrating the COM-B Model with the TDF allows for a more detailed examination of the psychological and environmental influences on behavior, as these frameworks together cover a broad spectrum of internal and external determinants. This integration has been supported by previous literature, such as the work by Cane et al. [11], which showed how the TDF can be mapped onto COM-B to provide a more granular understanding of behavior change interventions.

By analysing these components and their interconnections, researchers can identify not only the barriers that hinder behavior change but also the enablers that facilitate it, thereby designing more effective interventions. The combined use of COM-B and TDF is particularly beneficial in settings where complex behaviors are influenced by a myriad of factors, as it allows for a nuanced approach to diagnosing and addressing behavioral issues.

Design

The study design was a qualitative descriptive study and consisted of in-depth, open-ended, non-directive, semi-structured individual interviews. A content analysis of the interviews was conducted using the COM-B Model in combination with the TDF. The COREQ checklist was used to ensure transparency during the presentation of this manuscript. The detailed COREQ checklist is provided in an additional file [See Additional file 1].

Participants

Convenience sampling was used to select professionals who practiced private care in large cities or metropolitan areas in Spain. The eligibility criteria were that the professionals had a degree in psychology, had completed the general health master’s degree (MPGS) or equivalent, and practiced in private practice. In addition, minimal knowledge of digital psychology (video conferencing, artificial intelligence) is needed. All participants signed a consent form for interviews and further data processing. Fourteen interviews were conducted in total. The demographic data of all participants are presented in Table 3. Among all the participants, 86% were female and 14% were male. Most (50%) were aged between 40 and 49 years. With respect to psychological guidance, the most common approaches were cognitive-behavioural and integrative approaches, each represented by 29% of the participants. More than half (57%) had more than ten years of experience, and all professionals worked with adults and adolescents.

Data collection

The design of the interviews was based on the TDF domains and was developed by the research group (DF, SI, and MA) of the Behavior Design Lab of the Universitat Oberta de Catalunya. The interview scripts were structured into two blocks. The first block included closed-ended questions aimed at collecting demographic data from participants, such as their psychological school of practice, years of experience, and types of patients and diagnoses they commonly manage. The second block comprised open-ended questions designed to explore psychologists’ perceptions of GenAI adoption. Initially, these questions assessed the participants’ level of technological ability and their routine use of technology in daily practice. Next inquiries delved into their familiarity with GenAI tools, their opinions on these technologies, their awareness of AI products and possibilities, and their thoughts on AI potentially handling more complex tasks like billing or preliminary diagnostics and treatments. Discussion also covered how they perceived their peers would react to using AI and how their clients might respond, along with their own readiness to integrate such technologies into their practice.

A pilot interview was conducted by the investigator (DF) to ensure that the questions were easily understandable and followed a logical order. This pilot was not included in the analysis. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min, the sessions were audio recorded, and additional notes were taken during each session. Participants were given the opportunity to contact the investigator after their interviews if they wished to withdraw their data or receive a copy of the findings.

Analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim using OpenAI’s Whisper Artificial Intelligence tool [14]. To ensure confidentiality, any personally identifiable information was removed. Data analysis and the assignment of barriers and enablers were performed using Atlas.ti software [15].

Based on these transcripts, an iterative content analysis was conducted focusing on two main topics:

Behavioral Barriers: Factors perceived by psychologists as barriers to the adoption of GenAI in therapy.

The data analysis procedure included the following steps: Two investigators (SI and DF) independently examined the qualitative data transcribed from the interviews, performing deductive classification of barriers and facilitators. Their encodings were then integrated and unified into a single document. A third investigator (MA) reviewed and compared these classifications to identify any discrepancies, ensuring all findings were robust and consistent. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus among the research team.

Once the data analysis was completed, a code screening stage was conducted to select those factors identified in the responses of at least three psychologists. This criterion ensured the removal of barriers or facilitators identified only in isolated responses.

Each identified barrier and facilitator were further classified into a component of the COM-B model and associated with a specific domain of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). This process aimed to figure out how each factor influenced behavior. These classifications were shared, analysed, and agreed upon within the research team to ensure a comprehensive and accurate representation of the data.

Results

The COM-B model, in combination with the TDF domains, provided a structure to identify psychologists’ perceptions regarding the adoption of GenAI in care psychology. Eighteen main factors related to the seven TDF domains were identified that significantly influenced the decision to accept or reject the use of GenAI in therapy. Of these, 12 factors belonging to 5 domains functioned as barriers, showing the resistance of some psychologists to integrate GenAI, whereas 6 factors associated with 5 domains functioned as enablers, promoting the acceptance of this technology. TDF domain: knowledge

The “knowledge” domain refers to psychologists’ understanding of GenAI and its applicability in therapeutic interventions. The data obtained from the interviews indicate that there is a deficiency in this knowledge, which is a significant barrier to the adoption of GenAI. Most psychologists expressed uncertainty or misunderstanding about the capabilities and usefulness of GenAI, affecting their willingness to integrate these technologies into their therapeutic practice. This is illustrated by a participant who expressed concerns about their lack of training:

“I am not sure how I could use AI in my practice. I would need more training on what I can and cannot do.” (Interview 1).

This statement reflects a broader trend among participants, emphasizing the need for educational programs to increase awareness and competence in AI tools.

TDF domain: environmental context and resources

The “environmental context and resources” domain highlights external factors that influence psychologists’ willingness to adopt GenAI. One of the most prominent concerns identified was the risk associated with data privacy and security, particularly regarding the confidentiality of session recordings and patient information. As one participant expressed:

“There is a fear of what happens to all information. If a session is being recorded, where is that session going? This information is very confidential and needs to be well explained.” (Interview 1).

This concern underscores the need for clear regulatory frameworks and data protection policies to ensure that AI tools comply with ethical and legal standards in psychological practice.

In contrast, a factor in this domain was identified as facilitator, the potential for AI to reduce administrative workload, allowing psychologists to focus more on patient care. One of the most frequently mentioned benefits was the possibility of having an AI-powered assistant to help with appointment scheduling, documentation, and follow-up tasks. This was illustrated by one participant:

“…it’s that I think it’s super fascinating…, because then there’s also one thing, …, writing the medical records is the horror…” (Interview 4).

This statement reflects a broader sentiment among psychologists that administrative duties consume a significant portion of their time, detracting from direct therapeutic work. The use of AI for automating documentation and other routine tasks could be a valuable tool for improving workflow efficiency, reducing burnout, and allowing clinicians to dedicate more time to patient interactions.

TDF domain: Social/professional role and identity

The social/professional role and identity domain focuses on how psychologists perceive their professional roles and identities in relation to the introduction of GenAI. A key barrier identified was the fear of judgments from colleagues, which reflects concern about how other professionals may perceive that using GenAI in therapy may be associated with not being a good professional. One participant expressed concerns about the potential stigma associated with integrating AI into therapy:

“And what judgment would you have if your colleagues used it? that they are not thinking about themselves. I would have that fear…” (Interview 2).

This quote reflects an underlying apprehension about how AI adoption might be interpreted as unprofessional or inconsistent with established therapeutic norms. Such concerns highlight the importance of institutional endorsement and peer discussions to normalize the integration of AI in psychological practice.

The “social/professional role and identity” domain also highlights the importance of institutional endorsement in shaping psychologists’ willingness to adopt AI. A key facilitator identified in the study was the potential role of professional organizations in legitimizing AI-assisted practices and providing ethical guidance. As one participant explained:

“…a good part of my colleagues need a lot of the support of the psychologists’ college or an authority that tells them they can use it and like……” (Interview 6).

This statement underscores the hesitation among professionals to integrate AI into their practice without clear institutional guidance. The endorsement of AI technologies by regulatory bodies or professional associations could provide a sense of security, ensuring that ethical and practical concerns are addressed. Such support may be essential in promoting broader acceptance of AI-assisted interventions among psychologists.

TDF domain: Social influences

The “social influences” domain highlights the role of societal norms and generational differences in shaping attitudes toward AI adoption. One factor that emerged as a facilitator was the perception that younger generations, particularly Gen Z and teenagers, are more open to AI-based interventions. One participant noted this generational shift in attitudes:

“Younger people, more Gen Z and teenagers, that’s why maybe yes, because it truly grinds them the roll and I think they don’t have as much data protection awareness…” (Interview 7).

This statement suggests that digital-native generations may be more willing to engage with AI tools in therapy, as they are accustomed to interacting with technology in various aspects of their lives.

TDF domain: beliefs about capabilities

The “beliefs about capabilities” domain reflects psychologists’ concerns about their ability to adapt to AI-driven changes in their profession. One major barrier identified was the fear of being replaced or professionally devalued as AI becomes more advanced. One participant articulated this concern:

“There’s a feeling, I think, collective that artificial intelligence is doing things that we would never have imagined, and sectors are being removed, right?” (Interview 7).

This statement reflects a broader apprehension about job displacement, a concern that has been observed in other fields integrating AI-based solutions. While AI tools may offer valuable support in administrative and diagnostic tasks, some professionals worry that their expertise could be undervalued or that reliance on AI could alter traditional therapeutic roles. Addressing these concerns through professional development initiatives and clear role delineation could help mitigate resistance to AI adoption in psychological practice.

TDF domain: beliefs about consequences

The “beliefs about consequences” domain highlights psychologists’ concerns regarding the potential negative impact of AI on the therapeutic relationship and patient acceptance. One of the key barriers identified was the perception that certain patient groups, particularly those with paranoia or heightened fears, might strongly resist AI-based interventions. One psychologist illustrated this concern:

“There are patients who wouldn’t want it… most of all, paranoids. I mean, patients with paranoia or with many fears… this wouldn’t be accepted for me.” (Interview 1).

This statement reflects a broader concern among clinicians that AI could be met with resistance from vulnerable patient populations, particularly those with mistrust in technology or heightened anxiety about surveillance. These perceptions suggest that successful AI integration in therapy may require tailored approaches, including transparent communication, informed consent strategies, and careful consideration of patient profiles to ensure ethical and effective implementation.

TDF domain: optimism

The “optimism” domain highlights psychologists’ positive attitudes and curiosity toward the potential of GenAI in therapeutic practice. While some professionals expressed scepticism or concern, others demonstrated a strong interest in understanding how AI could enhance their work. This openness to technological innovation was reflected in the following statement:

“I would see this as extremely interesting… if I saw someone who had incorporated it and used it like that. I’d love to ask you. How do you do it? What do you do? Curious about how they simply, how it is working, and how they react… And how do you use it…” (Interview 4).

This statement suggests that exposure to real-world use cases and peer experiences could play a significant role in fostering AI adoption. Observing how AI is effectively integrated into therapy by colleagues may help reduce scepticism and increase psychologists’ confidence in its application. Future training programs and pilot initiatives could leverage this curiosity by demonstrating concrete examples of AI-assisted interventions in psychological care.

Discussion

The application of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) has proven to be an effective method for structuring interviews and analysing behavioral determinants in health contexts [12, 13, 16, 17]. Sorting psychologist perceptions by COM-B categories and TDF domains allows information to be efficiently organized and contextualized, establishing a solid foundation for future interventions.

In this study, seven TDF domains were identified within the three main components of the COM-B model: psychological capability, opportunity (physical and social), and reflective motivation.

In the psychological capacity category, the knowledge domain focused on psychologists’ understanding of GenAI and its application in therapy. The main barriers identified included insufficient knowledge of AI tools, their potential, and a lack of skills for their proper use in therapeutic settings. These findings align with previous studies on AI adoption in mental health, which highlight the importance of training programs to increase digital literacy among clinicians [18, 19].

In the COM-B Opportunity category, two key components were differentiated: physical and social opportunities. The first, represented by the domain “environmental context and resources,” identifies barriers, such as concerns about data privacy and security, as well as the lack of clear regulations to regulate the use of AI in psychology. These concerns are consistent with prior research indicating that the absence of regulatory frameworks remains a major barrier to AI adoption in healthcare [20, 21].

On the other hand, social opportunity, which includes the domains ‘Social/professional role and identity’ and ‘Socially influences’, highlights how social norms significantly impact the adoption of GenAI, revealing the influence of colleagues.

In the category of reflective motivation, expectations and beliefs about the efficacy and consequences of GenAI adoption were discussed. Within this framework, the ‘beliefs about capabilities’ domain underscores confidence or lack of confidence in the ability to use GenAI efficiently, showing concerns about its influence on less experienced psychologists. The ‘Beliefs about Consequences’ domain contemplates perceptions of the potential positive and negative effects of GenAI, such as fear that technology will damage the therapeutic link or that there will be rejection by clients. Finally, optimism encompasses the overall attitude towards technology, highlighting both its future utility and its ability to improve clinical practice.

Although the current study on the adoption of GenAI by healthcare psychologists is pioneering in its field, the results are aligned with those obtained in the research referenced in [22], where opinions were collected from therapists on how AI could help improve accuracy in the application of CBT techniques.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Psychologists under 30 were not included, omitting the perspective of a generation that might have different views on AI. The research focused solely on clinical psychologists in the private sector in a specific geographic setting, which limits the generalization of the results. Including professionals from the public sector could have enriched the findings. Moreover, not all psychologists were at the same level of familiarity or acceptance of AI technologies, which could have influenced their responses and, consequently, the study findings.

The constant evolution of AI is another significant limitation, as rapid technological advances can quickly render the results obsolete, altering attitudes and perceptions about the identified barriers as professionals become more familiar with these technologies.

From a methodological viewpoint, the interpretation of qualitative interview data could be heavily influenced by the researchers’ theoretical framework and biases, potentially affecting the identification and analysis of barriers and facilitators. Furthermore, the use of a deductive approach, based on predetermined theoretical categories, may have constrained the identification of additional factors beyond those covered by the COM-B and TDF frameworks, potentially overlooking emergent themes.

Additionally, the gender imbalance in the sample (86% female and 14% male) could influence the results, particularly regarding perceptions of AI.

Lastly, the absence of quantitative metrics limits the ability to measure the prevalence or intensity of perceptions and attitudes towards AI, restricting the generalization of the findings beyond the cases studied.

Conclusions

This study provides insight into the barriers and facilitators influencing the adoption of GenAI in psychological practice, offering a structured framework using the COM-B model and TDF to identify key factors affecting behavioral change.

The study highlights that technological knowledge gaps, ethical concerns, and regulatory uncertainty represent major barriers to AI adoption. At the same time, perceived efficiency, customization, and professional curiosity serve as motivating factors. These findings align with previous research on technology adoption in clinical settings, reinforcing the importance of digital literacy, ethical guidelines, and institutional support in shaping AI acceptance among psychologists.

Future research should continue exploring how training, regulatory frameworks, and real-world implementation can facilitate AI integration into psychological care. As AI technology evolves, ongoing research will be essential to ensure that its adoption aligns with ethical principles and enhances—rather than disrupts—therapeutic practices.
