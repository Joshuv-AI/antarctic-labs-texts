First report of the comparative effectiveness of insect visitors for cranberry pollination and yield in Chile
Authors: Bruna K. Pinheiro-Costa, José N. Mesquita-Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiotic pollination sustains not only key ecological processes but also improves fruit set and fruit quality in pollinator-dependent crops like cranberries (Vaccinium macrocarpon). Distinguishing the subset of pollinators among floral visitors and combining this information with their frequency in flowers is essential for optimizing pollination services in cranberry crops, which improves fruit set through biotic pollination and overcoming the limitations of self-pollination. However, the relative contribution of different pollinator taxa to cranberry agricultural outcomes is entirely unknown in the Southern Hemisphere. Therefore, we compared the pollination performance and contribution to fruit yield of different floral visitors in commercial Chilean cranberry fields. Additionally, we sought to determine whether the sexual phase of the flower, mediated by dichogamy and herkogamy, co-influences pollination effectiveness. We assessed the performance of pollinators based on single-visit tests and their subsequent outcomes, such as fruit set, weight and viable seed set. Visit frequency was also recorded through 10-minute observations taken at five intervals throughout the day, in three cranberry orchards in southern Chile.ResultsPollination performance and visitation frequency varied significantly between groups of flower visitors. The honey bee, the most frequent floral visitor, significantly increased fruit set compared to unvisited control flowers. Notably, the positive effect of honey bee visits on the fruit set is also reflected in fruit quality parameters, such as fruit weight or viable seed number. Despite their high frequency of visitation to cranberry flowers, flies demonstrated low pollination efficiency. Although no significant effect on fruit set was detected in relation to the temporal separation of staminate and pistillate phases, a pronounced effect on fruit quality was observed. Flowers visited by insects during the pistillate phase produced significantly heavier fruits and more viable seeds than unvisited flowers.ConclusionWhile various insects visited the cranberry crops in southern Chile, only the honey bees significantly increased fruit set and weight and viable seed set. Synchronizing pollinator activity with the female receptive phase is important for effective pollen transfer and maximizing fruit quality. Promoting pollinator stability and diversity may optimize pollination services and ensure resilient crop production in changing agricultural landscapes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08402-3.

Background

Despite the importance of distinguishing between true pollinators and mere floral visitors, many pollination studies rely on indirect measures, such as visit frequency [1, 2] or anther-stigma contact [3], to infer the functional role of pollinators. However, these proxies fail to capture pollination outcomes [4]. A high visitation rate does not necessarily translate to effective pollination [5, 6], particularly when insects deposit low-viability [7, 8] or heterospecific pollen [9], or when multiple visits damage the pistil [10], or clog the stigma and inhibit pollen tube growth [11]. In other cases, flower visitors may visit a particular sexual phase in dioecious or dichogamous plants, thereby hindering legitimate pollen flow [5, 6, 12]. Importantly, while visitation frequency is a useful indicator, it alone does not guarantee pollination success.

Instead, it should be assessed through direct measurements of pollination outcomes, including stigmatic pollen deposition or subsequent female reproductive parameters such as fruit set, seed number, and fruit weight [2, 5]. However, both approaches present limitations and carry distinct biological and practical implications. A significant fraction of pollen grains deposited on a stigma may not germinate, develop pollen tubes, or successfully fertilize ovules, ultimately failing to contribute to the final number of mature seeds [13, 14]. Furthermore, in many plant species, post-pollination processes (e.g., pollen competition, selective ovule abortion, resource limitation, and maternal control) can greatly affect reproductive outcomes, thereby weakening the link between pollen deposition and potential viable seed production. In contrast, female reproductive success, commonly measured as fruit quality and seed set, reflects realized yield relative to potential fruit or seed production of the plant [5]. This metric integrates not only pollen delivery but also all subsequent developmental processes that determine reproductive outcomes [15]. As a result, seed set and fruit quality are particularly relevant for applied pollination studies, especially in agricultural and commercial contexts, where the goal is maximizing marketable yield [15].

Effective pollination is not only a key ecological process but also improves agronomic traits such as fruit set and fruit quality in pollinator-dependent crops like American cranberries (Vaccinium macrocarpon; hereafter referred to as cranberry) [16–18]. Similar to other species in the genus Vaccinium (Ericales: Ericaceae), the cranberry is considered a buzz-pollinated crop that requires anther vibration to release pollen [19]. Although cranberry flowers are self-compatible, they exhibit protandrous spatial separation (herkogamy), with the staminate phase occurring before the pistillate phase [20]. The staminal column is formed by the grouping of anthers with terminal poricidal dehiscence. After the staminate phase, the single pistil becomes viscid and is extruded beyond the tips of the anthers (dichogamy) [20–22], marking the receptivity of the stigma and the onset of the pistillate phase [23]. Despite clear morphological and functional differences between these floral phases, it remains unclear whether female reproductive success varies between them. Differences in stigma position [24, 25] may influence pollen receipt, pollen quality, and subsequent fertilization success. However, the extent to which these phase-specific traits translate into differences in fruit set, seed number, or seed quality has not been consistently demonstrated on cranberry.

The morphological specialisations of cranberry flowers, such as the temporal (dichogamy) and spatial (herkogamy) separation of staminate and pistillate functions, and the presence of poricidal anthers, primarily prevent self-fertilisation, thereby increasing their dependence on biotic pollination [21–23]. Consistent with this, self- pollination in cranberries rarely results in fruit production [23, 26], when it does occur, it leads to reduced fruit set and lower seed number compared to cross-pollinated flowers, indicating negative effects on both fruit quantity and quality [26, 27]. In contrast, numerous studies have demonstrated the critical role of insect visitation in sexual reproduction, which strongly influences variation in fruit set and yield in cranberry crops [28]. In North America, where cranberry is native, flowers are visited by a diverse assemblage of native bees. More than 80 bee species, including managed pollinators like bumble bees (Bombus spp.) and honey bees (Apis mellifera), are associated with this crop [23, 29–31]. Among these visitors, bumble bees and leaf-cutting bees (Megachile spp.) have been identified as particularly efficient pollinators of cranberry [23, 32], indicating that the most effective pollinators are those capable of vibrating the anthers through buzz pollination. However, the decline of native pollinator populations, coupled with increasing production pressures, has led to the widespread practice of supplementing pollination with honey bee hives, even though these bees are relatively inefficient at pollinating Vaccinium crops [29, 33].

Over the last three decades, Chile has emerged as a major producer in the global cranberry market, becoming the leading producer outside North America [34]. Currently, the country’s cultivated area is approximately 700 hectares, concentrated mainly in southern Chile, especially in the Los Ríos region [35]. The country’s annual production exceeds 26,000 metric tons [36], making Chile the third largest producer in the world, and the leading producer in the Southern Hemisphere. In 2020, Chilean cranberry production accounted for about 19% of global production [28]. Despite its significant production relevance, scientific research on cranberry cultivation remains largely unexplored in Chile and across the Southern Hemisphere [28]. The lack of research into cranberry pollination in Chile, the largest producer of cranberry in the southern hemisphere, leaves a critical knowledge gap regarding how this crop interacts with local pollinator assemblages and environmental conditions [37]. Understanding cranberry pollination dynamics in this region is essential not only for identifying the effective pollinators operating in these novel agroecosystems, but also for ensuring stable fruit production and developing region-specific management practices that focus on the most effective pollinators [38].

Although stigmatic pollen deposition has been well studied in cranberry cropping systems [23, 32], there is still limited comparative information on pollinator contributions when evaluated through fruit set and fruit yield [28]. In the agricultural context, fruit set and quality are the primary outputs of crop production, key indicators of productivity, quality, and management efficiency [39, 40]. One of the most effective methods to improve the quality of fruit crops is to promote sufficient pollination, which influences fruit shape, color, flavor, and shelf life [41, 42]. Assessments of effectiveness per visit offer a direct measure of pollination performance across different insect visitors, and can significantly improve cranberry fruit set through biotic pollination, overcoming the limitations of self-pollination [23]. However, the contributions of individual pollinator taxa, differences in their foraging behaviour, and how frequently they visit flowers translate into effective pollination services are not well understood globally and are particularly absent in the Global South. Therefore, our aim was to compare the contributions of different floral visitors to cranberry pollination and fruit yield in Chile, considering both visitation frequency and per-visit pollination performance. Additionally, we sought to determine whether the sexual phase of the flower, mediated by dichogamy and herkogamy, co-influences pollination effectiveness.

Methods

Study areas

We conducted fieldwork in three cranberry orchards in southern Chile between November and December 2023 and 2024 (Table 1). The cranberry orchards, ranging from 141 to 225 ha in size, were all conventionally managed and supplemented with colonies of exotic managed honey bees (Apis mellifera Linnaeus, 1758; Table 1). Native fragments of the southern temperate forest (Valdivian ecoregion) and cultivated vegetation form a heterogeneous mosaic of landscapes around the orchards. Local temperatures ranged from 14 °C to 31 °C, with an average of 20.2 °C. The average relative humidity was 45%, and wind speeds ranged from 0.11 to 16.8 km/h.

Study species

The cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon Aiton.: Ericaceae) is a domesticated, low-growing (30–60 cm), woody perennial vine that is native to the north-eastern part of North America [43]. Specimens are cultivated in specially prepared beds (typically 150–250 m long and about 50 m wide), where the plants form dense mats, usually in wet or flooded areas [37, 44, 45]. These beds have an artificially constructed soil profile comprising layers of loamy and sandy mineral soils, including peat- and gravel-rich material [43].

The zygomorphic flowers of the cranberry plant open sequentially in an acropetal pattern, progressing from lower positions to upward floral buds [46]. The nectaries are positioned at the base of the staminal column and produce a uniform amount of nectar during anthesis, ranging from 300 to 400 µg of dissolved sugar [20]. Individual flowers produce, on average, 7.000 pollen tetrads, with each tetrad containing four pollen grains.

Daily frequency of flower visitors

Orchards were visited sequentially, one per day, and this cycle was repeated for 15 non-consecutive days during the peak flowering period over two years. Visit frequency was conducted by three to six observers, totalling approximately 64 h of direct observation (Table S1). Within the selected cranberry bed, observations were made using randomly placed 1 × 1 m quadrats. Each observer stood in front of a quadrant for 10 min during five fixed time intervals (10:00–11:59, 12:00–13:59, 14:00–15:59, 16:00–17:59, and 18:00–19:59) and recorded all flower visitors that made contact with any reproductive floral structure. For each visitor, taxon, number of flowers visited, date, time, cranberry cultivar, and orchard ID were recorded. Insects that could not be identified in the field were captured and subsequently identified in the laboratory (Laboratorio de Ecología de Abejas, Universidad Católica del Maule), with species determinations validated by an insect taxonomist (V. H. Monzón). We adopted a criterion of a minimum of ten observations of a given insect taxon, while those insect taxa with between 10 and 20 observations were grouped. Visit frequency was calculated as the total number of visits per 10-min observation period for each flower-visiting taxon (honey bees, other bees, and flies) observed in each quadrant, combining the observations of all observers during a given time interval [47].

Pollination efficiency and fruit yield

Simultaneously with the observations of the daily frequency of flower visitors, we conducted 600 h of sampling effort for the single-visit experiments (10:00–18:00) across the three studied orchards (Table S1). These single-visit experiments were performed in the same cranberry beds and under similar climatic conditions to assess pollination efficiency and fruit yield. Cranberry flower visitors were categorized as pollinators or ineffective pollinators based on their contribution to fruit set and yield, following the single-visit protocol [48]. One bed of the cranberry (cultivar Pilgrim) was randomly selected for the pollination experiments in each orchard. Within the selected bed, 10 sampling sites were established, each at least 10 m apart. At each sampling site, we enclosed as many flower buds as possible in tulle bags (8 × 6 cm; mesh size 0.5 mm), removing any open flowers to ensure only unvisited flowers remained. Visits were considered valid when flower visitors contacted the anthers or the stigma. Once the flowers had opened, we removed the bags and allowed a single insect to visit the flower cluster between 10:00 and 18:00. To standardize exposure, we ensured that experimental flowers were 1–4 days post-anthesis at the time of visitation. A set of flowers was bagged during the budding stage and kept bagged throughout, excluding floral visitors (control of unvisited flowers). We recorded the sexual phase of each flower at the time of insect visitation, along with the corresponding experimental treatment (unvisited or insect-visited flowers), cultivar, orchard, date, and time. Immediately after each visit, we identified (or collected) the visiting insect and marked the visited flower with coloured wool yarn (N = 108 flowers; Table S2). The set of unvisited flowers was also marked with a different colour of yarn to serve as a control group (N = 53 flowers; Table S2).

The insects captured in single-visit experiments were killed using absolute alcohol and preserved in individually labelled vials for taxonomic analysis. Bee specimens were identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level using a combination of interactive and dichotomous keys [49–51]. The dipterans were identified and categorized following [52–54]. These identifications were validated by comparison with reference specimens maintained in the entomological collections of the Laboratorio de Ecología de Abejas at the Universidad Católica del Maule, by the insect taxonomist V. H. Monzón. Voucher specimens of all collected individuals are deposited in the Entomological Collection of the Laboratorio de Ecología de Abejas, Universidad Católica del Maule (UCM), Chile.

Just after a floral visitor left the experimental flowers, we re-bagged them. After approximately 100 ± 3 days, we checked whether these flowers had set fruit (fruit set). The formed fruits were collected and weighed in the field using a precision balance (Dblue R DBPDWS32S, 0.001 g resolution). Viable seeds were then counted in the laboratory following the method described by [27] and illustrated in Fig. S1a. Insects whose visits resulted in significantly higher fruit than the control of unvisited flowers were classified as pollinators, while those with no significant effect or a negative effect were classified as ineffective pollinators [47]. To compare the relative contribution of the different flower visitors to fruit yield, we measured the weight of the fruit (fruit weight) and the number of viable seeds per fruit (seed set).

Statistical analysis

We used R version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, 2020) to build Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) and test our predictions using the glmmTMB package [55]. Orchard identity and sampling date were incorporated as random intercepts in all models to account for potential non-independence and temporal or spatial variation in the data. If the minimum threshold of ten visits was not reached for the single-visit test, the flower-visitor species was grouped into respective higher taxonomic categories (genus, family, and order).

To evaluate whether the frequency of floral visitation varied among visitor groups, we fitted a GLMM using a truncated negative binomial distribution and a log link function. The model included the visitor group, the time interval, and their interaction as fixed effects. To compare the ability of different floral visitor groups to promote fruit set, we fitted a GLMM with a binomial distribution and a logit link function. In this model, fruit set was included as the binary response variable (1 = fruit developed; 0 = no fruit developed), and flower visitor group was included as a fixed effect. To estimate variation in fruit weight among flower-visitor groups, we fitted a zero-inflated Tweedie model. Viable seed set (response variable) was analysed using a zero-inflated GLMM with a negative binomial distribution.

Additionally, to assess the effect of the sexual phase of visited flowers on their ability to promote fruit set compared to unvisited flowers, we fitted a binomial GLMM with fruit set as the binary response variable (1 = fruit developed; 0 = no fruit). Differences in fruit weight among sexual phases were analysed using a Tweedie model with a log link function and no zero-inflation component. To evaluate whether seed set varied between sexual phases, we fitted a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution (nbinom2) and no zero-inflation component. The number of developed seeds was used as the response variable, with sexual phase as a fixed effect.

We evaluated the model fit using diagnostic plots from the DHARMa package [56]. Only models that showed no signs of overdispersion, outliers, or deviations from residual uniformity were selected for interpretation. Post hoc comparisons among pollinator groups were performed Tukey multiple comparisons using the glht function (multcomp package). The contribution of each visitor to the expected fruit weight was estimated using the estimated marginal means (EMMs) derived from the GLMM and back-transformed from the log scale to the original scale (grams). EMMs and pairwise comparisons between the treatments and the control flowers were obtained using the emmeans package [57]. We determined statistical significance via Type II Wald Chi-square tests (Anova function, car package; [46]). To assess whether fruit weight was associated with the number of developed seeds, we performed a Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (rs). Significance was determined using Student’s t-test, using the R statistics package.

Results

The cultivated cranberry variety (Pilgrim) studied has flowers measuring 7.2 ± 1.74 mm in length and containing an average of 25.1 ± 2.6 ovules. Flowering occurs over a prolonged period, extending from November to January. Individual flowers remain open for approximately 5–7 days, although some may persist longer. Hermaphrodite flowers exhibit spatial and temporal separation of the reproductive structures. Initially, they are in a staminate (male) phase, which typically lasts 2–3 days. This is followed by a pistillate (female) phase, which lasts 3–4 days and is characterized by stigma elongation and receptivity. At the beginning of the pistillate phase, the petals may acquire a pinkish coloration and become fully reflexed, bending upward and folding beneath the pedicel (Fig. 1). Fruit development follows flowering, and berries typically ripen during the first three weeks of March.

Frequency and abundance of floral visitors

A total of 1,118 flower visits were recorded across all cranberry orchards. Twenty-seven morphospecies of floral visitors were observed and categorised into three groups: honey bees (Apis mellifera Linnaeus, 1758), flies (Diptera), and other bees (Table S3). The “other bees” group comprises native sweat bees (Callistochlora spp., Halictidae) and introduced bumblebees (Bombus terrestris (Linnaeus, 1758)) (Fig. 1). The flies group included the species of the order Diptera: Platycheirus chalconota (Philippi, 1865), Palpada sp., Allograpta exotica (Wiedemann, 1830), Eristalis tenax (Linnaeus, 1758), and Toxomerus vertebratus Rondani, 1863 (syrphids), as well as Oxysarcodexia spp. (Sarcophagidae) (Fig. 1).

Visitation frequency varied significantly among the three groups (GLMM χ² = 200.1, df = 4, p < 0.001; Fig. 2). Flies exhibited lower visitation frequency compared to honey bees (Tukey-adjusted multiple comparisons, β = 1.29 ± 0.33, z = -3.88, p < 0.001) and wild bees (β = 1.12 ± 0.43, z = -2.61, p = 0.024). No difference in visitation frequency was detected among honey bees and other bees (β = -0.17 ± 0.45, z = -0.38, p = 0.923). The frequency of visits varied across time intervals (χ² = 11.81, df = 4, p = 0.019; Fig. 3); however, the interaction between time interval and visit frequency between groups was not significant (χ² = 8.73, df = 4, p = 0.365).

Effect of flower visitors on pollination efficiency and fruit yield

The “other bees” group did not meet the minimum visitation threshold required for inclusion in the pollination efficiency analyses. Consequently, the final efficiency comparisons included only managed honey bees and flies (Diptera), as these species provided sufficient visits for statistical analysis.

The fruit set of cranberry varied depending on the flower visitor group (GLMM: χ² = 7.03, df = 2, p = 0.029; Fig. 4; Table 2). The probability of fruit set was higher for flowers visited by A. mellifera than for the control of unvisited flowers (Tukey-adjusted multiple comparisons β = 1.06 ± 0.41, z = 2.56, p = 0.027; Table 2). In contrast, visits by flies had no significant effect on fruit set compared with unvisited controls (β = 0.19 ± 0.69, z = 0.28, p = 0.956). However, no significant difference was found in the number of fruits produced by flowers visited by A. mellifera and Diptera (β= -0.87 ± 0.66, z = -1.32, p = 0.377; Table 2).

The flower visitor group also affected fruit weight (GLMM: χ² = 11.14, df = 2, p = 0.004; Fig. 4). The fruit produced by flowers visited by A. mellifera was heavier than the fruit produced by unvisited flowers (Tukey-adjusted multiple comparisons β = 1.15 ± 0.36, z = 3.20, p = 0.004; Table 2), with an estimated mean weight of 0.36 g (95% CI: 0.19–0.70 g). Fruits from unvisited flowers had the lowest weight (0.12 g; 95% CI: 0.05–0.27 g), while fruits from flowers visited by flies were intermediate in weight (0.17 g; 95% CI: 0.05–0.57 g) and did not differ significantly from unvisited fruits (β = 0.40 ± 0.63, z = 0.63, p = 0.798). No significant difference was found between the weight of fruit produced by flowers visited by A. mellifera and Diptera (β= -0.75 ± 0.59, z = -1.27, p = 0.400; Table 2). Representative photographs of fruits resulting from the different single-visit treatments are presented in Fig. S1 b–d.

Cranberry showed a strong positive correlation between fruit weight and seed development (rs = 0.85, p < 0.001; Fig. S2). The number of viable seeds produced per fruit of cranberry also depended on the type of flower visitor (GLMM: χ² = 10.98, df = 2, p = 0.004; Fig. 4). Fruits from flowers visited by A. mellifera contained more viable seeds than fruit from the control of unvisited flowers (Tukey-adjusted multiple comparisons β = 2.04 ± 0.52, z = 3.92, p < 0.001), with 1.80 viable seeds per fruit (95% CI: 0.56–5.77). In contrast, fruits from the control (0.23 seeds; 95% CI: 0.05–1.06) and those visited by dipterans (0.28 seeds; 95% CI: 0.03–2.98) produced significantly fewer seeds. There was no difference in seed set between Diptera and control (β = 0.19 ± 1.19, z = 0.16, p = 0.985), or between A. mellifera and Diptera (β= -1.85 ± 1.13, z = -1.67, p = 0.204; Table 2).

Effect of flower sexual phase on pollinator efficiency and fruit yield

The sexual phase of the cranberry flower during the insect visitation affected both fruit weight (χ² = 7.19, df = 2, p = 0.027) and seed set (χ² = 11.80, df = 2, p = 0.002). However, no significant effect on fruit set was detected (χ² = 4.47, df = 2, p = 0.107; Fig. 5; Table 2). Flowers visited by insects during the pistillate phase produced fruits heavier than unvisited flowers (Tukey-adjusted multiple comparisons β = 1.00 ± 0.39, z = 2.54, p = 0.029), and contained more viable seeds (β = 2.10 ± 0.59, z = 3.57, p < 0.001; Table 2). Fruit weight and seed set did not differ between the pistillate phase and the control of unvisited flowers (Table 2). Furthermore, no significant differences in fruit weight were observed between the staminate and pistillate phases of cranberry flowers (β = 0.04 ± 0.41, z = 0.11, p = 0.994) or viable seed formation (β= -0.76 ± 0.61, z = -1.26, p = 0.414; Table 2).

Discussion

This study represents the first investigation of cranberry pollination in the Southern Hemisphere, revealing the contribution of key pollinator taxa and highlighting their potential roles in supporting fruit yield in Chile. Various insect species visited cranberry crops in southern Chile, but only non-sonicating honey bees were effective pollinators, improving overall fruit set and weight. Meanwhile, native flies, despite their relatively high visitation frequency in cranberry flowers, exhibited lower pollination efficiency. Evaluating pollination based solely on visitation frequency or abundance, without linking it to fruit set and quality, may overlook the functional significance of different pollinator taxa. These findings provide an important baseline for evaluating pollination dynamics and developing management strategies in this region, while also expanding the global understanding of cranberry pollination ecology and crop-specific pollination requirements.

Cranberry flowers were visited by native flies (Diptera) and sweat bees (Halictidae), as well as by introduced bumblebees and honey bees. The diversity of flies, particularly hoverflies (Syrphidae), was notable, accounting for 81% of the recorded morphospecies, a pattern comparable to that reported in studies from the Northern Hemisphere [58, 59]. Among these, Toxomerus vertebratus, Eristalis tenax, and species of the genus Platycheirus were especially prominent, with the first two mirroring the hoverfly community commonly observed in North American cranberry crops [59]. Adult syrphids forage on cranberry flowers for both pollen and nectar and are considered potential pollinators because they can transport substantial amounts of pollen [60]. Notably, Eristalis tenax has been reported to prefer cranberry pollen, carrying pollen loads composed of more than 90% grains of this species, values substantially higher than those recorded for species of the genus Toxomerus [59]. However, most fly visits were not associated with effective pollination, resulting in improved fruit set or weight. This asymmetry in plant–pollinator interactions is particularly evident for dipterans. Indeed, few studies have confirmed the role of flies as effective pollinators of outdoor crops, particularly of Vaccinium flowers (blueberries and raspberries) [36, 57, 58, 61].

Non-Apidae bee diversity in Chilean cranberry orchards was comparatively low relative to that reported in North American studies [29]. In the native range, cranberry pollinator composition is well characterized and typically dominated by sonicating bees [30–32, 62]. The presence of relatively few bee pollinator species in Chilean crops compared to northern cultivation systems implies reduced functional redundancy and potentially greater vulnerability of pollination services [63, 64]. Conversely, plant communities associated with low-diversity pollinator assemblages often experience reduced pollination efficiency and crop yields [65] and become more sensitive to environmental disturbances [66]. In contrast, diverse pollinator communities enhance functional redundancy and resilience, ensuring more stable pollination services across spatial and temporal environmental variability [65, 67]. Although native sonicating halictid bees occurred at frequencies comparable to those of honey bees, they were not detected in pollination efficiency experiments, preventing a clear assessment of their role as pollinators of economically important crops [68]. Nonetheless, their presence in cranberry crops suggests a potential, yet underexplored, contribution to pollination services. Further research should evaluate their pollination effectiveness under field conditions and identify management strategies that could promote their persistence and functional contribution within cranberry agroecosystems.

As a result of low bee diversity, growers often rely on supplemental pollination with managed bees, particularly honey bees, to compensate for limited wild pollinator diversity and stabilize crop yields. All orchards studied supplemented pollination with managed bees at a density of two colonies per hectare. However, the role of honey bees in pollinating cranberries and other so-called “buzz-pollinated crops” remains controversial [19]. They are unable to perform floral sonication or vibration, processes that release pollen more efficiently from buzz-pollinated plants [69]. This limits their ability to promote pollen release and deposition on stigmas [70]. However, our pollination performance tests revealed that the honey bee was the only flower visitor to significantly increase cranberry fruit set and quality, evidencing its unexpected effectiveness as a pollinator and promoter of fruit yield. This study showed that pollination services provided by honey bees can increase cranberry fruit production by up to 2.85 times in relation to unvisited flowers. The effect is even more pronounced for fruit weight and seed production, which increase by up to four times the fruit mass and seven times the number of viable seeds, respectively, compared to self-pollinated flowers. A strong positive correlation between fruit weight and the number of developed seeds was observed, indicating that seed set is a key determinant of fruit growth in cranberries. The cultivar Pilgrim studied contains an average of 25.1 ovules per flower (maximum potential seed set). However, fruits produced after a single visit by honey bees contained an average of 1.80 viable seeds per. Assuming a relationship among pollen deposition, ovule fertilization, and seed development, this suggests that achieving maximum seed set would require 13–15 visits from honey bees per flower.

Nevertheless, cranberry fruits are relatively small and require only a limited number of pollen tetrads to achieve successful fertilization [23, 29]. Previous studies on the cranberry cultivar ‘Stevens’ indicate that approximately 5–8 pollen tetrads are required to achieve fruit set, representing the minimum pollen load necessary to initiate fruit development [23]. Berry mass increases only marginally beyond eight tetrads, whereas seed set continues to increase with incremental stigmatic pollen loads of up to 16 pollen tetrads [23]. Importantly, stigmatic pollen loads exceeding 16 tetrads did not result in further increases in fruit set or seed production. The stabilization of fruit set and seed production indicates a threshold beyond which additional pollen deposition does not translate into enhanced reproductive output. None of the single-visit experiments achieved maximum seed set, and berry weight was substantially lower than that reported for commercial orchards in the Northern Hemisphere [32, 71, 72]. Although honey bees were the only effective pollinators, the pollination ecosystem service they provided was insufficient to maximize reproductive output and expected fruit quality. Consequently, honey bee pollination alone may not meet the reproductive requirements of cranberry crops under Chilean production conditions, highlighting the potential need for improved pollinator management or complementary pollination strategies to enhance yield and fruit quality [73]. The putative most effective pollinators of cranberries (i.e., sonicating bees) are scarce or insufficient for the massive flowering of cranberries [74, 75]. Other bee visitors that have not been tested for efficiency but are capable of sonicating could be equally effective, or even more effective, if their functional characteristics align with floral morphology [76]. Because we were unable to directly assess the effectiveness of sonicating bees, future research should prioritize identifying strategies to improve their presence in this context and evaluating the contribution of native bee species capable of buzz pollination to crop pollination efficiency. These pollinators may be particularly important for crops with specialized floral traits, such as cranberry, in which pollen release is enhanced by vibration [77]. While our finding underscores the importance of managed honey bees in modern agriculture [78], it also suggests that pollinator management does not account for the functional traits typically associated with specialized flowers, such as cranberry flowers, when selecting pollinators to supplement services [2, 39, 70].

Our findings highlighted the importance of interpreting pollination dynamics within the context of specific ecological and floral traits of the crop system (e.g., hercogamy and dichogamy [79]. Notably, while no effect on fruit set was detected in relation to the temporal separation of staminate and pistillate phases, a clear effect on fruit weight and seed set was observed on visits to pistillate phases. Thus, the optimal cranberry crop yields depend not only on the identity of floral visitors but also on floral phenology, as both factors influence the likelihood of achieving optimal fruit yield and quality. Specialized floral traits such as dichogamy and hermaphroditism play an important role in reducing the probability of self-pollination in cranberries [22]. We found that visits during the pistillate phase of cranberry flowers significantly increased fruit yield, resulting in heavier fruits with more seeds. Although reproductive outcomes may vary between phases due to differences in function, both phases depend on synchronised interactions among floral receptivity, resource availability, and the consistent presence of floral visitors to ensure reproductive success [80, 81]. During the staminate phase, pollinator visits primarily facilitate pollen removal, while in the pistillate phase, ensuring the deposition of conspecific pollen is essential for fertilization [22, 82]. In this sense, the complementary availability of both nectar and residual pollen appears to confer an ecological advantage on cranberry dichogamous flowers, allowing pollinators to continue visiting flowers even after the primary pollen supply has been depleted [83, 84]. The continuous supply of nectar, especially during the pistillate phase, prolongs floral attractiveness and increases the likelihood of effective pollen transfer while the stigmas are receptive, thus enhancing overall reproductive success [85, 86].

Nevertheless, large gaps remain in our knowledge of native pollinator species and their interactions with cranberry flowers, emphasizing the need for further research. Understanding the interaction between floral traits and pollinator activity patterns can inform other strategic decisions in field operations, aiming to improve the management of pollination in cranberry crops. These traits can directly affect pollen transfer efficiency and, consequently, fruit quality parameters [87]. The functional traits of insects, such as sonication behaviour [70, 88], foraging patterns [89], and body size [90, 91], differ markedly among taxa of floral visitors. Accordingly, measuring flower visitors’ foraging time and observing their behavior (e.g., nectar or pollen collection) can provide valuable insights into pollinator effectiveness and help clarify which behaviors contribute most to plant reproductive success. These traits are the most likely to influence cranberry-pollinator interactions and pollination effectiveness. Despite the recognized importance of buzz pollination for crops, such as cranberry [19], due to sample limitations in our study, we were unable to directly assess the effectiveness of sonicating bees. Thus, future research should prioritize evaluating the role of multiple insect functional traits, especially those of bees capable of buzz pollination, in crop pollination efficiency.

Conclusions

In summary, our results confirm the importance of animal-mediated pollination in enhancing cranberry fruit production and improving fruit quality compared to self-pollination. Although several native insect groups, especially dipterans, were frequent floral visitors, their contribution to fruit production was negligible. Honey bees, the most frequent floral visitors, significantly increased fruit set compared with unvisited control flowers. Notably, the positive effect of honey bee visits on the fruit set is also reflected in fruit weight, which is indicative of fruit quality. Flowers visited by insects during the pistillate phase produced significantly heavier fruits and more viable seeds than unvisited flowers. This result is particularly relevant because self-pollination in cranberry rarely leads to successful fruit production, making insect-mediated pollen transfer essential for reproductive success. Visitation patterns differed among visitor groups and varied over time, indicating temporal structuring of pollinator activity. Alterations in the temporal patterns of pollinator activity, caused by environmental fluctuations, changes in pollinator abundance, or shifts in flowering phenology, could reduce pollination efficiency and consequently, fruit yield. In the Chilean context, where information on the contribution of native pollinators to crop production remains limited [92] and plant-pollinator interactions are diverse [93], future research should focus on identifying key functional pollinators and developing regional pollinator guides. Such efforts will be essential for improving pollination management, enhancing fruit production, and promoting biodiversity conservation across Chile’s diverse agricultural landscapes.
