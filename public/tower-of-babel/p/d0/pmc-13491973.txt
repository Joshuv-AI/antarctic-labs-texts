Isolated pulmonary Cryptococcus neoformans infection presenting with severe pulmonary involvement: a case report
Authors: Boon Hau Ng, Hsueh Jing Low, Nik Nuratiqah Nik Abeed, Nor Safiqah Sharil, Cheong Xiong Khee, Rose Azzlinda Osman, Andrea Yu-Lin Ban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary cryptococcosis is an uncommon fungal infection typically seen in immunocompromised hosts. In individuals with only mild immunosuppression, the disease is rare, often mimicking bacterial pneumonia, leading to diagnostic delays and inappropriate treatment.Case presentationWe report a 50-year-old Chinese man with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus and myasthenia gravis on low-dose corticosteroids who presented with fever, cough, and hypoxemic respiratory failure. Initial imaging suggested severe community-acquired pneumonia, and broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered, but there was no clinical response. Chest CT revealed bilateral patchy consolidations, and bronchoalveolar lavage cultures confirmed Cryptococcus neoformans. Lumbar puncture excluded central nervous system involvement. The patient was initially treated with amphotericin B, but therapy was complicated by acute kidney injury, requiring a switch to flucytosine and fluconazole. Despite this, he demonstrated marked clinical and radiological improvement, with near-complete resolution at 6 months.ConclusionPulmonary cryptococcosis can masquerade as community-acquired pneumonia, delaying appropriate management. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with prolonged corticosteroid use, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, and poor response to antibiotics. Early recognition and timely antifungal therapy are vital to improving outcomes.

Introduction

Pulmonary cryptococcosis, caused primarily by Cryptococcus neoformans, is an uncommon fungal infection that predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals. While the infection may remain confined to the lungs, it can disseminate in patients with impaired cell-mediated immunity, such as those with HIV/AIDS or on immunosuppressive therapy. In patients with only mild or subtle immunosuppression, pulmonary cryptococcosis is rare and often presents with non-specific clinical and radiological features, which can complicate and delay diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old Chinese man presented with a 2-week history of productive cough and intermittent low-grade fever. He was fatigued and had been unable to work and remained at home without prior antibiotic therapy. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, diagnosed 5 years earlier, and myasthenia gravis, for which he was taking pyridostigmine 60 mg daily and oral prednisolone 20 mg daily. He had no prior vaccination history. He worked as a bus driver, did not smoke or consume alcohol, and lived in a high-rise apartment in the city.

On presentation, he was alert and oriented. He was febrile (39 °C), tachycardic (heart rate of 110 beats per minute), and tachypneic (respiratory rate of 26/minutes) with a blood pressure of 128/78 mmHg, and oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. There was no cyanosis or pallor. Chest examination revealed bilateral crepitations over the lower lung zones with symmetrical chest expansion and no evidence of effusion on percussion. Cardiovascular examination was unremarkable. There were no skin lesions, lymphadenopathy, or neurological deficits. Arterial blood gas analysis showed hypoxemia (pH 7.38, PaO2 56.5 mmHg, PaCO2 36 mmHg, HCO3− 26 mmol/L). Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (WBC 14 × 109/L), mild anemia (Hb 11 g/dL), thrombocytosis (platelets 560 × 109/L), and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP 56 mg/L) and poor glycemic control (HbA1c 9.5%). The renal profile was within normal limits (urea 6.2 mmol/L, sodium 140 mmol/L, potassium 3.9 mmol/L, creatinine 68 µmol/L). A chest radiograph showed bilateral reticulonodular opacities (Fig. 1A). Given the clinical and radiological findings, a working diagnosis of severe community-acquired pneumonia was made. The patient was initiated on intravenous co-amoxiclav and azithromycin. After three days of empiric antibiotic therapy, the patient showed no clinical improvement and remained dependent on supplemental oxygen, prompting escalation to intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam. Sputum culture, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear, MTB GeneXpert, and bacterial cultures were all negative. To further evaluate the non-specific chest radiograph findings and assess the extent of lung involvement, a contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) of the thorax was performed, revealing bilateral patchy consolidation in a peribronchiolar and subpleural distribution (Fig. 1B and C). Flexible bronchoscopy was performed to directly visualize the airways, which were diffusely inflamed with thick mucus, and bronchoalveolar lavage samples were sent for microbiological analysis to identify pathogens not detected by routine sputum testing.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were sent for microbiological analysis, including fungal culture, respiratory pathogen PCR, and MTB GeneXpert. BAL fungal culture subsequently grew Cryptococcus neoformans, while GeneXpert and culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative. After establishing the diagnosis, a lumbar puncture was performed; India ink staining and cryptococcal antigen testing of the cerebrospinal fluid were both negative, excluding CNS dissemination.

The patient was initiated on intravenous amphotericin B but developed acute kidney injury after one week. The planned treatment regimen included oral flucytosine (25 mg/kg QID) for one month, followed by fluconazole 400 mg twice daily for six weeks, and then 200 mg once daily as maintenance for one year. During his two-month hospital stay, he showed progressive clinical improvement, including successful weaning from supplemental oxygen. A repeat chest radiograph performed just prior to discharge demonstrated interval improvement of the consolidation.

Screening for HIV was negative. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, and a repeat thoracic CECT scan demonstrated near-complete resolution of the patchy consolidation (Fig. 1D and E).

Discussion

Pulmonary cryptococcosis is a rare fungal infection, most commonly caused by Cryptococcus neoformans and, less frequently, C. gattii. While it typically occurs in profoundly immunocompromised individuals, there is increasing recognition of cases in patients with only subtle or partial immunosuppression [1, 2]. In this patient, although HIV negative, the combination of diabetes mellitus and chronic corticosteroid therapy for myasthenia gravis likely impaired immune defenses, predisposing him to cryptococcal infection [3, 4].

Chest imaging is essential for the initial detection of pulmonary cryptococcosis, but its findings are often non-specific and can mimic other pulmonary diseases. Reported CT patterns include clustered nodules, solitary nodules with or without cavitation, peribronchovascular consolidation, miliary nodules, and crazy-paving appearances [5–8]. In immunocompetent hosts, bilateral pulmonary nodules with or without cavitation are most commonly observed [5–8]. In this patient, CT thorax revealed diffuse patchy consolidation, a pattern more typical of bacterial or mycobacterial infections, which contributed to the diagnostic challenge.

The non-specific clinical presentation and overlapping radiological features make pulmonary cryptococcosis difficult to differentiate from bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, malignancy, or other fungal infections. Sputum cultures are often unreliable due to contamination or low sensitivity [9]. While India ink staining remains a traditional method for diagnosing cryptococcal meningitis, its sensitivity is limited in pulmonary disease, particularly in immunocompetent hosts [9].

The gold standard for diagnosing pulmonary cryptococcosis remains microbiological or histopathological confirmation via bronchoscopy with BAL or lung biopsy. BAL cultures offer high sensitivity and specificity, allowing differentiation from other infectious and non-infectious mimics. In this case, a definitive diagnosis was established through BAL culture, which grew C. neoformans while excluding Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Treatment of severe pulmonary cryptococcosis is guided by disease severity and the patient’s immune status. Current IDSA and CDC guidelines recommend induction therapy with intravenous amphotericin B combined with oral flucytosine (25 mg/kg QID) for two weeks, followed by consolidation with fluconazole 400–800 mg daily for eight weeks and maintenance therapy with fluconazole 200 mg daily for six to twelve months [10]. In this patient, intravenous amphotericin B was initiated but discontinued after one week due to acute kidney injury. Flucytosine was continued for one month as part of the planned induction therapy, followed by consolidation with fluconazole 400 mg twice daily and planned maintenance at 200 mg daily for 1 year.

Despite modification of the regimen, the patient showed progressive clinical improvement, including successful weaning from supplemental oxygen, and a repeat chest radiograph prior to discharge demonstrated interval improvement of the reticulonodular opacities. This case highlights that treatment may need to be individualized based on organ function and patient tolerance while still achieving favorable outcomes in severe pulmonary cryptococcosis. In this patient, antifungal therapy led to significant clinical and radiological improvement, with interval resolution observed on follow-up imaging.

Conclusion

This case highlights that, although uncommon, pulmonary cryptococcosis should be included in the differential diagnosis of non-resolving pneumonia, particularly in patients with prolonged corticosteroid use and poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.
