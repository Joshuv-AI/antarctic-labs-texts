Mortality outcomes between pregnant women booked for antenatal care and unbooked pregnant women
Authors: Abdulrahim Rouzi, Nora Sahly, Abdullah Mohammed Kafy, Rana A Alamoudi, Renad Mazen Abualsaud, Wejdan A Alsheri, Wasayf M Almehmadi, Shahad T Khayyat, Razan M Altumaihi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Perinatal fetal mortality and maternal mortality remains relatively high in Saudi Arabia. Antenatal care aims to improve outcomes; however, evidence to demonstrate its impact on outcomes in Saudi Arabia is lacking.OBJECTIVES: Investigate whether booking status for antenatal care impacted outcomes for pregnant women and identify outcomes that predicted booking status.DESIGN: Retrospective observational cohort study.SETTINGS: Tertiary care centerPATIENTS AND METHODS: All Saudi women admitted to the labor or delivery units between January 2011 and December 2019 were included. Outcomes were compared between booked and unbooked women, and logistic regression was used to identify outcomes that predicted booking status, adjusted for age.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Booked/unbooked status and perinatal mortality of Saudi women.SAMPLE SIZE: 10 781 women; 9546 (88.5%) booked, (11.1%) 1192 unbooked.RESULTS: Unbooked mothers had higher incidences of meconium-stained liquor (P=.040), ruptured uterus (P=.017), and blood loss >1000 mL during cesarean deliveries (P=.003), but a lower episiotomy rate (P<.001). Perinatal fetal mortality and maternal mortality were equivalent between the two groups. Episiotomy, higher birth weight, higher mother age, perinatal death, delivery mode, onset of labor, and use of analgesics were all independent predictors of the mother being booked when adjusted for all outcomes and age.CONCLUSIONS: The rate of perinatal and maternal mortality in this cohort was relatively low and equivalent between booked and un-booked mothers, contrary to our expectations. More analysis of the socioeconomic data may explain this striking result.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective chart-review with incomplete data retrieval that affected the completeness of data retrieved. The results of the multivariate analysis cannot be used to infer causality because the study is observational.

INTRODUCTION

The maternal mortality ratio in Saudi Arabia remains relatively high at approximately 17 per 100 000 live births.1 Higher risk groups in this country have been identified as older multiparous women, those with low income, and those with no education.2 This public health concern requires further investigation to identify potential solutions to improve outcomes. One such solution that is widely employed is booking pregnant women for antenatal observation and car. Prenatal observation and care aim to identify higher-risk women, identify deviations from normal pregnancy physiology early, and ensure adequate precautions are in place for birth.3 The overall aim is to prevent potentially avoidable complications of pregnancy and birth.4 It has been demonstrated that prenatal observation and care has favorable outcomes for both mother and fetus regardless of the presence of factors such as increasing age, parity, and comorbidities that make the pregnancy ‘higher-risk’.5 The association between being unbooked during pregnancy and poorer maternal and fetal outcomes has been demonstrated in several countries;6–8 however, most studies are from a decade ago. In the current study, we aimed to identify the impact of booking status on perinatal and maternal mortality which has not been studied recently. Secondary end points lookedat outcomes that predicted booking status.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The study period was from January 2011 to December 2019. The study was approved by King Abdulaziz University Hospital Institutional Review Board (Number 536–21) and performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations in Saudi Arabia and the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of the type of the study, patient consent to review their medical records was not required. All Saudi women admitted to the labor or delivery units between January 2011 and December 2019 were included. Patients were identified by screening electronic health records retrospectively. The following baseline data were collected for each patient: booked/unbooked status, age at presentation, parity, gravid status, mothers’ weight, and number of previous abortions. Outcomes included gestational age, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes,9 perinatal death, spontaneous/induced onset of labor, delivery mode (spontaneous vaginal, emergency/elective cesarean delivery, use of ventouse), use of analgesia, type of analgesic, total dosage of analgesic, oxytocin augmentation, uterine tachysystole, uterine hyperstimulation, meconium-stained liquor, non-reassuring fetal heart pattern, use of magnesium sulphate, birth weight categories, offspring gender, maternal complications (shivering, diarrhea, ruptured uterus, death, hepatitis B, human immunodeficiency virus, blood loss, and episiotomy).

All baseline and outcome data were collected by scrutinizing medical notes and electronic health records. Patients were not contacted to collect data. Unfortunately, the percentage of patients who had incomplete data and specifically lack of socioeconomic data were not determined because of the study design. Baseline data including booked/unbooked status is presented as total counts and percentages (total in the cohort as the denominator). Continuous baseline and outcome data were categorized, then the cohort was split into two groups: booked mothers and unbooked mothers. The two groups were compared in terms of baseline and outcome data by using the chi-squared test. Missing data were handled by imputation (most prevalent value method). Multivariate regression analyses (using the imputed data) were conducted to quantify the adjusted effect of baseline characteristics and outcomes on booking status. Binary logistic regression with backward elimination method was utilized. Further regression analysis using case-wise deletion was performed as a sensitivity analysis to determine the validity of the imputation method. Significance was set as P<.05. All analyses were conducted using IBM's SPSS statistical software (version 23.0).10

RESULTS

In total, 10 781 women were identified as meeting the inclusion criteria. The number of women who were booked was 9546 (88.5%), the number of unbooked women was 1192 (11.1%) (Table 1). Booking status was missing for 43 (0.4%). There was no significant difference between the groups in terms of age categories (P=.132). There was a significantly higher proportion of primigravid women in the booked group compared to the unbooked group and significantly fewer women who had parity of 1–4 or ≥5 in the booked group compared to the unbooked group (P=.006). There were no significant differences between the booked and un-booked groups in terms of mother's weight (P=.160), gravidity (P=.507), and number of previous abortions (P=.813).

Analgesic intake was significantly higher in the booked group (P<.001); however the total dose of analgesic was equivalent between the groups. The unbooked group had a higher incidence of meconium-stained liquor compared to the booked group (P=.040); there was also a higher rate of ruptured uterus for un-booked mothers (P=.017); a higher rate of blood loss >1,000 mL in cesarean sections for unbooked mothers (P=.003), but a lower rate of episiotomy in unbooked mothers (P<.001). Oxytocin augmentation, uterine tachysystole, uterine hyperstimulation, use of nitroglycerine, maternal diarrhea, maternal death, incidence of hepatitis B, incidence of human immunodeficiency virus, and blood loss in spontaneous vaginal delivery were equivalent between the two groups.

DISCUSSION

In this cohort of 10 781 mothers there were 139 (1.29%) perinatal deaths and 1 (0.009%) maternal death. The majority of mothers were booked, and at baseline, differed significantly only in terms of parity in comparison to their unbooked counterparts. Some outcomes differed between the two groups that were expected, such as frequency of full-term births, more spontaneous vaginal deliveries in the unbooked group versus induced labor, higher birth weight in the booked group, and less blood loss in the booked group undergoing cesarean delivery (owing to a higher frequency of emergency cesarean deliveries).

Perinatal fetal mortality and maternal mortality rates were equivalent between the two groups, which is in contrast to several studies in the literature.5–8,11

Potential reasons for this finding are differences in the populations studied (which has been demonstrated in other parts of the world),12 advances in emergency care/deliveries for unbooked mothers in comparison to studies reported several years ago,13 and confounding baseline factors. Furthermore, the lack of significant difference is probably due to the fact that unbooked women have been followed up at other institutions and elected to have the delivery at our institution for mainly financial reasons to avoid paying out of pocket for the costs of delivery. This indicates that unbooked pregnant women had a financial disadvantage and came from less affluent families. Unfortunately, we did not retrieve data on the socioeconomic status of such women. Similarly, the rates of hepatitis B and human immunodeficiency virus rates were equivalent between the two groups in this study. This may be a reflection of the low overall incidence in the study area in comparison to many studies reporting on mothers in other areas of the African continent where overall rates are higher and as such are more likely to be adequately powered to detect such differences.14,15 Additionally, healthcare efforts to educate populations in preventative strategies over the last few decades is likely to have had an impact regardless of a mother's booking status.16,17

Independent predictors of booked status in this study included increasing age, and several outcomes (episiotomy, birth weight, perinatal death, delivery mode, onset of labor, and the use of analgesics). This is in contrast to several studies that suggest that being booked is associated with improved outcomes.5–7,11 There are several potential reasons for this unexpected finding: differences in the prenatal observation and care that booked mothers receive,18,19 emergency care for unbooked mothers could differ significantly in this hospital in comparison to areas where a similar cohort has been studied. Finally, our multivariate analysis accounted for confounding from other outcomes, but did not account for confounding from several potentially relevant baseline characteristics, which could heavily influence these results.

There are several strengths to this study, the number of included mothers is large, spanning nine years of recent practice. The majority of variables had infrequent missing data, and our sensitivity analysis using case-wise deletion produced a similar final model with similar odds ratios and confidence intervals when compared to the analysis on imputed data, suggesting the imputation method was robust. However, there are several limitations to acknowledge. First, the retrospective nature of the study leading to potentially less complete and reliable data compared to a prospective one. Despite including a large cohort, the data all originates from one hospital which limits the generalizability of the results. The overall frequency of fetal (n=139) and maternal mortality n=1) were low, but a larger cohort with more power could potentially detect differences that this study did not. Due to the observational nature of the study, causality cannot be inferred from the multivariate analyses results. Finally, we cannot make conclusions about the influence of booking status on outcomes, adjusted for other relevant baseline variables, based on the analyses described.

In conclusion, the rate of perinatal and maternal mortality in this cohort was relatively low and equivalent between booked and unbooked mothers, contrary to our expectations. More analysis of the socioeconomic data may explain this striking result.
