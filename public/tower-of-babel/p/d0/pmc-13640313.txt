‘It’s not just about food’: Living with celiac disease as a woman
Authors: Narges Naderi, Bardia Amidi, Javad Yoosefi Lebni, Saleh Azadbakht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCeliac disease (CeD) is a chronic autoimmune condition requiring lifelong adherence to a gluten-free diet. This disease is associated with a considerable psychosocial burden, particularly among women. Despite the relatively high prevalence of CeD in Iran, there is limited data on the lived experiences of Iranian women living with the condition.ObjectiveTo explore the lived experiences and challenges of women living with CeD in Lorestan Province, Iran.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological study was conducted in 2025. Twenty women with a confirmed diagnosis of CeD participated in semi-structured face-to-face or telephone interviews. The data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method with the support of MAXQDA software. Trustworthiness was ensured employing Guba and Lincoln’s criteria.ResultsParticipants described living with CeD as a multidimensional burden that extended far beyond food restrictions. They experienced notable psychological distress, including sadness, anxiety, and helplessness, mainly due to living with a long-term condition that is often misunderstood by others. Limited awareness among family members and friends often led to tension and strain in relationships. In addition, participants reported experiencing stigma and mockery, and many felt they had to keep their illness secret, which led to feeling socially isolated. The participants also reported considerable challenges in maintaining a strict gluten-free diet. The high cost, limited access, and poor quality of gluten-free products contributed to financial strain and, in some cases, dependence on others. Furthermore, a delayed diagnosis and inconsistent medical advice added to participants’ uncertainty and reduced their trust in healthcare providers. These experiences affected their daily routines, family responsibilities, and social life. Thus, CeD affected nearly every part of women’s personal, social, and family life.ConclusionCeD has a major impact on women’s mental health, social life, finances, and family relationships. To address these issues, care should be more patient-centered and involve collaboration among different healthcare professionals. Improved training for healthcare providers and stronger psychological and social support can help patients manage the condition. Policy-level actions are also required to increase the availability and affordability of gluten-free products.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CeD) is an autoimmune disorder in which the consumption of gluten triggers an immune response that damages the small intestine, leading to both gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms [1]. CeD affects around 1% of the global general population [2], whereas its prevalence in Iran is about 2% [3]. CeD occurs more frequently in women with a female-to-male ratio of about 2–2.5 to 1 [4].

CeD is a chronic disease with widespread psychological, social, and emotional consequences [5]. Its management requires strict and lifelong adherence to a gluten-free diet (GFD) and regular medical monitoring [6]. In many cases, the involvement of family members in adjusting diet and lifestyle is also needed [7]. These demands may affect daily functioning and quality of life among this group [8]. A study by Crocker et al. in the United Kingdom reported that women with CeD had poorer health-related quality of life scores than men with CeD [9]. Research also shows that female CeD patients report greater social limitations and worries with regard to their disease and adherence to a GFD [10].

Although the psychological and social effects of CeD have been widely documented, women may experience the disease differently due to the responsibilities they often carry within their families and daily lives. In many societies, women are primarily responsible for preparing meals, providing family care, and managing the household [11–13]. Therefore, adhering to a strict GFD may influence not only disease management but also interactions with family members and involvement in social life [5]. Understanding women’s experiences within this social context helps clarify how family roles and societal expectations influence the way they live with CeD.

Given these gendered and contextual dimensions of illness experience, a deeper understanding of women’s lived experiences with CeD is needed, particularly in Iran, where the disease is relatively more prevalent and its management may impose significant psychosocial and economic burdens. Qualitative research enables an in-depth exploration of individuals’ lived experiences, perceptions, and challenges [14, 15]. Thus, the present study aimed to explore the challenges faced by women with CeD in Lorestan Province using a qualitative phenomenological approach.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in Lorestan Province in 2025 with the goal of in-depth exploration of living experiences of women with CeD. A phenomenological design was employed. This decision was made as this design allows for a more thorough investigation of participants’ experiences, their perceptions, and the meanings they attribute to their illness [16].

Participants

Twenty women over 18 years of age from Lorestan province were included in the study. Of these, 11 were interviewed face-to-face and 9 by telephone. All participants had a medically confirmed diagnosis of CeD for more than 6 months and were able to communicate and participate in the interview. Participants were excluded from the study if they were unwilling to have their interview recorded or discontinued their participation before the completion of the interview.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews using purposive and snowball sampling. A list of patients was initially prepared from the records of the Gastroenterology Clinic of Shahid Rahimi Hospital in Khorramabad. Interviews were conducted either face-to-face or by telephone, depending on participants’ availability and scheduling constraints. No obvious differences were observed in the depth or richness of participants’ responses across interview modalities. Additional women with CeD were subsequently identified and recruited through recommendations from the initial participants.

The interview location was chosen by the participants. The interviews were conducted only in the presence of the participant and the interviewer. Given that the study participants were female, the interviews were conducted by a female medical student (N.N.) who was skilled in qualitative research. The interviewer introduced herself and explained the objectives and procedures of the study before starting each interview. The interviewer also assured the participants that their data would remain confidential. The average duration of the interviews was 45 min, with the longest lasting 70 min and the shortest 20 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. In addition, the interviewer documented nonverbal cues, including tone, emotions, and pauses during the interviews.

The interview questions were designed based on a literature review and the research team consensus (Table 1). Following the collection of demographic information, main and supplementary questions were asked to comprehensively explore participants’ experiences and perspectives. As interviews progressed, the research team continuously reviewed the content of each interview to assess the emergence of new meanings and experiential patterns. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved. This was considered to have occurred when no new themes or meaningful insights emerged from the data [17, 18]. Following the 18th interview, no additional themes were identified. To verify that saturation had been achieved, two further interviews were completed, and agreement on data saturation was confirmed after the 20th interview.

Data analysis

The data were organized with MAXQDA 2018 software and analyzed following Colaizzi’s seven-step method [19]. The analysis began with verbatim transcription. Each interview was fully transcribed into Microsoft Word documents. N.N. and B.A. then read transcripts repeatedly to gain both a general and in-depth understanding of the content. Subsequently, they conducted initial line-by-line coding, focusing on significant statements and meaningful units that reflected participants’ experiences. Following initial coding, J.Y.L., N.N., and B.A. compared the codes to identify similarities and differences. Similar or related codes were organized into broader categories and subcategories. These categories were then further developed into main themes representing the primary features of participants’ experiences. The analysis involved repeatedly reviewing the transcripts, codes, and themes to ensure that the findings remained grounded in the data. To enhance rigor, interpretations were reviewed collaboratively within the research team until consensus was reached. Finally, the obtained results were presented as a comprehensive description of the studied phenomenon. Relevant participant characteristics, such as care-related responsibilities and family context, were included alongside quotations to provide contextual understanding of participants’ experiences. Trustworthiness

The present study followed Guba and Lincoln’s criteria to ensure trustworthiness and rigor [20]. Credibility was strengthened through the recruitment of a diverse sample. Participants were selected from various socio-economic and demographic backgrounds. This was intended to capture a variety of experiences. Prolonged engagement with participants, as well as the use of subsequent questions, increased the data’s depth and accuracy. Member checking was used to verify the findings by sharing the researchers’ interpretations and coded summaries with five participants. Four independent qualitative experts reviewed the findings to enhance confirmability and reduce bias. Based on their feedback, revisions were made to ensure that the results captured only the participants’ experiences, not the personal views of the researcher. Dependability was ensured through the documentation of all research processes. In addition, all members of the research team participated in the coding and analysis process. The final categories were reviewed and reached by consensus. Finally, transferability was improved by clearly describing the study context, participant characteristics, and data collection procedures. Participant quotes were provided to support the applicability of the findings to other contexts. Moreover, four eligible women with CeD, who were not interviewed, were provided excerpts from the interviews. They confirmed that the participants’ perspectives reflected their own experiences.

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the Lorestan University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.LUMS.REC.1404.263). All ethical guidelines were strictly followed. In face-to-face interviews, written informed consent was obtained from the patients, while in telephone interviews, verbal consent was obtained. Participants were informed about the study’s goal and procedures. They were told that their information would be kept confidential. They were also given the option to withdraw at any point. Audio recordings were deleted after data analysis.

Psychological–emotional challenges

This category consists of the subcategories of depressive symptoms and helplessness, fear and anxiety, and reproductive and parenting-related concerns. These subcategories represent the emotional and psychological challenges faced by patients with CeD.

Depressive symptoms and helplessness

The participants often experienced deep psychological challenges after receiving the diagnosis. Most initial experiences included feelings of discouragement and depression, with predominant emotions of sadness. In contrast to a transient reaction, these emotional responses intensified over time, affecting mood and overall quality of life. In addition, participants reported severe fatigue and helplessness, which significantly limited the necessary motivation to perform daily activities and maintain usual functions.I felt so bad; it was like I had cancer. Our spirits were down. All of us, our whole family. We cried day and night. We looked at it differently. I thought, for example, that I was on the verge of cancer. (Participant 2, married, university student)

Furthermore, several participants described severe irritability and a quick temper. These findings show that the illness affected not only emotional well-being but also interpersonal interactions and social relationships.I have become very impatient. I get nervous all the time because of this illness. My temperament has, in my opinion, become very bad; I have become irritable. Fear and anxiety

Several participants expressed a general feeling of fear and uncertainty. They experienced an intense anxiety about the illness being perceived as a dangerous condition. In particular, there was a specific concern about the perceived similarity to cancer, which exacerbated their constant anxiety.When I heard the diagnosis, I had a terrible feeling. As if I had cancer. I looked at it in a different way. I thought that I was going to die. (Participant 11, unmarried, financially dependent on brother)

Participants often described a constant anxiety about disease progression and the possibility of comorbidities or secondary complications. Many of them expressed their distress about the chronic and lifelong nature of the disease and fear of its perceived incurability and uncertain long-term outcomes. Social challenges

This category consists of social stigma, scrutiny, and misunderstanding, social withdrawal and isolation, and interpersonal and family challenges. These subcategories highlight the social challenges that participants faced and also detail how misunderstanding, judgment, and stigma lead to emotional distress and feelings of alienation.

Social stigma, scrutiny, and misunderstanding

Participants frequently complained about challenges caused by a lack of understanding of the disease among their social circle. Many mentioned that their relatives’ limited understanding or oversimplification of the illness led to its seriousness being downplayed. Discussion

This research shows that CeD among women is not only an experience of chronic gastrointestinal illness. Instead, it should be understood as a highly gendered and socially embedded condition. As opposed to being limited to dietary restrictions or biomedical treatment, the disease reshapes women’s emotional lives, social identities, family roles, and everyday activities. Women living with CeD reported being continuously entangled with cultural expectations of being a “proper” woman, social interactions around food sharing, and forms of economic and social dependency. The analysis of women’s narratives indicates that managing CeD was not confined to biomedical self-management. It also involved a continuous negotiation of its social meanings. A more complex interpretative reading of the findings points to the phenomenon of “socially amplified chronicity” (see Fig. 1), in which dietary restrictions are translated into emotional suffering, social isolation, and insecurity. The onset of the disease disrupted personal identity by producing feelings of inferiority, guilt, and imperfection while also exposing women to fears and uncertainties regarding fertility and motherhood, shaped by culturally rooted expectations of maternal roles and responsibility. At the same time, coping with the disease took place under material and organizational constraints, including lack of economic independence, inadequate healthcare guidance, and limited access to gluten-free products, which further restricted women’s agency.

The socio-cultural context of food and belonging in Lorestan

The lived experience of CeD for women in Lorestan involves an ethical economy of food, hospitality, and belonging that is rooted in culture. Consequently, food should not be viewed simply from the nutritional aspect since it is the central factor for assessing femininity and moral worth. Women exist within a social environment in which food preparation, sharing, and eating take on significant moral meaning. Dietary restrictions cannot be readily seen in clinical terms but more as social obligations that include compliance, appreciation, and participation in social activities. Within this context, any inability to eat food shared by others, takes on symbolic significance and illness becomes a question of physical needs versus morality. This cultural ideology is particularly noticeable in how food refusal is perceived. According to the participants’ narratives, it appears that when a woman refused food, she was questioned incessantly, laughed at, and her claims were always taken with skepticism and considered exaggerated. This indicates an unspoken notion that food presented during social and familial interactions should be eaten, as eating it implies recognition and acceptance. Thus, food refusal is not perceived as a neutral act. It is interpreted as a sign of nonconformity to the norms of femininity expected from women. Another important contextual aspect is that the disease is perceived from a perspective of morality, by relating it to concepts such as “sin,” causing more suffering and self-blame. The disease is placed within the context of the larger moral world in which misfortune is associated with individual or family moral character, thus causing more feelings of guilt, flaw, and inadequacy. Additionally, mockery, gossip, and repeated questioning act as informal mechanisms of social control, promoting conformity to socially accepted norms of participation in meals. These processes contribute to social withdrawal, concealment of illness, and progressive isolation.

Conceptual contribution to the literature

CeD has been described in prior qualitative research as a long-term disorder that involves dietary management along with significant psychosocial implications [5, 21]. The chronic nature of CeD implies the need for a lifelong commitment to follow a GFD. This itself introduces continuous practical and emotional demands into everyday life [22]. Research has shown that individuals with CeD maintain a heightened sense of awareness regarding their diets, as even slight doubts about the contents of food items, how the food is prepared, or possible cross-contamination with gluten may cause ongoing worry [23]. Apart from dietary challenges, patients with CeD often feel stressed psychologically after being diagnosed with the condition, especially in the early stages of adaptation [5, 8]. Another challenge caused by CeD lies in its impact on social life, since food-related activities constitute an integral part of socializing [24, 25]. Evidence synthesis points to CeD being not just experienced as an illness but as a lifelong process, which requires constant adjustments in terms of identity, social interaction, and emotions [5]. Specifically, existing literature has focused on anxiety about being exposed to gluten, difficulties in adhering to the diet, and feelings of loss from restrictions on food consumption [25, 26]. Moreover, people with CeD report that their day-to-day life gets restructured on grounds of food safety concerns [27, 28]. Ordinary activities such as buying groceries or eating out may become mentally exhausting due to the need to plan and consider potential risks beforehand [21, 29].

Though the above research offers a good base for understanding CeD impact on individuals, it mostly frames experiences from the perspective of personal adjustment, dietary compliance, emotional distress, and problems with social functioning. Stigma has been viewed through the prism of misunderstanding [5], while the economic strain caused by the illness has been framed as a structural impediment involving food access and affordability [21, 30]. In the same way, disruption of identity is usually considered a psychological challenge arising from diagnosis and subsequently stabilizing with time [5].

The study demonstrates that being on a CeD diet involves more than just food exclusion and individual coping. It is grounded in culturally defined interpretations of food practices, where food consumption takes place within social settings. In such situations, the eating practices of individuals with CeD often become subject to scrutiny and appraisal. This suggests that stigma is not merely based on ignorance but rather is a process in which the legitimacy of disease undergoes constant testing through daily interactions. Medical explanations do not always suffice in removing uncertainties, as social norms concerning communal eating also affect perceptions. This pattern of needing to explain oneself leads to a sense of social pressure and otherness, whereby individuals anticipate being questioned or misunderstood even before engaging in social situations. Such feelings could lead people to avoid social gatherings or participate less in food-related events.

Moreover, while earlier research focused primarily on financial burden in terms of affordability and access to gluten-free products, we highlight the interconnectedness between financial hardship and gender dynamics. The ability of women to comply with the necessary dietary restrictions is determined by both their financial situation and the gender dynamics that influence gluten-free food purchasing. Indeed, the necessity to postpone and even forego dietary purchases was mentioned by several of our study participants despite recognizing their importance. This was not solely because of cost, but also because their needs were not always prioritized within household decision-making. Such results indicate that the financial problems associated with CeD involve more than the cost of and access to gluten-free foods. They are also experienced in social contexts that affect one’s access to resources and capacity to take actions recommended for dietary treatment.

The research also contributes to the knowledge on identity disruption in CeD. Contrary to the notion that the individual experiences an internal adjustment process after diagnosis, the findings show that feelings of difference, inferiority, and embarrassment arise continually because of the interactional nature of such experiences. Everyday situations involving food become sites where difference is publicly produced and made visible. This suggests that identity is not just altered by diagnosis but is continuously shaped through social encounters. Our results also challenge the underlying premise of the existing research that increased awareness and adaptation alone will be able to relieve the psychological and social burden associated with CeD. Even in the case of individuals who had sufficient knowledge about their dietary needs, distressing experiences continued due to structural constraints in food availability, economic pressure, and culturally embedded expectations around food sharing and hospitality.

Lived contradictions in celiac disease experience

There is a set of ongoing tensions that shape how CeD is lived in everyday life. These tensions reflect not simple adaptation but continuous negotiation between competing demands. Women’s accounts suggest that dietary control, while being essential for coping with the condition, can cause sustained psychological exhaustion due to the constant need for vigilance across many social and practical contexts. The choice to disclose one’s health problem versus keeping it confidential can also have mixed consequences. Disclosure reduces the risk of harm but exposes individuals to judgment and stigmatization, while concealment may protect social dignity but limits support and increases vulnerability. Likewise, maintaining belonging and positive social relations involves walking a tightrope between protecting one’s health by rejecting offered food and preserving harmony in social interactions. In a similar manner, adhering to dietary requirements leads to a contradictory situation of feeling responsible and capable of self-regulation on one hand and being less spontaneous, active, and socially engaged on the other.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. We used a semi-structured interview guide and it allowed us to explore participants’ experiences and maintain consistency in interviews at the same time. Our participants were from various socio-economic backgrounds; hence, this contributed to the study by representing a wider variety of experiences. Investigator triangulation among several researchers enhanced credibility and reduced possible bias. The analysis was also reviewed by four independent qualitative experts to further enhance credibility. Another strength of this study is that it represents an understudied population in Iran, adding worthwhile insights that may help healthcare providers and policymakers better support Iranian women with CeD. However, our study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. Selection bias may have occurred, as most of our participants were recruited from one gastroenterology clinic, and this approach may have excluded women who do not access formal healthcare. Furthermore, all participants were from Lorestan Province. This limits geographic and cultural diversity within Iran, and the results may not be generalizable beyond this specific regional and cultural context. A number of participants were reluctant to take part due to concerns about secrecy surrounding the illness. This was resolved by explaining the study intentions, ensuring the confidentiality of interview data, and allowing participants to choose a convenient time and place for the interview. It is also possible that participants’ responses were influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias.

Significance for research, policy, and practice

This study’s findings provide an invaluable perspective with important implications. Our results indicate that future research needs to examine gender-specific experiences of living with CeD. This is particularly important because of the additional psychological burden, social stigma, and economic reliance of women living with CeD. More research is also necessary to explore specific challenges across different contexts and to develop programs that address the multidimensional needs facing women with CeD. We underscore the need for systemic change, including expanded insurance coverage for gluten-free products, subsidized gluten-free food programs, and training for healthcare providers to improve diagnosis of CeD and address psychosocial needs. In the Iranian healthcare system, however, gluten-free basic food products are currently not covered by insurance plans. Given the severe financial burden and food insecurity reported by participants, a national-level policy change is needed to recognize gluten-free staple foods as medically necessary items under basic health insurance packages. In this context, the introduction of a monthly electronic voucher program for patients with CeD may represent a feasible and practical policy option. There also need to be policy efforts addressing the variance in the geographic distribution of gluten-free products. The availability of gluten-free products in Iran is heavily skewed towards urban centers, while people in smaller cities and rural areas experience severe scarcity, as well as higher transportation costs. Hence, there is a clear need for decentralization policies to ensure that regional pharmacies, as well as government-supported food outlets, operate within provincial health networks. Evidence from this study shows that we need a patient-centered approach to care that goes beyond just managing diet. Health care providers should screen for psychological distress and provide comprehensive education at diagnosis. They should also establish and offer ongoing support. With regard to the Iranian clinical setting, our results have revealed an important knowledge deficit among healthcare providers at the time of diagnosis, which led to misunderstanding and increased patient anxiety. To address this issue, structured continuing medical education programs concerning CeD need to be designed for general practitioners, internists, and gastroenterologists within the Iranian Ministry of Health framework. These programs must focus on identifying atypical presentations, post-diagnostic counseling, and communication approaches that reduce patient anxiety. There is an opportunity for dietitians, psychologists, and social workers to form multidisciplinary care teams. We also need to create peer support networks and community resources to help lessen feelings of isolation and improve quality of life. This is vital to foster awareness of successful individuals living with CeD and helps challenge the stereotype that the condition limits one’s personal, educational, or professional achievement. Addressing these complex challenges requires coordinated efforts in research, policy, and practice. This coordination is key to improving outcomes and well-being for women living with CeD.

Conclusion

For women, CeD went much further than mere dietary management and affected several aspects of their lives. It had implications for their psychological well-being, leaving them feeling sad, anxious, and inadequate. This is often accompanied by feelings of guilt and fear related to fertility and motherhood. CeD also influenced their social relationships, as stigma and the need for secrecy led many to withdraw rather than seek support. There was also a financial dimension to these experiences. This arose both from the cost of gluten-free foods and from the marginalization of women’s dietary needs within the household. Delayed diagnosis and limited provider knowledge further contributed to these challenges. These difficulties reinforced each other, consistent with what we describe as socially amplified chronicity.

Supporting women with CeD requires significant changes. The healthcare system needs to shift away from the biomedical model and it must address the psychological and social dimensions of the disease. Policy actions should prioritize increasing gluten-free food access through increased insurance coverage and subsidized food programs. Geographic access equity should also be considered when planning gluten-free food options. To improve quality of care, provider education and systematic screening for psychological comorbidities should be incorporated into standards of care. Implementation of peer support groups and public education campaigns may help to decrease stigma and social isolation.
