Examining associations between social vulnerability and maternal morbidity among a multicentre cohort of pregnancies complicated by placenta accreta spectrum disorder in New York City
Authors: Nicola Francesco Tavella, Henri Mitchell Rosenberg, Alexandra Mills, Thomas Owens, Lois Brustman, Georgios Doulaveris, Itamar Futterman, Meghana Limaye, Teresa Janevic, Rebecca Hope Jessel, Angela Teresa Bianco
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlacenta accreta spectrum (PAS) disorder is a source of severe obstetric morbidity and mortality worldwide. The objective of this paper was to evaluate the potential relationship between social vulnerability and severe maternal morbidity in a cohort of patients delivering a pregnancy complicated by PAS.MethodsA retrospective review of 323 deliveries at three academic medical institutions between January 2013 and June 2022 was included in the analyses. Patients were those with a histopathologically confirmed case of PAS. The composite morbidity outcome included such maternal complications as mechanical ventilation, injury to organs and transfusion of 4+units of red blood cells. Social vulnerability was measured by assigning subjects a value of the Childhood Opportunity Index based on their home zip code. Logistic regression models were employed and adjusted for potential confounders.Results73% of our sample experienced composite severe maternal morbidity at the time of their delivery. There were no statistically significant associations between social vulnerability and severe surgical morbidity, either as a composite or individually, within the multivariate regression models.ConclusionOur results do not support the hypothesis that social vulnerability is associated with severe maternal morbidity in deliveries complicated by PAS. The present study suggests that the relationship between social vulnerability and obstetrical surgical morbidity is more complicated than can be assessed by the present linear regression models.

Introduction

Placenta accreta spectrum disorder (PAS) results in increased maternal morbidity and risk of mortality. PAS results from an abnormal interaction of placental villi and uterine myometrium thus precluding placental separation.1 2 A consensus panel convened within the Perinatal Subcommittee of the Society for Pediatric Pathology developed standard terminology to ensure reproducible grading of PAS.3 The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) recently incorporated this into a more complete clinical diagnostic classification for PAS.4 Higher grades of invasion are associated with worse clinical outcomes.5 The incidence of PAS is rising, due to a greater incidence of uterine surgeries—especially multiple caesarean deliveries.6 7 PAS is associated with higher rates of postpartum haemorrhage, peripartum hysterectomy and length of hospital stay.8 9

Socially constructed factors may influence patterns of morbidity and mortality in pregnancies complicated by PAS.10 One group explored ways in which migration status can further complicate already difficult outcomes related to PAS and pregnancy.11 Associations between socioenvironmental factors—especially neighbourhood exposure to pollutants—and development of abnormal placentation have been reported.12 13 Some studies paint a complicated picture; one systematic meta-analysis found that lower socioeconomic status was negatively associated with the risk of developing PAS.14 A large prospective cohort found no association between demographic factors and PAS development.15 Such relationships with social factors are further complicated by interrelationships with clinical comorbidities.16 Understanding the mechanisms by which PAS intersects with socioenvironmental factors is critical to understanding the pathophysiology of PAS. While recent works have begun to explore the intersection of socioenvironmental factors and PAS,17 further research is critical to improving disparate health outcomes.

The concept of social vulnerability (SV) emerged from understanding risk burdens associated with spatial and environmental hazards and the impacts of these risks on health. Cutter et al developed an early measure of SV in the USA at the county level, incorporating variables including median dollar value of occupied homes, proportions of different racial and ethnic groups, proportion of residents living below the poverty line and differences in employment distributions.18 19 Numerous measures have been developed in recent years to quantify SV. One such measure, the Childhood Opportunity Index (COI), uses 29 factors from three influential domains: health and environment, education and socioeconomics.20

Recent research has explored the association between SV and surgical morbidity throughout a milieu of disciplines. Several studies have found that patients with higher SV values experienced increased surgical morbidity.2123 In some cases, a mediating factor was hospital quality, with patients at greater SV more likely to go to a lower-quality hospital, which in turn led to a greater risk of surgical morbidity.24 There is a paucity of data, however, examining associations between SV and surgical morbidity specific to obstetrics and gynaecology.

The goal of the present study was to examine associations between SV and severe morbidity in a sample of patients whose deliveries were complicated by PAS.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients with histopathologically confirmed cases of PAS at three academic medical centres. All three academic medical centres are located in the same urban area of the USA and serve similar patient populations. All patients who delivered at our institutions between 1 January 2013 and 30 June 2022, with pregnancies complicated by PAS were included.

Demographic and clinical data were abstracted from electronic medical records and compiled in an internal Research Electronic Data Capture instrument for analysis. The statistical software package R was used to conduct the analyses. Demographic data included racial identity, ethnic identity, primary language, health insurance status, zip code and prepregnancy body mass index (BMI). COI values were determined by identifying patients’ zip codes from their mailing addresses in their medical records and abstracting the corresponding index value from the public database.20 Obstetric history including history of caesarean section, history of dilation and curettage, history of myomectomy, history of low placentation and use of assisted reproductive technology. Pathology data included FIGO grade of placental invasion (accreta, increta, percreta), as well as the presence or absence of caesarean scar dehiscence. The following variables were included in consideration of maternal morbidity: transfusion of four or more units of red blood cells, administration of more than one vasopressor, admission to intensive care unit (ICU), intubation and/or mechanical ventilation, and surgical injury to surrounding organs. Maternal mortality was considered, but there were no cases of this outcome in our cohort. A composite morbidity variable was developed where a positive outcome was indicated when any of the above morbidity outcomes were met. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess correlations between the different variables included in the composite morbidity variable.

Associations between morbidity outcomes and SV were examined using logistic regression models. Multivariable models controlled for age, prepregnancy BMI, smoking history, gravidity, parity, history of uterine surgery (caesarean delivery, myomectomy, dilation and curettage), the number of prior caesarean deliveries, grade of placental invasion, low placentation, the use of reproductive technology, gestational age at delivery and involvement of a multidisciplinary team (documentation of care management meeting among providers in maternal–fetal Medicine, anaesthesia, paediatrics, gynaecological oncology, etc). Covariates were chosen a priori and included to control for logical confounders and maximise model fit to the data. R2 values were calculated to assess model fit relative to the maximum likelihood ratio, and these are displayed for the multivariate models. These models were conducted first among the entire study sample, and then among samples stratified by race and ethnicity, as well as institution of delivery. In the case of race and ethnicity, stratifying by these socially constructed identities furthered our examination of adverse maternal morbidity at the intersections of SV and systemic bias. Data were reported according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Discussion

Our results found a complex relationship between SV and severe maternal morbidity in this cohort of patients. Certain components had some significant correlation, but overall, the composite measure did not. This fits with other research which links social factors with obstetric surgical morbidity in a complicated fashion. One large retrospective study found excessive caesarean composite morbidity among non-white patients.25 A separate retrospective cohort of patients undergoing abdominal myomectomy revealed similar racial and ethnic disparities in composite maternal morbidity.26 A systematic review of 83 obstetrical studies on social determinants of health reflected this trend as well, with socioeconomic position or individual social factors repeatedly predicting maternal morbidity and mortality.27 These data suggest that social factors influence surgical morbidity outcomes for obstetric patients.

Other research has corroborated a lack of significance between SV and obstetric surgical outcomes. One large study found no multivariate association between SV and caesarean delivery rates among a sample of nulliparous, term, spontaneous vertex patients.28 In a separate large study of over nine million deliveries, numerous comorbidities were associated with a diagnosis of uterine prolapse, however, there were few social determinants found to have a significant association.29 This might suggest that SV influences morbidity outcomes through mediators of clinical comorbidities, rather than doing so directly as proposed in the present study.

In our analyses, we stratified by race and ethnicity as well as institution to examine identity-specific and location-specific associations between SV and severe maternal morbidity. Intersections of access and advantage complicate racial and ethnic disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality.30 31 Such intersections likely have a compounded effect on increasing the risk of maternal morbidity, including among cases of PAS. Further statistical exploration would illuminate these influences, but that is beyond the scope of the present study.

One important limitation of this study is the use of COI as the exposure outcome. SV is a very complex concept, incorporating a milieu of factors, and while the development of index is undoubtedly relevant it is still not a perfect measure of one’s vulnerability. One group of geographical social scientists found that such measures fall short, even when highly specific to a geographical area because measuring SV requires a more sophisticated model incorporating weighted configurations.32 This is revealed in our model fit statistics, which were largely poor in the overall dataset (R2<0.3). Some researchers suggest using a latent confirmatory factor analysis to validate measures of SV, although this requires a larger dataset than can be achieved for a rare condition at a single institution.33 Nevertheless, more specific geographical areas—say, census-tract-level as opposed to zip-code-level—yield more precise measures of SV.34 For unique cities, like New York, further modifications can be made to adjust the measure better to a specific population.35 A limitation of our study was our reliance on zip-code-level indicators since some sites did not have the ability to abstract census-tract-level data. Another limitation here is that patients may have changed address between time of delivery and time of data abstraction, so length of time from delivery to present is likely a factor in these analyses. While there is evidence throughout the literature of associations between SV and adverse health outcomes, these associations are not necessarily applicable to different populations.

To that end, there are limitations to how our study applies to broader populations. Our results on maternal morbidity may not be generalisable given that our institutions implement a standardised multidisciplinary approach to delivering suspected PAS cases, including comprehensive blood banking resources, ICU collaboration and specialised services such as radiographic embolisation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and level-4 neonatal intensive care services. Patients with PAS delivering at less resource-rich institutions likely face excess perinatal morbidity. A majority of our sample lived in higher vulnerability environments (>50%) such that our understanding of relationships between SV and maternal morbidity may be different than from a sample of patients with an overall lower vulnerability level. Much of our sample had private health insurance and a prepregnancy BMI <30 kg/m2, potentially skewing our results away from those relationships among populations with higher BMI values and public health insurance. Further, there are clinical factors outside the scope of the present study which likely influence maternal morbidity, including characteristics of the surgical procedure.

Nevertheless, to our knowledge, there have been no previous studies of SV in a cohort of obstetric patients complicated by PAS. Here, we examined a measure of SV at the zip code level against a dataset of a decade of deliveries complicated by PAS with histopathological confirmation of abnormal placentation. That being said, census tract-based measures are more precise and consistent than measuring similar characteristics at the zip code level, pointing to future needs in this investigation. Such methods are particularly important in larger urban areas like New York City where zip-code-level data are not sufficiently accurate for analysis of social disparities.36

Conclusion

The present study examined associations between the SV and severe maternal morbidity in a cohort of patients delivering pregnancies complicated by PAS. The analyses did not find any significant association, but this is the first attempt to explore the pathways between SV and health outcomes in the setting of obstetrical morbidities. Future attempts should apply more sophisticated statistical methods to larger datasets yielded from institutional collaboration.
