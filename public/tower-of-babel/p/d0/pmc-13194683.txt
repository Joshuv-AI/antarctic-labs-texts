Depression among infertile women attending Gynecology Referral Clinics in West Arsi and Bale Zones hospitals, 2025
Authors: Solomon Seyife Alemu, Endale Aboabe, Kefelegn Zemedkun, Negeso Gebeyehu Gejo, Mohammedamin Hajure, Addisalem Workie Demsash, Ginenus Fekadu, Geleta Nenko Dube, Yalo Daba, Lema Fikadu Wedajo, Gemechu Gelan Bekele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe World Health Organization identifies infertility as a major public health issue, affecting about 17.5% of people globally. Depression among infertile women has bidirectional physiological, social, and economic effects. However, limited data exists in Ethiopia, where infertility is often linked to social discrimination. Investigating this issue is especially important in low-income countries. This study was aimed at assessing the prevalence of depression and its associated factors among infertile women attending Gynecology Referral Clinics in West Arsi, East Bale, and Bale Zone’s hospitals in Oromia, Ethiopia, in 2025. Facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 423 women from February 25 to April 25, 2025. Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and participants were selected through systematic random sampling. Data were collected via structured interviews and card reviews using Kobo Toolbox and analyzed with SPSS 26. Factors associated with depression were identified using binary and multivariable logistic regression at 95% CI and P < 0.05. In this study 412 of the total 423 women participated in the study, obtaining a 97.4% response rate. The prevalence of depression among infertile women was 53.40% (48.56%, 58.24%). Women aged between 30 and 39, 2.34 (1.05, 3.81); primary infertility, 1.69 (1.06, 2.61); infertility duration ≥ 10 years, 2.40 (1.14, 5.04); poor family support, 2.23 (1.05, 4.73); and lifetime Intimate Partner Violence (IPV), 1.74 (1.34, 2.66), were identified factors. Over half of infertile women in this study experienced depressive symptoms. Significant factors included age, type and duration of infertility, family support, and partner violence. The findings reveal a double burden of infertility and depression. Integrating psychological support, mental health education, and coordinated gynecologic–psychiatric care is recommended.Keywords: Depression, Infertility, Associated factors, Women, EthiopiaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Back ground

In accordance with a report by the World Health Organization (WHO), infertility is now recognized as the foremost global public health issue in recent years. One in six adults globally, or about 17.5% of the population, suffers from infertility1. Infertility has an enormous impact on a person’s mental health in addition to other physiological, social, and economic effects2. The majority of people in the infertile group suffer from prominent mental health issues, including depression3.

One of the most prevalent mental health issues is depression, a serious but treatable mental disorder that has a detrimental impact on a person’s thoughts, feelings, and behavior4. Depression is the most common mental disorder that greatly affects the infertile population because of its bidirectional impacts on the infertile group5. Over 44% of infertile women globally have experienced depression in the past few years, which is an alarming increase6. The problem is relatively prevalent in both high- and low-income nations. Data from studies revealed that the prevalence of depression among infertile women was 28.03% in high-income countries and 44.32% in low- and middle-income nations, indicating that the issue is more severe in low-income nations like Ethiopia7. Ethiopia is one of the low-income countries where the trend of infertility has increased dramatically in the current era, which is justified by 24.2% of the report from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey8.

The infertile population suffers from depression due to a variety of reasons, including not being able to conceive, social consequences, disputes over marriage, and cost of infertility treatments9,10. It affects both men and women similarly on a worldwide level11. However, in many low-income nations, such as Ethiopia, spouses and society at large are mostly to blame for the women, as they are responsible for the case12. Furthermore, they think that a woman is only seen as a complete woman after she becomes a mother. This results in gender discrimination and inequality between men and women7. As a result, the issues challenges women and make them more vulnerable to mental illnesses like depression.

Depression in infertile women impacts their physical health, social life, and adherence to treatment, often leading to prolonged infertility and treatment discontinuation. In many African countries, including Ethiopia, cultural and spiritual beliefs may discourage couples from seeking modern medical treatment, opting instead for traditional practices7,13. This can complicate the situation, and if these methods fail, women may experience further depression. The emotional burden is intensified by societal pressure and stigma surrounding childlessness. Consequently, affected women may face isolation, reduced self-worth, and long-term psychological distress14.

“Mental health and health for all” is one of the Sustainable Development Goals set forward by the United Nations. Thus, by 2030, the experts in the world community have reached an agreement on these strategies. For instance, Goal 3 states the health of mothers and children, which may include maternal mental health. Depression is among the most common mental disorders that affect the most valuable group, so focusing is important as a nation15,16. It is important to find out about depression among infertile women in low-income countries like Ethiopia, where society assigns women the role of the family and where women experience discrimination based on their gender. Even though in African countries, in particular Ethiopia, a lot of studies has been conducted on infertility and its associated factors, there are a few studies that focus on assessing mental health problems among infertile women. Despite the bidirectional impact of depression among infertile women, there is a lack of information about depression among infertile women in Ethiopia. Thus, this study aims to find out the prevalence of depression among infertile women and associated factors in the West Arsi, East Bale, and Bale zones of Oromia, Ethiopia.

In the current era, infertility is a public concern that affects the majority of the population around the globe. Mental health problems, specifically depression, associated with infertility have a devastating impact on the lives of infertile women, especially in low-income countries like Ethiopia, where gender inequality exists. Thus, midwifery- or clinician-led early psychological interventions in infertility clinics are more effective in collaboration with routine infertility clinic care. Therefore, the findings of this study will provide information that may help clinicians strengthen or improve the care they are providing in infertility clinics. Thus, mothers will be the primary beneficiaries of the study.

Methods

Study area, study design and period

The facility-based cross-sectional study design was employed in the West Arsi, East Bale, and Bale zones’ hospitals. West Arsi and Bales zones are zones found in the Oromia Region of south-east Ethiopia. The hospitals are providing care for the catchment areas of the zonal population as well as their neighbors. Based on the estimated 2022 population, the west Arsi has a total population of 2,929,894, of whom 13.85% are urban residents, and in both Bale zones more than 1.8 million people resided17. The majority of people in the West Arsi, East Bale, and Bale zones derive their living through farming. Those zones have a total of 7 public and 6 private hospitals that routinely provide infertility care in addition to medical services. Data was collected between February 25 and April 25, 2025.

Population

Source population

All women who were diagnosed with infertility attended Gynecology Referral Clinics in West Arsi, Bale, and East Bale zone hospitals.

Study population

Women who were diagnosed with infertility attended Gynecology Referral Clinics in West Arsi, Bale, and East Bale zone hospitals during the data collection period.

Eligibility criteria

The study included women who were diagnosed with infertility and attended follow-up gynecology referral clinics in the designated areas. Excluded from the study were women whose infertility diagnosis had not been confirmed at the time of data collection, those attending infertility clinics for other gynecological issues, and women who became pregnant during their follow-up.

Sampling technique and procedure

The study was conducted among infertile women attending hospitals found in the West Arsi, Bale, and East Bale zones. Initially, with the facilitation of the zone’s health bureau information, we identified the hospitals that actively provide infertility care service. Based on our assessment, West Arsi, Bale, and East Bale have a total of 13 hospitals, 7 public and 6 private, which provide routine infertility care services. Then, from those hospitals, by using WHO sampling recommendations, we take more than half (53.8%) of the hospitals. Four public hospitals, namely Shashemenne Compressive Referral Hospital, Dodola General Hospital, Goba Referral Hospital, and Gindhir General Hospital, and three private hospitals, namely Essa Hospital, Faya General Hospital and Arsi Negele Medical College Hospital, were thereby selected by the lottery method and included in our study.

Following that, a proportion of women who followed up at each hospital were obtained, and then the calculated sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital proportional to their population size. Finally, the study sample was selected by systematic random sampling techniques (Fig. 1).

Study variables

Dependent variable

Depression among infertility women.

Independent variables

Socio demographic factors: Age of women, Religion, Ethnicity, Marital status, Year of since marriage, Types of marriage, Allow husband to remarry, Educational status, Occupation, Monthly income, and Residences.

Reproductive and Service related factors: Types of infertility, Duration of infertility, Cause of infertility, Age of seeking treatment, Duration of treatment, Types of fertility treatment, History of miscarriage, History of STD and History of gynecological surgery.

Medical and social related factors: History of medication uses, History of chronic disease, Substance uses, Social support, Intimate partner violence, marital satisfaction, and Willingness to adopt children.

Operational definition

Depression: Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Following established clinical thresholds, scores were categorized as follows: 0–4 (minimal/no symptoms), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), and 15 or above (severe). For the purposes of this study, a cut-off score of 5 or higher was utilized to define the presence of depressive symptoms. This threshold was selected based on prior validation studies within the Ethiopian population, which reported a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 67%4,18–20.

Social support: For social support measurement, the Oslo Social Support Scale (OSLO) was used. The OSSS-3 sum score then was operationalized into three broad categories of social support based on the score as follows. Scores of 3–8 (poor social support code ‘1’), 9–11 (moderate social support code ‘2’), and 12–14 (strong social support code ‘3’) were given21.

Intimate partner violence (IPV): Was assessed by a 13-item tool adapted from WHO to assess domestic violence against women in low-income country settings. It includes physical violence, sexual violence, and psychological violence. Any woman who was the victim of at least one type of IPV was coded as “1” and considered as they faced IPV, and anyone who was not the victim was coded as “0”22.

Marital satisfaction: Marital satisfaction was measured by the Relationship Assessment Scale (“RAS”). This instrument has seven questions that are rated on a Likert scale from 1 to 5. The total number of responses ranged from 7 to 35. Thus, in this study, the level of satisfaction was rated as low satisfaction if the score was between 7 and 14, average satisfaction if the score was between 15 and 21, and high satisfaction if the score was between 22 and 3523.

Average monthly income: Was categorized as above poverty line and below poverty line based on the current classification system by the World Bank group as (US$2.15) or 279.5 Ethiopian Birr (ETB) daily or 8,385 ETB monthly. Those whose monthly income is below poverty line was coded as “0” and above poverty line was coded as “1”24.

Data collection tool and procedure

By using a structured questionnaire, both interviews and patient card reviews were used for data collection for this study. The data was collected by Kobo Toolbox software (Kobo Organization, Cambridge, MA; available at https://www.kobotoolbox.org). The questionnaires have questions about socio-demographic factors, reproductive and service-related issues, social support, intimate partner violence, marital satisfaction, and questions assessing the depression symptoms. The investigative instruments employed in this study possess a robust foundation of both validity and reliability within the Ethiopian context. The questionnaire was prepared in the English language and later translated into Afan Oromo for data collection. Four bachelor degree holders and two supervisors were recruited for data collection. Data collectors explained the objectives of the study for participation by reading the consent sheet loudly in the Afan Oromo language. Then data was collected by face-to-face interviews after they completed infertility clinic services. Data collection was supervised by supervisors on a daily basis.

Data quality control

In order to ensure the quality of the data, a pretest was done in the Melka Oda General Hospital on a sample of 44 women (10% of the sample size). During the pretest, the internal consistency was checked by Cronbach’s alpha; the value was 0.93. Accordingly, modifications were made to the questionnaires after the pretest. Training was given for two days on the objectives of the study, Kobo Toolbox software, data collectors, and participant safety for both data collectors and supervisors. Further, an orientation supported by practical demonstrations on methods of interview and card review on Kobo Toolbox software, with emphasis on the introduction, on communication to assess outcomes correctly, and on respecting the cultural norms of the community, was given for both data collectors and supervisors. Collected data was checked for completeness on a daily basis by data collectors and supervisors. Finally, data cleaning was done by using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY; available at https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

Data processing and analysis

The collected data from Kobo Toolbox was exported to SPSS version 26 software for providing value, cleaning, and further analysis. Initially, descriptive statistics such as mean, standard deviation, and percentage were determined. Then the association between the outcome variable, depression, and each independent variable was first seen in the bivariate binary logistic regression model. In the second step, independent variables having a p-value < 0.25 were retained and entered into the binary logistic regression model for multivariable analysis. The degree of association between outcome and independent variables was determined using the OR with a CI of 95% and p-value. A multi-collinearity test was done using co-linearity statistics among the independent variables, variance inflation factor, and tolerance (VIF = 2.5). The model goodness of fit was tested by the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test the result was 0.81. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered as a cutoff point to declare there was a statistically significant association between dependent and independent variables.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was officially obtained from the Madda Walabu University Shashemenne Campus School of Health Science Ethical Review Committee, with all participating hospitals granting the necessary administrative approval. Following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, informed written consent was obtained from each participant after a thorough explanation of the study’s purpose and their right to withdraw at any time. The research team took steps to ensure data collection occurred in private, low-stress settings, with protocols in place to pause interviews if clinical needs required it. To protect participant anonymity and data security, no personal identifiers were recorded on the questionnaires; additionally, digital records were stored in encrypted, password-protected files accessible only to the primary investigators.

In terms of marital status, 393 (95.4%) of the study participants were in union, and the mean and standard deviation of the participants’ ages were 35.09 (± 5.70 SD). It was found that 345 (83.7%) of the respondents had been married in the last years, and 355 (86.2%) were monogamous. About 189 (45.9%) study participants had primary education. Furthermore, 362 (87.9%) of the study participants had monthly incomes below the poverty level. Lastly, 211 (51.2%) of the study subjects lived in rural areas (Table 1).

Factors associated with depression among infertile women attending Gynecology Referral Clinics in West Arsi, East Bale, and Bale zones hospitals in 2025

In binary logistic regression, eight variables—age, residences, types of infertility, duration of infertility, willingness to adopt children, family support, relationship satisfaction, and lifetime IPV were candidates for multivariable logistic regression. Finally, five variables—age, types of infertility, duration of infertility, family support, and lifetime IPV fulfilled the criteria of association with depression among infertile women.

Age of study participants was among the factors associated with depression among infertile women. The odds of having depression among infertile women aged between 30 and 39 were 2.34 times higher compared to women aged between 18 and 29 (AOR = 2.34; 95% CI: 1.05, 3.81). Women who were diagnosed with primary infertility were 1.69 times more likely to develop depression than women who were diagnosed with secondary infertility (AOR = 1.69; 95% CI: 1.06, 2.61). Women whose infertility duration was ≥ 10 years were 2.40 times more likely to develop depression compared to those whose duration was < 5 years (AOR = 2.40; 95% CI: 1.14, 5.04).

Family support and lifetime intimate partner violence were the factors that were associated with depression among infertile women. The odds of having depression among women whose poor family support were 2.23 times higher than their counterparts (AOR = 2.23; 95% CI: 1.05, 4.73). Women who faced lifetime IPV were 1.74 times more likely to develop depression compared to those who did not face lifetime IPV (AOR = 1.74; 95% CI: 1.34, 2.66) (Table 4).

Discussion

Infertility has become a serious public concern in both high-income and low-income countries, including Ethiopia recently. One of the most common mental health issues affecting this group is depression. Depression has a broad social, physiological, and psychological impact, particularly on women in low-income nations like Ethiopia, where gender-based violence is prevalent. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms and associated factors among infertile women attending hospitals in West Arsi, Bale Zone, and East Bale Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, in 2025. As revealed by this study, the prevalence of depressive symptoms in this specific group was 53.40% (95% CI: 48.56%, 58.24%). Factors such as age, types of infertility, duration of infertility, family support, and lifetime IPV are associated with depression among infertile women in the study area.

The finding for this is in accordance with the studies conducted in Nigeria25 and Hungary26, which were 52.7% and 58%, respectively. This due to the fact that parenting is often heavily emphasized by society; infertility can have a tremendous emotional impact on women. Additionally, social norms in Ethiopia and Nigeria that place pressure on women to conceive, increasing psychological vulnerability. Limited social support and high out-of-pocket medical costs further intensify stress. This truth is alignment with previous scholarly observations; our findings suggest that the profound emotional impact of infertility on women is deeply rooted in the heavy societal emphasis placed on parenting27. The similarity with Hungary may reflect transitional economic instability. It may also indicate higher diagnostic sensitivity and better recognition of mental health conditions28.

However, this study’s result is higher than that of a study done in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, which found 42.1%. This could be because the previous study was carried out in a capital city where gender equality is more widely accepted. Additionally, those study participants had greater access to the health system, emotional support, and coping mechanisms than the study participants in this study. This study’s prevalence is also greater than that of studies done in teaching hospitals in Southern Nigeria29, Northwest Nigeria30, and Uganda31, which were 42.5%, 44.6%, and 44.3%, respectively. Additionally, this survey’s findings are greater than those of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia32 study, which came in at 37.2%. The socioeconomic, cultural, and acceptability of gender equality differences among study participants could be the possible justification. Additionally, there were differences in the tools and cut points employed, as well as in the health system’s response to such care. Lately, because the number of infertility cases has increased in the modern era, the prevalence may be impacted by variations in study duration33,34.

Furthermore, the finding of this study is lower than the studies conducted in Ghana35, Asia36, and Iraq37, which were 62%, 72.54%, and 68.9%, respectively. This disparity may be caused by the fact that the research was carried out in several cultures with varying reactions to cases of infertility, including cultural norms and discrimination. Limited access to mental and reproductive health care, and there may be other factors among certain groups.

Age of women is among the factors identified in this study. Depressive symptoms were 2.34 times higher among women 30 to 39 compared to women aged between 18 and 29. This is supported by the studies conducted in Ghana35 and Hungary26. This might be due to a woman’s age being physiologically crucial for reproduction; as she ages, her chances of becoming pregnant naturally diminish because of a decline in reproductive function, specifically in relation to the quantity and quality of eggs38. Relationship dynamics and personal timing might make these sentiments worse. This realization may result in feelings of mental health issues. Furthermore, a woman’s age-related social impact may contribute to this.

Women diagnosed with primary infertility were 1.7 times more likely to develop depressive symptoms. This is supported by the studies conducted in Nigeria25, Ghana35, and Iraq37. This could be because depression might have a greater impact on primary infertility because of the psychological and emotional difficulties that come with the first experience of not being able to conceive. However, people with secondary infertility might already have known the happiness of parenthood, so their circumstances are different; they might still feel depressed and frustrated, but they might not be as emotionally burdened as those with primary infertility.

Duration of infertility is another factor that was identified in this study. Women whose infertility was more than ten years were more likely to experience depressive symptoms. This is supported by the studies conducted in Ghana35 and Iraq37. One possible explanation for this could be that women’s ages grow with duration, reducing the likelihood of conception. Furthermore, they may face loss of follow-up and disinterest in medical follow-up as extended periods, leading to a rise in cases. This suggests that women’s general mental health may be improved by early identification and treatment of infertility.

Family support and lifetime IPV were the factors that were associated with depressive symptoms among infertile women. Women who had poor family support were 2.2 times more likely, and women who faced lifetime IPV were 1.74 times more likely, to develop depressive symptoms. This is supported by the studies conducted in South Nigeria29 and Uganda31. Infertile women’s depressed symptoms are greatly exacerbated by intimate partner violence (IPV) and a lack of family support. During a difficult period like infertility, feelings of loneliness and inadequacy can be made worse by the absence of emotional and practical support from family39. Furthermore, women who experience IPV could experience emotional trauma that exacerbates their mental health issues and raises the prevalence of despair.

Implication and limitation of the study

The findings of this study necessitate a paradigm shift toward integrating mental health services directly into infertility care. Specifically, healthcare facilities should implement routine psychological and safety screenings to identify depressive symptoms and those with primary infertility lasting over a decade. Furthermore, the strong link between poor family support and mental distress suggests that interventions should focus on couple-based counseling. While this study provides a vital roadmap for policy, certain limitations must be noted; the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of a definitive causal link between infertility and depression. Additionally, we utilized highly experienced data collectors to minimize social desirability bias.

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

This article claims that slightly more than one in two infertile women in the study area experienced depressive symptoms. Infertile women’s depressive symptoms have been associated with poor family support, primary infertility, infertility lasting longer than ten years, women aged thirty to thirty-nine, and intimate partner violence.

Recommendations

To health professionals

Health professionals, particularly gynecologists and those working in gynecological units, must recognize the rising issue of mental health, specifically depression, among individuals facing infertility. It is essential that they not only address reproductive health but also consider psychological counseling as part of routine care. Healthcare providers should be vigilant in identifying symptoms of depression in women undergoing infertility treatments and refer them to mental health professionals for comprehensive support. To prevent both infertility-related complications and mental health issues like depression, early diagnosis and timely treatment should be strongly encouraged. Additionally, healthcare providers should promote collaboration and mutual support among women, their partners, and families to help mitigate the psychological burden and prevent further mental health problems.

To concerned bodies

The zonal health offices in West Arsi, Bale, and East Bale should enhance the focus of health professionals and health extension workers on addressing psychological issues, such as depression, in the context of infertility care. Hospitals should also prioritize raising awareness and providing health education about the impact of infertility on mental health, emphasizing the importance of timely infertility treatment, family support, and the prevention of intimate partner violence. Both governmental and non-governmental organizations working in maternal mental health should place greater emphasis on screening and treating depression in women facing infertility. Furthermore, providing targeted training for healthcare professionals will strengthen their ability to effectively screen for and treat depression alongside infertility, improving overall care for affected individuals.
