Being a woman researcher in dangerous laboratories: biosafety level 3 and tuberculosis
Authors: Kübra Yıldırım, Orhan Koçak, Meltem Baran, Cemilenur Ataş, Esra Tanyel Akçit, Majda Licina, Nurgül Bozkurt, Mustafa Çoban, Mehmet Akif Gün, Seçkin Özler, Tarana Oktan, Ece Şimşek, Ahmet Yılmaz Çoban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies on the tuberculosis pathogen Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacillus require a biosafety level 3 laboratory. This study aims to investigate the gender distribution of researchers working in the most hazardous tuberculosis laboratory.MethodsIn this study, the original articles indexed in Web of Science between 1970 and 2021, which include phenotypic testing methods in which the susceptibility results of drugs used in the treatment of tuberculosis are determined, were reviewed.ResultsAs a result of our analysis, it was found that female researchers were included as first name in the publications at a rate of 49%, unlike other fields of science, and 51% came from lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. According to the incidence rates announced by the World Health Organization in the years when the articles were published, it was seen that female researchers participated in more studies in regions with a high incidence of tuberculosis to ('x̄' = 89.94).ConclusionAccording to our data, it was found that the proportion of female researchers was high in the laboratory with a high risk of contamination of biosafety level 3. The high proportion of female researchers in hazardous areas is striking. Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13010-025-00177-z.

Introductıon

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which can manifest in many organs, particularly the lungs, and is spread among humans through the respiratory tract [2]. Ranked 13 th on the list of leading causes of death worldwide in 2019, TB is projected to be the second leading cause of death from a single infectious agent after COVID- 19 [10]. Tuberculosis, whose history dates back to ancient times, is an infectious disease that is difficult to control, treat, and research/study by scientists doing academic research in the field. This pathogen, which is classified as Risk Group 3 in the international classification [9], is transmitted by the respiratory route and poses a serious biosafety risk to health and laboratory workers, as it can be spread by direct or indirect contact with active TB patients [6, 7, 11, 22]. In its Laboratory Biosafety Guide, the WHO describes biosafety levels using a four-level classification system. These biosafety levels include the design features, structure, containment facilities, protective equipment, practices, and operational procedures required to work with agents in different risk groups [9, 21]. WHO biosafety guidelines require that any manipulation of specimens suspected of containing MDR M. tuberculosis be performed in a biosafety level 3 (BSL- 3) facility [8, 17]. This is because these laboratories are containment facilities designed to allow the manipulation of aerosol-transmitted pathogens.

However, given the requirements of BSL- 3 standards, most low-income countries with a high TB burden may not be able to meet the necessary conditions; therefore, WHO recommends the use of liquid culture systems for low- and middle-income countries [14]. Risk levels of TB laboratories are classified by WHO as low, medium, and high, as shown in Table S1 (Supplementary Table S1).

Of these, high-risk laboratories have the minimum design features necessary to safely manipulate tuberculosis cultures. It is designed to work with M. tuberculosis organisms in high volumes and concentrations and to perform procedures that increase the risk of aerosol propagation. Noteworthy in the design of these laboratories are the following: two separate entry doors are required to create a vestibule leading into the laboratory. This design provides a physical barrier to the outside laboratory areas. It also allows for the creation of a one-way airflow (negative pressure) into the laboratory under the influence of the biosafety cabinet. The vestibule should have facilities to separate clean clothing from soiled clothing. In addition, the entrance doors should be self-closing. Personal protective equipment, lab coats and respirators should be worn. Aprons should have strong front panels and be liquid resistant. Decontamination and waste disposal are other important aspects of these high-risk laboratories. An autoclave should be located near the tuberculosis containment laboratory to ensure that culture tubes containing tuberculosis bacilli are sterilized before disposal.

All other infectious waste must be removed from the TB containment laboratory for proper disposal. It should be transported in sealed plastic bags or containers according to local regulations. In addition to these safety precautions, minimizing the formation of aerosols is important in reducing the risk of infection in the laboratory. Aerosol formation can be prevented by several microbiological techniques used during application [21]. The difficulty of this study area is that tuberculosis laboratories and the practices performed in these laboratories require high level biosafety procedures to minimize the risk of transmission.

So are there more female or male researchers working in this high-risk field? The answer to this question, which is the main purpose of our study, is very clear. While the rate of male researchers in laboratory-based work areas is high, there is no study that examines the rate of female workers in the field of tuberculosis, which is classified as a hazardous laboratory. To date, while the rate of male researchers in laboratory-based work areas is high, there has been no study investigating the rate of female workers in the field of tuberculosis, which is classified as a hazardous laboratory. This is the first study to determine the proportion of female researchers working in hazardous laboratories. According to the results we obtained, the rate of female researchers working in the tuberculosis laboratory was higher than the rate of male researchers.

On the other hand, scientific research needs to be funded to ensure the sustainability of studies, especially in the basic sciences. Gender-neutral support from these funds will contribute to the development of both basic and health sciences. Whether there is gender inequality in the allocation of scientific funding is an important issue. In particular, it is important to identify the gender of those who work in Biosafety Level 3 laboratories and those who use the funds, to determine whether there is gender inequality in hazardous laboratory work, and to determine new policies accordingly. For this reason, one of the aims of our study is to determine the proportion of male and female funders of studies conducted in biosafety level 3 laboratories.

The main effect of the socio-economic level of the country on this result is undeniable, but the question should be asked and discussed whether it is the courage of female researchers or the abstention of male researchers that is behind the high rate of female researchers in tuberculosis studies with high biohazard potential. In addition, the question of how we can support the sustainability of basic research to improve human health should also be discussed.

Methods

Search strategy and article selection

The qualitative study examined hazardous laboratories for tuberculosis. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement was used to structure the design of this study, including articles in the Web of Science (WOS) between 1970 and 2021 (Fig. 1) 1197 articles were identified in the first phase. Using the inclusion and exclusion criteria and the prism technique, the study was completed on a total of 430 articles.Fig. 1Dangerous Laboratories in Tuberculosis via PRISMA. * As part of the exclusion criteria, we excluded non-in vitro studies; therefore, we excluded three articles whose full text we had access to but which were not in vitro studies

The research was completed using two groups of keywords. The first group of keywords is related to"tuberculosis", the second is related to"laboratory biosafety hazard levels". The papers related to tuberculosis on the Web of Science in the first group are determined by using the keywords "tuberculosis or Mycobacterium tuberculosis". Moreover, the papers related to laboratory biosafety hazard levels on the Web of Science as the second group were determined by using keywords such as "agar proportion","broth microdilution","absolute concentration","resistance ratio","method","crystal violet","decolorization assay","nitrate reductase assay","malachite green decolorization assay","resazurin microtiter assay","E-test". Determination of Gender

The following steps were used to describe the gender data obtained in this study, based on the list of authors created at the end of the screening criteria;Accessing the information indicating the gender of the authors in the list within the scope of the information contained in the business addresses specified in their articles. At this stage, no gender information could be obtained for any of the authors.Determination of gender from the photographs on the authors' corporate addresses and social media accounts (Facebook, Instragram, Linkedin, researchgate). At this stage the names and photographs were verified by three different members of the research team. The majority of the data used in the study was also obtained at this stage. No special search engine or server could be used to obtain the data at this stage. The evaluation was made within the scope of publicly available information.For the authors whose photographs could not be found, the names of men and women belonging to the countries were identified by scanning.

Analyses

All data obtained were first transferred to an Excel file. Descriptive statistics such as frequency analysis were used for data analysis. Data entry was done by six researchers. data entry was done by a total of 5 groups in pairs. when there was uncertainty between two people, it was verified by a third person.

Limitations of the study

Our first limitation of this study is that only Web of Science data was used and only full-text publications were included. Only names, photos, and information from professional job sites such as researchgate, linkedin, etc. were used to determine author gender. Respondents were not asked what gender they identified as. This is another major limitation of the study.

When the gender distribution of articles was evaluated according to the first 4 names, it was found that 48.2% of the articles produced as a result of research conducted in high-risk laboratories were produced by female researchers (Table 2). Distribution of tuberculosis articles produced in high risk laboratories by gender

According to Casad et al. [5, 13], there has recently been widespread interest in the underrepresentation of women in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM); however, progress toward gender equality in these fields has been slow. Even more alarming, these gender disparities are exacerbated when examining women's representation within STEM departments in academia. The rhetoric that men are more likely to be involved in dangerous and risky work does not apply to a high-risk safety laboratory such as tuberculosis. In this context, it can be said that women and men work equally in tuberculosis studies that require high-risk safety.

Laboratory-based research requires a lot of equipment and consumables. Therefore, in addition to the cost of human resources, this type of research requires a very large budget for the purchase of materials. Therefore, the studies need to be funded by an organization. Since the monthly salaries of researchers, especially in developing countries, are barely enough for living expenses, it is necessary to allocate an extra budget for experimental materials.

In the analysis by first name, 50.9% of the supported articles are female and 49.1% are male (Table 2). In the analysis by first name, the first name was female in 51.2% of the studies, the second name was female in 47.2%, the third name was female in 53.3%, and the fourth name was female in 46.2%. In the total number of studies, men benefited more from the funds. In the total number of male–female articles, this ratio was 49.4% for women and 50.8% for men (Table S2).

Casad et al. [5, 13] state that funding is an important tool in the training of women researchers and their continuation in academia. In our study, it was observed that women benefited less from research funding than men. However, this difference is quite small and in this context it can be said that women are in a similar situation to men in terms of receiving funding, especially if they work in hazardous laboratories. Women researchers seem to be treated equally to men in terms of funding if they work in a high-risk laboratory.

When the distribution of the status of receiving support from any fund according to gender in the articles published as first name is female, 51.9% of the supported articles are women and 48.1% are men (Table S2).

Of the total number of articles, 51.2% were published with women as the first name, 47.2% with women as the second name, 53.3% with women as the third name, and 46.2% with women as the fourth name. Regardless of the order of names, men were more likely to benefit from funding in the total number of studies. In the studies that benefited from funding, this ratio was 49.4% for women and 50.7% for men.

When the gender distribution of tuberculosis studies published in WOS is analysed according to the countries'levels of development, it is seen that in the articles where the first name is female, the highest number of women researchers are from upper middle income countries 51 (n = 91) and lower middle income countries 51 (n = 50), followed by high income countries 46 (n = 63) and low income countries 39 (n = 39), respectively. Considering the WHO regional classification, it was found that the highest number of female researchers were from the Americas (61 (n = 78)), followed by South East Asia, 57 (n = 34), Europe, 43 (n = 53), Africa 39 (n = 14), Western Pacific 35 (n = 20).

It was found that the average Time Cited in WOS Core of the publication of the first cited female study on tuberculosis was x̄ = 9.23, while x̄ = 31.65 for males. Our aim in this evaluation was to assess whether the citation value, which increases with the quality of the publication, is related to the use of gender-specific funding. In fact, the high level of funding in articles originating from laboratory studies is closely related to the quality of the study. The high level of equipment used in the experiments or the study of more factors in the study are completely related to the funds used and contribute directly to the citation. For this reason, although the difference is statistically significant, the fact that studies with a male first name are cited more often should not be taken to mean that they are of higher quality when evaluated with these data alone. Meanwhile, we would like to emphasize that the fact that the fund is used by men may increase the citation rates of male authors. In addition, the fact that the standard deviation is very high, especially for men, may also be due to the highly cited articles creating outliers.

It was found that the mean tuberculosis incidence rate of researchers whose first name was female was x̄ = 89.94 and x̄ = 84.52 for males (p < 0.05). (Table S5; Table S6; Table S7).

Conclusion

Female academics face problems resulting from sexist attitudes. Gender discrimination and inequalities continue to affect the distribution of women in academia. Research has shown that women are underrepresented in higher academic positions, with significant disparities in the distribution of female faculty by institutional prestige and rank [24]. This underrepresentation is particularly pronounced in senior positions, as evidenced by the fact that only 18% of professors in the United Kingdom are women [4]. Research has shown that gender discrimination is a major factor limiting women's academic career progression in various fields, including medicine and STEM [3]. Gender discrimination persists in academia, as evidenced by the challenges faced by women academics, including subtle forms of discrimination, the glass ceiling phenomenon, and differences in contractual status and access to academic hierarchies [12, 16, 15]. In academia, women face systemic barriers such as biased promotion systems, traditional family norms, division of labor, and institutional resistance to gender equality.[1, 19]. The academic environment has also been scrutinized for reproducing gender inequality, particularly in academic publishing, where gender inequality among academics persists [8]. Overall, these findings underscore the need to address gender discrimination and systemic inequalities that impede women's participation and advancement in academia, which can arise from sexist distinctions in economic life as well as the roles and responsibilities that society places on the female gender. Gender roles require female academics to compete with their male counterparts in business life and to take on the basic responsibilities of cleaning the house and eating in their private lives. In this case, the female academician assumes roles such as wife, mother, and researcher. The intermittent or continuous conflict of these roles seems to be inevitable [20, 23].

According to our study findings, the top five countries where the proportion of female researchers is higher than male researchers are Brazil, Belgium, Cuba, Sweden and the United Kingdom.

Germany, China, Turkey, Uganda and Pakistan are the top 5 countries where the proportion of male researchers is higher than the proportion of female researchers. According to these data, the gender of researchers is shaped by country, independent of continents and development levels of countries.

Considering the name order of the researchers, the highest female to male ratio was found to be second name (1.02) first name (0.96) third name (0.92) fourth name (0.80). Considering the female and male ratios, while the ratios in the first and second names are equal to each other, the female to male ratio is relatively lower in the fourth name.

It has been shown that although academic women have education, experience, and similar professional qualifications, they are unable to reach senior management positions [18]. On the other hand, the sustainability of basic research should be improved regardless of gender.
