Fintech’s role in carbon emission efficiency: dynamic spatial analysis
Authors: Ying Zhang, Min Chen, Shen Zhong, Mingyu Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Against the backdrop of digitization and global warming, fintech plays a crucial role in accelerating the growth of green finance, driving innovation in the financial industry, and catalyzing the low-carbon transformation of economic activities. This paper utilizes city panel data from 2007 to 2019 to examine the direct impact of fintech on carbon emission efficiency (CEE), the transmission channels of green technological innovation and green finance, and the spatial spillover effects, using dynamic panel models, mediation effect models, and dynamic spatial Durbin models (SDM). The study finds that: (i) Fintech significantly improves CEE, and this conclusion remains robust after accounting for potential endogeneity issues and conducting robustness tests. (ii) Mechanism analysis reveals that green finance and green technological innovation are the primary channels through which fintech influences CEE. (iii) Results from the dynamic SDM model indicate that fintech has a significant positive spatial spillover effect on CEE, with the long-term spillover effect being smaller than the short-term spillover effect. (iv) Heterogeneity analysis reveals that fintech’s improvement effect on CEE is mainly evident in eastern regions, emerging first-tier and first-tier cities, and non-resource-based cities. Our research provides new insights for policymakers on achieving China’s dual-carbon goals through the promotion of fintech development, green finance, and green technological innovation. It also aids in the coordinated development of fintech among cities and the formulation of differentiated policies, providing a theoretical foundation and empirical support for future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74834-2.

Introduction

In 2022, global energy demand escalated significantly, leading to a notable surge in CO2 emissions from energy consumption and industrial production, peaking at 36.8 gigatons—a 0.9% increase from 2021. If this trend continues, CO2 emissions from existing and planned fossil fuel infrastructure will exceed the 1.5 °C target1. CO2 is a primary catalyst of climate change and associated extreme weather events. The persistent accumulation of CO2 in the atmosphere intensifies the greenhouse effect, leading to a steady rise in global temperatures. The effects of global warming may push components of the climate system to critical thresholds, triggering tipping points, substantial economic losses, and increased uncertainty2,3. Pressing climate challenges threaten sustainable economic development, prompting urgent global action to combat climate change, reduce dependence on fossil fuels and lower carbon emissions4. Energy conservation and emission reduction remain global focal points. In response to the climate crisis, the Chinese government has set dual carbon objectives: achieving carbon peaking by 2030 and attaining carbon neutrality by 2060. Promoting economic and social development towards greener, lower-carbon paradigms is crucial for high-quality development. A low-carbon economy aims for greater economic output while minimizing resource consumption and carbon emissions. The key question is how to optimize economic output, minimize carbon emissions, and enhance CEE to achieve dual-carbon objectives and high-quality economic development while keeping input factors constant. This issue commands the attention of the Chinese government, enterprises, and scholars, requiring resolution in both academic and practical spheres.

Meanwhile, global fintech is growing rapidly, with investments totaling $113.7 billion in 2023. China’s fintech companies and products are becoming increasingly sophisticated, forming an integral part of the country’s financial services ecosystem. In the first half of 2023, fintech funding in mainland China grew by 461% year-on-year, totaling $3.026 billion. Fintech, as a financial innovation grounded in underlying technologies such as big data, artificial intelligence (AI), and blockchain, not only supports high-quality, sustainable economic development but also serves as a catalyst for low-carbon economic growth through enhanced financial services. Theoretically, fintech addresses information asymmetry, thereby enhancing the quality of financial services and improving resource allocation efficiency5, which in turn promotes energy conservation6. Fintech provides innovative solutions to climate change, significantly contributing to global sustainable development by addressing climate risks7, reducing carbon emissions and facilitating economic green transformation. Exploring the impact of fintech on CEE and its underlying mechanisms is crucial for identifying pathways to enhance CEE through financial innovation. This research holds significant theoretical and practical value for advancing China’s transition to green, low-carbon, and sustainable development.

CEE is generally defined as the maximum economic gain obtained from carbon emissions generated by social activities. It is an important indicator of both economic and emission reduction efficiency. CEE is measured from two main perspectives: single-factor8–10, which is the output per unit of carbon emissions, and full-factor efficiency11–13. Single-factor CEE ignores the influence of production factors like capital and labor on the substitution effect of energy. It fails to account for the multidimensional nature of CEE and does not reflect potential technical efficiency or the complex role of environmental and social factors. Total-factor CEE, which constructs a production frontier using multiple input factors and balances desired and undesired outputs, partially overcomes the limitations of single-factor CEE. This method optimizes productivity and the positive externalities of environmental protection14, highlighting the coordinated relationship between economic growth and emission reduction15. Regarding influencing factors, existing research indicates that factors such as technological innovation, industrial structure, environmental regulation intensity, green finance (GF), energy efficiency, foreign direct investment, and population density have varying degrees of influence on CEE16–20.

Research on fintech primarily explores its impact on agricultural services, banking risks, corporate efficiency, green finance, energy efficiency, climate risk, economic development, and inclusion21–28. With increased academic attention on environmental pollution and low-carbon transition, the relationship between fintech and environmental pollution or CO2 emissions has been well-argued, but no consensus has been reached. Some academics argue that fintech is negatively correlated with carbon emissions, suggesting that the development of technologies like cryptocurrencies and blockchain have increased the demand for energy and resources29. Cryptocurrency “mining” hardware requires substantial electricity, driving the economy to use fossil energy, thereby increasing CO2 and natural gas emissions30. Another perspective suggests that fintech can effectively reduce regional carbon emissions and mitigate environmental pollution. Fintech, through low-carbon consumption methods, provides environmental benefits27, promotes green and low-carbon economic development31, and accelerates global green transformation32. Fintech has introduced a new paradigm, reshaping the traditional financial landscape33,34. It reduces information asymmetry in financial markets, optimizes the efficiency of financial resource allocation35, expands the scope of financial services36, and tightens credit constraints for high-pollution enterprises37. Additionally, fintech supports the development of renewable energy, encourages its consumption, reduces fossil fuel use, and lowers CO2 emissions38,39.

Although the relationship between fintech and carbon emissions is relatively well-studied, research on fintech’s impact on CEE is still limited. Some studies have used static models to examine the relationship between fintech and CEE at the national and provincial levels. For instance, Firdousi et al. (2023) analyzed static panel data from 26 MSCI emerging markets and found that integrating fintech with renewable energy helps curb carbon emissions39. Xu et al. (2023) used a fixed-effects model to analyze provincial-level static panel data in China from 2011 to 2020 and found that fintech, by contributing to the development of GF, reduced carbon emission intensity40. Few studies have utilized static models to analyze the relationship between fintech and carbon emissions at the city level. Cheng et al. (2023) used data from 253 Chinese cities from 2011 to 2019, measuring fintech with the urban financial inclusion index. They analyzed the impact of fintech on carbon emissions with a static panel model and found that fintech reduces carbon emissions through industrial structure, financing constraints, and technological innovation41. Additionally, the diffusion of fintech suggests the potential for significant spatial effects between fintech and CEE. Teng and Shen (2023) analyzed 276 Chinese cities, utilizing the super-efficiency SBM-DEA method to measure CEE and employing static panel models for their analysis. They found that fintech enhances CEE by increasing government expenditure or fostering green innovation, with this effect exhibiting significant spatial spillover13. Moreover, because CO2 emissions are a dynamic process and regional CEE demonstrates temporal inertia, analyzing the relationship between fintech and CEE through the lens of dynamic spatial spillover is more advantageous for enhancing regional green development and achieving more efficient carbon emissions management.

The existing literature has extensively examined the impact of fintech on CEE using static models, yet there is a noticeable absence in research exploring this relationship through dynamic modeling. Specifically, the dynamic spatial spillover effects of fintech on CEE remain underexplored. Given the rapid integration of finance and technology, alongside accelerated global efforts to achieve carbon reduction targets, it is both necessary and meaningful to explore how fintech influences CEE and to propose relevant policy implications. To fill this gap, this paper starts with an in-depth analysis of the mechanisms through which fintech influences CEE. It then applies the super-efficiency SBM-GML method and a dynamic SDM, utilizing sample data from 256 Chinese cities between 2007 and 2019, to investigate fintech’s impact on CEE and its dynamic spatial spillover effects.

Compared to existing research, this paper introduces several key innovations: (1) It is the first to analyze the impact of fintech on CEE using a dynamic model. Unlike previous studies that relied on static panel models, this paper adopts a dynamic approach to examine how fintech influences CEE and the associated dynamic spatial effects. The evolution of fintech and shifts in CEE often involve time lags and cumulative effects, driven not only by temporal dynamics but also by spatial correlations. The dynamic panel model captures the evolving relationship and lag effects between fintech and CEE, while the dynamic Spatial Durbin Model (SDM) accounts for both temporal and spatial dimensions, allowing for the analysis of spatiotemporal interaction effects. This approach enables a more accurate assessment of fintech’s implementation effects and long-term impacts. Conclusions drawn from dynamic model analyses can assist governments in formulating long-term plans and strategies, thereby strengthening their capacity to address climate change and environmental protection. (2) This paper proposes mechanisms and pathways through which fintech influences CEE. By incorporating GF and GTI, the study constructs an analytical framework to examine the relationship between fintech and CEE, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The study identifies two primary pathways through which fintech impacts urban CEE: GF and GTI. Additionally, the study examines the mechanisms through which fintech influences the spatial spillover effects of CEE from these two perspectives, providing a new insight on how fintech can drive improvements in CEE. (3) This paper utilizes prefecture-level city data to assess the impact of fintech on urban CEE and its heterogeneity. While most existing studies have concentrated on national and provincial levels, exploring fintech’s effects on environmental benefits, carbon emissions, and carbon intensity, research at the city level remains limited, especially concerning fintech’s impact on total-factor CEE. City-level data provide a more granular spatial scale, capturing specific urban characteristics and differences, thereby yielding more precise and reliable conclusions. Furthermore, while a city’s carbon emissions are shaped by historical factors, geographic location, and resource endowments, its CEE reflects the outcomes of economic decision-making and environmental planning42. Unlike previous studies that focus on the quantitative effects of fintech on carbon reduction, this paper adopts a qualitative approach, emphasizing how to minimize carbon emissions at a given level of output. Given the significant variations in geographic location, economic development, and resource endowments among cities, this study also conducts a heterogeneity analysis from three perspectives: urban regional location, city size, and resource endowments. This analysis identifies the unique needs of different city types, providing policymakers with tailored recommendations to enhance the specificity and effectiveness of policies.

The remainder of the study is structured as follows: The second part analyzes the intrinsic mechanisms of fintech and CEE and proposes research hypotheses. The third part constructs the models, selects variables, and describes the data sources. The fourth part explores and analyzes the empirical results. The fifth part includes further discussion, heterogeneity tests, and spatial spillover analysis. The sixth part presents the conclusions and corresponding policy recommendations.

Theoretical analysis and research hypothesis

The impact of fintech on CEE

Fintech plays a pivotal role in facilitating the green, low-carbon transformation of economic development and the enhancement of CEE. Firstly, fintech, with data as its core, inherently possesses green and low-carbon attributes. It exhibits characteristics of low energy consumption, minimal pollution, renewability, and recyclability, resulting in a comparatively small environmental burden cost. Secondly, fintech companies leverage state-of-the-art internet and big data technologies to actively engage in environmental conservation through various means, playing a significant role in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals43. For instance, Alibaba’s Ant Forest integrates fintech with a low-carbon lifestyle by applying information technology to individual carbon management. By promoting the low-carbon behaviors of Alipay users and helping to sequester carbon as trees grow, Ant Forest changes people’s lifestyles, enhances their low-carbon awareness, and contributes to carbon reduction and economic and social benefits44. Thirdly, fintech, leveraging underlying technologies such as blockchain and the Internet of Things (IoT), relies on regional telecommunications infrastructure development to optimize transaction processes through online platform services. Digital modes such as mobile banking and NFC mobile payments reduce the frequency of customers physically visiting bank counters, saving the shoe-leather cost of on-site office operations45,46. Additionally, digital applications such as e-contracts, e-authentication, and e-signatures reduce the environmental costs of offline transactions47. While improving financial efficiency, fintech also reduces transaction costs and material resource consumption, alleviates resource constraints, and reduces carbon emissions and energy consumption per unit of urban output. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

Fintech can effectively improve CEE.

Influence mechanisms of fintech on CEE

Fintech is becoming an essential driving force to empower GF and facilitate the economy’s green, low-carbon transformation35. Financial intermediation theory posits that financial intermediaries can leverage economies of scale and scope to reduce losses in uncertain environments and lower participation costs for individuals48. This encourages greater and more effective participation of economic agents in financial market activities. Fintech plays a similar role by driving an information revolution in the financial industry34. Leveraging technological advantages, fintech mitigates the high costs associated with information asymmetry between borrowers and lenders, reduces transaction costs, and alleviates information asymmetry. Additionally, by creating and utilizing a diverse range of complex financial instruments, fintech expands financing channels49, providing crucial support for the diverse financing needs of green finance43.

Functional finance theory posits that the financial system transfers resources across time and space, provides price information, and facilitates resource allocation50. Fintech drives financial innovation and development, thereby enhancing and extending the utility of these financial functions. On the one hand, traditional financial institutions are often unable to accurately assess the accuracy of information submitted by enterprises and the actual utilization of funds when facing enterprises applying for green loans. Fintech can utilize advanced technologies such as big data and cloud computing to accurately mine customer information and the authentic operating status of enterprises, addressing the issue of information asymmetry in financial institutions’ identification of low-carbon assets and the disclosure of carbon asset information51. Fintech helps financial institutions accurately identify green credit applications, preventing enterprises from disguising high-pollution projects as green projects or embellishing environmental information disclosure to meet financing needs52. Thus, promoting the development of GF53. Furthermore by leveraging blockchain technology, fintech ensures the subsequent traceability of green funds, playing the role of ex-post monitoring49, thereby preventing the occurrence of moral hazards. Each node of blockchain technology contains complete transaction information. The transaction data obtained by financial institutions through the hash algorithm is almost impossible to forge. Therefore, financial institutions can dynamically track green loans in the pre-lending, lending, and post-lending phases. Once risks are identified, they can take measures to mitigate them, ensuring the safe development of GF. On the other hand, fintech also helps financial institutions expand service scope and reduce the costs associated with information collection and processing54. Fintech leverages technological tools such as big data and cloud computing to uncover concealed information and address the challenge of missing GF data. It provides convenience and personalized services to the long-tail market, expands participation in GF, and improves accessibility to finance55.

Furthermore, GF can enhance CEE by influencing capital costs and directing the flow of funds. First, GF impacts capital costs, steering businesses and cities toward green transitions, thereby contributing to energy conservation and emission reduction. For instance, green credit policies offer financial support and preferential interest rates to environmentally friendly companies, while imposing credit restrictions and punitive interest rates on high-pollution, high-emission enterprises. This approach encourages these enterprises to undergo a green transformation56, thereby reducing emissions per unit of output and improving CEE. Second, the resource allocation function of green finance guides the flow of funds53, compelling high-energy-consuming enterprises to transition and enhance their CEE. To avoid penalties and seize development opportunities, investors favor sectors with low environmental risk. Consequently, successful companies increasingly incline toward green and environmentally friendly industries when seeking financing, leading to the aggregation of financial resources in low-carbon sectors. This, in turn, compels high-energy-consuming enterprises to transform, thereby improving regional CEE. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2

Fintech can enhance CEE by promoting GF.

Fintech promotes the development of GTI by alleviating the challenges companies face in securing financing for high-risk R&D activities57, thereby enhancing CEE. Bengt Holmstrom (1989) emphasized that GTI demands significant upfront capital investments, involves extended profit cycles, and presents considerable challenges in risk estimation58. Compared to traditional technological innovation, GTI places greater emphasis on environmental and ecological benefits59, adheres to low energy consumption, and involves a higher degree of innovation. It demands more capital for transformative innovations60 and is more dependent on financial and economic conditions. Credit constraints can exacerbate short-sighted behavior in firms, leading them to allocate more resources to short-term production rather than R&D, thereby inhibiting innovation61. Fintech can effectively alleviate financing constraints for green technological innovation, enhancing the capabilities of enterprises in green technology R&D62,63, thereby promoting higher levels of innovation and the dissemination of green technologies. Specifically, relying on deep learning algorithms, fintech can enable financial institutions to quickly and accurately identify the green R&D capabilities and innovation needs of the real economy57. Technologies such as big data can comprehensively collect enterprise information, reduce the threshold of financing in the financial market64, accelerate the financial institutions’ funding of green innovation projects51, and provide financial security for GTI.

Moreover, GTI follows the characteristic of low energy consumption, breaking away from the high-carbon nature of conventional technological innovation. The essence of GTI lies in internalizing external environmental pollution while achieving economic benefits62,65, emphasizing resource conservation and environmental protection66, promoting the utilization and development of clean energy67, which can effectively enhance the efficiency of resource and energy utilization68,69 and is a crucial factor in promoting sustainable development67. GTI effectively promotes the improvement of CEE by reducing the dependence of regional development on energy consumption and lowering the energy intensity and carbon intensity of the production process. GTI not only detects, traces, and controls carbon emissions at the source but also provides technical assistance for the capture, utilization, and storage of CO2. This provides a potential means for end-of-pipe treatment of CO2 emissions70, and gives rise to green technology dividends and spillover effects, ultimately reducing carbon emissions and enhancing CEE. Based on the analysis above, we propose Hypothesis 3:

Hypothesis 3

By promoting GTI, fintech can enhance CEE.

Spatial economics theory posits that economic activities in geographically related areas exhibit spatial autocorrelation, indicating close connections between the economic activities of neighboring regions. The economic and environmental benefits resulting from fintech development in one region can spread to surrounding areas through channels such as investment and technological cooperation. This spillover effect not only fosters economic growth in adjacent areas but also promotes environmental protection, ultimately enhancing CEE. Fintech overcomes the geographical limitations of traditional financial markets, facilitating financing and investment in green projects, thereby generating cross-regional spillover effects71. Through fintech platforms, different regions can share green financial resources and information, engage in joint financing and project cooperation, and form regional mutual aid effects, collectively advancing low-carbon development. The development of fintech drives substantial technological innovation and knowledge accumulation, which effectively spreads between cities through industry conferences, cooperative projects, and employee mobility72. This diffusion of knowledge and technology significantly enhances CEE in neighboring areas. GTI, supported by fintech platforms and their network effects, rapidly expands to broader regions, accelerating the adoption and application of energy-saving and emission-reduction technologies. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes Hypothesis 4:

Hypothesis 4

The impact of fintech on CEE exhibits spatial spillover effects.

We use a spatial weight matrix based on geographic-economic distance. This matrix reflects regional spillover and radiation effects of economic factors and the spatial impact of geographic distance. The elements of the matrix are the product of the inverse of the shortest highway mileage between region i and region j, and the ratio of the annual average per capita GDP of region i to the annual average per capita GDP of all regions.

Variable

Explained variables

The primary methods for measuring efficiency include parametric methods, such as Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA), and non-parametric methods, such as Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA). Compared to parametric methods, non-parametric methods do not require pre-established functional forms or assumptions about a priori conditions. They effectively avoid parameter weighting subjectivity75, offer clear advantages in evaluating the efficiency of decision-making units with multiple inputs and outputs76,77, and are widely used in assessing all-factor CEE78. Additionally, the traditional DEA model only addresses the correspondence problem based on inputs or outputs and ignores radial and angle choices, leading to variable slackness and accuracy issues79. In contrast, the super-efficient Slack-Based Measure (SBM) model accounts for non-desired outputs and corrects slack variables, overcoming the non-comparability issue between effective Decision-Making Units (DMUs) obtained with traditional DEA model measurements. Therefore, this paper uses the super-efficiency SBM model proposed by Tone (2002) to measure CEE within a total factor framework80. When ρ = 1, it indicates that the DMU is efficient, whereas ρ < 1 signifies inefficiency and the potential for improvement.

To measure productivity changes over time, Chung et al. (1997) integrated the directional distance function, which accounts for undesired outputs, into the Malmquist model originally proposed by Sten Malmquist (1953), leading to the development of the Malmquist-Luenberger (ML) index81. This index reduces measurement errors by considering undesired outputs and has been widely used in empirical analysis. However, the ML index still faces challenges related to non-transitivity and the possibility of no solution in linear programming. To address these limitations, Oh and Lee (2010) introduced the Global Malmquist-Luenberger (GML) index, which uses a global reference approach to overcome these issues82. This method enhances the comparability of efficiency calculations over time. Unlike the super-efficiency SBM-DEA model, which only compares decision-making units, the combination of the super-efficiency SBM-DEA model with the GML index allows for tracking changes in CEE over time. This integration enables the analysis of dynamic trends in CEE across various time periods. The formula of GML index is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} GML_{t}^{{t + 1}} (x^{t} ,y^{t} ,z^{t} ,x^{{t + 1}} ,y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ) & = \frac{{1 + D_{0}^{t} (x^{t} ,y^{t} ,z^{t} ;y^{t} ,z^{t} )}}{{1 + D_{0}^{{t + 1}} (x^{{t + 1}} ,y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ;y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} )}} \\ & \quad \; \times \frac{{(1 + D_{0}^{G} (x^{t} ,y^{t} ,z^{t} ;y^{t} ,z^{t} ))/(1 + D_{0}^{t} (x^{t} ,y^{t} ,z^{t} ;y^{t} ,z^{t} ))}}{{(1 + D_{0}^{G} (x^{{t + 1}} ,y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ;y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ))/(1 + D_{0}^{{t + 1}} (x^{{t + 1}} ,y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ;y^{{t + 1}} ,z^{{t + 1}} ))}} \\ & = GEC_{t}^{{t + 1}} \times GTC_{t}^{{t + 1}} \\ \end{aligned}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GEC_{t}^{{t+1}}$$\end{document} measures the degree of convergence of each city’s evolution towards a pre-production state from period t to t + 1, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GTC_{t}^{{t+1}}$$\end{document} measures the dynamic change in the outward expansion of the production possibilities frontier or the degree of technological innovation in each city from period t to t + 1.

In summary, this paper utilizes the super-efficiency SBM-GML model to measure CEE and its decomposition indices. Labor input, energy consumption, and fixed capital stock are considered input indicators, with regional GDP as the desired output and CO2 emissions as the undesired output.

In terms of input indicators, we use the number of employees per unit at the end of the year to represent labor input, and the total energy consumption in each year to describe energy factor input. Specifically, the consumption of natural gas, liquefied petroleum, and electricity for society as a whole is converted into standard coal (10,000 tons) by a specific factor. The capital input is expressed in terms of fixed capital stock estimated by perpetual inventory issuance. Considering the availability of data, this paper chose the year 2000 as the base period for calculation. The capital deposit in the base period is expressed as the total fixed asset investment in 2000 divided by 10%. We use the total fixed asset investment instead of total capital formation data at a city level and use the fixed asset price index of the province where the city is located (previous year = 100) for deflating.

In terms of output indicators, the desired output is expressed as the real GDP of each prefecture-level city, deflated to eliminate price effects, using 2000 as the base period. CO2 emissions are used to represent undesired outputs. Considering that there is no unified calculation standard for carbon emissions at a city level, meaning there is no direct data on energy consumption and no urban carbon emissions inventory, this study draws on Chen et al. (2020) method of combining DMSP/OLS and NPP/VIIRS nighttime light data83. Based on a series of corrections and reclassifications and using the spatial and temporal overlapping areas of the two-image data, we further linearly fit the NPP/VIIRS data to obtain continuous and stable nighttime light data and invert them to estimate the CO2 emissions of the city.

Given that the GML index measures the growth rate of CEE, reflecting changes relative to the previous year, this paper adjusts the GML index as follows: Setting 2007 as the base year, the CEE value for 2007 is fixed at 1. The CEE value for 2008 is then calculated by multiplying the base year value by the GML index for 2008. Similarly, the CEE value for 2009 is obtained by multiplying the 2008 CEE value by the GML index for 2009, and this process continues for subsequent years. This method generates the CEE values for each city across different years.

Core explanatory variables

Refer to Song et al. (2021) to measure the city’s fintech level84. Firstly, search the company name or business scope containing keywords such as “Big Data”, “Cloud Computing”, “IOT”, “Fintech”, etc., in the “Tianyancha” database website, while eliminating samples of companies that have been operating for less than one year or have poor operating conditions. Secondly, delete the fields such as “Above except. business” and “Not allowed to engage in. business” that exist within the business scope. Thirdly, financial-related keywords such as “credit” “payment” and “finance” are vaguely matched with the company’s business scope, with successfully matched samples retained. Finally, we sum up the number of results from the search at the same city level to get the number of fintech enterprises in each city per year, representing the level of fintech development in the specific region.

Mechanism variables

Based on the above theoretical analysis, this paper utilizes the harmonious coupling degree between financial development and green development to positively evaluate the degree of urban GF. Precisely, by referring to the studies of Xie and Hu (2023), GF is measured by utilizing the coupled coordination degree of urban financial development and green development85. The formula is as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{G}}{{\text{F}}_{i,t}}=2 \times \sqrt {\frac{{{\text{FIN}}{{\text{A}}_{i,t}} \times {\text{GRE}}{{\text{E}}_{i,t}}}}{{{{({\text{FIN}}{{\text{A}}_{i,t}}+{\text{GRE}}{{\text{E}}_{i,t}})}^2}}}} \times \frac{1}{2}\left( {{\text{FIN}}{{\text{A}}_{i,t}}+{\text{GRE}}{{\text{E}}_{i,t}}} \right)$$\end{document}

FINA is the standardized financial development, measured by the ratio of loan balances of urban financial institutions to GDP, according to Li and Shen (2019)86. GREE is the standardized green development, estimated the pollutant emission measure per unit of city GDP. The total pollutant quantity is the sum of municipal, industrial wastewater, sulfur dioxide, and dust emissions, and the indicator has been positively treated, referring to the methodology of Liu and Wen (2019)87. The higher the harmonious degree between financial and green development, the higher the growth of GF.

In the mechanism test of how fintech promotes GTI and enhances CEE, this paper refers to Tao et al. (2021) to measure GTI88. It retrieves each city’s number of green patents granted according to the date of the patent application and the green patent code. This data is used to measure the GTI in each city annually from the State Intellectual Property Office (SIPO) patent database.

Controlled variables

Most scholars regard the level of economic development, government intervention, foreign direct investment, human capital level, R&D investment, etc., as essential factors affecting CEE. Therefore, this paper controls for these influencing factors as follows: (1) Economic development level: measured by real GDP per capita, deflated with 2000 as the base period, with the quadratic term of the economic development level added to verify the existence of an environmental Kuznets curve; (2) Industrial structure: measured by the ratio of value added of the secondary sector to GDP; (3) Government intervention: measured by the ratio of the difference between local public finance expenditure and science & technology expenditure to GDP; (4) Economic agglomeration: measured by the actual GDP output per unit land area; (5) Informatization level: measured by the ratio between per capita postal and telecommunications business volume and per capita GDP; (6) Support on innovation: measured as the proportion of sci-tech expenditures to fiscal expenditures; (7) Science and technology support: measured as the ratio of sci-tech expenditures to GDP; (8) Population density: measured as the number of people in a unit of land area, specifically the ratio of resident population to total land area. The population density variable is introduced with a lag of one period due to the apparent dependency characteristics and lag of this variable.

Data

This study examines Chinese prefecture-level and above cities from 2007 to 2019. Significant data gaps for Taiwan, Macau, Hong Kong, Tibet, and Hainan Province, as well as the reclassification of cities like Laiwu, Bijie, Pu’er, Chaohu, Simao, and Tongren under “county-to-district” policies, led to the exclusion of these provinces and cities from the analysis. Additionally, cities such as Baiyin, Haidong, and Karamay were removed due to missing key data. This process resulted in a panel dataset of 256 cities. Relevant data derives from various sources such as the “China Urban Construction Yearbook”, “China Energy Statistical Yearbook”, “China Statistical Yearbook”, “China Metropolitan Yearbook”, the CEI database, “Tianyancha” website. Some missing data are supplemented by interpolation. All monetary indicators are adjusted to constant prices in the year 2000 according to the price index to eliminate the interference of inflation. We use imputation methods to fill in missing data. In addition, the variables have been standardized to eliminate, as much as possible, the interference of high variable skewness and kurtosis, and the influence of the scale. The respective definitions of the variables are shown in Table A1 of the Appendix.

Empirical results and analysis

To test the research hypotheses, this paper uses various estimation methods to empirically analyze the impact of fintech on China’s CEE. Before conducting regression analysis, the paper employs the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to test for multicollinearity among the main explanatory variables. The results indicate that only the variables “Science and technology support” and “Support on innovation” have VIF values exceeding 5, specifically 5.81 and 5.91, respectively. The VIF values for other variables are all below 5, with an average VIF of 3.19, significantly less than the empirical threshold of 10. This effectively controls the impact of multicollinearity in the regression analysis.

Benchmark regression results and analysis

This study uses the stepwise regression method to regress the complete sample data. Furthermore, since the dynamic panel model constructed in this article contains lagged terms of the explanatory variables, the model may have an endogeneity problem. Therefore, the one-step difference generalized method of moments (DIFF-GMM), two-step DIFF-GMM, and two-step system generalized method of moments (SYS-GMM) are used to regress the model, respectively, to ensure the robustness and accuracy of the estimation results.

Table 1 reports the results of the benchmark regression using stepwise regression to examine the impact of fintech on CEE. Columns (1)-(10) estimate model (2) and column (11) estimates model (1). Columns (1)-(9) control for city-fixed effects, while columns (10) and (11) control for both year and city-fixed effects. As shown in Table 1, the estimated coefficients of FINT are all positive at the 1% level when the control variables are added sequentially. This indicates that fintech can effectively improve a city’s CEE, corroborating previous research by Teng and Shen (2023), Wu (2024), and Wang et al. (2024)13,89,90. These studies argue that fintech plays a crucial role in reducing carbon emissions at both corporate and regional levels. Our research findings offer additional evidence that fintech has effectively promoted CEE, thereby validating Hypothesis 1. The estimates of the lagged first-order coefficients of CEE are all statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that CEE is influenced by the efficiency of the previous period and exhibits inertia characteristics.

Table 2 reports the results based on model (2) of the three generalized method of moments (GMM) estimation methods. Columns (1), (2), and (3) show the results for one-step DIFF-GMM, two-step DIFF-GMM, and two-step SYS-GMM, respectively. According to the results in Table 2, the results of AR(1), AR(2), and Hansen’s test indicate that the model is set up reasonably, and its estimation results are accurate. The regression coefficient of the L.CEE is significantly positive, indicating CEE exhibits inertia characteristics. The coefficient of FINT is positive, indicating that fintech significantly affects CEE. A possible reason is that fintech enhances financial efficiency while reducing carbon emissions through digital office practices and the promotion of low-carbon behaviors. Consequently, this improvement in financial efficiency increases urban CEE, further validating Hypothesis 1.

According to the results of control variables in Tables 1 and 2, ED significantly negatively affects CEE. In contrast, the examined coefficient of the square item of the level of economic development (ED_2) is quite positive, which confirms the presence of the environmental Kuznets curve and the “Pollution Shelter Hypothesis”. Initially, due to the scale effect, economic growth will lead to an increase in energy consumption, thus reducing CEE. However, once economic development reaches a threshold, the technological effect will reduce the inputs of the elements per unit of output. The structural effect will reduce emissions, thereby increasing CEE. The coefficients of IS, SI, and lagged one-period population density (L.PD) are significant. The regression coefficients of EA are all positive, while the GI, the IL, and the STS do not pass the significance test.

Endogenous analysis

Referring to Huang et al. (2019), we chose the number of landline telephones per million people in 1984 and the total volume of postal and telecommunication services in 1984 as instrumental variables for fintech91. Specifically, the total telecommunication business in the previous year is multiplied by the number of fixed-line telephones per million people in each city in 1984 and the total postal and telegraphic business in 1984, respectively. The resulting cross-multiplier term is used as an instrumental variable for the current period of the city’s fintech.

After determining the instrumental variables, the 2SLS method is used for regression. Columns (1) and (4) of Table 3 report the regression results, where column (4) includes a lagged one-period term for CEE. The results of the Anderson LM test, CD Wald F test, and Sargan test show that the selected instrumental variables are appropriate, and the obtained estimates are reliable. The regression results indicate that the findings of this paper remain robust after using the instrumental variables approach to address endogeneity issues. The results in Table 3 once again confirm that the findings of this paper are robust.

Robustness test

To guarantee the reliability and robustness of the findings, this study conducts robustness tests using the SYS-GMM method by changing the measurement of the explained variables and explanatory variables, and adjusting the sample size and other dimensions. The results of the robustness tests are reported in Table A2. First, we use the carbon emission intensity measured from the single-factor perspective as a proxy for the CEE in the robustness test, which is the ratio of total urban carbon emissions to GDP. The regression result in column (1) of Table A2 of the Appendix shows that the coefficient of FINT is still significantly positive, and the result remains reliable after replacing the CEE indicator. Second, referring to Li et al., (2020), we re-estimate the level of urban fintech64. Using web crawler technology, we searched 48 “keywords + city” in Baidu News Advanced Search by year and summed up the number of search results from the same city’s perspective to get the total search volume of each city per year. We then logarithmically processed it to obtain the city’s fintech indicator. As shown in column (2) of Table A2, fintech still significantly improves CEE. Third, the CEE is re-measured using the super-efficiency SBM-ML and the fintech calculated by Li et al. (2020) for the robust test64. As shown in column (3) of Table A2, the findings of this paper still hold even if the fintech and CEE measures are replaced simultaneously. Fourth, the sample size is narrowed by excluding municipalities to conduct the analysis. The result is presented in column (4) of Table A2, and the estimated coefficient of FINT is positive, indicating that fintech promotes CEE. Thus far, the results of the four robustness tests show that the findings of this paper are reliable.

Mechanism analysis

The previous results show that fintech can enhance CEE. We further investigate its impact mechanism and whether fintech can positively affect CEE by promoting the development of GF and facilitating GTI. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 4 present the impact of fintech on GF. The estimated coefficients of FINT are significantly positive at the 5% level, indicating that fintech promotes the development of GF, which supports the findings of previous studies. Zhou et al. (2022) argues that fintech provides technological support for green credit, thereby improving the efficiency of capital allocation53. This aligns with expectations, as fintech mitigates information asymmetry between banks and enterprises, enhancing the efficiency of green financial services and improving CEE. Based on the above analysis, it can be concluded that fintech improves CEE by promoting the development of GF, thereby validating Hypothesis 2.

The impact of fintech on GTI is presented in columns (3) and (4) of Table 4. The positive coefficient of FINT indicates that fintech improves GTI, consistent with previous research findings. Liu (2023) found that fintech development increases both the scale and efficiency of firms’ green innovation initiatives63. This enhancement is achieved through two primary pathways: expanding credit availability within cities and increasing financial support from local governments. Our results are consistent with expectations, as fintech broadens financing channels for green projects, alleviating the financing constraints faced by GTI and thereby improving CEE. Consequently, it can be concluded that promoting GTI is one of the mechanisms through which fintech enhances CEE, validating Hypothesis 3.

Spatial spillover analysis

Before analyzing the spatial spillover effects of the impact of fintech on CEE, we use Moran’s I index to determine the existence of spatial autocorrelation between regional CEE. Moran’s I ranges between − 1 and 1. A value greater than 0 indicates high-high or low-low clustering, while a value less than 0 indicates high-low clustering. Table A3 of the Appendix reports the global spatial autocorrelation test results for CEE and fintech. Moran’s I index for CEE is negative from 2007 to 2010, indicating substantial differences in carbon emissions policies and technologies among regions. This led to the coexistence of high-efficiency and low-efficiency areas in close proximity. From 2011 to 2019, the index was positive, suggesting that improvements in CEE had a broader spatial diffusion effect. Enhanced technology diffusion, policy coordination, and regional cooperation contributed to a trend toward uniformity in CEE levels. Although Moran’s I index is not significant in 2010 and 2011, the significant spatial autocorrelation in the other years sufficiently indicates that CEE are generally spatially correlated. The reason for this result may be that 2010 and 2011 were critical transition periods from negative to positive spatial autocorrelation for CEE. During this time, policy and technological adjustments were underway in different regions, leading to instability and fluctuations in the spatial pattern, thus reducing significance. Specifically, at the Cancun Climate Change Conference in 2010 and the Durban Conference in 2011, China committed to increasing its emission reduction efforts and actively participating in international climate cooperation. Additionally, the development of clean energy in China began to show effects in 2010 and 2011. However, due to differences in regional resource endowments and levels of policy support, the pace of clean energy development varied across regions. During this period, China strengthened its environmental protection efforts, enacting regulations to close highly polluting enterprises and implementing stricter emission standards. Due to different implementation levels and speeds across regions, some areas rapidly improved their CEE, while others adjusted more slowly due to economic and technological constraints. This variability also contributed to changes in spatial autocorrelation. These factors had different degrees of impact and occurred at different times across regions, leading to inconsistencies and fluctuations in the spatial distribution of CEE in 2010 and 2011. As a result, these years exhibited non-significant spatial autocorrelation.

Before conducting the regression analysis, the LM test is used to determine whether there is a spatial error or lag term. Then, the LR and Wald tests are used to determine whether the SDM will degrade to a SAR or SEM model. Furthermore, the Hausman test is required for spatial panel data to determine whether to use a random or fixed effects model. Based on the results in Table A4, we finally choose the spatiotemporal fixed effects SDM model and dynamic SDM model to estimate the spatial spillover effect of fintech on CEE.

Furthermore, since the coefficients of the SDM model cannot directly reflect the impact of fintech on CEE, the total spatial effect is decomposed into indirect and direct effects using the partial differentiation method proposed by LeSage and Pace (2008)92. In addition, fintech-driven spatial spillovers account for more than 50% of the total effect, further confirming the critical contribution of spatial spillover effects from interregional flows of fintech to CEE. A possible reason is that regional governments and financial institutions, through fintech platforms, can better coordinate the formulation and implementation of green finance policies, promoting interregional green finance cooperation. Additionally, advanced green technologies and projects can be implemented in one region and rapidly disseminated to other regions via fintech platforms. This creates significant spatial spillover effects, playing an important role in enhancing overall CEE, thereby validating Hypothesis 4.

The coefficient of L.WCEE is significantly positive, indicating that the CEE exhibits persistence and lagged nature in both temporal and spatial dimensions. The CEE of neighboring cities in the previous period significantly influenced the contemporaneous CEE of the local area positively. If the CEE of neighboring cities in the preceding period is positive, the current local CEE will experience further enhancement, creating a virtuous cycle. The spatiotemporal decomposition results indicate that the short-term and long-term direct, indirect, and total effects of fintech are significantly positive, indicating that fintech exerts a positive promotional influence on both local and neighboring cities’ CEE. This illustrates that with more advanced technology and balanced economic development in the long run, fintech’s impact on CEE in local and neighboring cities or regions gradually diminishes. A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that in the long run, the externalities from fintech, which are based on technological innovations, become smaller. Thus, the long-term effect is smaller than the short-term effect.

Heterogeneity tests

Considering the significant differences in geographic location, economic development, and resource endowment of each city, which may lead to discrepancies in the CEE enhancement effect of fintech, we analyze the heterogeneity in terms of city-region location, city rating, and city resource endowment to adhere to the research purpose of this paper. The specific estimation results are shown in Table A5 of the Appendix.

Regional location

Due to the vastness of China’s land area, there are considerable discrepancies in economic and social development between regions, inevitably resulting in significant disparities in human capital, economic growth, and technological capabilities. We categorize the sample cities into three groups: eastern, central, and western regions, based on the provinces in which the cities are located. The regression results in Columns (1)-(6) of Table A5 show significant regional differences in the impact of fintech on CEE. The coefficient of fintech in the eastern region is significantly positive, while the coefficients in the central and western areas are insignificant, indicating noticeable regional differences in the role of fintech on CEE. Compared with the west and central regions, the eastern region has more abundant market resources, better infrastructure, a favorable location advantage, a better talent development environment, and a higher level of human capital, providing a solid foundation for the development of fintech.

City rating

We divide the sample cities into two groups according to the “Ranking of Chinese Cities’ Business Attractiveness 2022 Research”: one group includes first-tier and emerging first-tier cities, and the other group consists of other cities. The regression results are reported in columns (7)-(10) of Table A5. The regression coefficient of FINT is significantly positive in the group of emerging first-tier and first-tier cities but fails to pass the significance test in the other group. This indicates that the positive impact of fintech on CEE is reflected in the emerging first-tier and first-tier cities. Relative to other cities, first-tier and emerging first-tier cities are more mature in terms of innovation vitality, business resource aggregation, infrastructure construction, and financial resource coverage. Fintech companies are more inclined to develop in higher-tier cities, leading to a more significant contribution of fintech to CEE.

Resource endowment

According to the National Sustainable Development Plan for Resource-based Cities (2013–2020), we separate the sample cities into resource-based and non-resource-based groups. Columns (11)-(14) of Table A5 present the regression results, showing that the estimated coefficients of FINT are significantly positive in non-resource-based cities, while the group of resource-based cities fails to pass the significance test. This indicates that the positive impact of fintech on CEE is mainly reflected in non-resource-based cities. Resource-based cities are more dependent on natural resources, and over-reliance on natural resources crowds out investment in human capital and technological innovation. This leads to the loss of high-quality talent and a decline in the vitality of R&D and innovation. Moreover, the industrial structure of resource-based cities is relatively more homogeneous. Fintech has higher requirements for underlying technical support, and the development of fintech in resource-based cities has been slower, leading to less effective CEE improvements.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study offers new insights into how Fintech, as a novel financial model integrating digital technologies, can be more effective than traditional finance in enhancing CEE, thereby catalyzing the green, low-carbon, and circular development of the financial services economy. We analyze data from 256 prefecture-level and higher cities in China between 2007 and 2019, employing dynamic panel models and dynamic SDM models. Through rigorous estimation and evaluation, we assess the impact of fintech development on enhancing CEE in cities. The research reveals the following key points: (1) Fintech significantly enhances CEE. The study confirms that fintech plays a crucial role in improving CEE, with this conclusion holding robust across various measures of fintech and CEE, and highly statistically significant. This suggests that governments should encourage fintech innovation to achieve energy-saving, emission-reduction, and sustainable growth goals. (2) Fintech promotes CEE through GF and GTI. Mechanism tests indicate that GF and GTI are the primary channels through which fintech impacts CEE. (3) Fintech’s impact on CEE exhibits significant positive spatial spillover effects. The local development of fintech positively influences the CEE of neighboring regions, although this impact tends to weaken over the long term. Our findings support regional cooperation but highlight the need for sustained efforts to maintain fintech’s long-term positive influence on CEE. (4) Fintech’s impact on CEE shows significant regional heterogeneity. The impact is more pronounced in the eastern regions, first-tier and new first-tier cities, and non-resource-based cities, suggesting that economically developed areas are better positioned to leverage fintech for improving CEE.

In contrast to previous extensive research, this study introduces a novel framework for assessing fintech’s carbon reduction effects, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms through which fintech influences CEE. In contrast to the static model analyses used by Cheng et al. (2023) and Teng and Shen (2023)13,41, we employed dynamic models to explore this issue, finding that fintech’s spatial spillover effects on CEE are more pronounced in the short term than in the long term. We provide a more comprehensive explanation for this phenomenon. By constructing dynamic panel models and dynamic SDM models, our research deepens the understanding of the fintech-CEE relationship, offering a more nuanced comprehension of fintech’s carbon reduction effects. Additionally, by incorporating green finance and green technological innovation as mediating variables, this study broadens the research perspective on the fintech-CEE relationship, further enhancing the understanding of their interaction mechanisms.

Building on the main findings, we propose the following four policy recommendations: (1) Strengthen the fintech ecosystem. Given the overall positive impact of fintech on CEE, the government should expedite the deployment of digital infrastructure—such as big data, AI, cloud computing, and blockchain—to establish a strong foundation for fintech’s comprehensive development. Encouraging major banks to collaborate with fintech firms in developing and sharing fintech service platforms to enhance the capabilities of small and medium-sized banks. Moreover, establishing fintech industrial parks in key cities and special economic zones, supported by tax incentives and policy measures, can attract fintech enterprises and cultivate a thriving fintech ecosystem. (2) Promote the integration of fintech with green finance and green technological innovation. Given that green finance and green technological innovation are key channels through which fintech enhances CEE, financial institutions should harness big data and AI technologies to improve the precision and reach of green investments. The government should establish a “Green Fintech Innovation Center” to foster collaboration between fintech companies and green technology enterprises, spurring the creation of innovative financial products. Creating a dedicated green finance fund to meet the specific financing needs of sustainable projects. Promoting the adoption of blockchain and smart contracts to further increase transparency and efficiency. Additionally, implementing a “green rating” system and developing a comprehensive risk assessment database for green projects to empower financial institutions to make more informed decisions when evaluating and investing in green initiatives. (3) Provide Long-term Innovation Support. This study demonstrates that fintech exerts significant positive spatial spillover effects on CEE, with short-term impacts being more pronounced than long-term effects. To counter the slowing diffusion rate of fintech and diminishing marginal effects over time, the government should establish a dedicated long-term R&D fund. The “Green Fintech Innovation Fund” would support continuous research and development, encouraging ongoing innovation in green fintech. These funds should prioritize high-impact projects, particularly in critical areas such as carbon emission monitoring, AI applications, and green financial product development. (4) Promote the coordinated development of fintech between regions. Given the heterogeneity in improving carbon efficiency through fintech across different cities and regions, cities should tailor their fintech infrastructure and supporting service systems based on their specific economic conditions and development needs. Eastern regions, first-tier and new first-tier cities, and non-resource-based cities should focus on advancing high-end fintech innovation. In contrast, central and western regions, lower-tier cities, and resource-intensive cities should prioritize strengthening basic fintech infrastructure. The government could establish a “National Fintech Cooperation Alliance,” regularly hosting fintech innovation forums and exchange activities to promote coordinated development across regions.

While this study offers valuable insights into the relationship between fintech and CEE, it has certain limitations. First, the analysis primarily focuses on the direct pathways through which fintech influences CEE, without considering the moderating effects of other factors, such as policy environments and market mechanisms. Future research should incorporate these moderating variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the fintech-CEE relationship. Second, the temporal and spatial scope of this study is limited to data from Chinese prefecture-level cities between 2007 and 2019. Future research could expand the sample to include county-level data and extend the temporal coverage for a more in-depth analysis. Furthermore, future studies should incorporate multidimensional fintech indicators and utilize more refined methods for calculating CEE to enhance the robustness and accuracy of the findings.
