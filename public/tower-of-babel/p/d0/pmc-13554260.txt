Associations of extreme temperature and relative humidity with hematological disease mortality in Chuzhou, China
Authors: Xunhua Li, Ming Shao, Ming Han, Jingjing Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to quantify the associations between extreme temperatures, relative humidity, and mortality risk in patients with hematological diseases within a subtropical urban environment, and to identify vulnerable demographic subgroups. We conducted a time-series analysis using data from Chuzhou, China, spanning 2019 to 2024. Daily mortality counts for hematological diseases (ICD-10 codes C82-C95), meteorological variables (daily mean temperature and relative humidity), and air pollution data were analyzed. A Poisson generalized linear model combined with a distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) was applied to assess nonlinear and delayed effects, with adjustments for long-term trends, day of the week, and air pollutants. Stratified analyses were performed by age and gender. A total of 1,651 hematological disease deaths were included. A U-shaped relationship was observed between daily mean temperature and mortality, with minimum risk at approximately 22 °C. Extreme heat exhibited a delayed but prolonged effect, with risk emerging after 7 days and persisting. In contrast, extreme cold triggered a more immediate response, with significant risk appearing at lag 5 days. An optimal relative humidity threshold was identified at 76%. Both high and low humidity increased mortality risk, with low humidity showing an earlier effect (lag 2 days) than high humidity (lag 5 days). Stratified analyses revealed males and younger individuals (< 65 years) were particularly vulnerable to these environmental stressors. Extreme temperatures and abnormal humidity levels are significant environmental risk factors for mortality in hematological disease patients, with distinct temporal patterns and demographic variations. These findings underscore the need to incorporate this vulnerable population into climate‑adaptive public health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61435-4.

Introduction

Hematological disease (HD), encompassing leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, along with non-malignant disorders such as aplastic anemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, represent a significant and growing global health burden1,2. Collectively, they account for approximately 6–8% of all new cancer diagnoses worldwide, with incidence rates exhibiting a concerning upward trend, particularly in aging populations3. Despite considerable advances in targeted therapies and supportive care, mortality associated with hematological diseases remains substantial, contributing to a disproportionate share of cancer-related years of life lost4. The epidemiological landscape is further complicated by the unique vulnerability of this patient population. Driven by the dual burden of inherent hematologic dysfunction and intensive therapy-induced myelosuppression, these patients exhibit profoundly compromised homeostasis, rendering them highly susceptible to severe infectious, thrombotic, and systemic organ complications5,6. This intrinsic frailty suggests they may constitute a critical, yet understudied, subgroup at extreme risk from environmental stressors, potentially making non-infectious external factors, such as meteorological extremes, significant modifiers of their survival outcomes.

In recent years, the greenhouse effect has been accumulating as a result of increased carbon emissions, which has led to a gradual rise in the global average temperature. Climate change has also led to frequent weather extremes, such as heat waves and cold snaps. These extreme events also pose a serious threat to human health. The potential link between climate variables and hematological mortality is anchored in a robust pathophysiological rationale. Thermoregulation is a metabolically demanding process that imposes systemic stress. For patients with compromised hematopoiesis and cardiovascular reserve, this stress can be destabilizing. Extreme cold is known to induce vasoconstriction, increase blood pressure, and elevate plasma fibrinogen concentration and blood viscosity, creating a pro-thrombotic state7. Given that patients with hematological malignancies already bear a 4- to 7-fold increased intrinsic risk of thrombosis, this cold-induced pro-thrombotic state represents a particularly critical synergistic hazard8. Conversely, extreme heat triggers a cascade of responses including hemodynamic alterations, dehydration, and a systemic inflammatory state, which can exacerbate pre-existing cytopenias, induce electrolyte imbalances, and precipitate cardiovascular events9,10. Critically, relative humidity modulates the physiological impact of temperature. Elevated humidity inhibits evaporative cooling, amplifying heat stress, whereas reduced humidity compromises the respiratory mucosal barrier, elevating infection risk in neutropenic patients11,12. Despite these compelling mechanisms, epidemiological evidence remains conspicuously scarce. Notably, within China, research on the climate-health relationship remains underdeveloped for regions with distinctive geographic and climatic profiles, particularly in ecologically sensitive and densely populated zones like the Yangtze River Basin.

The hematology patient population, which is large, clinically complex, and potentially exquisitely sensitive to thermal stress, represents a priority group for targeted adaptation strategies. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by conducting a detailed time-series analysis to quantify the associations between extreme temperatures, relative humidity, and mortality attributable to hematological diseases in a representative urban setting of China. Our findings are expected to provide crucial evidence to refine early warning systems, inform clinical guidelines for patient management during extreme weather events, and ultimately contribute to reducing the environmental disease burden in this highly susceptible population.

Materials and methods

Study area

Chuzhou is a key prefecture-level city in Anhui Province, situated in eastern China (31°51′–33°13′ N, 117°09′–119°13′ E) (Figure S1). Covering an area of 13,518 square kilometers, it lies within a subtropical monsoon climate zone and serves as an important component of the Nanjing Metropolitan Circle in the Yangtze River Delta region. As of 2024, the city’s permanent population reached approximately 4.08 million, with an urbanization rate of 62.5% and notable interregional migration inflows. Nevertheless, the rapid pace of urbanization and industrial expansion has significantly exacerbated environmental pressures, leading to changes in the trends of major air pollutants over time. As a result, air quality has emerged as a growing public health priority in recent years (Figure S2).

Data collection

All mortality data (including demographic variables such as gender and age) registered at the Chuzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention were collected from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2024. According to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) for newly diagnosed diseases, the number of deaths from hematological disease (C82 ~ C95) per day was counted. Additionally, the meteorological data and air pollutant data from Chuzhou Environmental Monitoring Center and China Meteorological Data Network (http://data.cma.cn/) from January 1, 2019 to December 31, respectively. The meteorological data include the daily mean temperature (DMT, °C), relative humidity (RH, %). And the atmospheric pollutant data include the average daily concentration of PM2.5, PM10, SO2 and NO2(Figure S2). Meteorological data were derived from China’s national monitoring network, which, under standards GB/T 35,221 − 2017 and GB/T 35,237 − 2017, integrates multi-station observations to generate regionally representative estimates.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted on daily deaths from HD, meteorological conditions (DMT, RH) and air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, SO2, and NO2), respectively. And spearman rank correlation analysis was used to test the correlation between meteorological factors. Given that daily HD mortality represents a low-frequency outcome, a quasi-Poisson distribution was adopted as the link function. A distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) embedded within a Poisson generalized linear model (PGLM) framework was applied to estimate the lagged and non-linear effects of DMT and RH on daily HD mortality13,14. This approach allowed for the quantification of exposure-response relationships while adjusting for potential confounders including long-term trends, day of the week, and holidays. Model fit and appropriate degrees of freedom were selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and residual diagnostics15. In addition, the stability of the model was affected by the multicollinearity between meteorological factors and atmospheric pollutants. Consequently, variables with a relevant values of less-than 0.7 was added to the model16.

Extreme exposure levels were defined as the 5th percentile (representing low extremes) and the 95th percentile (representing high extremes) of DMT and RH, with the optimal threshold set as the reference value. This approach aligns with standard practice in environmental health studies for characterizing extreme meteorological conditions. The cumulative lag effects of low and high temperature and humidity on hematological disease mortality were examined accordingly. Results are reported as cumulative relative risk (RR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To flexibly capture the nonlinear exposure-response relationships and delayed effects, we constructed cross-basis matrices for daily mean temperature and relative humidity using the crossbasis function from the DLNM package. For each meteorological variable, the exposure-response relationship was modeled with a natural cubic spline (fun = “ns”) using 3 degrees of freedom (df), with internal knots placed at equally spaced quantiles of the variable distribution. This choice of df was guided by prior studies and the AIC, balancing model fit and parsimony while avoiding overfitting. For the lag-response dimension, we specified a maximum lag of 14 days based on existing evidence that temperature-related health effects can persist over multiple weeks17,18. The lag structure was also modeled using a natural cubic spline (fun = “ns”) with 3 df, with knots placed at equally spaced values in the log scale of the lag space to allow more flexibility at shorter lags where effects are expected to change more rapidly. This specification enables the model to capture both immediate and delayed effects while maintaining sufficient smoothness. Stratified analyses were conducted by age group (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years) and gender (male and female) to evaluate potential differences in susceptibility to daily temperature exposure.

Ethics approval

This study utilized aggregated population-level data, including demographic variables such as age and gender, with no inclusion of personally identifiable information. Informed consent was obtained by the respective healthcare institutions during the original data collection process. Ethical approval for this study was exempted by Chuzhou City Vocational College, which also provided institutional support for the research. All procedures involving human participants complied with the ethical standards of the institutional/national research committee, the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments, and comparable ethical standards.

Software used

All statistical analyses were performed by R software (version 4.3.1,www.r-project.org). In addition, “DLNM” and “spline” packages were used to fit the model. Geographical data were sourced from the “geojsonio” and “sf” packages, while all visualizations in this study were generated using the “ggplot2” package. All statistical tests were two-tailed, P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive summary

Table 1 presents the distribution of hematological disease deaths, major ambient pollutants, and meteorological factors in Chuzhou from 2019 to 2024 (2192 days in total). A total of 1,651 deaths from hematological diseases were recorded, corresponding to a daily average of 0.45 deaths. The daily mortality rate was higher among males (0.26 deaths) than females (0.20 deaths), and among the elderly (≥ 65 years, 0.25 deaths) than younger individuals (< 65 years, 0.20 deaths). Demographically, males accounted for 56.71% of all deaths, while the elderly constituted 55.09%. The mean daily temperature was 17.28 °C (range: −5.50 °C to 34.83 °C) and the mean relative humidity was 76.18% (range: 36.75% to 100.00%). The mean daily concentrations of air pollutants were as follows: PM₂.₅, 36.77 µg/m³ (range: 8.00–161.00 µg/m³); PM₁₀, 60.95 µg/m³ (range: 13.00–418.00 µg/m³); SO₂, 8.01 µg/m³ (range: 3.00–20.00 µg/m³); NO₂, 27.69 µg/m³ (range: 3.00–112.00 µg/m³). Data on primary pollutant designation were not available for the study period.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between key meteorological factors and air pollutant concentrations (Figure S3). DMT showed statistically significant positive correlations with PM2.5, PM10, SO2, and NO2 (all P < 0.001); however, none of the correlation coefficients exceeded 0.70, indicating weak to moderate associations. In contrast, DMT was negatively correlated with RH (r = -0.63, P < 0.001). Consistent with prior environmental studies, PM10 and PM2.5 exhibited a strong positive correlation (r = 0.90, P < 0.001). To avoid multicollinearity in subsequent regression models, only PM10 was retained as a covariate in further analyses.

Lagged effects of DMT on HD mortality

The RR results of HD mortality with the lag time of DMT are shown in Fig. 1. In this study, we observed that the RR of DMT on HD mortality was U-shaped curve, and temperature 22 °C was the lowest risk of death. The risk effect of the 95th percentile of DMT (30.89 °C) on hematological disease mortality did not emerge on the same day of exposure but was observed at a lag of 7 days (lag 7, RR = 1.039, 95% CI: 1.012–1.066; Fig. 2A), and persisted through lag 13. The cumulative effect peaked at a lag interval of 0–13 days (RR = 1.576, 95% CI: 1.071–2.320; Fig. 2B). In contrast, the 5th percentile of DMT (2.46 °C) exhibited an earlier risk signal, also appearing at lag 5 (RR = 1.033, 95% CI: 1.005–1.061; Fig. 2C). The cumulative effect associated with cold exposure increased gradually, becoming statistically significant from lag 9 onward (Fig. 2D).

Lagged effects of RH on HD mortality

The optimal RH threshold was identified as 76% (Figure S3). Similar to the pattern observed for DMT, exposure to the 95th percentile of RH (98.02%) did not show an immediate mortality effect. Instead, a delayed risk emerged at a lag of 5 days (lag 5, RR = 1.046, 95% CI: 1.012–1.082; Fig. 2E), and the cumulative effect began to appear from day 9 and continued to strengthen thereafter (Fig. 2F). In contrast, under extremely low humidity conditions (55.09%), a significant risk was detected earlier, at a lag of 2 days (lag 2, RR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.001–1.005; Fig. 2G), and the cumulative effect became evident from lag 3 onward (Fig. 2H).

Attributable burden

During the 6-year study period (2019–2024), a total of 1,651 deaths from hematological diseases were recorded. Approximately 3.0% of these deaths, corresponding to 49 deaths (average 8 deaths annually), were attributable to extreme temperatures. Extreme heat (above the 95th percentile, > 30.89 °C) contributed 1.6% (n = 27) of deaths, while extreme cold (below the 5th percentile, < 2.46 °C) accounted for 1.3% (n = 22). For relative humidity, extreme conditions were responsible for 1.8% of deaths (30 deaths, average 5 deaths annually), with extreme high humidity (> 98.02%) contributing 1.1% (n = 18) and extreme low humidity (< 55.09%) contributing 0.7% (n = 12).

Stratified analysis

In the stratified analysis, the effects of both the 95th and 5th percentiles of DMT on HD mortality remained significant among males, younger individuals, and the elderly (Figs. 3A and 4). However, no significant association was observed for females under high-temperature conditions, whereas a significant effect was identified under low-temperature conditions (Fig. 3B). Regarding humidity, no significant effect of high-humidity conditions was observed on HD mortality in males, while the effect of low humidity was consistent with the overall findings (Fig. 5A). Notably, the pattern was reversed among females (Fig. 5B). Humidity appeared to have no significant effect on the elderly, whereas younger individuals were notably affected under both high- and low-humidity conditions (Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analysis

To examine whether the observed associations were confounded by air pollutants, we performed stepwise adjustment for pollutants in the models, and the results remained robust (Figure S4-S5). When assessing the effects across different lag periods (14 days, 21 days and 28days), the overall trend remained consistent. However, the presence of lagged effects at various time intervals indicates that extreme humidity and temperature may exert long-term influences (Figure S6-S7). The optimal apparent temperature (AT) threshold was identified as 26 °C. Neither high (39.6 °C) nor low (-1.6 °C) apparent temperature showed immediate mortality effects; the risks began to appear at lag 6 and lag 5, respectively (Figure S8). Furthermore, we assessed the robustness of our findings by varying the degrees of freedom in the spline functions and by testing more extreme percentiles of temperature and humidity. The results remained consistent across these alternative specifications, further supporting the validity of our main conclusions (Figure S9-S12).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide robust epidemiological evidence that both extreme temperatures and deviations in relative humidity are significant environmental risk factors for mortality from hematological diseases in a subtropical urban setting. The observed U-shaped relationship between daily mean temperature and mortality, with the minimum risk at approximately 22 °C, aligns with the classical temperature-mortality associations documented for cardiovascular and respiratory diseases19,20. This reinforces the concept that the human body operates within a thermoneutral zone, beyond which compensatory physiological mechanisms become strained.

Importantly, our analysis reveals distinct lag patterns. Extreme heat exhibited a delayed but prolonged effect, emerging after a week and lasting up to two weeks, whereas extreme cold triggered a more immediate risk response. This temporal dissociation likely reflects different underlying pathophysiological pathways. Heat stress may precipitate a cascade of systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress over days, culminating in fatal outcomes in patients with compromised hematopoiesis and immune surveillance21,22. The delayed effect aligns with the time required for thermal stress to induce inflammatory cytokine release, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and alter blood rheology, potentially exacerbating existing cytopenias or triggering thrombotic events in susceptible individuals23,24. In contrast, the prothrombotic and vasoconstrictive effects of cold, mediated through sympathetic activation, increased platelet aggregation, and elevated fibrinogen levels, can rapidly destabilize patients with pre-existing hematologic vulnerabilities, leading to earlier adverse events25. Notably, the finding that the 5th percentile of DMT induced risk earlier than the 95th percentile may reflect differences in physiological adaptation. Acute cold exposure imposes immediate cardiovascular demand, while heat-related pathophysiology often involves cumulative multi-organ injury.

A novel contribution of this study is the identification of an optimal relative humidity threshold (~ 76%) and the quantification of risk at both extremes. The effects of high humidity are biologically plausible through several interconnected pathways. Primarily, it impedes evaporative cooling, which intensifies cardiovascular strain under thermal stress. This strain, alongside hemoconcentration induced by fluid loss, further contributes to an increased risk of hypercoagulability26,27. Conversely, low humidity compromises the integrity of the respiratory mucosa, impairs mucociliary clearance, and heightens susceptibility to respiratory pathogens. This poses a critical concern for neutropenic patients and individuals with impaired humoral immunity28. Our observed lag patterns further support this mechanistic distinction: low humidity exhibited an earlier risk effect (lag 2), consistent with the rapid compromise of mucosal barriers and subsequent infection risk. In contrast, the effects of high humidity manifested later (lag 5), which may reflect the cumulative impact of thermoregulatory challenge and progressive cardiovascular strain.

The stratified analyses revealed significant demographic heterogeneities in susceptibility, which merit mechanistic interpretation. Males demonstrated heightened vulnerability to both heat and cold extremes, potentially attributable to androgen-mediated differences in thermoregulatory physiology, variations in body surface area-to-mass ratio, or an elevated baseline inflammatory state29,30. In contrast, females exhibited a distinct pattern: while they showed marked susceptibility to cold, no significant heat effect was observed. This discrepancy may involve the vasodilatory properties of estrogen and its modulatory role in endothelial function31. The pronounced effect of humidity on younger individuals, but not the elderly, may indicate that younger hematology patients rely more on intact mucociliary and immune defenses, whereas the elderly experience competing mortality risks from cardiopulmonary comorbidities. The reversed gender pattern in humidity susceptibility further suggests sex-specific differences in mucosal immunity or respiratory physiology that merit targeted investigation32.

The quantification of the attributable burden reveals that approximately 3.0% (49 deaths) and 1.8% (30 deaths) of total hematological disease deaths were attributable to extreme temperature and humidity, respectively. These findings highlight that although the relative risks are modest, the attributable burden is not negligible and warrants consideration in public health planning for this vulnerable population. This study is subject to several limitations that should guide the design and focus of future investigations. First, the ecological nature of the study design restricts causal inference at the individual level and precludes adjustment for potentially significant confounders, including specific therapeutic protocols (e.g., chemotherapy timing, immunosuppressive dosage), genomic susceptibility markers, and individual behavioral adaptations. Second, exposure assessment relied on meteorological data from a single integrated source. Although China’s national monitoring network has provided spatially representative measurements since 2018, some exposure misclassification is inevitable. Third, the single-city scope constrains the external validity of our findings. Climate-mortality associations are known to vary across regions due to differences in latitude, urban heat island intensity, population acclimatization, and healthcare infrastructure. Future research should aim to integrate individual-level clinical data with high-resolution, personally relevant exposure measurements across diverse geographical and climatic contexts.

The rise in global surface temperatures is an established fact. Accordingly, further research is needed to examine the relationship between diverse climatic conditions and populations across different regions, so as to jointly address the health impacts of climate change.

Conclusions

This study establishes a clear association between extreme temperature and humidity conditions and increased mortality risk in patients with hematological diseases. While heat exposure manifests through delayed yet prolonged health impacts, cold triggers more immediate adverse effects. Our analyses further identify males and younger patients as particularly vulnerable subgroups.
