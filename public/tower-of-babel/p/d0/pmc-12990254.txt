Introduction

With every step of development in the technological era, microwave technology is bound to accompany us. In various fields such as industry (Albuquerque et al., 2021), medicine (Tan et al., 2023), communication (Idrus et al., 2022), and military (Majcher et al., 2020) that are involved in our lives, microwave technology plays a crucial role. However, the proposition that microwave radiation may cause harm to health has been confirmed by numerous studies (Lai et al., 2021) (Singh et al., 2020) (Magiera and Solecka, 2019). Therefore, in addition to the application of microwave technology, more and more research is focused on the assessment of its damage to multiple systems (Lin et al., 2021) (Li et al., 2023) (Yin et al., 2023). The heart, as one of the organs that are sensitive to microwave radiation, numerous studies have shown that its structure and function will be damaged after being exposed to radiation. (Longevity, 2023). Moreover, due to its special discharge and energy storage forms, systematic research on the damage effect and possible mechanisms of cardiac injury is not only a difficulty in this field but also a bottleneck restricting its sustainable development research.Table 1..

It is well known that the cardiac electrical signal is rich, and electrophysiology is the most important manifestation of its function. Regarding the heart itself, after microwave radiation, the evaluation of the beating frequency and conduction direction of myocardial cells can directly reflect the changes in its electrophysiological function (Neyroud et al., 2024) (Niu et al., 2023). In addition, every beat of the heart cannot do without energy supply. Mitochondria, as its most important energy supply station, plays a crucial role. Generally, mitochondria convert chemical energy through the oxidative phosphorylation process into ATP, which is the main energy supplier for myocardial cells, meeting the energy requirements for the continuous and high-intensity contraction activities of myocardial cells. Moreover, mitochondria can control the flow of intracellular Ca2+, releasing Ca2+ and other signal molecules to participate in the signal transmission process between myocardial cells, thereby regulating the contraction and relaxation activities of myocardial cells (Focusing on mitochondrial form and function, 2018).

Given the crucial roles of mitochondria and Ca2+ in the heart (Lahiri et al., 2021), it is worthy of in-depth exploration whether there is a mechanism related to them that plays a role in microwave radiation-induced cardiac electrophysiological damage. Therefore, we focused our attention on the related research centered on autophagy, hoping to investigate whether the energy release disorder and Ca2+ content fluctuations caused by microwave radiation can serve as triggering factors for autophagy, and whether the fluctuations in autophagy levels can disturb the process of cardiac signal transmission.

Based on the above background, this study selected human cardiomyocytes as the biological model. This design significantly enhanced the transfer value of the research results to practical applications, and provided key reference basis for practitioners in the field of electromagnetic radiation protection. In terms of research innovation, in current studies on microwave radiation-induced cardiac damage, research focusing on the autophagy mechanism is still scarce (Hao et al., 2022) (Zhang et al., 2022) (Zhang et al., 2025), and reports specifically involving electrophysiological functions are even rarer. Therefore, this study systematically explored whether microwave radiation induces changes in autophagy levels in multipotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs), and deeply analyzed the correlation between these changes and electrophysiological function damage, aiming to reveal the specific mechanism of autophagy in the electrical dysfunction of iPSC-CMs caused by microwave radiation.

Materials and methods

Cell source and culture

The iPSC-CMs cells used in this study were purchased from Help Therapeutics Co. These are terminally differentiated ready-to-use highly purified cardiomyocytes, mainly composed of ventricular myocyte-like cells with autonomous electrophysiological activity, along with a small amount of atrial muscle and sinoatrial node-like cells. The cells we received were cryopreserved cells, which were stored in liquid nitrogen for future use. The supporting medium, resuscitation solution and culture medium used in the experiment were all provided by Help Therapeutics Co.

Cell resuscitation method: First, added the myocardial cell seeding medium (F00201) to the cell culture dish to ensure that the medium evenly covered the bottom of the dish. Incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Took the frozen cells from the liquid nitrogen storage tank and transferred cells to the water bath at 37℃. Submerged the lower part of the frozen tube in the water bath and gently shoook it for 1–––2 min until only a pea-sized ice block remained in the tube. Opened the tube cover in the clean bench, and transferred the cell suspension to a 50 mL centrifuge tube. Gently rinsed the frozen tube with 1 mL of culture medium(F00301), drip by drip and gently shook the centrifuge tube. The culture medium was pre-added with 1% double antibiotics(Gibco, 15140122) and 10% FBS(Gibco, 10099141). Used 1 mL of culture medium(F00301) to gently rinse the frozen tube, drip by drip and gently shook the centrifuge tube, adding 1 mL of culture medium for each drip and shaking 5 times. Used an electric pipette gun combined with a 10 mL pipette to add the remaining 9 mL of culture medium. The first 4 mL was added at a frequency of shaking 5 times per drop, and the last 5 mL was added at a slightly faster speed, either every 2 drops or every 3 drops. Centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at room temperature, then discarded the supernatant. Resuspended the cells in 1 mL of resuscitation medium(F00901), gently stirred until no visible cell clumps remained. Then added the remaining 4 mL of resuscitation medium(F00901) and gently stirred to mix evenly.

After counting using a cell counter (Countstar, China), cells were inoculated at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells into 35 mm cell culture dishes (Corning, USA) in a cross-pattern to ensure uniform cell distribution. The cell inoculation density for differentiation identification and high-resolution optical mapping technology was 3 × 105 cells to a 15 mm inner diameter laser confocal culture dish (NEST, China); for immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry, it was 3 × 104 cells to an 8 mm diameter cell slide (Solarbio, China); for scanning electron microscopy preparation, it was 1.5 × 106 cells to a 35 mm cell culture dish. They were placed in a cell culture box at 37℃ and 5% CO2 for cultivation. After 48 h of cultivation, the resuscitation culture medium was replaced with the cell culture medium, and the medium was changed every 2 d.

Cell identification

A cell differentiation kit (Thermo Fisher, USA,A25973) was used for immunofluorescence staining of iPSC-CMs cultured for 5 d to verify differentiation. Briefly, culture medium was aspirated, and fixation and permeabilization agents were sequentially added, each incubated for 15 min at room temperature, followed by blocking solution for 30 min. Primary antibodies (Nkx2-5 for cardiac mesoderm, TNNT2 for cardiomyocytes,1:400) were diluted in blocking buffer, added, and incubated at room temperature for 3 h. After washing, appropriate secondary antibodies were added and cells incubated at room temperature for 1 h(1:400). DAPI (NucBlue®, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used for nuclear staining. Observation under laser confocal microscopy was performed (Nkx2.5: red, TNNT2: green, DAPI: blue).

For sarcomere, Cx43, PKP-2, and ZO-1 staining, α-actinin (Thermo Fisher, A7811), Rabbit polyclonal to Connexin 43 (Abcam, ab235585), PKP-2 Monoclonal Antibody (Thermo Fisher, PA5-102795), and ZO-1 Polyclonal Antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-19090) were used for immunofluorescence after 5 d of recovery. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beijing Kainuo, China) and permeabilization (Shanghai Beyotime, China), cells were blocked with 3% BSA (Solarbio, China), incubated with primary (1:400) and then secondary (1:400) antibodies in 3% BSA at 4°C overnight and at room temperature for 1 h, respectively. DAPI-containing mounting medium (Vector Labs, USA) was used. Imaging of sarcomeres (red), Cx43 (green), PKP-2 (green), ZO-1 (red), and DAPI (blue) was performed using 450, 488, and 594 nm channels.

Exposure and grouping

The drugs used in this study were AICAR (Acadesine, MCE, HY-13417). The drugs were first dissolved in ultra-pure water and then diluted to 10 mM. Subsequently, The drugs were diluted to 1 mM and 0.5 mM using the culture medium for use. The drugs were administered 24 h before irradiation to ensure their full effect.

Cells were divided into four groups: control (C), S-band radiation (R), drug control (AC), and S-band radiation plus drug (AR). The C group received no treatment; the R group was exposed to S-band microwave radiation; the AC group received drug treatment only; the AR group received both radiation and drug treatment.

Exposure was performed using a laboratory-constructed S-band microwave radiation source: frequency 2.856 GHz(communication and military affairs), average power density 30 mW/cm2, single exposure duration 30 min, once. Dishes were placed centrally under the radiation source on a circular irradiation platform, which rotated steadily during exposure (see Fig. 1).

S-band microwave radiation source schematic diagram.

Flow cytometry

Culture medium was aspirated, and cells washed thrice with DPBS (without CaCl2 and MgCl2, 3 min each), then digested at 37°C for 5 min. After 80% cell detachment, suspensions were collected and digestion terminated with triple-volume neutralization solution (cell culture medium + 10% FBS). Cells were pelleted (1,640 rpm, 5 min), washed and resuspended in 1 mL DPBS, and counted to adjust density to 1 × 105cells/mL. LIVE/DEAD™ fluorescent dye solution (1 μL, Invitrogen, L34970) was added, incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, washed, resuspended in 1 mL DPBS with 1% BSA, and analyzed it within 1 h using a flow cytometer(BeamCyte-1026). Set the excitation light at 488 nm, collected the fluorescence signal through the FITC channel (with a 530/30 nm filter), and analyzed the ratio of live cells (negatively stained) to dead cells (positively stained) using the FlowJo (v10.0.7) software. This method is used to assess cytotoxicity/death rate.

High-resolution optical mapping

At 6 h post-radiation, cells were washed, and 2 mL calcium staining solution was added, incubated at 37°C for 25 min in the dark. After washing, cardioplegic solution was added and incubated for 15 min. Data were collected at 35 ± 2°C using the real-time fluorescence mapping system (region: 100 × 100 pixels, 500 Hz, exposure 1.8 ms, gain: sensitive, single illumination ≤ 10 s). Signals were analyzed using OMapRecord and OMapScope software. The solution was prepared as shown in the table below.

Transmission electron microscopy

At 6 h post-radiation, cells were collected using a soft silicone spatula, pelleted (10,000 rpm, 10 min), then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (2 h), post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (1 h), dehydrated in graded ethanol, transitioned in acetone, and embedded in Epon812 resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were stained with lead and uranium, and images acquired with a Hitachi transmission electron microscope. Five fields per group were analyzed for mitochondrial quantification using ImageJ.

Mitochondrial function

ATP Detection (MCE, HY-K0314-100 T): Removed the culture medium, added 60 μL of lysis buffer to each well to lyse the cells. Used a pipette to agitate the lysis buffer to ensure complete cell lysis. Centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min, and took the supernatant. Added 100 μL of ATP detection working solution to the detection wells. Incubated at room temperature for 3–5 min to consume the background ATP. Set up the detection conditions on the multi-functional microplate reader. Added 10 μL of sample or standard to the detection wells and shook for 5 s to mix. Immediately measured the RLU using the multi-functional microplate reader. Standard curve drawing: Plotted the standard curve with ATP concentration as the x-axis and RLU as the y-axis. Calculated the ATP concentration in the sample based on the standard curve.

Mitochondrial membrane potential JC-1 fluorescence detection (MCE, HY-15534): Removed the cell culture medium, washed the cells once with DPBS, added 2 mL of JC-1 staining solution with a concentration of 10 μg/mL, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After the incubation, washed the cells twice with DPBS and then added 2 mL of DPBS, which was used for observation.

Transcriptomics

First, after radiation, the cell RNA was extracted and fragmented into fragments of approximately 300 base pairs (bp) in length. Using the RNA as a template, a first strand cDNA was synthesized using 6-base random primers and reverse transcriptase. Then, the second strand cDNA was synthesized using the first strand cDNA as a template. After the library construction was completed, PCR amplification was used to enrich the library fragments. The library was quality-checked using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The total concentration and effective concentration of the library were then detected. Based on the effective concentration of the library and the required data volume, libraries with different Index sequences were mixed in proportion. The mixed libraries were uniformly diluted to 2 nM. Through denaturation, single-stranded libraries were formed. After the samples were extracted, purified, and sequenced, these libraries were sequenced in both ends using the second-generation sequencing technology based on the Illumina HiSeq sequencing platform.

Filtered the original off-machine data, and aligned the high-quality sequences obtained after filtering to the reference genome of the species. Based on the comparison results, calculated the expression level of each gene. Further conducted expression difference analysis, enrichment analysis and clustering analysis on the samples.

Immunofluorescence

At 6 h post-radiation, LC3B (Abcam, USA) and SQSTM1 (p62, Abcam, USA) expression and localization were assessed by immunofluorescence. After removal from medium and transfer to 12-well plates, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min), permeabilized (15 min), blocked in 3% BSA (1 h), and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: LC3B (1:400), p62 (1:200) in 3% BSA. Secondary antibody incubation (1:400, Goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody, Alexa 488 conjugate, Abcam, USA) was performed for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Slides were mounted using DAPI-containing medium. Imaging was performed at 450 nm and 488 nm, and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ to calculate LC3B and p62 average fluorescence intensity.

Western blot

At 6 h post-radiation, LC3II/I and p62 expression was assessed in iPSC-CMs.

(1) Protein Preparation

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS (×3), lysed in RIPA buffer (Help, China) containing protease inhibitors (100:1) for 10 min, and lysates homogenized, collected, and pelleted (12,500 rpm, 4°C, 15 min). Supernatant was mixed with 5 × loading buffer (1:4), denatured at 95°C for 15 min, cooled, and briefly centrifuged.

(2) SDS-PAGE

Precast SDS-PAGE gels were assembled, buffer added, wells loaded with markers(ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd,19001,10–180 kDa) and proteins, and resolved at 80 V initially, then 120 V until completion.

(3) Transfer and Immunoblotting

PVDF(polyvinylidene difluoride) membranes (activated in methanol, Millipore, USA) and gels were assembled in transfer cassettes, and proteins transferred at 200 mA constant current, 150 min on ice. Blocking was done in 5% BSA; membranes were washed and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies LC3B (1:2000), p62 (1:2000), and GAPDH (1:10,000, all Abcam, USA). After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using ECL substrate and analyzed with ImageJ.

All the experiments in this study were conducted with 3 independent tests, and each experiment included 3–6 samples. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Results

Identification of iPSC-CMs Differentiation

iPSC-CMs, as a cell model that closely resembles the beating rhythm of the human heart, has unique advantages in electrophysiology that are unmatched by other cell lines or primary rat cardiomyocytes (Thomas et al., 2022). The specific manifestation is that iPSC-CMs are entirely derived from humans and carry the complete human genome. Therefore, they can more realistically reflect the responses of human cardiomyocytes to pathological stimuli or drugs, significantly enhancing the clinical relevance and translational potential of research findings, and avoiding misinterpretation due to species differences. The iPSC-CMs model, as an advanced in vitro cell model, is a typical non-animal alternative method. It can simulate the physiological and pathological processes of the human heart without using experimental animals, providing a reliable platform for this research (Bourque et al., 2023). The application of human iPSC-CMs models perfectly embodies the 3R principle, replacing animal experiments with non-animal models. By using the iPSC-CMs model, we can completely avoid the use of experimental animals in many research fields, thereby reducing the number of animals used and their suffering. Moreover, the standardization and repeatability of the iPSC-CMs model make the experimental design more optimized, enabling more efficient acquisition of accurate and reliable experimental data and improving the quality of research. In this study, the cell types and differentiation status of iPSC-CMs were first identified. Based on the previous experience of this research (unpublished data produced by team colleagues), iPSC-CMs that had been resuscitated for 7 d were selected as the subsequent experimental model. Comprehensive research was conducted on the structural proteins of iPSC-CMs.

As shown in Fig. 3A, 5d after cell resuscitation, immunofluorescence staining was performed on the myocardial cell markers TNNT2 and Nkx2.5. TNNT2 showed green fluorescence and was stably highly expressed; Nkx2.5 showed red fluorescence and had a large number of positive cells. In mature myocardial tissue, TNNT2, as a structural protein, is a key component of the contraction unit of cardiac muscle cells and thus maintains a consistently high level of expression. On the other hand, Nkx2.5, as a key transcription factor for early heart development, its expression level gradually decreases as the cardiac muscle cells mature. The expression of Nkx2.5 in mature myocardium is significantly lower than that in the early differentiation stage (Cao et al., 2023). iPSC-CMs were mostly centered around cells with strong beating, showing a clear clustering trend, and the myofilaments were arranged neatly. These results indicate that 5 d after iPSC-CMs resuscitation, the overall cell state was good, conforming to the characteristic phenotypes of mature myocardial cells.

This study focused on the changes in autophagy and electrophysiological functions of iPSC-CMs after microwave radiation. Since the density of the connections between adjacent cells is the basis for the successful transmission of electrical signals, the following experiments selected the key structural connection proteins in the intercalated discs of cardiomyocytes for observation. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cardiomyocytes 5 d after resuscitation. As shown in Fig. 3B, alpha-actinin, a key structural protein of the sarcomere, displayed neatly arranged myofilaments with clear Z-lines. As shown in Fig. 3C, Cx43, which is reported to be the main protein component of the gap junction in cardiomyocytes, exhibited a punctate membranous pattern (Xinxin et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3D, PKP-2, which plays a key role in the assembly of cell connections such as desmosomes, presented a smooth, continuous, and clearly defined membranous boundary (Godsel et al., 2010). The above results indicate that iPSC-CMs have an overall good structural and functional state, meeting the requirements of subsequent experiments in this study.

Electrophysiological changes in iPSC-CMs after microwave radiation

During the generation of the action potential in myocardial cells, the 0-phase Na + influx causes depolarization of the cell membrane, thereby activating Cav1.2, allowing Ca2+ to enter the cell and activating the ryanodine receptor of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This induces calcium release, resulting in a large amount of Ca2+ entering the cytoplasm from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Subsequently, the Ca2+-ATPase in the sarcoplasmic reticulum recycles Ca2+ back to the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This entire process is known as a calcium transient. The activation time of Ca2+ conduction refers to the time required for the calcium transient to reach its maximum ascending speed, which is the duration of the Ca2+ release process (Pereira et al., 2023). APD30/80 can reflect the heterogeneity of the repolarization process. An increase in the APD30/80 ratio indicates delayed late repolarization of myocardial cells (Gintant et al., 2020). Therefore, by analyzing the time, speed, absolute heterogeneity of Ca2+ conduction, and the APD30/80 ratio, the changes in Ca2+ release in iPSC-CMs after microwave radiation can be clearly determined. CaTD90, which is the 90% duration of the calcium transient, refers to the time required for the intracellular calcium ion concentration to recover from its peak to 90% of its resting level or baseline level to evaluate the cell's ability to release and recycle Ca2+. The peak amplitude of the calcium transient represents the maximum concentration of Ca2+ entering the cell. In summary, based on the high sensitivity of iPSC-CMs to electromagnetic radiation, in this part of the experiment, we introduced two time points of 3 h and 6 h after radiation to detect electrophysiological changes, in order to clarify the impact of radiation on the early and mid-stage Ca2+ conduction rate and provide data support for subsequent experimental design.

We injected calcium ion fluorescent dyes into the cardiac cells and used a high-speed camera to record the fluorescence changes, which reflected the alterations in intracellular calcium ion concentration and allowed us to synchronously record the calcium signal dynamics. As shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 3, 3h after microwave radiation, the activation time of intracellular Ca2+ conduction significantly decreased (p = 0.03, Fig. A, G), the conduction rate of Ca2+ significantly increased (p < 0.001, Fig. B, H), the absolute heterogeneity of Ca2+ conduction increased (p = 0.008, Fig. J), and the APD30/80 ratio was disordered (p = 0.004, Fig. C, I); 6 h after radiation, the activation time of intracellular Ca2+ conduction significantly decreased (p = 0.003, Fig. A, G), the conduction rate of Ca2+ significantly increased (p < 0.001, Fig. B, H), and the APD30/80 ratio was severely disordered (p < 0.001, Fig. C, I). The calcium transient time course (p = 0.04, p < 0.001, Fig. D, K) and amplitude (p = 0.03, p < 0.001, Fig. E, L) of iPSC-CMs significantly decreased at 3 h and 6 h after radiation, and obvious differences could be observed in the waveform plots (Fig. F).

The above results indicate that the electrophysiological function of iPSC-CMs is a sensitive functional indicator after microwave radiation. Through the study of the intracellular Ca2+ conduction process, it was found that the activation time significantly decreased, which led to an increase in conduction speed and ultimately manifested as the heterogeneity of electrical signals. When the myocardial cells were stimulated, electrical stimulation was generated and propagated within the cells, causing calcium ions to be released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the cytoplasm, thereby triggering muscle contraction. The waveform diagram can visually illustrate this phenomenon, and the difference between depolarization and repolarization is very significant. Therefore, we believe that the changes in the electrophysiological function of iPSC-CMs are the most important phenotype of the radiation damage effect. Based on the changes in intracellular electrophysiological function, we chose the observation time point of 6 h after radiation as the most obvious change for the subsequent experiments.

Ultrastructure and mitochondrial injury in iPSC-CMs

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to assess iPSC-CMs ultrastructure. At 6 h post-exposure, control group cells displayed clear, intact intercalated discs, distinctive Z-lines, abundant mitochondria, and occasional vacuolization or swelling. In the radiation group, discs appeared blurred, large numbers of mitochondria showed vacuolation, swelling, and cristae loss (Fig. 5A–F). Quantification in 30 μm2 images showed a decreased total number of mitochondria (p < 0.001, Fig. 5G), increased mitophagy (p < 0.001, Fig. 5H), and increased abnormal mitochondria (p < 0.001, Fig. 5I) compared with controls.

Normal mitochondria synthesize ATP through the oxidative phosphorylation process, providing most of the energy for myocardial contraction, electrical signal conduction, and ion balance. The structural integrity and functional stability of mitochondria directly determine the pumping efficiency of the heart. Excessive autophagic mitochondria and cristae-deficient mitochondria can directly lead to insufficient ATP supply, causing disruptions in signal transmission, dysfunction or death of cardiac muscle cells (Focusing on mitochondrial form and function, 2018). Given these findings, we focused on mitochondrial function. ATP content significantly decreased 6 h after radiation (p = 0.006, Fig. 5J). JC-1 staining showed increased green fluorescence at 6 h, indicating decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5K).

During structural observations, we captured feature images of mitochondrial damage. Considering their abundance and roles in energy transfer and autophagy, we aimed to delineate the full spectrum of mitochondrial ultrastructural injury. At 6 h post-radiation (Fig. 6A–D), the radiation group exhibited vacuolation, cristae loss, and formation of mitophagosomes. These forms of damage suggest severity—vacuolation and cristae depletion, especially, indicate significant structural and functional disruption.

The dynamic electron microscopy technique captured the progression of mitochondrial autophagy, confirming that it consists of four key stages: (i) the formation of an isolation membrane around the mitochondria (Fig. 6E), where the red markings represent the initial form of the isolation membrane; (ii) the involvement of lysosomes (Fig. 6F), with the green box indicating the lysosomes; the lysosomes and the isolation membrane together enclose the damaged mitochondria; (iii) the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes (Fig. 6G), with the blue part representing the autophagosome; it approaches the lysosomes; (iv) the digestion and renewal of mitochondria (Fig. 6H), with the orange part representing the digested mitochondria. Mitochondria is a dynamic process. What we captured through the electron microscope is only a part of it. The lysosomes digest the substances within the autophagosomes of mitochondria, and the digested mitochondria also present different appearances (Fig. 6I-L), and each mitochondrion may have multiple autophagosomes, which helps to accelerate the clearance of damaged mitochondria (Fig. 6M−P).

As SEM is considered the gold standard for autophagy imaging, our data objectively demonstrate dynamic mitophagy in iPSC-CMs post-microwave exposure. These findings make validation of autophagic changes in subsequent experiments essential.

Observation further revealed myelin-like concentric circles—evidence for advanced autophagy-mediated degradation of organelles, in line with previous reports (Chakraborty et al., 2020) (Ajoolabady et al., 2022). Therefore, it is likely that microwave radiation elevates autophagy in iPSC-CMs, to be validated below.

Critical role of ULK1 after microwave radiation

Integrated analysis of differential gene expression (transcriptomics) and autophagy gene sets indicated significant overlap in differentially expressed autophagy genes (Fig. 7A), suggesting marked microwave effects on the autophagy transcriptome of iPSC-CMs. Functional enrichment highlighted significant associations with nuclear fragment microautophagy, autophagy, and related processes (Fig. 7B). Network and key gene analyses identified ATG4C, ATG4B, ATG4D, ATG9B, ATG2A, ATG101, ATG5, and ULK1 as central regulatory hubs post-radiation (Fig. 7C–D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that autophagy genes are enriched in multiple disease and signaling pathways, implying that microwaves may affect physiological and pathological states via modulation of autophagy-related genes (Fig. 7E–G).

In summary, this joint transcriptome–autophagy analysis verifies both the upregulation of autophagy after radiation and its pivotal role in cellular adaptation and injury, and identifies potential key regulatory genes and pathways for further mechanistic investigation.

Autophagy level is significantly increased in iPSC-CMs after microwave radiation

LC3 is a widely accepted marker of autophagy (Xu et al., 2024) (Jarocki et al., 2024). LC3B is the predominant isoform expressed in cardiomyocytes, participating critically in autophagosome membrane formation and autophagy activation. LC3B elevation reflects upregulated autophagy. p62/SQSTM1 acts as an autophagy cargo receptor targeted for lysosomal degradation; thus, reduced p62 indicates enhanced autophagy flux.

At 6 h post-radiation, LC3B immunofluorescence in iPSC-CMs (Fig. 8A, C) was markedly elevated in the radiation group compared to controls (p < 0.001). p62 levels, by contrast, were significantly decreased (p = 0.002, Fig. 8B, D). Western blot revealed increased LC3II/I (p = 0.001) and decreased p62 (p = 0.02) in irradiated cells (Fig. 8E–G), consistent with immunofluorescence results. These findings demonstrate that autophagy levels are significantly elevated after microwave radiation—supporting a key mechanistic link between increased autophagy and electrophysiological dysfunction in iPSC-CMs.

Effect of AICAR on autophagy level in iPSC-CMs after microwave radiation

AICAR is widely used in the field of regulating myocardial cell energy metabolism and autophagy balance (Viana et al., 2008). On one hand, it activates AMPK to inhibit the mTORC1 pathway, removing the negative regulation on autophagy and inducing the formation of autophagosomes. In energy stress conditions, the activation of AMPK may directly promote autophagy to clear damaged organelles. On the other hand, AICAR inhibits autophagy through mechanisms independent of AMPK activity. This AMPK-independent inhibitory effect mainly occurs by directly interfering with the core protein complex at the initiation stage of autophagy, thereby blocking the formation of autophagosomes (Zong et al., 2019). Autophagy is a metabolic process by which cells degrade and recycle their own components, and AICAR can interfere with this process, reducing the formation and degradation of autophagosomes. Based on the above research and the mechanism of AICAR's action, this study intends to select AICAR as an agent to regulate autophagy levels, hoping to explore the role of autophagy in the electrical physiological function damage of iPSC-CMs caused by microwave radiation.

To eliminate the adverse effects of AICAR on cells, based on the differences in cell membrane permeability, flow cytometry will be used to detect the fluorescence intensity of live/dead cells. Since the active dye can penetrate the damaged cell membrane of dead cells and react with free amines inside and on the cell surface to produce strong fluorescence. While live cells react with the dye on the cell surface to produce relatively weak fluorescence (Rehman et al., 2019 Dec). Therefore, based on the literature research, we selected two different concentrations of AICAR (Kothari et al., 2022), expecting to detect its effect on iPSC-CMs through flow cytometry, determining the drug concentration of action while excluding its effects on cells.

As shown in Fig. 9A-B, compared with the C group, after treating the cells with 0.5 mM AICAR, the activity of iPSC-CMs did not show significant changes, while after treating the cells with 1 mM AICAR, the mortality rate of iPSC-CMs significantly increased (p = 0.035). Therefore, we chose 0.5 mM AICAR as the drug concentration for subsequent intervention.

At 6 h after microwave radiation, as shown in Fig. 9C and E, compared with the C group, the expression level of LC3B in the R group significantly increased (p < 0.001); compared with the R group, after giving AICAR, the LC3B level in the AR group significantly decreased (p < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 9D and F, compared with the C group, the expression level of p62 in the R group significantly decreased (p = 0.005); compared with the R group, after giving AICAR, the p62 level in the AR group significantly increased (p < 0.001). These results indicate that AICAR significantly reduced the autophagy level in the irradiation group, suggesting that reducing the autophagy level may be a new mechanism for improving the damage of iPSC-CMs caused by microwave radiation. Next, we verified this conjecture through electrophysiological verification.

AICAR ameliorates microwave-induced electrophysiological dysfunction in iPSC-CMs

As shown in Fig. 10, at 6 h post-exposure, the APD30/80 ratio in the R group was significantly lower than in C (p = 0.004). After AICAR treatment, AR group APD30/80 recovered to control levels (p = 0.01). For CaTD90 and calcium transient amplitude, both were markedly reduced in R compared to C (p = 0.01 and p = 0.008, respectively); post-AICAR, AR group showed significant increases versus R or AC (p = 0.003, p < 0.001). These results demonstrate that inhibition of autophagy by AICAR ameliorates microwave-induced electrophysiological abnormalities in iPSC-CMs, suggesting that autophagic pathways may contribute to the electrophysiological defects induced by microwave exposure.

Discussion

The heart, as the circulatory power organ of the living body, is also one of the sensitive target organs to microwave radiation. In recent years, more and more evidence from related research in this field has shown that microwave radiation can affect the structure and function of the heart (Elliott et al., 2023). However, after analyzing the mechanism, it was found that there is no clear injury mechanism.

Autophagy has been clarified as being important for the life body (Carnovale et al., 2023). How to effectively distinguish its dual-edged sword effect is the core issue in this field. Effective intervention based on excessive and insufficient autophagy is an important means to solve its effect (Chen et al., 2020).

Based on this, this study systematically explored the effect of microwave radiation on the electrophysiological function of iPSC-CMs and its internal mechanism. In the selection of biological models, human-derived cardiomyocytes were chosen as the biological model of the research topic, which has significant advantages in cardiac electrophysiological function and future application in the human body. Our results demonstrate that microwave radiation causes severe electrophysiological disorders in iPSC-CMs, characterized by abnormal calcium transient conduction and increased heterogeneity of action potential repolarization. Further research revealed that this functional damage is closely related to the abnormal increase in the level of cell autophagy induced by radiation, and inhibiting autophagy through drug means can effectively alleviate the electrophysiological abnormalities, suggesting that targeting the autophagy pathway may be a potential strategy to deal with myocardial toxicity caused by microwave radiation.

Our early research in this group found that microwave radiation can cause autophagy in cardiomyocytes (Yin et al., 2023). Specifically, typical autophagic process structures such as myelin-like structures in mitochondria and a large number of aggregated lysosomes appeared. Therefore, based on these evidences, we designed this study systematically. We expected to clarify the effect of microwave radiation on cardiomyocyte damage and focus on exploring the evidence related to autophagy and clarifying its role in it. We planned to systematically observe the various stages of autophagy, capture characteristic autophagy electron microscope photos to clarify the expression change pattern of autophagy after microwave radiation, and based on this, give intervention, focusing on observing the changes in the electrophysiological function of cardiomyocytes after intervention. Finally, we hoped to clarify the role of autophagy in the electrophysiological damage of cardiomyocytes caused by microwave radiation.

This experiment first carried out research on the effect of microwave radiation on the damage of cardiomyocytes. For the functional study, we mainly used the mappinglab cursor detection system (Kofron et al., 2021) (Mills, ,2025) to detect the speed of Ca2+ transmission between cardiomyocytes and the speed of intracellular Ca2+ changes to observe the condition of cardiomyocytes damaged by microwave radiation. We focused on observing the relevant indicators of calcium ion concentration changes during the calcium transient process, such as the activation time of Ca2+ conduction, the rate of Ca2+ conduction, the absolute heterogeneity of Ca2+ conduction, and APD30/80. These indicators all showed significant changes after microwave radiation, again verifying that electrophysiological function is one of the sensitive indicators of myocardial cell damage caused by microwave radiation.

In the observation of structure, this study used a transmission electron microscope system to observe the changes in ultrastructure of iPSC-CMs caused by radiation. The intercalated discs and mitochondria are the most damaged ultrastructural components. A large amount of evidence of mitochondrial damage has been obtained. Different ultrastructural changes represent different degrees of damage. Through literature research, it is known that mitochondrial damage is an important condition for the occurrence of autophagy (Ogura et al., 2023), and the gold standard for measuring autophagy − electron microscopy − is the most important evidence to determine the structural basis of autophagy occurrence (Shi et al., 2025). Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, we focused on collecting evidence related to autophagy. Fortunately, electron microscopy revealed that microwave radiation induced the accumulation of numerous lysosomes and autophagosomes in myocardial cells, with lysosomes and myelin-like structures distributed throughout mitochondria. Notably, images captured the degradation of damaged mitochondria into particulate fragments during late-stage autophagy, releasing energy and proteins. These ultrastructural findings, encompassing both structural changes and dynamic processes, confirmed the occurrence of autophagy in myocardial cells post-radiation. Further validation was achieved by quantifying autophagy markers LC3B and p62, which demonstrated a significant increase in autophagic activity following microwave exposure.

Based on the above experimental results, we believe that the damage model established in this study was successful. The increase in autophagy level in myocardial cells is accompanied by abnormal electrophysiological functions. Therefore, conducting research on the autophagy damage mechanism from a molecular biology perspective will be an important means to explain the role of autophagy. Transcriptomics analysis suggests that ULK1 is a key node in the autophagy regulatory network after radiation. We selected AICAR as an autophagy regulator and found that it can effectively reverse the increase in autophagy level caused by radiation at a safe concentration, significantly improving the damaged electrophysiological functions.

Through a combination of direct experimental evidences, this study suggests that modulating autophagy levels may alleviate electrophysiological dysfunction in iPSC-CMs induced by microwave radiation, highlighting the potential regulatory role of autophagy in this process. However, although this study has confirmed the association between autophagy and the abnormal electrophysiological functions of iPSC-CMs caused by microwave radiation through a series of experiments, the complex interaction mechanism between autophagy and electrophysiological functions has not yet been fully clarified. The Mapping Lab cursor detection system can be combined with other advanced technologies, such as proteomics technology, to deeply explore the specific molecular mechanisms by which autophagy affects electrophysiological functions from multiple levels of protein and cell functions.

In conclusion, the evidence of electromagnetic radiation-induced damage to iPSC-CMs in this study is objective and direct. However, to fully clarify its injury mechanism, it is still necessary to combine multi-dimensional detection methods and intervention means for regulation in different biological models. Currently, the selection of biological models in this field is extremely limited, especially due to the exclusion of metal conductivity limitations in real-time biological effect studies. These all greatly restrict the development of the discipline of electromagnetic biological effects! Only by replicating the clear effects of electromagnetic damage can we explore the mechanism of damage and protection, and we will continue to adhere to this research direction, hoping to conduct more in-depth exploration of the mechanism on the basis of clarifying the foundation of the effect!
