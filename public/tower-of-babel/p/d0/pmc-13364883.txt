Impact of severe varus deformity on the outcome of mobile-bearing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty: a comparison of patients with varus deformity of less than and more than 15 degrees using matched-pair analysis
Authors: Arun B. Mullaji, Anand Kumar Gupta, George K. George, Rufiat Kazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe outcome of patients undergoing UKA with larger preoperative deformity (> 15º varus) and lesser deformity is sparsely compared in the literature. This matched-pair retrospective study attempts to fill this research gap by comparing the effect of preoperative and postoperative residual varus on outcome.MethodsPatients undergoing UKA with preoperative varus deformity > 15º (HKA > 15º group) were selected and matched for age and gender with those with < 15º of preoperative varus deformity (HKA < 15º group). There were 72 age and gender-matched pairs knees. HKA was measured and Forgotten Joint Score, OKS, VAS and WOMAC scores were obtained at a minimum follow up of 5 years. Postoperative residual varus was classified into 3 groups based on postoperative HKA: varus < 3º, varus 3 to 7º and varus > 7º.ResultsMean correction of deformity was significantly more (p < 0.001) in the HKA > 15º group (10.5º) when compared to HKA < 15º group (4.5º). There was no significant difference in outcome measures OKS (p = 0.21), FJS (p = 0.35) and VAS (p = 0.6) between patients in the two groups. There was no significant correlation between the preoperative and postoperative HKA of the patient with postoperative OKS, FJS and VAS in the two groups. There was no statistically significant difference between FJS (p = 0.73) of the three postoperative varus groups. Excellent to good OKS scores were obtained in 98% & 96% of patients in HKA > 15° and HKA < 15° groups respectively.ConclusionUKA performed in selected patients with large preoperative deformities (> 15º varus) fulfilling Oxford criteria can give satisfactory outcomes in the short to medium term comparable with those with lesser deformity. There was no association between postoperative residual varus and patient reported outcome scores.

Introduction

The standard indications for unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) performed for anteromedial osteoarthritis are knees exhibiting < 15º varus, with an intact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and preserved cartilage in the lateral and patellofemoral compartments. The optimum limits of both the preoperative deformity (< 15º varus) and postoperative alignment (0 to 7º varus) have been stated as important determinants for survival of the implant [1–5].

The question whether mobile-bearing UKA can be successfully deployed to address larger varus deformities (> 15º) has not been addressed. With larger deformities (> 15º), ACL may be incompetent [6, 7]. Additionally, in knees with significant varus deformity, performing UKA may result in substantial residual varus with 14% of knees in 7 or more degrees of postoperative varus after an Oxford mobile-bearing UKA [8]. However, the functional outcomes of performing UKA on deformed knees with greater than 15º of varus, and specifically those which exhibit considerable postoperative residual varus, remain unknown. The series reported by Kleebad et al. included only 8 cases of robotically-assisted fixed-bearing UKAs with > 15º varus [9]. Seng et al. included 15 cases with greater than 15º of varus for which fixed-bearing UKAs were implanted and soft-tissue releases performed to correct deformity [10]. In the series reported by Itou et al. [11], the 36 cases that were classified as ‘severe’ deformity had a mean varus deformity of 8.5º ± 2.9º. The mean value of preoperative deformity in another study of the most severely deformed group was 12.5º ± 1.3º [12].

This study seeks to address this knowledge gap by comparing the outcomes of UKA in patients with large preoperative deformities exceeding 15 degrees of varus to those with lesser deformity. No releases were performed; no specific postoperative alignment was targeted; osteophytes were meticulously resected and medial soft-tissues appropriately tensioned. The aims of the study were: (1) to compare the amount of deformity correction and outcomes between the two groups; (2) to analyse the relation between preoperative deformity and postoperative residual varus with postoperative outcomes in the two groups; and (3) to compare the proportion of patients with satisfactory outcomes in the two groups. The null hypothesis was that UKA performed for large deformities results in poorer outcomes compared to those performed in patients with lesser deformities. The secondary hypothesis was that patients with higher postoperative residual varus exhibit poorer outcomes.

Methods

This was a retrospective study obtained from the records of patients who underwent UKA from January 2016 to December 2020. All patients undergoing UKA had an intact ACL on preoperative MRI scans and the integrity was assessed after arthrotomy by stressing the ACL with a Mixter forceps. All patients were operated upon by the senior surgeon (ABM). We selected UKA patients with preoperative varus Hip knee ankle angle (HKA) more than 15 degrees (HKA > 15º group) and these were matched for age and gender with UKAs done in patients with less than 15 degrees of preoperative varus HKA angle (HKA < 15º group). This was done using the case-control matching command in Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 25.0, IBM Corporation, USA) for MS Windows. Patients were followed up for a minimum of 5 year. There were 72 age and gender matched pair knees. The demographic details of the patients have been summarised in Table 1.

Surgical technique

The technique recommended by the Oxford group was followed. All patients received Oxford phase 3 UKA implants (Biomet, Bridgend, UK). The thigh was supported in a thigh holder and surgery was performed in the “hanging-leg” position. A quadriceps-sparing approach was used. No soft tissue releases were performed. After excision of medial femoral and tibial osteophytes and those in the intercondylar notch, tibial resection was first performed, and flexion gap assessed. The tibia was resected using an extramedullary jig. The amount of tibial resection was dependant on the deformity and amount of wear. Less resection was performed for higher deformity or lesser MPTA and vice versa. After this, the posterior femoral condylar resection was performed. An equal extension gap was created by graduated milling of the distal femur. Cemented components were implanted after trialling.

Radiographic evaluation

A standardized protocol was employed to obtain full-length hip-to-ankle radiographs, ensuring the patellae were oriented anteriorly and both feet were positioned for equal distribution of weight. These radiographs were used to measure the mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA), mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) as depicted in Fig. 1. The difference between preoperative and postoperative HKA (ΔHKA) was also calculated.

Functional scores

The preoperative Oxford Knee Score (OKS), visual analogue score (VAS) and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores were noted from the record. The postoperative outcome was assessed using Forgotten Joint Score (FJS), OKS, VAS and WOMAC scores. The scores obtained at the last follow-up during outpatient visit (mean 7.2 years with range 5 to 9 years) were noted. None of the patients had undergone revisions or reoperations at the last follow-up.

Data analysis

Data were entered in an Excel spreadsheet version 16.66.1 (Microsoft, Corp., Redmond, Washington, USA). Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 25.0, IBM Corporation, USA) for MS Windows.

Shapiro Wilk test of normality was conducted to determine whether the data was normally distributed. The results indicated that data for all the 4 QOL measures (OKS, WOMAC, VAS and FJS) was not normally distributed (P < 0.001). For HKA measures, postoperative HKA and ΔHKA were normally distributed in both groups, whereas preoperative HKA was not normally distributed in the HKA > 15º group. Correlations between HKA parameters and postoperative PROMs were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The postoperative residual varus was classified into 3 groups based on postoperative HKA: Group 1 (varus < 3o), Group 2 (varus 3 to 7o) and Group 3 (varus > 7o). Kruskal Wallis test was used to detect whether there was any significant difference between these subgroups in terms of the Forgotten Joint Score. The proportion of patients in both groups corresponding to excellent (> 41), good (34 to 41), fair (27 to 33) and poor (< 27) outcomes on the basis of OKS was tabulated.

A sensitivity analysis was performed using G*Power for the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test. With 72 participants in each group and a two-tailed α of 0.05, the present sample size provided 80% power to detect an effect size of d = 0.48. A post hoc analysis assuming an effect size of d = 0.48 yielded an achieved power of 0.80.

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that patients who underwent UKA for HKA > 15° of varus deformity did not demonstrate inferior outcomes or failure in the mid-term compared with patients who had mild and moderate (HKA < 15º) varus alignment. This led us to reject our null hypothesis. Similarly, the secondary hypothesis that the quantum of residual varus postoperatively in large deformities compromised outcomes was also rejected.

The usage of UKA has increased significantly over the past decades, accompanied by growing interest in its clinical outcomes and survivorship. The traditional guidelines of UKA have been expanded; recent studies recommend that even patients with significant flexion contractures or varus alignment > 15º could obtain a pre-disease state following UKA and experience excellent outcomes [13–15]. The present study too indicates that perioperative varus deformity does not affect mid-term outcomes in UKA.

There are limited studies on UKA performed for severe varus deformity. In the study by Itou et al. [11] included 36 cases of ‘severe’ deformity which had a mean varus of 8.5° ± 2.9°. There were 8 knees with > 15º varus in the series reported by Kleeblad et al. [9]; 15 cases with greater than 15º of varus for in another study [14]; in the series reported by Wu et al. [12], 12.5º ± 1.3° varus was the mean value of the group with the most severe deformity. The latter three studies found no difference in the outcomes of UKAs performed for deformed and less deformed knees.

The surgical goal in medial compartment UKA is to achieve some degree of varus alignment; however, there is no unanimity on the precise target and the amount of residual varus that can be accepted without compromising the outcome. There are a few studies which have analysed the correlation between residual varus after UKA with outcome. Wu et al. [12] retrospectively compared outcomes in patients who underwent Oxford UKA based on the change in alignment being ≤ 4°, 4 to < 7° and ≥ 7°. They found no statistically significant relationship between the variation of perioperative alignment and KOOS-JR scores. Itou et al. did not find any significant difference in FJS-12 between severe and less severe varus knees [11]. Ji et al. [16] suggested that < 5° residual varus is optimum, but the limitation of their study was that there were no clinical outcomes studied to support their assumption. Kleeblad et al. in their series of robotically-assisted UKA have considered optimal alignment to be ≤ 4° and acceptable alignment to be 5 to 7º varus, though the general consensus appears that varus should not exceed 7º [2–5, 9, 16]. In the present study, this range of postoperative HKA 0 to 7° in the group HKA < 15° was achieved in 94.44% of patients and 58.33% of patients in group HKA > 15°. However, further subgroup analysis of the three residual varus groups revealed no association with the FJS scores between patients with residual varus of 3° or less, 3 to 7° or > 7°.

Avoiding severe undercorrection is recommended to prevent medial compartment overload, which may be associated with accelerated polyethylene wear particularly in fixed-bearing UKAs [2, 3, 17]. With modern polyethylene inserts, this may be less of a concern in fixed-bearing designs. In the Oxford mobile-bearing design, there is even less of a concern of undercorrection leading to polyethylene wear due to the fully conforming round-on-round articulation between the spherical femoral component and the meniscal bearing with the exact reciprocal curvature. This round-on-round design with a conforming bearing may also be more forgiving for kinematically-aligned UKAs where the implants are positioned parallel to the kinematic axes of the knee but may be non-orthogonal on the tibia; however, there are only a few small series of such UKAs [18]. In the standard Oxford technique which we have followed, when both components are aligned with the mechanical axis of the respective bones, variable amounts of residual varus (up to 10º) do not seem to be detrimental as the contact stress on the polyethylene is unchanged due to the fully-conforming shape of the bearing.

In terms of technique, it is worth highlighting the fact that large deformities in our series warranted a minimal resection on the tibial side. Also, no releases were performed. Meticulous excision of medial tibial osteophytes was performed as well as those on the medial femoral condyle, particularly beneath the medial collateral ligament. Removal of osteophytes from the posterior part of the medial femoral condyle was accomplished with the use of a wide, curved gouge after the posterior femoral resection and this helped to correct flexion contractures. Careful removal of the anvil osteophyte and the intercondylar osteophytes encroaching on the ACL and impinging against the anvil osteophyte helped to achieve full extension. When caught between femoral component sizes, we chose one size smaller in order to get a thicker bearing to balance the flexion gap. In larger deformities, seldom was there a need to mill the distal femur after the initial milling; occasionally 1 mm and rarely 2 mm of milling was needed. Any loose bodies posteriorly were extricated with a curved disc forceps.

In a recent meta-analysis of 13 articles with 1888 patients, comparing UKA and TKR, Deng et al. concluded at the 5-year follow-up that “UKR instead of TKR should be performed in patients with late-stage isolated medial knee OA” [19]. The recently-published TOPKAT randomised controlled trial data showed that at 10 years there were no significant differences between UKAs and TKRs in clinical outcomes, reoperations, or revisions. However, UKAs had a lower complication rate and was more cost-effective [20]. Liddle et al. reported that, “patients undergoing TKR are at increased risk of medical complications; they are twice as likely to have a venous thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, or deep infection, three times as likely to have a stroke, and four times as likely to need blood transfusions. As a result, these patients are four times more likely to die in the first 30 days after surgery and about 15% more likely to die during the first 8 years”. These findings ought to be discussed with patients and their caregivers especially when the patient has significant co-morbidities and also has a severe deformity which may be amenable to UKA [21].

The limitation of this study was that the sample size of two groups is small, this stems from the fact that we wanted to pursue a matched-pair analysis and that here is a paucity of cases with severe varus which are amenable to UKA. The findings of this study apply only to carefully selected patients meeting strict indications for UKA and should not be generalized to all patients with severe varus deformity. We only choose two matching variable (age and gender) and survivorship analysis was not done. We did not include BMI, activity level, comorbidity and pre operative scores in match paring which can influence the sample size and outcome. Another limitation is the fact that we did not compare UKA with total knees replacements nor with high tibial osteotomy. The follow-up period is also a minimum of 5 years, and these patients are being keenly followed up to identify implant loosening and polyethylene wear. The presence of a ceiling effect is another probable limitation, particularly with FJS scores, which may sensitivity to detect potential differences between groups.

The strengths of this study are that it is the largest series of severe varus deformities exceeding 15 degrees treated with Oxford mobile-bearing UKA and we have performed a matched-pair analysis with a cohort of less deformed knees. We have measured outcomes using a plethora of PROMS – OKS, VAS, WOMAC and FJS. Not only have we compared outcomes with the preoperative deformity but also with postoperative alignment. We have further sub-divided the residual postoperative outcome into three grades and compared FJS across these grades.

Conclusion

UKA performed in selected patients with large preoperative deformities (> 15º varus) fulfilling Oxford criteria has no statistically significant difference in outcomes at the short to medium term follow-up compared to those with lesser deformity (< 15º varus). There is no statistical association between FJS scores and postoperative residual varus after UKA in both the deformity groups. However long-term follow-up and larger cohorts will lend further credence to our findings.
