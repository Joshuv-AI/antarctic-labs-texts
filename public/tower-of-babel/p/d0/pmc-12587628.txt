The relationship of distributed leadership with teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction in Türkiye: does psychological resilience make a difference?
Authors: Mehmet Nezir Çevik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the literature, the mediating role of teacher subjective well-being and the moderating role of psychological resilience in the association of distributed leadership with professional satisfaction have not been sufficiently examined through a moderated mediation model, particularly within centralized educational systems such as Türkiye. This study aims to examine the moderating role of psychological resilience in the association between distributed leadership on professional satisfaction through teachers’ subjective well-being in Turkish schools. In accordance with the quantitative method, 645 teachers from 24 provinces of Türkiye participated in this cross-sectional study and the data were tested with moderated mediation model. The results of the study revealed a significant and positive relationship between distributed leadership, psychological resilience, teacher subjective well-being, and professional satisfaction. In addition, it was determined that as psychological resilience increased, the association of distributed leadership with teacher subjective well-being and the indirect association of distributed leadership with professional satisfaction through teacher subjective well-being became stronger. In other words, it was concluded that distributed leadership is associated with higher professional satisfaction through its relationship with teachers’ subjective well-being depending on psychological resilience. In the study, it is suggested that school leaders should strengthen distributed leadership practices and create environments to support teachers’ psychological resilience as an important strategy to promote teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction.Keywords: Distributed leadership, Psychological resilience, Subjective well-being, Professional satisfaction, Moderated mediation model

Introduction

Distributed leadership is defined as a leadership approach that emphasises teachers’ participation in decision-making processes and the sharing of responsibilities, thereby strengthening a culture of collaboration and collective responsibility [1]. Teachers’ subjective well-being, which includes life satisfaction and positive emotions in professional processes, reflects their motivation at work and overall mental well-being responsibility [2]. Professional satisfaction, on the other hand, expresses teachers’ satisfaction with their professional roles, work environment, and career expectations responsibility [3]. In this respect, subjective well-being offers a direct and personal dimension of well-being, encompassing a broader concept of well-being, while professional satisfaction focuses more on the dimension of satisfaction related to the profession responsibility [4]. Psychological resilience, on the other hand, defines individuals’ capacity to adapt to stress and difficulties and to preserve and develop their psychological resources responsibility [5]. Increasing accountability pressure, constantly changing educational policies and uncertainties in the working environment are associated with teachers’ sense of professional satisfaction [6]. Professional satisfaction is considered a critical area of study as it includes individuals’ emotional responses towards one’s job and is related to variables such as intrinsic motivation, trust in the manager, stress level, leadership, and subjective well-being [7]– [8].

This study adopts a multi-theoretical conceptual framework based on Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [9], the Protective Factors Model (PFM) [10], and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [11] to explain the relationships among distributed leadership, teachers’ subjective well-being, psychological resilience, and professional satisfaction. SDT [9] emphasizes that fulfilling basic psychological needs such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness supports intrinsic motivation, subjective well-being, and professional satisfaction. In this context, distributed leadership fosters teachers’ participation in decision-making processes, thereby enhancing their sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which in turn promotes intrinsic motivation and professional satisfaction [12]. PFM [10] highlights the importance of individual and environmental supports in strengthening psychological resilience against stress and risk factors. Within the school setting, the collaborative and participatory climate fostered by distributed leadership functions as an environmental protective factor, helping teachers cope with stress and burnout and supporting their subjective well-being [13]. SCT [11] complements this perspective by emphasizing the reciprocal interaction between personal beliefs (e.g., self-efficacy, resilience) and environmental conditions. Building on this theoretical background, the literature further supports the relationships among professional satisfaction, subjective well-being, and leadership styles [14] and highlights the significant role of psychological resilience in these relationships [15]. Indeed, teachers with higher psychological resilience are reported to cope more effectively with stressors, and consequently, their levels of subjective well-being and professional satisfaction are positively associated with their resilience [16]– [17]. Previous studies also indicate that distributed leadership alleviates workload-related stress and fosters a more positive school climate, thereby enhancing teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction [18]– [19]. In conclusion, the integration of these three theories provides a comprehensive and robust theoretical foundation that explains how distributed leadership addresses teachers’ psychological needs, supports psychological resilience, and translates into increased subjective well-being and professional satisfaction.

Leadership in schools is associated with teachers’ professional satisfaction, subjective well-being, and motivation by shaping the overall quality of the educational environment [19]– [20]. School leaders, who have a crucial role especially in coping with adverse working conditions, can improve teachers’ work experiences and enhance their professional satisfaction [12]. In recent years, the understanding of leadership has moved away from traditional hierarchical models towards more participatory and sharing approaches, and this transformation has also manifested itself in educational institutions [21]– [22]– [23]. This change in the perspective on leadership has highlighted the distributed leadership model, which does not limit leadership processes only to school principals and encourages teachers’ active participation in decision-making processes [13–24].

Research has shown that adopting a distributed leadership approach enhances teachers’ professional satisfaction, strengthens their organizational commitment and psychological resilience, and allows them to perceive greater meaning in their roles within the school [25]– [26]– [27]. Similarly, the literature indicates that teachers’ psychological resilience plays a significant role in the relationship between subjective well-being and professional satisfaction [28]. In other words, teachers with high level of psychological resilience are able to cope with stress factors more effectively under the influence of distributed leadership, their levels of professional satisfaction and subjective well-being increase, and they show more commitment to their jobs [16]– [17].

Theoretical and empirical studies in the literature [29]– [30] frequently emphasize the relationship between psychological resilience and leadership and management styles. In dynamic and challenging environments such as schools, psychological resilience enables individuals to regroup in the face of stress, failure, and adversity, thereby enabling them to maintain their personal well-being and exhibit effective leadership behaviors [30]. For example, distributed leadership motivates and empowers teachers to exceed expectations, while psychological resilience contributes them to cope with challenging conditions and emerge even stronger from this process [31]. In addition, research shows that there is a positive relationship between servant leadership, distributed leadership, and inclusive leadership and psychological resilience [32]– [33]. Psychological resilience is also regarded as an essential personal resource that supports leaders to exhibit visionary leadership and clearly define the organizational vision even under uncertainty and pressure [30]. Similarly, there are empirical studies suggesting that high levels of psychological resilience aid leaders to exhibit distributed leadership behaviors more effectively by reducing uncertainties [34] and that different leadership styles can increase psychological resilience [31]– [32]. Uncertainties and stress factors encountered in the school environment can hinder leaders from exhibiting effective distributed leadership behaviors, which in turn may negatively affect teachers’ professional well-being [35]. Although previous studies have examined distributed leadership, psychological resilience, teachers’ subjective well-being, and professional satisfaction separately, there is still a lack of research investigating how these variables interact within a single model. At the same time, centralized educational structures such as Türkiye may limit the autonomy of school administrators [36]. Previous research suggests that distributed leadership is associated with teacher-related outcomes such as professional satisfaction, subjective well-being, motivation, and commitment indirectly through school-level factors [37]– [38]. In this context, the present study examined the moderating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ professional satisfaction through their subjective well-being and tested a moderated mediation model involving psychological resilience. Accordingly, the study aims to answer the following research questions:

Is there a significant relationship between distributed leadership, teachers’ subjective well-being, and their professional satisfaction based on teachers’ perceptions?

Is there a significant relationship between distributed leadership, teachers’ subjective well-being, and their professional satisfaction based on teachers’ perceptions?

Does teachers’ subjective well-being mediate the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school leaders’ distributed leadership and their professional satisfaction?

Does teachers’ subjective well-being mediate the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school leaders’ distributed leadership and their professional satisfaction?

Does psychological resilience moderate the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being?

Does psychological resilience moderate the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being?

Does psychological resilience moderate the indirect relationship of distributed leadership on professional satisfaction through teachers’ subjective well-being?

Does psychological resilience moderate the indirect relationship of distributed leadership on professional satisfaction through teachers’ subjective well-being?

Research context and conceptual model

Although the centralized and hierarchical structure of the Turkish education system limits the decision-making autonomy of administrators, distributed leadership practices offer significant potential by supporting intra-school collaboration and participatory management [39]. Teachers’ professional conditions in Türkiye can negatively affect their subjective well-being and professional satisfaction due to factors such as heavy workload, limited professional development opportunities, and low professional autonomy [40]. In this context, school administrators’ adoption of a distributed leadership approach can enhance professional satisfaction and psychological resilience by ensuring teachers’ active participation in decision-making processes. Moreover, these practices may strengthen teachers’ subjective well-being by supporting them to feel more valuable and competent in a collaborative school climate [23]. In addition, distributed leadership can positively influence teachers’ professional satisfaction and well-being by reinforcing their self-efficacy perceptions [41]– [42]. Therefore, the present study aims to examine the moderating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction in developing countries such as Türkiye, which has a centralized and bureaucratic education system. Literature review and hypotheses of the study

The relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ professional satisfaction

Distributed leadership, which evaluates leadership in terms of dynamic relationships between leaders and followers [21], has received increasing attention both in academia and in practice in developed countries [43]. Distributed leadership is a dynamic process in which leadership roles are shared within a group rather than a single individual [44]. By encouraging the sharing of leadership work across individuals and roles, this approach increases the collective capacity of the organization and engages more stakeholders in decision-making [13]. Indeed, studies have shown that distributed leadership plays a significant role in teacher professional satisfaction, serving as a strong predictor and demonstrating a direct relationship with it [45]– [46]– [47]. However, some studies indicate that different leadership styles are related to staff’s psychological states, behaviors, and professional satisfaction both positively and negatively, and that the association of distributed leadership may be significant but limited in different cultural contexts [3] While some studies show a negative association between distributed leadership and teachers’ professional satisfaction and empowerment [48], these findings suggest that the relationship is complex and may vary depending on contextual factors such as culture, implementation quality, and organizational constraints. Occupational satisfaction is defined as the emotional reactions that occur as a result of individuals’ subjective evaluations of their occupations and their general appreciation of their jobs [42]. Professional satisfaction, which is shaped by the sense of success and achievement arising from the nature of the job, contributes to the achievement of the organization’s goals by increasing the motivation and productivity of employees [49]. Moreover [50], define distributed leadership practices as an important emotional factor related to teachers’ professional satisfaction [51]. also consider the principal’s display of distributed leadership as a factor associated with teachers’ professional satisfaction. Therefore, the first hypothesis of the study is as follows:

H1: There is a positive and significant relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ professional satisfaction.

The relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being

Distributed leadership is a decisive force for school improvement [12] and can create a supportive school environment where teachers feel subjectively well [20] and are willing to maintain their professional commitment [19] by alleviating negative working conditions. Subjective well-being is based on the theoretical framework developed by [52] and addresses teachers’ positive psychological functioning in work life in three main dimensions. Teaching efficacy refers to the teacher’s belief in meeting professional requirements effectively. School engagement refers to the quality of relationships and a sense of belonging in the school community. Joy of teaching encompasses feelings of enthusiasm, satisfaction and gratitude for the teaching process. Research shows that distributed leadership determines the working conditions of schools and teachers’ subjective well-being and satisfaction [22–53]. There are also studies suggesting that distributed leadership reduces stress in teachers [54] and supports subjective well-being [20–55]. Although many studies emphasise that distributed leadership is positively associated with teachers’ subjective well-being, there are also critical findings regarding this relationship. For example [56], stated that distributed leadership does not have a direct meaningful effect on subjective well-being, but that the relationship emerges only through organisational trust. Similarly [57], showed that the structural form of distributed leadership can have negative indirect effects on teachers’ professional satisfaction and motivation. Furthermore, some studies highlight that implementation issues such as role ambiguity and power conflicts may negatively affect subjective well-being by increasing teachers’ stress [58]. These findings reveal that the associations of distributed leadership may vary depending on the context and form of implementation. In line with this conceptual and empirical framework, the second hypothesis of the study is as follows.

H2: There is a positive and significant relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being.

The relationship between teacher subjective well-being and professional satisfaction

Teachers’ professional satisfaction is a crucial factor in determining their subjective well-being in educational settings [59]. Teachers with high levels of subjective well-being are more satisfied with their profession, which strengthens their professional commitment [60]. On the other hand, low subjective well-being also leads to lower professional satisfaction, which in turn leads teachers to experience more stress and burnout [61]– [62]. While a large body of research has demonstrated a strong and positive relationship between professional satisfaction and subjective well-being among teachers [59–62], the findings of the present study indicate that this relationship is not constant and direct in every context. For example [63], found that differences experienced by teachers in public and private schools, as well as structural factors such as job security and salary conditions, significantly influence this relationship, while a systematic review conducted by [64] showed that workload, student behaviour, and the school environment can weaken this positive relationship between professional satisfaction and subjective well-being. Similarly, research conducted in Czech schools has shown that chronic stress and burnout reduce professional satisfaction, which in turn negatively affects teachers’ well-being [65]. These findings indicate that the relationship between subjective well-being and professional satisfaction is context-dependent and shaped by both individual and organisational factors. Providing working conditions that support teachers’ subjective well-being [66] can increase teachers’ professional satisfaction at both individual and organizational levels [67]. Therefore, based on the previous findings in the literature, the third hypothesis of the study is as follows:

H3: There is a positive and significant relationship between teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction.

The mediating role of teachers’ subjective well-being in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction

In the literature, it is emphasized that distributed leadership is significantly associated with teachers’ professional satisfaction [68]– [69]. Distributed leadership approach of school administrators makes it possible to delegate authority to teachers. This enhances teachers’ professional satisfaction and subjective well-being levels by reducing their workload and professional pressure [41]. It is also stated that distributed leadership is one of the main factors determining teachers’ professional satisfaction [70]. As a matter of fact, distributing leadership roles among teachers allows them to assume leadership responsibilities and actively contribute to school development. In other words, distributed leadership is positively related to teachers’ professional satisfaction by increasing their subjective well-being levels [71]. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong and positive relationship between teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction [72]– [73], yet the underlying mechanisms of this relationship have not been fully explored. Meta-analyses and longitudinal studies reveal that the association of subjective well-being on increasing professional satisfaction is stronger than the association of professional satisfaction on subjective well-being [73]– [74] In other words, as an individual’s overall life satisfaction and happiness increase, so does their professional satisfaction [74]. Furthermore, each of the social, psychological, and emotional dimensions of subjective well-being significantly is related to professional satisfaction [75]. Psychological capital and social capital play a mediating role in the relationship of subjective well-being on professional satisfaction, meaning that employees with high levels of well-being develop greater psychological and social capital, leading to higher professional professional satisfaction [72–75]. Despite these findings, the literature has only implied, but not explicitly examined, the potential mediating role of subjective well-being and the moderating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction. Filling this gap is important to clarify the conditions under which distributed leadership can most effectively increase teachers’ professional satisfaction through subjective well-being. Accordingly, subjective well-being enhances teachers’ self-perception and contributes positively to their professional satisfaction [42–76]. Within this framework, it is posited that teachers’ level of subjective well-being may amplify the association of distributed leadership practices on their professional satisfaction. Based on this rationale, the fourth hypothesis of the study is formulated as follows:

H4: Teachers’ subjective well-being mediates the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction.

Moderating role of psychological resilience on the relationship between distributed leadership and teacher subjective well-being

Research on the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being [12]–13– [18–77] highlights that distributed leadership plays a key role in enhancing teachers’ well-being. However, one of the factors that may be related to the strength of this relationship is psychological resilience [12]. Psychological resilience is a dynamic process that refers to the capacity of individuals to adapt and recover from negative life events such as stress, trauma, and adversity [78]. Psychological resilience is percieved as a key determinant of professional well-being among teachers, as it reflects their capacity to adapt positively to challenges and to maintain a constructive approach in interpersonal relationships [79]. Indeed [77], concluded that psychological resilience is positively associated with teacher well-being, school leadership and teacher subjective well-being. Similarly [18], comprehensive review of teacher subjective well-being supports the critical role of psychological resilience in this process. However, findings in the literature regarding the moderating role of psychological resilience are not always consistent and may vary depending on contextual and individual factors. For example, a study conducted in Turkey found that the association of school leaders’ empowering leadership behaviours on teacher psychological resilience differed depending on variables such as gender and the leader’s tenure [80]. In this context, the fifth hypothesis of the study has been determined as follows:

H5: Psychological resilience plays a moderating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being.

Moderating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction through subjective well-being

The research demonstrates that distributed leadership plays an important role in enhancing teachers’ professional satisfaction [81]. Moreover, it is also emphasized that subjective well-being functions as a mediating variable in this relationship and strengthens teachers’ professional satisfaction [41]– [42–71] However, the strength of this indirect effect may be related to teachers’ level of psychological resilience. Teachers with high levels of psychological resilience may benefit more from distributed leadership practices and show higher levels of subjective well-being and, thus, professional satisfaction [82]. Teachers with lower levels of psychological resilience may benefit less from the positive associations of distributed leadership due to the stress, burnout, and difficulties they encounter [13–61]. Some studies emphasise the positive effects of leadership on teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction [83]. However, there are also findings suggesting that this effect may vary depending on the context; for example, it has been shown that teacher autonomy plays a mediating role in the effect of distributed leadership on teachers’ emotional labour [55]. Findings regarding the moderating role of psychological resilience are not always consistent; some studies have found that this association varies depending on factors such as gender or organisational culture [80]. These mixed results indicate that the role of psychological resilience in the connection between distributed leadership, teachers’ subjective well-being, and professional satisfaction is context-dependent. Accordingly, the sixth hypothesis of the studyhas been determined as follows:

H6: The moderating role of psychological resilience exists in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction through subjective well-being.

Method

The research was conducted within the framework of a cross-sectional design based on the quantitative research method [84].

Participants and data collection process

The study sample consisted of 665 teachers employed in public schools across 24 provinces representing the western, central, eastern, northern, and southern regions. Teachers in these schools, which are part of Türkiye’s centralised and bureaucratic education system, have a limited degree of autonomy, and the sample includes teachers from all school levels, from pre-school to high school. The participants were selected through convenience sampling method, taking into account different geographical regions and socio-cultural structures. It is a fact that the inclusion of individuals from diverse socio-economic and cultural backgrounds contributed to the representation of different population groups. A 28-item online scale form was used as the data collection tool. The forms were distributed to teachers via WhatsApp and e-mail due to their time and cost efficiency [85]. At the end of the data collection process, 800 teachers received the online form; however, 665 teachers responded to the online form. This corresponds to a return rate of 83%. However, following an outlier analysis, the responses of 20 teachers were excluded from the dataset. As a result, the analyses were conducted on the data obtained from 645 teachers. Additionally, to minimize the effects of social desirability and common method bias, the scale items were presented in a clear and comprehensible manner, following the sequence of dependent, mediator, moderator, and independent variables [86] and the principle of anonymity was strictly adhered to during the data collection process. Accordingly, of the participating teachers, 356 (55.2%) were female and 289 (44.8%) were male. In terms of teaching level, 149 (23.1%) were preschool teachers, 162 (25.1%) were primary school teachers, 184 (28.5%) were secondary school teachers, and 150 (23.3%) were high school teachers. Regarding educational attainment, 504 teachers (78.1%) held a bachelor’s degree, while 141 (21.9%) held a postgraduate degree. The average length of professional experience among all participants was 13.24 years (SD = 7.91).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and its recent modifications. All participants who participated in this study were adults and had been clearly informed of the study’s nature and purpose. They voluntarily participated and provided informed consent in writing before any data collection began. For this study, approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Siirt University, a public university in Türkiye with the number 8922.

Variables and data collection tools

Independent variable: distributed leadership scale (DL)

The DL is a unidimensional scale consisting of 10 items, developed by [87]. Participants respond to the items (e.g., “Teachers are ensured to actively participate in all change and development efforts in the school”) using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 5 (Always). The results of the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) conducted within the scope of the research revealed that the construct validity of the scale was at an acceptable level (x²/sd = 3.27; RMSEA = 0.059; SRMR = 0.022; CFI = 0.99; TLI = 0.98). The reliability of the scale was evaluated with Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient and a high level of internal consistency was obtained (α = 0.93). In the analyses conducted to determine the convergent validity of the scale, the Average Variance Explained (AVE) value was calculated as 0.56 and the Composite Reliability (CR) value was calculated as 0.92.

Moderator variable: brief psychological resilience scale (PR)

In this study, the Brief Resilience Scale (PR) developed by [88] was employed to assess teachers’ levels of psychological resilience. The scale was adapted into Turkish by [89]. The Perceived Stress Scale, which exhibits a unidimensional structure (e.g., “I get through difficult times with very little distress”), is composed of six items and employs a five-point Likert-type response format (1 = Not at all appropriate, 5 = Completely appropriate). Three of the scale items are reverse items. The Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results of the current study showed that the unidimensional structure of the scale was compatible with the data (x²/sd = 2.10; RMSEA = 0.041; SRMR = 0.017; CFI = 0.99; TLI = 0.99). The internal consistency of the scale was found to be high and Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient was calculated as 0.86. In the convergent validity analysis, the AVE value was 0.51 and the CR value was 0.72.

Mediating variable: teacher subjective well-being scale (SWB)

The scale was developed by [52] and adapted into Turkish by [90]. Participants respond to the items on the scale using a four-point Likert-type rating system, ranging from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always). The scale has two sub-dimensions, namely “school engagement” (sample item: I feel that I belong to this school) and “teaching efficacy” (sample item: I have achieved a lot as a teacher), and each dimension consists of four items. The CFA results in the context of the research indicated that the two-factor structure of the scale was within the appropriate ranges (x²/sd = 2.96; RMSEA = 0.055; SRMR = 0.016; CFI = 0.99; TLI = 0.98). Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.86, 0.81 and 0.91 for school engagement, teaching efficacy and total scale respectively. According to the convergent validity analyses, the AVE values for school engagement, teaching efficacy and total scale were 0.60, 0.58 and 0.59, and the CR values were 0.85, 0.84 and 0.95, respectively.

Dependent variable: professional satisfaction subscale (PS)

The professional satisfaction scale, which is a subscale of the teacher happiness scale, was developed by [91]. The scale used for this study consists of four items (sample item: Teaching is a source of joy for me) and the items are answered on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). For the current study, the subscale was found to be within the statistically appropriate value range (x2/sd = 0.85; RMSEA = 0.000, SRMR = 0.003, CFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.00). In addition, the Cronbach alpha (α) reliability coefficient of the PS was 0.93, the AVE value for convergent validity was 0.78, and the CR value was 0.93.

In the literature, Cronbach’s Alpha (α) coefficients of 0.70 and above are considered acceptable in terms of reliability [92]. The Cronbach Alpha values of all scales used in this study range between 0.81 and 0.93, meeting the threshold value and indicating that the internal consistency of the scales is sufficient. CR values of 0.70 and above are also an accepted criterion [93]. The CR values for all structures included in the study range from 0.72 to 0.95, which is above this threshold. Therefore, appropriate values have been achieved in terms of structural reliability. The threshold value recommended in the literature for AVE is 0.50, and results above this value indicate that sufficient convergent validity has been achieved [94]. The AVE values calculated in the study range from 0.51 to 0.78, indicating that the relevant criterion has been met. When evaluating the fit indices related to CFA, it is recommended that CFI and TLI values be 0.90 or higher, x²/df be less than 3 or 5, and RMSEA and SRMR values be below 0.08 [95]. The CFA results obtained within the scope of this study indicate that the fit values for all scales are statistically appropriate and acceptable.

Control variables

There are empirical findings in the literature (e.g [96]. – [97]) that demographic variables are effective on professional satisfaction. Accordingly, gender, education level and professional seniority variables were included in the model as control variables.

Data analysis

The research data were analyzed utilizing Mplus version 8.3 and Process Macro version 3.5.3.programs. Hypotheses H1, H2, H3 and H4 were evaluated with Process Macro Model 4, while hypotheses H5 and H6 were assessed with Process Macro Model 8. The statistical analyses started by examining the descriptive characteristics of the variables and the interrelationships among them. Direct and indirect associations were evaluated using the bootstrapping method with 5,000 samples. Confidence intervals were calculated at the 95% level and effects were considered significant if they did not include zero [98]. Furthermore, fit indices such as x²/df, RMSEA, SRMR, CFI and TLI were used to evaluate the fit of the measurement models with the data. The fact that the fit criteria are within acceptable limits indicates that the model is valid [95–99].

Prior to the analysis, preliminary checks were performed on the dataset. Since the data were collected online, no missing values were found. Based on the Mahalanobis distance, 20 outliers were identified and sunsequently excluded from the analysis The skewness values ranged from-0.626 to 0.411, and the Kurtosis values ranged from-1.249 to-0.495, both of which fall within the acceptable range of ± 1.5, thereby confirming univariate normality [100]. In Q-Q graphs, observations are distributed close to the baseline, and multivariate scatter diagrams are consistent with the assumption of multivariate normality. Pearson correlations, VIF, tolerance, and condition indices were analyzed for multicollinearity risk; Durbin-Watson coefficient was analyzed for autocorrelation problem. Correlation coefficients below 0.80 (see Table 1), VIF values less than 5 [1.115 to 1.178], tolerance values greater than 0.20 [0.849 to 0.897] and condition indices below 30 [15.104 to 26.297] indicate that there is no multicollinearity problem. In addition, the Durbin-Watson coefficient of 2.086 indicates that there is no autocorrelation problem in the study [93].

Findings

In this section, before presenting the findings related to each hypothesis, descriptive statistics and correlation analysis findings are given.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis findings

The arithmetic mean and standard deviation values of the main variables in the study are presented in Table 1, showing that the means of DL, PR, SWB, and PS variables are 3.70 (SD = 0.61), 4.15 (SD = 0.54), 3.61 (SD = 0.40), and 4.34 (SD = 0.67), respectively. In terms of SD values, participants’ perceptions of professional satisfaction (SD = 0.67) differ more than distributed leadership (SD = 0.61), psychological resilience (SD = 0.54) and subjective well-being (SD = 0.40). According to Pearson correlation analysis, positive and significant relationships were found between DL and PR (r = 0.273, p < 0.01), SWB (r = 0.337, p < 0.01) and PS (r = 0.482, p < 0.01), and between PR and SWB (r = 0.252, p < 0.01) and PS (r = 0.327, p < 0.01). A strong positive relationship was found between SWB and PS (r = 0.600, p < 0.01).

Statistical findings related to hypotheses

The statistical findings of the research on hypotheses were tested within the context of the model developed. Accordingly, a weak relationship was found between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction (β = 0.31, SE = 0.032, p < 0.01; [LCI = 0.2518; UCI = 0.3743]) and subjective well-being (β = 0.33, SE = 0.038, p < 0.01; [LCI = 0.2610; UCI = 0.4081]); subjective well-being was found to be moderately related to professional satisfaction (β = 0.49, SE = 0.031, p < 0.01; [LCI = 0.4339; UCI = 0.5558]). Furthermore, subjective well-being was found to play a weak but significant mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction (β = 0.16, SE = 0.022, p < 0.01; [LCI = 0.1243; UCI = 0.2125]). All these findings support hypotheses H1, H2, H3, and H4.

In order to determine how the effect of distributed leadership on subjective well-being changes depending on the level of teachers’ psychological resilience, H5 of the study was tested. As seen in Fig. 2, psychological resilience has a weak yet significant positive moderating effect on the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being (β = 0.10, SE = 0.037, p < 0.01; [LCI = 0.0357; UCI = 0.1826]). This finding of the study confirms hypothesis H5. As shown in Table 2; Fig. 3, the positive association between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being becomes stronger at higher levels of psychological resilience (low: β = 0.19, SE = 0.049, p < 0.01, [LCI = 0.0960; UCI = 0.2916]; medium: β = 0.30, SE = 0.038, p < 0.01, [LCI = 0.2275; UCI = 0.3785]; high: β = 0.41, SE = 0.057, p < 0.01, [LCI = 0.2998; UCI = 0.5245]).

Discussion and conclusion

This study aims to explore how distributed leadership relates to teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction, and whether psychological resilience makes a difference in this relationship. In this context, the findings reveal that distributed leadership significantly increases teachers’ professional satisfaction. Teachers’ active involvement in school management leads to increased professional satisfaction [42]–43– [71–104]. In this respect, distributed leadership is a strategic tool for school administrators to enhance teachers’ professional satisfaction. However, comparative studies show that its effects vary depending on the context, being stronger in collectivist cultures such as Shanghai and England, and weaker in more individualistic contexts such as the USA and Australia [13, 105]. In highly centralised education systems such as Türkiye, where teachers’ participation in decision making processes is structurally limited, it may be more difficult to realise the positive effects of distributed leadership. These findings highlight the potential of distributed leadership to increase teacher satisfaction, while also drawing attention to the need to consider contextual and cultural dynamics.

A positive relationship has been identified between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being. This relationship can be explained by distributed leadership strengthening teachers’ sense of belonging and creating a more supportive school environment. Consistent with the literature, distributed leadership clarifies teachers’ roles [20], encourages their participation in the decision-making process [13], and fosters a culture of collaboration and shared responsibility. These mechanisms help reduce stress, improve mental and physical health, and enhance teachers’ subjective well-being [22–53]. From a practical perspective, this suggests that headteachers who share leadership more effectively can create healthier working environments where teachers feel supported and valued. However, the effect of distributed leadership on subjective well-being may be mitigated by contextual factors such as school size, available resources, and broader systemic constraints. In highly centralised systems such as Türkiye, where teachers’ autonomy is limited, the positive effects on subjective well-being may not be fully realised. Therefore, distributed leadership should be understood not only as a management tool but as a holistic approach that supports both the professional and personal dimensions of teachers’ lives and whose effectiveness is shaped by organisational and cultural conditions.

According to the third hypothesis, there is a strong and significant positive correlation between teachers’ subjective well-being and their professional satisfaction. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that teachers’ subjective well-being increases their professional satisfaction [60–66]. When teachers feel psychologically well and are satisfied with their lives, they tend to perceive their professional roles as more meaningful and fulfilling. Subjective well-being is closely related to individuals’ life satisfaction, happiness levels, and positive emotional states, and has a significant impact on professional satisfaction [69]. Practically, this suggests that educational policies and school environments that support teachers’ subjective well-being, such as reducing role ambiguity, strengthening peer support, and recognising teachers’ contributions, may indirectly increase professional satisfaction. Furthermore, it is known that subjective well-being plays a mediating role between professional satisfaction and depressive symptoms and contributes to a reduction in depressive symptoms [106]. However, this positive effect may not be the same in all contexts: for example, in schools with limited resources or under high-stress conditions, the link between subjective well-being and professional satisfaction may be weaker. Therefore, context-specific interventions are necessary to strengthen this relationship. The findings of this study highlight the need to develop strategies to increase professional satisfaction in educational settings, particularly in developing countries such as Türkiye.

Another finding of the study is that teachers’ subjective well-being acts as a mediating variable in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction. This finding is consistent with previous research [18–107]. Subjective well-being may enable teachers to experience positive emotional experiences related to their work life and perceive their profession as more meaningful. Improved subjective well-being positively influences teachers’ emotional and cognitive evaluations of their work, which in turn reinforces their professional satisfaction. This is supported by findings in the literature indicating that subjective well-being plays a decisive role in teachers’ perceptions of professional satisfaction [108]. Practically, this result suggests that school administrators creating participatory decision-making environments, recognising teachers’ contributions, and strengthening psychosocial support mechanisms can indirectly contribute to professional satisfaction by increasing subjective well-being. However, this mediating effect may not be equally strong in every context. Particularly in schools with heavy workloads, limited resources, or high accountability pressures, the mediating role of subjective well-being may be weakened. Conversely, this effect may be more pronounced in collaborative school cultures or environments with high professional autonomy and peer support. These contextual differences align with studies showing that subjective well-being functions as a psychological mechanism that strengthens the effect of distributed leadership practices on professional satisfaction [22–81]. Consequently, it can be stated that teachers’ subjective well-being plays a mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction, and that the fourth hypothesis is supported by these findings.

The study concluded that psychological resilience plays a moderating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being. This result corroborates previous research highlighting the effect of distributed leadership on teachers’ subjective well-being [12–108]. Psychological resilience is considered a critical moderator variable in the relationship between distributed leadership and subjective well-being, as it enhances teachers’ ability to cope with and manage stressful situations [13]. In practice, when school leaders encourage teachers’ participation in decision-making processes and create participatory environments, teachers with high levels of psychological resilience can leverage these opportunities to enhance their own well-being. Furthermore, while distributed leadership is seen as an important factor in promoting teachers’ subjective well-being, psychological resilience determines the strength of this effect [77–108]. In other words, psychological resilience acts as a buffer mechanism that protects teachers from the negative effects of stress factors and enhances the benefits of distributed leadership on subjective well-being [109]. However, this moderating effect is not uniform. In schools with excessive workloads, limited organisational support, or rigid hierarchical structures, even resilient teachers may struggle to translate distributed leadership into higher subjective well-being. Overall, these findings indicate that psychological resilience plays a crucial role and that the effectiveness of leadership practices is shaped by the interaction between organisational conditions and individual characteristics.

Within the scope of the study’s final hypothesis, it was concluded that psychological resilience plays a moderating role in the indirect effect of distributed leadership on professional satisfaction through subjective well-being. The results of this study are consistent with previous research showing the indirect effect of distributed leadership on teachers’ professional satisfaction [42–81]. Furthermore, although there is limited research directly examining this model, the findings conceptually align with studies highlighting the role of subjective well-being in related [41]– [42–71]. When teachers experience distributed leadership practices such as participation in decision-making, delegation of responsibilities, and recognition of their professional contributions, they feel more supported and valued, which increases subjective well-being and, consequently, professional satisfaction. However, this pathway may operate more strongly for teachers who are resilient in coping with stress and change. Empirical evidence shows that teachers with high levels of psychological resilience benefit more from distributed leadership and increase their subjective well-being and professional satisfaction levels [82]. Conversely, teachers with lower psychological resilience may benefit less from the positive effects of distributed leadership on subjective well-being and professional satisfaction because they are more affected by professional stress factors and difficulties [13]. Consequently, these findings indicate that educational institutions should implement policies that promote distributed leadership and strengthen psychological resilience in order to enhance teachers’ subjective well-being and professional satisfaction.

Theoretical implications

This study provides a theoretical explanation of the interaction between distributed leadership, psychological resilience, subjective well-being, and teachers’ professional satisfaction. In the literature, it is emphasized that distributed leadership is a leadership approach that enhances teachers’ professional satisfaction and subjective well-being by encouraging their participation in decision-making processes [43]. Research findings indicate that teachers’ subjective well-being serves as a key determinant of their professional satisfaction, and that these two variables are mutually influential [67]. In addition, psychological resilience has been found to be a critical factor that strengthens the association between distributed leadership and teachers’ subjective well-being [77]. In particular, it is understood that teachers with high psychological resilience benefit more from distributed leadership practices and their professional satisfaction levels increase accordingly [31]. In this context, it can be stated that this study presents a novel perspective to the theoretical discussions by revealing the mediating role of subjective well-being and the moderating effect of psychological resilience in the relationship between distributed leadership and professional satisfaction.

Practical implications

The practical implications derived from the research findings underscore the significance of fostering distributed leadership practices within educational settings as a means to enhance teachers’ professional satisfaction and subjective well-being. Specifically, school administrators actively involving teachers in decision-making processes and sharing leadership roles can strengthen professional satisfaction, motivation, and overall subjective well-being of teachers [43]. In schools with a centralised and hierarchical structure, headteachers can strengthen distributed leadership by establishing participatory decision-making mechanisms and distributing responsibilities transparently and fairly within a governance framework. In this context, providing teachers with the opportunity to contribute to shaping the curriculum, take responsibility for extracurricular activities, and exercise their professional autonomy, while also recognising their professional contributions, helps them feel valued and supported. Policy makers, taking into account teachers’ heavy workload and limited professional autonomy, can design professional development programmes aimed at strengthening their psychological resilience. Training focused on stress management, coping strategies, and adaptability skills, in particular, helps teachers deal more effectively with professional challenges and enables them to benefit more from distributed leadership practices. Therefore, both school principals and education authorities can transform structures such as departments, boards, and commissions into more participatory and collaborative platforms, thereby ensuring teachers’ effective participation in decision-making processes. Furthermore, it has been determined that teachers with high psychological resilience benefit more from distributed leadership practices and their subjective well-being levels enhance more strongly in this direction [31–108]. Therefore, this study provides strong and applicable evidence for school leaders to adopt a distributed leadership approach as well as to develop strategies to increase teachers’ psychological resilience.

Limitations of the study and suggestions for future research

Certain limitations should be taken into consideration in the evaluation of the research findings. The fact that the data were collected cross-sectionally and at a single time does not allow the determination of causal relationships, and experimental or longitudinal studies are needed to reveal such connections. Although the study collected data from teachers working in different regions of Türkiye, it is possible to reach different results if the sample is expanded. In this study, data was collected only from teachers, and headteachers’ self-assessments regarding distributive leadership were not included in the process. This situation reflects the multidimensional nature of leadership from only one perspective and may lead to methodological limitations (e.g., common method bias) that can arise from collecting data from a single source. The data collection process was conducted using online tools. While this method offers advantages in terms of accessibility, it may have made it difficult for teachers with limited online connectivity or those less active in online environments to participate in the study. Consequently, the sample may have reflected urban teachers with higher digital literacy to a greater extent, potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to the entire teacher population. Therefore, this methodological limitation should be taken into account when evaluating the results obtained. Furthermore, as the data was collected anonymously online, it was not possible to access information about the schools or institutions to which the participants belonged. Consequently, the possible clustering structure (e.g., the likelihood of teachers from the same school giving similar responses) could not be statistically controlled. It is recommended that future research collect school or regional information during the data collection process and apply multilevel modelling techniques. Since the study was conducted in the context of Türkiye, where teacher autonomy is relatively low, it is difficult to generalize the results obtained to Western countries. In this context, it would be useful to conduct similar studies in Western countries and conduct comparative analyses.
