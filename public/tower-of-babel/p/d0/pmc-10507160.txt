The essential role of the ERK activation in large T antigen of BK polyomavirus regulated cell migration
Authors: Jiun-Wen Wang, Yi-Jung Li, Hsin-Hsu Wu, Hsiang-Hao Hsu, Ming-Yang Chang, Robert YL Wang, Ya-Chung Tian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•BKPyV infection and its expression of both large and small T antigens induce phosphorylation of host ERK1/2.•Inhibition of ERK1/2 effectively blocked BKPyV infection and inhibited TAg- and tAg-induced cell motility.•TAg plays a role in regulating the protein expression and enzyme activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9.•TAg regulated MMP-9 through the ERK signaling pathway, whereas MMP-2 was regulated through an ERK-independent pathway.

Introduction

BK polyomavirus (BKPyV) belongs to the genus beta-polyomavirus of the family Polyomaviridae and was first isolated from a renal transplant patient by Gardner et al. (1971), Moens et al. (2017). Multiple genes are present within the 5 kb genome of BKPyV, such as TAg, tAg, VP1, VP2, VP3, and agnoprotein. These proteins fulfill roles in both regulatory (TAg, and tAg) and structural (VP1, VP2, and VP3; agnoprotein for virion release) capacities (Cubitt, 2006).

The regulatory proteins, TAg and tAg, are produced by alternative splicing of a single RNA precursor, resulting in a common N-terminal region and different C-terminal regions (Oliveira et al., 1999). In addition, a third early protein, called truncTAg, was identified in 2009, which is expressed after alternate splicing of the mRNA encoded by TAg and shares the first 133 amino acids with TAg (Abend et al., 2009). These proteins are multifunctional, serving not only in the production of virion progeny but also in the modulation of various cellular signaling pathways by hijacking host machinery (Moens and Macdonald, 2019). For example, TAg plays a crucial role in facilitating effective viral replication by virtue of its nuclear localization signal (NLS), enabling TAg to accurately localize within the nucleus. and containing a J domain that interacts with heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) (Helle et al., 2017; Kelley and Georgopoulos, 1997). In addition, all three early proteins share a conserved ``LXCXE'' motif that interacts with the retinoblastoma protein (RB) and its families RBL1 (p107) and RBL2 (p130) (Harris et al., 1996, 1998). TAg binds to RB and excess elongation factor 2 (EF2), leading to the activation of genes involved in S-phase progression and promoting viral DNA synthesis (DeCaprio and Garcea, 2013). Through the regulatory proteins, BKPyV can interfere cellular processes such as the cell cycle, cell survival, DNA damage repair, transcription, and translation and evade immune surveillance (Moens and Macdonald, 2019).

Extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2, belonging to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, undergo activation through a phosphorylation cascade involving MAPK kinases (MAPKKs) and MAPKK kinases (MAPKKKs). This activation is triggered by cytokines, growth factors or mitogen-induced signals conveyed by RAS GTPases (Cargnello and Roux, 2011; Lavoie et al., 2020). The ERK signaling pathway controls critical cellular processes such as proliferation, survival, growth, metabolism, migration, and differentiation (Lavoie et al., 2020). In addition to cytokines and growth factors, several RNA and DNA viruses also affect the host MAPK-ERK pathway. For example, influenza virus, HIV, and HCV exploit the ERK signaling pathway to regulate viral replication and host cytokine production (Pleschka, 2008). Human adenovirus (HAdV), human papillomavirus (HPV), herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1), HSV-2 and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) can modulate the MAPK-ERK pathway, leading to enhanced viral replication, dysregulation of host cell cycle, altered immune response, and increased cell survival (DuShane and Maginnis, 2019). Polyomaviruses can also stimulate the ERK cascade (DuShane and Maginnis, 2019). The Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway is activated during murine polyomavirus and JC polyomavirus (JCPyV) infection (DuShane et al., 2018; O’Hara and Garcea, 2016).

While the stimulatory effects of the ERK signaling pathway on BKPyV replication have been documented (Seamone et al., 2010), the activation of the ERK signaling pathway during BKPyV infection and its potential influence on host biofunctions remain unclear. Moreover, the specific viral component responsible for ERK activation has not been fully elucidated. In our study, we unveiled the pivotal role of TAg and tAg as primary regulators in ERK activation, and we demonstrated that inhibition of this activation results in impaired cell migration.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (hRPTECs) cell line (ATCC® CRL-4031™) was cultured in DMEM/F-12 1:1 (Gibco™ 11330032) supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (Gibco™ 10437028) and the hTERT immortalized RPTEC growth kit (ATCC® ACS-4007™). Once cells reach 80–90% confluence in a T75 flask, discard the culture medium, wash cells with 5 mL of DPBS and rinse with 1 ml 0.05% trypsin for 3 min. To terminate the trypsin activity, 5 mL of medium containing 1% FBS was added to the suspended cells and then centrifuged at 200 g for 3 min at room temperature. The cell pellets were collected, resuspend in complete medium, and a new batch of cells was passaged at a 1:5 ratio. The culture protocol of the HK-2 cell line (ATCC® CRL-2190™) was performed according to previously described methods (Tian et al., 2007).

Plasmids and lentivirus production

BKPyV TAg and tAg cDNAs were inserted using the pLAS5W vectors (Academia Sinica, Taipei, Taiwan). Lentiviral basal expression plasmids were generated by PCR amplification of sequences corresponding to BKPyV TAg, using the primer Lenti-T-NheI-F (5′GCTAGCATGGATAAAGTTCTTAACA) and Lenti-T-EcoRI-R (5′GAATTCAGCTTTTGGGGGT), and PCR amplification of tAg using Lenti-T-NheI-F (5′GCTAGCATGGATAAAGTTCTTAACA) and Lenti-t-NsiI-R (5′ATGCATAGCAAGCTTTAGATCT). The amplified PCR products were subjected to 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the bands corresponding to TAg and tAg cDNAs were purified using EasyPrep gel and PCR extraction kit (BioTools, New Taipei, Taiwan), and then inserted into pLAS5w.Pbsd (for t antigen) and pLAS5w.Ppuro (for T antigen). Lentiviruses were packaged according to the ViraPower™ Lentiviral Packaging Mix kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and all obtained constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing. The transient TAg construction followed the previously outlined procedure (Li et al., 2010). The transient tAg was synthesized by BioTools company (BioTools, New Taipei, Taiwan). The transient transfection protocol was as described in a previous study (Li et al., 2010), and HK-2 cells were lysed upon completion of each experiment.

Lentivirus base of protein expressing in hRPTEC cell

The lentivirus carrying the early gene of BKV was introduced into hRPTEC cells. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium containing 20 μg/ml Blastidin for the selection of small t antigen-transformed cells, 10 μg/ml puromycin for the selection of Large T antigen-transformed cells. The medium with selective antibiotics was renewed every three days until the cells reached approximately 80 to 90% confluence in T75 flasks. The cell culture protocol followed the previously described method.

Quantitative measurement of BKPyV viral titer

The viral load of BKPyV present in the culture medium was determined using qPCR as previously described (Li et al., 2009). Approximately 80% confluent hRPTEC cells were cultured in 6-well plates (CLS3516, Corning, MA, USA) and treated with 10 μM U0126 for 24 h or without treatment. Cells were then infected with BKPyV (MOI = 0.5 FFU/cell) for 2 h, followed by 72 h of incubation with or without replacing the complete medium with U0126. At the end of the incubation period, the supernatant was collected to determine the amount of viral load.

Western blot analysis

Protein extractions and Western blot analyses were performed according to previously described methods (Tian and Phillips, 2003). The primary antibodies used were: anti-BKPyV TAg and tAg, which were generated by Leadgene Biomedical (Tainan, Taiwan); anti-VP1 (MAB3204-M19), obtained from Abnova (Taipei, Taiwan); anti-GAPDH (ab181602), purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); and anti-p-FAK Y397 (8556S), anti-p-SRC (2101S), anti-SRC (2123S), anti-p-ERK (9101S), and ERK (4695S), all purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA). In addition, anti-FAK (sc-1688) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (CA, USA). The blots were then incubated with an appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody for one hour at room temperature. The target protein signal was detected using the ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection kit (#RPN2232, GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK), and the signal was visualized using the UVP ChemStudio Plus (Analytik Jena Co. Jena, Germany).

Transwell migration assay

Cells cultured to 90% confluence in T25 flasks (LacZ, tAg, TAg, TAg/tAg) were treated with 10 μM U0126 for 24 h. Afterwards, they were suspended with 0.05% trypsin and seeded into the inner chamber of a transwell (5.0 μm pores, CLS3421, Corning, MA, USA), filled with 200 µL of serum-free DMEM/F12 (Gibco™ 11330032) and the outer chamber filled with 600 µL of complete medium in the inner chamber. After 3 h, the cells were fixed with methanol for 5 min, stained with Liu's stain for 2 min, and then washed twice with PBS. Cells present on the lower surface of the filter were counted in four random microscopic fields (200×), while cells on the upper surface were removed using a cotton swab.

Gelatin zymography

Gelatin zymography was performed according to the same method as in the previous study (Tian et al., 2007). The hRPTECs stablely expressing TAg, and tAg, were cultured to 90% confluence in T25 flasks and treated with U0126 for 24 h. The supernatants collected from the cells were analyzed by gelatin zymography.

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the means ± standard error. The unpaired Student's t-test analysis was applied to compare these data. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to represent a significant difference.

Results

Next, we investigated whether BKPyV TAg or tAg produced at an early stage of infection could activate ERK signal. HK-2 cells were transfected with plasmids expressing tAg, TAg or TAg/tAg for 24 h and the expression of phosphorylated ERK1/2 was assessed. Western blot analysis showed increased expression of phosphorylated ERK1/2 in cells expressing tAg or TAg compared to cells transfected with an empty plasmid (Fig. 1(c)). To further investigate the effects of TAg and tAg on host cell function, we established stable cell lines expressing TAg or tAg in hRPTECs. Consistent with the transient transfection results, stable expression of TAg or tAg resulted in a significant increase in the expression of phosphorylated ERK1/2 compared with control cells expressing LacZ (Fig. 1(d), (e)). These findings suggest that expression of TAg and tAg activates ERK signal.

Blocking ERK activity inhibits BKPyV infection

To investigate the potential role of ERK activation in BKPyV replication, we examined the effect of inhibiting the ERK cascade. Western blot analysis showed that the increase in phosphorylated ERK1/2 expression induced by BKPyV infection was attenuated after pretreatment of hRPTECs with the potent ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126 for 1 h and incubation for 72 h prior to BKPyV infection (1 × 106 copies/mL) (Fig. 2(a)). After 72 h of infection, the expression of phosphorylated ERK was significantly higher in BKPyV-infected cells compared with mock-infected cells, whereas cells treated with two different concentrations of U0126 exhibited decreased levels of phosphorylated ERK and a significant inhibition of viral protein expression. Furthermore, quantification of viral titers in the culture medium by qPCR showed that inhibition of ERK by U0126 resulted in reduced replication of BKPyV compared to treatment with DMSO (solvent control) (Fig. 2(b)). To assess the cell migration ability, we performed a transwell migration assay. Our results showed that the number of migrating TAg- and tAg-expressing cells was significantly increased compared to LacZ-expressing cells (Fig. 3(b) and (c)). The number of TAg-expressing cells was significantly higher than that of tAg-expressing cells. Treatment with U0126 resulted in a significant decrease in the number of migrating cells in both TAg- and tAg-expressing cells compared to the control group treated with the DMSO vehicle. These findings suggest that both TAg and tAg contribute to cell motility, and that the ERK signaling pathway plays a key role in this response.

Expression of TAg and tAg regulate focal adhesion molecules in renal proximal tubular cells

The ERK signaling pathway critically regulates focal adhesion complexes, which include focal adhesion kinase 1 (FAK) and SRC kinase, allowing them to interact with the extracellular matrix (ECM). This interaction is essential to promote cell migration (Toutant et al., 2000). To investigate the involvement of ERK-regulated cell adhesion molecules in TAg- and tAg-mediated cell migration, we examined the expression levels of phosphorylated FAK and SRC in stable TAg- and tAg-expressing cells. Western blot analysis showed that TAg- and tAg-expressing cells exhibited elevated expression levels of phosphorylated ERK1/2 accompanied by increased phosphorylated FAK and SRC compared to LacZ-expressing cells (Fig. 4). MMP-9 is the primary MMP responsible for ECM degradation via the ERK signaling pathway

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are zinc-dependent enzymes responsible for ECM degradation, and their activity plays a critical role in regulating cell migration and invasion. (Moulik et al., 2014; Nwogu et al., 2020) We investigated whether MMP-2 and MMP-9 are associated with ERK inhibition of migration of TAg- and tAg-expressing cells. Zymography results showed that the activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9 was significantly increase in culture medium collected from TAg-expressing cells compared to LacZ-expressing cells, with more pronounced fold changes in MMP-9 (Fig. 5(a)). In contrast, tAg-expressing cells did not show any change in MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity (Fig. 5(a) and (b)). We further examined whether the differences in MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity between TAg- and tAg-expressing cells were due to changes in protein expression. Western blot analysis showed that MMP-2 and MMP-9 protein levels were significantly higher in the culture medium collected from TAg-expressing cells compared to LacZ-expressing cells (Fig. 5(c)). In addition, intracellular MMP-9 protein expression was elevated in TAg-expressing cells, while intracellular MMP-2 protein expression remained similar in TAg and LacZ-expressing cells (Fig. 5(d)). In contrast, tAg-expressing cells did not show any difference in MMP-2 and MMP-9 protein expressions compared to LacZ-expressing cells, either in culture medium or cell lysates. In addition, treatment with U0126 significantly reduced MMP-9 protein expression in culture medium and cell lysates of TAg-expressing cells, while having no effect on MMP-2 protein expression. These findings suggest that in TAg-expressing cells, MMP-9 plays a more important role in ECM degradation compared to MMP-2, and that the increase in MMP-9 activity is dependent on ERK signaling.

Discussion

Seamone et al. previously demonstrated that activation of the ERK signaling pathway enhances BKPyV replication, but ERK activation during BKPyV infection has not been fully characterized (Seamone et al., 2010). DuShane et al. found that ERK signaling in JCPyV regulates promoter activity and TAg transcription and contributes significantly to viral replication (DuShane et al., 2018). In our study, we observed early ERK activation during BKPyV infection in two different human renal proximal tubular cell lines, hRPTECs and HK-2, suggesting that BKPyV infection activates the ERK signaling pathway. Since ERK activation promotes BKPyV infection, our data suggest that ERK activation triggered by BKPyV infection has an amplifying effect to enhance viral replication. The importance of this is further emphasized by the significant reduction in BKPyV replication after treatment with U0126, a potent ERK inhibitor.

Recent studies have shown that phosphorylation of ERK occurs in the early stage of JCPyV infection, consistent with viral attachment and entry events (Nelson et al., 2012; Querbes et al., 2004). Our study showed that BKPyV infection activates ERK activation at an early stage and that this effect diminished after 24 h, indicating the involvement of the early BKPyV proteins as the major mediators. Activation of ERK was also evident in cells transiently overexpressing of TAg or tAg and in stable TAg- or tAg-expressing cells. In contrast, transient overexpression of the major late protein VP1 did not induce ERK activation (data not shown). These findings confirm that TAg and tAg are the main triggers for BKPyV-induced ERK activation. However, it should be noted that, in contrast to recent studies, BKPyV infection in a lung cell line (HEL-299) did not lead to ERK phosphorylation (Seamone et al., 2010). This observation may be attributed to tissue tropism, but further investigations is needed to test this hypothesis.

ERK signaling regulates various cellular processes, such as proliferation, survival, growth, metabolism, migration, and differentiation (Lavoie et al., 2020). Our study not only revealed the regulatory impact of ERK signaling pathway on BKPyV replication but also highlighted its key role in cell migration. We observed an increase in the number of migrating cells in TAg- and tAg-expressing cells, which was significantly reduced after treatment with an ERK inhibitor. Previous reports have shown that BKPyV infection enhances the motility of bladder cancer cells such as T24 and HTB-9 cells (Zeng et al., 2020). We further provide evidence for the importance of the ERK signaling pathway in BKPyV-induced cell migration. These findings provide evidence and mechanisms that support the increased cell motility observed after BKPyV infection and suggest the potential therapeutic implications for BKPyV-associated cancers.

Cell migration involves dynamic changes in cell and ECM (extracellular matrix) interactions, and binding between the ECM and integrins triggers signaling pathways involving paxillin, FAK, and SRC kinase (Lavoie et al., 2020). Phosphorylation of paxillin by SRC allows recruitment of ERK to nascent focal adhesions, while FAK, through its interactions with talin and paxillin binds to the nascent focal adhesions, leading to autophosphorylation of Tyr397 and subsequent binding to SRC (Lavoie et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2007). In this study, consistent with the observed increase in the number of migrating cells, cells expressing TAg and tAg showed significant phosphorylation signals of SRC and FAKY397, exceeding the levels of control cells. These findings suggest that alterations in the focal adhesion complex plays a key role in this cellular response.

MMPs involved in ECM degradation play an important role in cell migration (Moulik et al., 2014). Dysregulation of MMPs is associated with various cancers, where overexpression of specific MMP proteins contributes to cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis (Gialeli et al., 2011; Kumagai et al., 1999). Previous studies have shown that tAg of Merkel cell polyomavirus can activate MMP-9 and promote cell migration (Nwogu et al., 2020). In contrast, our findings suggest that TAg of BKPyV regulates MMP-2 and MMP-9. Although both TAg and tAg exhibit similar properties, such as cell transformation and their requirement for viral replication, different functions have been reported. TAg binds to pRb and p53 to induce cell transformation, whereas tAg interacts with phosphatase 2A to regulate cell transformation (Baez et al., 2017). Our study revealed that both TAg and tAg enhanced ERK activation and phosphorylation of SRC and FAKY397. However, TAg had a more pronounced effect on cell migration compared to tAg. In addition, TAg, but not tAg, induced the expression of MMP-9 and MMP-2. These results suggest that TAg and tAg have different roles in cell migration.

Imbalance of the ERK pathway is prevalent in various cancers (Guo et al., 2020). Recent studies have linked BKPyV to human cancers (Kenan et al., 2015, 2017). MMP-mediated degradation of ECM not only promotes tumor invasion but also influences the behavior of tumor cells and contributes to cancer progression (Itoh and Nagase, 2002). Our previous investigation identified MMP-9 as an important MMP involved in epidermal growth factor and transforming growth factor-β migration of renal proximal tubular cells via the ERK pathway (Tian et al., 2007). In the present study, by blocking ERK signaling, we observed a substantial reduction in TAg-enhanced MMP-9 activity and expression, suggesting that the ERK signaling pathway also regulates TAg-enhanced MMP-9. Interestingly, the TAg-mediated increase in MMP-2 expression and activity was not significantly affected by ERK inhibition, suggesting the existence of an ERK-independent pathway. In addition, TNF-α has been reported to induce MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity (Han et al., 2001; Lee et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2012), and our recent study reported a 2-fold increase in TNF-α transcripts after BKPyV infection (Li et al., 2023). Further studies on BKPyV infection, TAg/tAg expression and MMP-2/MMP-9 regulation are currently underway. Both MMP-2 and MMP-9 play roles in ECM degradation, but they exhibit different functions in various cell types. Although MMP-9 deficiency is protective against hepatic perfusion-reperfusion injury, MMP-2 deficiency exacerbates this hepatic injury (Hamada et al., 2008; Kato et al., 2015). The results of this study suggest that BKPyV TAg regulates MMP-9 and MMP-2 through ERK-dependent and ERK-independent pathways, respectively.

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the involvement of TAg and tAg in the regulation of cell motility. We found that TAg acts as the primary regulatory protein, promoting MMP-9 expression and activity, phosphorylation of focal adhesion molecules, and facilitating host cell motility through the ERK signaling pathway.
