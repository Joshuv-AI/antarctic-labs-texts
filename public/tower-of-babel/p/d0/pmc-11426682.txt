Exploring Current Practices, Challenges, and Innovations in the Nutrition and Feeding of Neonates, Including the Use of Human Milk, Fortifiers, and Formula: A Clinical Study
Authors: Partha K. Chaudhuri, Abha Madhur, Kumar Anshu, Shiromani Kumari, Ashutosh, Jyotsna Singh, Barapatla Sarangam, Pratik Sarkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Neonatal nutrition plays a crucial role in the growth and development of infants, particularly in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) where specialized care is essential. This study aims to explore current practices, challenges, and innovations in the nutrition and feeding of neonates, focusing on the utilization of human milk, fortifiers, and formula.Materials and Methods:A retrospective clinical study was conducted at a tertiary care NICU over a period of two years. Data were collected from medical records of neonates admitted to the unit. Information regarding feeding practices, types of milk (human milk, fortified human milk, or formula), fortification protocols, and associated clinical outcomes were analyzed.Results:A total of 150 neonates were included in the study. The majority (85%) received some form of human milk during their NICU stay, with 60% exclusively receiving human milk. Among those receiving human milk, 40% required fortification with a human milk fortifier due to inadequate growth parameters. Formula supplementation was necessary in 25% of cases due to maternal lactation insufficiency or medical contraindications to human milk. The mean duration of parenteral nutrition was 10 days, with a median time to full enteral feeds of 14 days. Infants receiving exclusively human milk demonstrated a lower incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) compared to those receiving formula (5% vs. 12%, P < 0.05).Conclusion:Human milk remains the preferred source of nutrition for neonates in the NICU setting, associated with lower rates of NEC and improved clinical outcomes. However, fortification with human milk fortifiers or supplementation with formula is often necessary to meet the nutritional requirements of preterm infants. Further research is needed to optimize fortification protocols and improve long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

INTRODUCTION

Neonatal nutrition is of paramount importance for the optimal growth and development of infants, particularly in the vulnerable population of preterm and critically ill neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Adequate nutrition during the neonatal period is crucial for neurodevelopmental outcomes, immune function, and overall health.[1] Human milk is widely recognized as the gold standard for infant feeding due to its unique composition, including bioactive components such as immunoglobulins, growth factors, and prebiotics, which confer numerous health benefits.[2] However, preterm infants often face challenges in achieving adequate growth solely through human milk feeding, necessitating fortification with human milk fortifiers (HMFs) or supplementation with a preterm formula to meet their nutritional requirements.[3]

Despite the numerous benefits of human milk, barriers to its exclusive use in the NICU setting include maternal lactation insufficiency, inadequate volume production, and medical contraindications such as maternal infections or medications.[4] Additionally, preterm infants have higher nutrient requirements than term infants, further complicating the provision of adequate nutrition through human milk alone.[5] Thus, the optimal feeding strategy for preterm infants remains a topic of ongoing research and debate, with a focus on balancing the benefits of human milk with the need for additional nutrient supplementation.

This study aims to investigate current practices, challenges, and innovations in neonatal nutrition and feeding, with a specific focus on the utilization of human milk, fortifiers, and formula in the NICU setting. By examining feeding practices and associated clinical outcomes, we seek to contribute to the understanding of optimal nutritional strategies for preterm and critically ill neonates.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This retrospective clinical study was conducted at a tertiary care neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) over a period of two years.

Study population

The study included neonates admitted to the NICU during the study period.

Data collection

Data were collected from electronic medical records of eligible neonates. Information regarding feeding practices, types of milk (human milk, fortified human milk, or formula), fortification protocols, and associated clinical outcomes were extracted.

Feeding practices

Feeding practices included the type of milk (exclusive human milk, fortified human milk, or formula) administered to the neonates during their NICU stay.

Fortification protocol

For neonates receiving fortified human milk, details regarding the fortification protocol, including the type and amount of fortifier used, were documented.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes assessed included growth parameters (weight, length, head circumference), duration of parenteral nutrition, time to achieve full enteral feeds, and incidence of complications such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and sepsis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using appropriate statistical methods, including descriptive statistics for the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were made using appropriate statistical tests, with P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study contribute to the understanding of current practices and challenges in neonatal nutrition, particularly regarding the utilization of human milk, fortifiers, and formula in the NICU setting. Our results highlight several important points that warrant discussion.

One of the key findings of this study is the high prevalence of human milk utilization in the NICU, with 85% of neonates receiving some form of human milk during their hospitalization. This is consistent with existing evidence emphasizing the importance of human milk as the preferred source of nutrition for preterm and critically ill infants.[1] Human milk provides numerous health benefits, including immunomodulatory properties, protection against infections, and optimal growth and development.[2] The American Academy of Pediatrics[3] recommends exclusive human milk feeding for preterm infants, as it has been associated with a lower incidence of NEC and other complications.

Fortification of human milk with human milk fortifiers (HMFs) is a common practice in NICUs to meet the higher nutrient requirements of preterm infants. Our study found that 43.3% of neonates received fortified human milk, with liquid HMF being the most commonly used fortifier. This reflects the need to supplement human milk to ensure adequate protein, energy, and micronutrient intake for optimal growth.[4] However, challenges exist in achieving appropriate fortification levels, as previous studies have reported variability in fortifier composition and inconsistency in nutrient delivery.[5]

Despite the benefits of human milk, formula supplementation was necessary in 16.7% of cases in our study. Formula supplementation may be indicated in cases of maternal lactation insufficiency, medical contraindications to human milk, or when infants fail to thrive on human milk alone.[6] However, formula feeding has been associated with an increased risk of NEC and other adverse outcomes compared to human milk feeding.[7] Our findings support this, as neonates receiving formula supplementation had a higher incidence of NEC compared to those receiving exclusive human milk.

The optimal feeding strategy for preterm infants remains a topic of ongoing research and debate. Strategies to improve human milk utilization and fortification protocols are needed to optimize nutritional outcomes and reduce the need for formula supplementation. This may include standardized fortification protocols, improved fortifier formulations, and enhanced support for maternal lactation.[8]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study underscores the importance of human milk in neonatal nutrition and the challenges associated with achieving optimal growth and development in preterm infants. Further research is needed to address these challenges and improve feeding practices in the NICU setting.
