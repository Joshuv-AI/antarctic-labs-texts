Acquired Multiple Acyl‐Coenzyme A Dehydrogenase Deficiency Associated With Sertraline in Sweden—A Nationwide Population‐Based Study
Authors: Sofie Sunebo, Emil Nilsson, Martin Engvall, Carola Hedberg‐Oldfors, Yan Shen, Inger Nennesmo, Olof Danielsson, Anders Oldfors, Hanna Appelqvist, Ulrika Lindgren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRecent studies describe an association between sertraline and acquired late‐onset multiple acyl coenzyme A dehydrogenase deficiency (MADD), a rare disorder affecting fatty acid metabolism. MADD was previously considered solely a genetic disorder caused by recessive pathogenic variants mainly in the ETFDH gene.MethodsA nationwide population‐based registry search was conducted to identify adult patients with sertraline‐associated MADD in Sweden 2014–2024. Patients with muscle biopsy reports of lipid storage myopathy or biochemical investigations showing acylcarnitine profile suggesting MADD were included. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed.ResultsWe identified 40 (30/10 female/male) patients using sertraline when diagnosed with MADD. Median age at symptom onset was 42 years and muscle weakness was the most common first symptom. All patients reported muscle symptoms during the disease course. Extra‐muscular symptoms, including sensory disturbance (29 patients) and fatigue (18 patients), were common. Most had lipid accumulation in muscle fibers, an abnormal acylcarnitine profile, a myopathic electromyography pattern, and elevated creatine kinase. Cyanocobalamin, folic acid, and proton‐pump inhibitors were the most frequent other medications at diagnosis. Five patients carried a heterozygous ETFDH variant. All patients were treated with riboflavin. Almost all (38/39) experienced clinical improvement. Acylcarnitine profile improved in all patients (31/31) with available data. The mean incidence was 1.24 patients per 100,000 adults receiving sertraline treatment per year.ConclusionsAcquired MADD associated with sertraline is the most common type of adult‐onset MADD in Sweden. The disease is generally responsive to riboflavin treatment. Patients presenting with muscle symptoms during sertraline therapy should undergo adequate evaluation to exclude MADD.

Introduction

Sertraline, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), is one of the most commonly used antidepressant drugs worldwide and is prescribed to 4.1% of the Swedish adult population [1]. Known muscular side effects include myalgia, muscle weakness, and rare cases of rhabdomyolysis [2]. Recently, an association between sertraline and acquired multiple acyl‐coenzyme A dehydrogenase deficiency (MADD), a fatty acid oxidation disorder affecting the mitochondrial beta oxidation, has been described by us and others [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

Fatty acid oxidation disorders lead to deficient energy production, and symptoms often derive from tissues with a high energy demand. The clinical spectrum is heterogeneous, and patients with a genetic cause usually develop symptoms during childhood [10]. Late‐onset fatty acid oxidation defects often present with myopathic symptoms, such as muscle weakness, myalgia, fatigue, exercise intolerance, or rhabdomyolysis [10]. In some cases, symptoms only present during demanding situations, such as fasting, infection, or physiological stress. Diagnosis is based upon a typical pattern of elevated acylcarnitines in blood, muscle biopsy showing lipid accumulation, and genetic testing. Notably, muscle biopsy and acylcarnitines can be normal despite a symptomatic disease [11].

Genetic MADD is mainly caused by pathogenic variants affecting electron‐transfer flavoprotein: ubiquinone oxidoreductase (ETFQO, encoded by ETFDH) or electron‐transfer flavoprotein (encoded by ETFA and ETFB). Variants in the genes FLAD1, SLC25A32, SLC52A1, SLC52A2, SLC52A3, and COASY have also been reported to cause MADD‐like disease [12, 13]. Three subgroups of MADD are described, where types I and II have neonatal onset, and type III (late‐onset MADD) from early childhood to late adulthood. Genetic late‐onset MADD (OMIM: #231680) is one of the most prevalent adult‐onset lipid storage myopathies and has a varying presentation including muscle weakness, exercise intolerance, and metabolic acidosis. Symptoms may arise in times of high energy consumption due to an imbalance between fatty acid oxidation flux and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) levels [14]. FAD is an essential coenzyme for many proteins functioning in diverse metabolic pathways, including beta‐oxidation and the electron transport chain. In most cases, genetic late‐onset MADD is treatable with high‐dose riboflavin [15, 16]. Riboflavin, a precursor of FAD, restores FAD synthesis and increases the stability of the ETFQO protein, thereby restoring the balance [14].

While no larger population‐based studies are available, emerging data suggest that sertraline is a common cause of MADD without a genetic explanation in adults [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. As of June 2025, the European Medicines Agency recommends MADD‐like disorder to be added to the sertraline product information as an adverse effect [17].

The aim of this nationwide population‐based study is to describe the characteristics of all adults diagnosed with sertraline‐associated MADD between 2014 and 2024, including comorbidities, investigational results and response to treatment. We also aim to describe the epidemiology of sertraline‐associated MADD in Sweden.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Medical Records

Patients were identified from the Swedish national referral centres for muscle biopsy (Departments of Neurology or Pathology at Linköping University Hospital, Karolinska University Hospital (Stockholm), Örebro University Hospital, Skåne University Hospital (Lund), Uppsala University Hospital and Sahlgrenska University Hospital (Gothenburg)) and metabolic disorder diagnostics (Centre for Inherited Metabolic Diseases at Karolinska University Hospital and the Department of Clinical Chemistry at Sahlgrenska University Hospital), covering the entire population of approximately 10.5 million inhabitants.

Inclusion criteria were: (a) lipid storage myopathy or acylcarnitine profile suggesting MADD (Figure 1A,B), (b) date of diagnosis from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2024, and (c) ≥ 18 years of age at diagnosis. Exclusion criteria were (a) higher probability of another diagnosis causing lipid storage myopathy or acylcarnitine profile suggesting MADD, by consensus of two or more of the authors (Table S1), (b) verified genetic cause of MADD or MADD‐like disease, (c) incomplete workup and (d) no sertraline use (Figure 1C). The date of diagnosis was defined as the date of the muscle biopsy, or, if muscle biopsy was normal or not performed, as the date of the first acylcarnitine profile consistent with MADD. A complete workup was defined as clinical examination, analysis of acylcarnitines, muscle biopsy and genetic analysis.

All available medical records, muscle biopsies and genetic analyses were reviewed, including data concerning muscle weakness, disease course, use of medications, heredity, neurophysiological study results and laboratory data. In case of missing data, relevant information was obtained directly from the patients.

Muscle Biopsies, Biochemistry, Neurophysiology and Genetic Analysis

Muscle biopsies were performed as open or percutaneous conchotome biopsies as part of clinical routine. The tissue was fresh frozen and stored at −80°C. Analysis of morphology, enzyme histochemistry, and immune histochemistry was performed according to standard protocols [18].

Plasma acylcarnitines were analyzed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry as a part of clinical routine. The reference intervals were based on a Swedish population from the Centre for Inherited Metabolic Diseases, Karolinska University Hospital, Stockholm. Acylcarnitine C4‐OH was not included in this study due to lack of a reference interval. Neurofilament light chain (NFL) was analyzed in cerebrospinal fluid and plasma as a part of clinical routine at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory, Department of Clinical Chemistry, Sahlgrenska University Hospital, Mölndal, Sweden.

Neurophysiological investigations were performed at specialized neurophysiological units according to local clinical routine.

Total genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue or blood according to standard protocols. Whole exome sequencing, whole genome sequencing, and analysis of mitochondrial DNA were performed as previously described. Briefly, potentially damaging variants were identified using each unit's routine panel for metabolic disease (> 127 genes) including ETFDH, ETFA, ETFB, FLAD1, SLC25A32, SLC52A1, SLC52A2, SLC52A3, and COASY [3, 4]. Genetically caused disease was defined as one variant with dominant inheritance or two homozygous or compound heterozygous variants with recessive inheritance, classified as likely pathogenic or pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [19].

Statistics

Epidemiological data on the Swedish population and pharmaceutical use were retrieved from Statistics Sweden [20], and The National Board of Health and Welfare [1], respectively. Population data were based on all inhabitants ≥ 18 years of age. However, data on pharmaceutical use were based on all inhabitants ≥ 20 years of age, because Swedish official data on pharmaceutical use is only available for five‐year age groups. The individuals retrieving a medication at a pharmacy at least once during a calendar year were counted as users of the medication. For patient events with unknown exact dates, the 15th of every month and June 30th of every year were used for calculations.

Calculations were made in Graph Pad Prism 10.0.2 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ethics

This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority, approval numbers 2023–06631‐01 and 2023–07591‐01, addendums 2024–06080‐02 and 2025–02442‐02. All included patients gave their written informed consent to participate.

Results

Patient Characteristics

The registry search identified a total of 86 patients with muscle biopsy showing lipid storage myopathy or acylcarnitine profile suggesting MADD in Sweden during 2014–2024. Of these, 40 patients fulfilled the criteria and were included in this study (Figure 1). Two of the patients were first‐degree relatives, but no other patients had a family history of metabolic or neuromuscular disease. No patients reported consanguinity. A limited diet was documented in four patients. None had a history of alcohol or drug abuse. Three patients described an infection temporally associated with symptom onset. Eighteen of the patients were partially described in earlier studies by Sunebo et al., and Hedberg‐Oldfors et al. [3, 4]. Symptoms

Muscular symptoms were reported in all patients, most frequently exercise intolerance, proximal muscle weakness, and myalgia (Table 1). Muscle weakness and exercise intolerance were also the most common first symptoms (Figure 2). Fifteen patients used a wheelchair during the disease course.

First reported symptom(s) in patients with sertraline‐associated MADD. Twenty‐eight patients reported one symptom at the time of onset, and 10 patients reported two or three symptoms starting at the same time. Other symptoms included fatigue (two patients), dyspnoea (two patients), and episodic vomiting (one patient). Two patients with symptom onset before the start of sertraline are not included. n, number of patients.

The most prevalent extra‐muscular symptoms included sensory disturbances, in many cases neuropathic pain in hands and feet. Signs and symptoms from the central nervous system included hyperreflexia in three patients and nystagmus in one patient. Eighteen patients had mental fatigue, in some cases pronounced. Four patients had a clinical diagnosis of metabolic crisis during the disease course. Dyspnoea was more common in women and dysphagia in men, but otherwise there were no apparent differences between men and women.

In 38 patients, symptom onset occurred after the start of sertraline, whereas one man and one woman described onset before start of sertraline use. The man had adult onset of episodic vomiting, sensory disturbance, myalgia, fatigue, and muscle weakness, which progressed after the start of sertraline. The woman had myalgia and exercise intolerance since childhood and no signs of aggravation after starting sertraline. None of the two had been examined with acylcarnitine profile or muscle biopsy before start of sertraline.

Diagnostics

Lipid storage, from slightly increased to very pronounced, was seen in muscle biopsy in 36 of 40 patients (Table 1). Both patients with symptom onset before the start of sertraline had a normal muscle biopsy. A MADD‐like acylcarnitine profile was seen during the disease course in all but four patients (Table 1 and Figure 3a). Three of these patients showed carnitine deficiency, and one patient who had started riboflavin treatment 1 week earlier showed a normal profile. Primary carnitine deficiency was excluded by genetic testing. Organic acids in urine were abnormal in 14 of 28 tested patients. Five patients had results indicating MADD, four showed increased discharge of lactate, and five showed unspecific changes. The maximal creatine kinase value ranged from 0.2–80 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), with a median value of 2 times the ULN.

Electromyography showed myopathic changes in 27 of the 37 examined patients, and neuropathy was seen on nerve conduction studies in 14 of 36 patients (Table 1). Repetitive nerve stimulation was performed in 13 patients with myasthenia gravis as a differential diagnosis and was normal in all. Magnetic resonance tomography was performed on muscles of the thigh (five patients) or whole body (three patients). All had signs of affected thigh muscles with oedema or fatty infiltration. Muscles in the lower leg and the erector spinae muscles were affected in all three patients examined. Some of the patients had asymmetric muscle involvement. Echocardiography was normal in 18 patients. Four patients had abnormal findings, including a slightly dilated left atrium, possible septal hypokinesia, slightly decreased systolic function, and hypertrabeculation of the apical part of the left ventricle.

Other laboratory findings included temporarily mild to moderately increased levels of liver enzymes in 19 patients. In 18 of them, no liver disease was diagnosed, and the increase was considered a sign of muscle injury. One patient had liver cirrhosis. Increased neurofilament light chain (NFL) in cerebrospinal fluid or plasma, a sign of neuronal axonal damage, was found in six of 13 investigated patients, in a range of 1.1–83 times the ULN. Twelve patients had been examined with magnetic resonance tomography of the brain. Two had nonspecific periventricular lesions, one had diffuse high‐intensity white matter lesions, one had signs of normal pressure hydrocephalus and two had a pineal cyst. The remaining patients had normal findings. Spirometry was performed in 14 patients and was within the normal range in all but three who showed signs of obstructive lung disease.

Medications and Comorbidities

The median age at start of sertraline, symptom onset, and diagnosis was lower for women than men, but there was no difference in the median dosage of sertraline. The length of treatment with sertraline before symptom onset varied between 0.3–20 years. There was no evident association between the duration of sertraline treatment before symptom onset and increased levels of NFL. Plasma concentration of sertraline was tested in two patients and was within therapeutic range in both. Worsening of symptoms was described as associated with an increased sertraline dose by 10 women and one man and was reported to be associated with simultaneous use of atorvastatin in two patients.

Twelve patients had tried another antidepressant before sertraline. Ten had used an SSRI or a selective serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), most commonly venlafaxine or fluoxetine (five patients each). One patient had earlier been diagnosed with serotonin syndrome during simultaneous treatment with fluoxetine and bupropion. At the time of diagnosis, duloxetine was used in combination with sertraline by one patient, but none of the other patients used any SSRI or SNRI except sertraline. Of the included patients, 75% were women, compared to 64% of adults prescribed sertraline in Sweden [1].

Other commonly used drug classes at diagnosis were proton‐pump inhibitors (17 patients, 43%) and beta blockers (9 patients, 23%), compared to 12% and 13% in the general population [1]. Cyanocobalamin and folic acid were used by 45% (18 patients) and 43% (17 patients) of patients respectively, compared to 6.0% and 3.7% in the general population (Table 2) [1]. Psychiatric comorbidity included depression (20 patients) and anxiety (14 patients). Other common comorbidities were chronic pain (8 patients), hypothyroidism (8), asthma (7), hypertension (7), and diabetes mellitus type 2 (5). For detailed information, see Tables S3 and S4.

Treatment and Follow‐Up

During the follow‐up time (median 2.5 years, range 0.4–12 years), all patients started riboflavin treatment (median dose 300 mg daily, range 15–600 mg daily), and 31 patients discontinued sertraline. Clinical improvement was seen in 38 of the 39 patients with available data, of which five fully recovered. None of the patients with increased NFL fully recovered. Remaining symptoms consisted of muscle weakness, neuropathy, as well as fatigue. Acylcarnitines in plasma improved in all 31 patients with available data (Figures 3 and 4). Both patients with symptom onset before starting sertraline responded clinically to simultaneous initiation of riboflavin and discontinuation of sertraline. Four patients received treatment with carnitine before riboflavin without any clinical effect. Simultaneously with riboflavin treatment, two patients received carnitine and six coenzyme Q10 without any apparent additive clinical improvement compared to riboflavin treatment alone. After sertraline discontinuation, at least 11 patients were started on another antidepressant (bupropion, mirtazapine, escitalopram, fluoxetine or duloxetine) without any deterioration of muscle symptoms.

Genetic Variants

Investigation of genes associated with MADD, other lipid storage myopathies and riboflavin transporter deficiency identified five patients (6.3% of alleles) with a heterozygous variant in the ETFDH gene (c.431 T>G, c.498G>A, c.1084G>A, c.1285G>C and c.1285+1G>A). These were classified as likely pathogenic or pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (Table S5) [19]. No likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant was found in the other examined genes.

For the five patients with a likely pathogenic or pathogenic ETFDH variant, the median age at the start of sertraline was lower (24 years) compared to those without such variants (34 years). The dose of sertraline and the time with sertraline treatment before symptom onset were approximately the same among patients with or without an ETFDH variant. Four of five patients with an ETFDH variant had an obvious deterioration of symptoms related to increased dosage of sertraline. All five patients had lipid accumulation in the muscle biopsy, elevated acylcarnitines, myopathic electromyographic findings, and elevated levels of creatine kinase. All improved by riboflavin treatment.

Epidemiology

Sertraline was prescribed to 4.1% of the population ≥ 20 years of age in Sweden 2024 (approximately 330,000 individuals), an increase from 2.8% in 2014 (Figure 5) [1]. The mean incidence for sertraline‐associated MADD during 2014–2024 was 1.24 persons per 100,000 treated persons aged ≥ 20 per year, corresponding to 0.44 patients per million adult inhabitants per year. The mean incidence of genetic late‐onset MADD was 0.077 per million adult inhabitants per year during the same period.

Discussion

We describe 40 patients with adult‐onset MADD associated with sertraline intake in a population‐based, nationwide study covering 11 years. This study provides detailed information on the clinical characteristics, diagnostics, and epidemiology of these patients.

The mean incidence of acquired MADD associated with sertraline was 1.24 patients per 100,000 inhabitants ≥ 20 years of age treated with sertraline, which was much higher than that of adult‐onset genetic MADD during the same period. Our data support earlier reports of sertraline‐associated MADD being more common in women [5, 8]. The incidence of sertraline‐associated MADD seemed to increase over time, with the highest incidence seen in 2024. This might be related to improved diagnostic methods and an increased awareness of MADD associated with sertraline following the first description in 2023 [21], but environmental and genetic factors that affect FAD metabolism must also be considered [14], especially since genetic MADD also appeared more frequently during the later part of the study period. The impact of environmental factors might also contribute to the large variation in time from the start of sertraline to symptom onset. Sertraline is recommended as a first‐line pharmacological treatment of depression and anxiety in Sweden (2025) and is one of the most prescribed antidepressants in the Western world. Even though only a minority of patients treated with sertraline develop MADD‐like disease, an effort needs to be made to understand these patients' increased susceptibility.

While this study did not show an evident trigger for the group as a whole, many different factors may contribute to the development of the disease. Poor nutritional status has previously been suggested to increase the risk of sertraline‐associated MADD [5, 6]. Four of the patients in our study had a documented restricted diet. None had clinical symptoms indicating riboflavin deficiency. However, many patients were prescribed cyanocobalamin and folic acid, which might suggest a general vitamin deficiency or a malnourished state. Interestingly, no nutritional or genetic susceptibility was found in the two individuals with symptom onset before sertraline administration.

The use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) was more frequent in our study than in the general population. Proton pump inhibitors are also available without prescription in Sweden, and the true frequency might be higher. An increased risk of vitamin and mineral deficiencies has been associated with PPIs. These risks are considered relatively low but may be of relevance in elderly and malnourished individuals [22]. Riboflavin cannot be synthesized by humans, and dietary intake and intestinal absorption are therefore necessary. Absorption of riboflavin is pH‐dependent [23] and occurs via riboflavin transporter 3, located in the proximal small intestine [24]. PPIs increase the luminal pH in the proximal duodenum [23]. While the effects of PPIs on riboflavin uptake have not been studied, it may be speculated that the use of PPIs decreases absorption of riboflavin, leading to diminished FAD levels and resulting in an imbalance between FAD levels and fatty acid oxidation flux.

The allele frequency of a likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant in EFTDH was 0.063 in this study. Demetriou et al. recently reported an allele frequency of 0.135 for potentially damaging variants in ETFDH in patients with a clinical and biochemical diagnosis of MADD (7 of 26 patients heterozygous), and an allele frequency of 0.007 in the general population based on data from gnomAD [8]. It can be hypothesized that these variants confer an increased risk of developing sertraline‐associated MADD. This is also in line with earlier studies suggesting that patients with a single heterozygous pathogenic variant in ETFDH can be symptomatic carriers [25]. In this context, it is of interest to note that muscle tissue in patients with sertraline‐associated MADD without any heterozygous pathogenic ETFDH variant showed significantly reduced levels of ETFQO protein compared to normal controls [4]. In our study, two patients without any ETFDH variant were first‐degree relatives, suggesting that yet unknown genetic factors may contribute.

The mental fatigue described by several patients might indicate involvement of the central nervous system. There are case descriptions of encephalopathy in late‐onset MADD [26], but reports on measurements of NFL in genetic MADD are scarce. Six patients in this study had increased levels of NFL, in three cases very high (> 29 times the ULN), supporting involvement of the central nervous system. Further studies on levels of NFL, disease course and outcome in late‐onset MADD with and without genetic cause are warranted.

Neuropathy was a frequent symptom in this study, affecting 39% of patients. This is similar to Jones et al. reporting neuropathy in two of four patients with sertraline‐associated MADD [9]. Neuropathy in genetic MADD has been described in case reports or small cohorts [27, 28, 29, 30], while a larger study showing neurogenic EMG findings had no information about symptoms of neuropathy [31]. Both genetic and sertraline‐associated MADD have been reported to mimic Guillain‐Barre syndrome [8, 32]. Further studies are needed to investigate the occurrence of neuropathy in both genetic and sertraline‐associated MADD.

An enhanced understanding of initial symptoms and other clinical characteristics described in this study can aid clinicians to recognize sertraline‐associated MADD at an early stage. When a suspicion of sertraline‐associated MADD arises, muscle strength should be tested and creatine kinase analyzed. It is, however, important to note that both creatine kinase and electromyography can be normal. If the suspicion of MADD remains, we recommend that the patient is subjected to further evaluation. This should include both plasma acylcarnitines and muscle biopsy, since none of these diagnostic investigations showed 100% sensitivity. Genetic testing should subsequently be conducted to distinguish sertraline‐associated MADD from genetically inherited forms.

The response of riboflavin treatment in sertraline‐associated MADD has been described as less pronounced than in genetic MADD [5, 6]. In our study, 97% of patients with available data reported clinical improvement after riboflavin treatment and sertraline discontinuation, but only 13% of patients fully recovered. Riboflavin treatment improved the acylcarnitine profile and further improvement was observed after discontinuation of sertraline. Notably, several patients had remaining slight deviations in acylcarnitines despite adequate treatment and clinical improvement or even full clinical recovery. We suggest that high‐dose riboflavin treatment and cessation of sertraline should be tried in all individuals with sertraline‐associated MADD. Future studies with a longer follow‐up time are needed to further evaluate long‐term treatment response.

Earlier studies on sertraline‐associated MADD have been performed on regional or single‐centre cohorts [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Sweden is well suited for population‐based studies due to the centralized care for neuromuscular and metabolic disorders, public health care accessible for all inhabitants, available population data, and statistics of drug use. However, this study is limited by its retrospective design. Investigations varied over time and by hospital. Data on riboflavin levels, body mass index, and details of nutritional status were not available for analysis. In a few cases of missing data, information was obtained directly from the patients, which introduces a risk of recall bias. Patients who could not be reached or who declined participation were not included, nor were individuals with mild nonspecific derangement of acylcarnitines. Patients with mild symptoms or minor pathological alterations may remain undiagnosed or might not have been identified using our search strategy. Therefore, the incidence in this study is probably an underestimation.

In conclusion, this study further supports the occurrence of MADD‐like disease as a rare adverse effect of sertraline. The incidence of sertraline‐associated MADD is considerably higher than that of genetic MADD among adults in Sweden. This study outlines the clinical characteristics of acquired MADD associated with sertraline and shows that the condition responds well to riboflavin treatment. This nationwide population‐based study may aid health care providers in identifying and treating these patients at an early stage to prevent severe iatrogenic harm.
