Biomechanics of custom glenoid components in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty—a narrative review of concepts for surgeons
Authors: Yash Sewpaul, Puneet Monga, Martin Petermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe glenoid bone loss remains a major challenge in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, as conventional implants often fail to achieve stable fixation. Three-dimensional (3D) printed, custom-made glenoid implants provide a patient-specific solution by optimizing bone–implant congruence, fixation strategy, and biomechanics. This narrative review synthesizes biomechanical principles and surgical considerations underpinning custom glenoid design, including central peg fixation, porous surfaces, bone–implant contact area, and the importance of computed tomography-based pre-operative planning. Current evidence suggests that implant stability is improved by secure central fixation, porous press-fit interfaces, and avoidance of excessive lateralization, which increases torque and failure risk. Custom implants allow tailored peg and screw placement to enhance fixation in compromised bone. Further studies are required to validate these principles and define long-term outcomes.

The bone-deficient glenoid remains a challenge in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) (Fig. 1). Custom-made glenoid implants represent a potential solution for addressing such bony deficiencies.7 Conventional glenoid implants typically feature a flat, convex, or augmented bone-facing surface, with one or more central pegs, compression screws, peripheral anti-torsion screws, or both. These implants are designed for conventional patterns of glenohumeral arthropathy.31 Optimal glenoid bone-implant interface congruence is achieved through appropriate concave reaming of the native glenoid, guided by the available bone stock. These techniques achieve sufficient primary stability of the glenoid implant in the absence of large bony defects.3 Although no specific threshold for required bone contact has been established, insufficient contact may compromise primary stability and hinder osseointegration.23 In cases of severe bone loss, achieving both primary implant stability and accurate positioning may not be feasible with standard designs, thereby justifying the use of custom implants.21Figure 1Three-dimensional reconstruction of scapula viewed from the anterolateral position with a severely bone-deficient glenoid due to shoulder arthroplasty infection and loosening.

Alternative options exist to address bony defects, including bony augmentation and eccentric glenoid baseplate placement.6,10,30 However, in cases of severe bone deficiency, achieving reliable primary stability is challenging as substantial bone loss compromises both fixation and intended implant position. The need to balance these 2 objectives underscores the complexity of such reconstructions. A minimum of 10 mm of glenoid penetration by the central peg is desirable to achieve primary stability.22 Additionally, the width of the glenoid vault reduces with advancing glenoid bone loss, thereby predisposing to suboptimal primary stability of conventional implants. The recent introduction of 3-dimensional (3D)-printed custom glenoid implants has shown promising clinical results and demonstrated sufficient primary stability.2,4,11,27

When considering computer-aided design for a custom-made glenoid implant, implant design, biomechanics, and additional surgical steps specific to the clinical case must be addressed, visualized, and discussed before manufacturing. The term “surgery before the surgery” can aptly describe this phase of presurgical planning. While both a design engineer and a surgeon contribute to the implant design, the surgeon ultimately holds responsibility for the treatment of the patient, making effective collaboration essential. Consequently, it is beneficial for the surgeon to understand the biomechanical principles of rTSA and their application to the design and fixation of implants created through additive manufacturing. The biomechanics of fixation and function of custom-made glenoid implants have not been published in the available literature. This review will discuss the biomechanics of custom glenoid implants and the implications for design and pre-operative planning.

Biomechanics of rTSA

Dr. Paul Grammont introduced novel concepts in reverse geometry shoulder prosthesis design in 1985 to address the failures of rTSA implants seen throughout the 1970s. The Grammont design placed the center of rotation (COR) at the surface of the glenoid.13, 14, 15 Even as this revolutionary change enhanced survivorship of such a design, mechanical challenges were observed related to such medialization of the COR. These included scapular notching, instability, and reduced range of motion. Since then, designs have evolved to lateralize the COR.16

In rTSA, shear forces are created predominantly by the deltoid muscle directed through the COR and work approximately in the cranial direction. A compressive force is present and is created by muscular activity (deltoid wrapping). The glenoid fixation (peg, screws–primarily friction, secondarily bony ingrowth) provides stability to resist the shear force and aid in load sharing. The compressive force acts in line with the glenoid fixation; shear forces act perpendicular to the glenoid fixation. A fixed COR exists due to implant constraint. Both compressive and shear forces exist when the arm is moved. These are always translated through the COR; however, the balance of compressive and shear forces changes throughout the range of motion.3 The Grammont-style design placed the COR as the bone-implant interface, reducing shear forces and thus stabilizing the bone-implant interface (Fig. 2). In lateralized designs, a lever arm between the bone–implant interface and COR is created. This results in shear forces generating a torque force at the glenoid baseplate, thereby increasing the mechanical demands on fixation (Fig. 3).Figure 2Diagram of a Grammont-style prosthesis. Achieving primary stability

An implant can be defined as mechanically stable if there is less than 150 μm of movement.26 Movement below this threshold allows bony ingrowth into a porous-coated metal surface on the implant. This definition does not encompass inter-prosthetic stability, such as the tendency for dislocation or subluxation of the implant. Intraoperatively, this amount of movement is impossible to quantify, with surgeons only able to detect gross instability of an implant. Therefore, surgeons must rely on fixation capabilities for implant stability.29

Whenever a force is applied to the glenoid prosthesis, the implant can behave in 3 different ways: it can slide, tilt/rotate, or remain stable. The interaction of these torques—sliding, tilting, and rotational forces—is depicted in Fig. 4. Movement can be avoided if a tight fit between the implant and bone can be achieved. In practice, this is achieved by inserting one or more pegs and screws into corresponding peg holes drilled into the native glenoid. In order to obtain primary stability, it is necessary for the glenoid to have a congruent fit of the implant and provide sufficient compression.32 Every glenoid implant—standard, revision, or custom-made—must combine principles of fixation to provide sufficient primary stability. With custom-made glenoid implants, it is often the case that due to metallic lateralization the COR is not on the bone–implant interface, hence creating a torque that potentially causes rotation. Whether an implant will more likely tilt or slide depends on various preconditions, like pivot points and the friction coefficient between the bony surface and the implant. Despite the advancements in computed tomography (CT) scanning and 3D printing, potential inaccuracies may arise in the manufacturing process, although such errors are unlikely to be clinically significant. Sliding of the glenoid implant is uncommon in rTSA but becomes more likely when managing severe glenoid bone loss with a custom-made implant. Two factors help mitigate this risk: a porous implant surface and a secure form fit, typically achieved by a central peg anchor at the medial unaltered glenoid. Notably, a glenoid implant with a central peg provides greater stability than one without.19 Patient-specific instrumentation-guided reaming and press fit impaction optimize fixation by preserving the medial cortical bone and ensuring a precise central peg, preventing implant sliding. In the authors' experience, a central glenoid peg is mandatory when using a custom-made glenoid implant.

Caution must be taken when drilling the central peg hole. Severe bone loss may result in a narrow bony boundary around the drill hole, thus risking a fracture of the bony margin by applying too much force. Such a fracture may result in the inability to sufficiently fixate the implant, hence failure of the surgery.

Implant tilting may occur when the COR is not on the bone–implant interface. A central peg mitigates tilting provided that it is sufficiently fixed. Tilting does not occur if all torque forces are neutralized. In the coronal plane, there are 2 main forces that are at work on an rTSA: shear and compression force. Three variables of this equation can potentially be influenced. First, the lever arm of the implant can be reduced by opting for a reasonable metallic lateralization (Fig. 5).12 Lateralization has long been the subject of biomechanical investigations; over-lateralization of the glenoid can increase shear forces across the implant, thus increasing acromial strain and therefore increasing the risk of acromial stress fracture.9 The combination of a custom glenoid implant with a lateralized rTSA design can result in a high metallic lever arm and lead to potential failure. Second, stability can be increased by increasing the distance between the contact points of the implant (Fig. 6). By increasing the area and distance between the points of fixation, larger shear forces can be tolerated before excessive movement of the implant. Therefore, implant stability can be increased if the resulting force is placed at the bone–implant interface. Rotational malalignment may lead to multiple areas of spot welds rather than a large area of contact, leading to instability. Malrotation of the implant is a potential risk for missing out scapular spine fixation as a whole. Fixation of the scapular spine is also supplementary to the glenoid surface and provides stability posteriorly in the axial plane.18 Addition of a second wire to prevent such malrotation is a valuable addition to the surgical steps to minimize the risk of such malrotation.1 The base of the coracoid can additionally serve as an additional fixation and can be consistently utilized.

Considerations for positioning

There are 6 degrees of freedom that define the glenoid implant positioning. In cases without severe glenoid bone deficiency, the glenoid surface or glenoid rim serves as an orientation for implant positioning. When considering the glenoid, there are 3 translational movements—anteroposterior (anterior and posterior translation), mediolateral (medial and lateral translation), and superoinferior (superior and inferior translation)—as well as 3 rotational movements: pitch (anteversion and retroversion), yaw (internal and external rotation), and tilt (superior and inferior tilt).3,34 In primary shoulder arthroplasty, anteroposterior and superoinferior offset of the glenoid is adjusted by the entry point of the central K-wire, and therefore the positioning of the central peg or screw. The mediolateral offset is determined by the reaming depth in primary cases and the amount of glenoid joint line restoration with metallic augmentation with custom-made glenoid implants. The position of the implant relative to the glenoid face determines the rotatory alignment.

Glenoid malpositioning is associated with higher complication rates such as inferior notching, dislocation, and early implant failure.5 Precise implant positioning is therefore a critical surgical goal—not only in primary cases, but particularly in revision shoulder arthroplasty where anatomical landmarks may be distorted. A single operation that ensures precise implant placement can help avoid complications and reduce the need for further surgery. Preliminary literature on custom-made glenoid implants suggests that accurate positioning in accordance with pre-operative planning is achievable using 3D-printed designs.24,25

Pre-operative planning

Clear indications for custom glenoid implants versus bone grafting remain undefined. In clinical practice, currently, a custom implant would be preferred in cases where humeral head autogenous bone grafting is not feasible. Based on a native CT scan of the affected shoulder and relevant patient-specific factors, implant design must address critical biomechanical considerations to achieve primary stability. Variables such as native bone quality, existing glenoid anatomy, and soft tissue tensioning contribute to the ultimate design and positioning of the definitive glenoid implant and its properties to create a stable construct, both through the porous backside surface of the implant, central peg, and number and position of screws.

The quality of the pre-operative CT scan can have potential effects on implant design. If a poor-quality CT is rendered or if there is an artifact within the scan, potential inaccuracies may occur in implant manufacturing, and a form fit of the remaining glenoid vault will not be achieved. This may lead to early complications of the implant and failure of the surgery. The advantages of 3D printing allow for precision reconstruction of the glenoid vault. It is still unclear about how artifacts of pre-existing implants should be managed. In the author's experience, implants such as single glenoid screws or a loosened polyethylene glenoid do not necessarily need removal, as long as bacterial infection can be confidently excluded. Metallic glenoid implants as part of an existing reverse or anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty may have to be removed before CT scan due to a large amount of artifact. Modern planning and segmentation techniques often allow such pre-operative planning, even in the presence of such implants. Using a recent CT scan minimizes mismatch due to ongoing bone remodeling, especially in inflammatory or degenerative conditions. A severe mismatch between the implant and glenoid cavity has been seen in as little as 5.9 months after the pre-operative CT scan.2

A benefit of custom glenoid implants is the ability to change the shape, position, and length of the central peg to best fit the glenoid vault, a component we believe is paramount in ensuring primary stability of the implant. Numerous options exist to ensure time-zero stability, including varying the number of screws, struts, outriggers into the coracoid or acromial processes, fixation onto the scapula, and altering the amount of porous backside surface that comes into contact with the glenoid (Fig. 8). Maximal contact between the implant surface and bone is crucial to ensure primary stability and promote bony ingrowth. It also allows for larger shear forces to be tolerated at the joint-implant surface. Implant lateralization must, however, be approached with caution (Fig. 10).33 Range of motion and implant stability can both be improved with increased lateralization (Fig. 11). Adequate surgical exposure is crucial for precise implant placement. Complete capsular release is required for patient-specific guide fitting, with careful osteophyte removal as per the pre-operative plan. Once the guide is in place, a central peg reamer is used (Fig. 12). To ensure the appropriate depth is reamed, a stopper custom to the implant is used. While extensive reaming is often unnecessary, surface scoring for punctate bleeding may enhance fixation by promoting bony ingrowth. Any deviation from the planned fit risks eccentric loading of the implant and potential component failure.Figure 12Axial and 3-dimensional computed tomography scan of custom implant in situ in a narrow glenoid cavity, necessitating caution when reaming the central peg hole.

Conclusion

The use of 3D-printed implants has emerged as a treatment option for severe glenoid bone loss. Their design must optimize shear and compressive forces, maximize bone–implant contact, and incorporate secure fixation strategies. Avoiding excessive metallic lateralization is preferred to reduce the size of the lever arm, thereby reducing torque creation through the implant, increasing implant stability, and lowering the risk of rotation and failure. A thorough understanding of these biomechanical concepts is essential for effective pre-operative planning. Using up-to-date CT scan use and surgeon-engineer collaboration ensures precise implant design and placement, further leading to better implant stability. As technology advances, further research is needed to refine biomechanical understanding to optimize long-term outcomes, making 3D-printed implants and reliable solution for complex glenoid bone loss.
