Paclitaxel promotes mTOR signaling‐mediated apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells by targeting MUC20
Authors: Meng Li, Zhen Feng, Rui Han, Benchuang Hu, Renfeng Zhang, Hui Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to analyze the effect of paclitaxel on the apoptosis of esophageal cancer cells in relation to MUC20.MethodsRT‐qPCR analysis, a CCK‐8 assay, western blotting, and flow cytometry were used to analyze the anticancer effects of paclitaxel treatment or OE‐MUC20 in vitro and in vivo.ResultsThe in vitro results showed that paclitaxel significantly induced MUC20 upregulation and that paclitaxel treatment or OE‐MUC20 significantly decreased esophageal cancer cell viability and increased mTOR signaling activation and apoptosis. In addition, PKM2, a key downstream molecule of mTOR signaling, similarly showed significant upregulation after paclitaxel treatment in cells with OE‐MUC20, and its expression was attenuated after treatment with mTOR inhibitors. In a nude mouse model, tumor growth was slow in the OE‐MUC20 group and accelerated after inhibition of mTOR signaling.ConclusionThese data suggest that MUC20 is an important target of paclitaxel in esophageal cancer and promotes apoptosis through activation of mTOR signaling.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a common malignancy of the gastrointestinal tract with poor prognosis and high mortality, and it is the seventh most common cancer worldwide. 1 In recent years, advances in minimally invasive surgical techniques, the development of radiotherapy techniques and equipment, and the availability of new chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic agents have significantly improved the survival and prognosis of patients with esophageal cancer. 2 Currently, the main therapeutic agents for esophageal cancer include combinations of platinum, paclitaxel, anthracycline, or pyrimidine analogs 3 and the targeted agents cetuximab, gefitinib, and trastuzumab. 4 Despite the development of new drugs for esophageal cancer, the unknown underlying mechanisms of drug targets limit the effectiveness of such therapies and thus the identification of effective therapeutic targets. Therefore, the identification of new drug targets, along with the elucidation of their mechanisms of action, holds great promise for clinical application.

Paclitaxel is a clinical therapeutic agent for esophageal cancer. 5 Paclitaxel exerts its cytotoxic effects by binding and stabilizing microtubules 6 and inducing apoptosis in a variety of cell types. Currently, paclitaxel targets caspase activation upstream of mitochondrial apoptosis to promote early stages of the apoptotic process, 7 which is carried out by a death receptor‐independent, caspase‐3/−8‐driven mitochondrial amplification loop. 8 Although these effects are related to the mechanism of paclitaxel‐induced apoptosis, more potential mechanisms need to be elucidated.

Mucins (MUC1‐MUC24) are a family of glycoproteins involved in cell signaling and barrier protection. They are associated with the progression of many malignancies, including gastric, pancreatic, ovarian, breast, and lung cancers. 9 Silencing of MUC20 ameliorates the malignant features of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells by inhibiting the HGF/MET pathway. 10 MUC20 may be an important predictor of recurrence and poor prognosis in CRC patients. Overexpression of MUC20 may increase the migration and invasion of CRC cells. 11 High expression of MUC20 drives tumorigenesis and predicts poor survival outcomes in endometrial cancer. 12 Although the functional importance of MUC20 in many cancers has been widely demonstrated, the function and expression of MUC20 in esophageal cancer remain unclear.

The induction of apoptosis in esophageal carcinoma cells by paclitaxel treatment was demonstrated to be accompanied by upregulation of MUC20 and activation of mTOR. Overexpression of MUC20 (OE‐MUC20) in vitro was found to be sufficient to induce the activation of mTOR and the induction of apoptosis, and blocking mTOR signaling inhibited the induction of apoptosis. Furthermore, we found that paclitaxel treatment or OE‐MUC20 resulted in abnormal upregulation of PKM2, possibly indicating another effect of paclitaxel on metabolic regulation.

METHODS

Reagents

PTX and Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) were purchased from Sigma (USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 0.25% (w/v) trypsin/EDTA, and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Thermo Fisher. All antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Rapamycin was purchased from MedChemExpress.

Cell culture

ECA‐109 cells are a human esophageal squamous carcinoma cell line and were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 units/mL penicillin and L‐glutamine (Sigma). The culture conditions were 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed with a cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8: MedChemExpress), hereafter referred to as CCK‐8 reagent, following the supplier's protocol. The cell suspension (100 μL/well) was added into 96‐well culture plates and precultured in an incubator for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the incubator for 1–4 h. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Annexin V/PI double staining

Cells were exposed to 10 nM paclitaxel for 48 h. Apoptosis was detected by using the Annexin V‐FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit. Specifically, ECA‐109 cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and suspended in binding buffer. The cells were stained with 5 μL of Annexin V‐FITC and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) under protection from light, and apoptosis was then analyzed using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). At least three independent experiments were performed.

Western blotting

After 48 h of paclitaxel treatment, ECA‐109 cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor solution and 1% phosphatase inhibitor solution. The cell samples were incubated in lysis buffer on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 12000 × g for 20 min before protein concentrations were measured with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein in each sample were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking in blocking powder solution for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody (1:1000) at 4°C overnight and then with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactions were visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence approach using a western blot detection kit (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Each immunoblot analysis was repeated three times.

Plasmids and transfection

Total mRNA from ECA‐109 cells was used as a template. The purified polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were inserted into the pIRES‐eGFP vector by priming to construct the pIRES‐eGFP‐MUC20 plasmid. ECA‐109 cells (2 × 105) were transfected with 3 μg pIRES‐eGFP‐vector or pIRES‐eGFP‐MUC20 using Lipo3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher), incubated for 48 h, and selected by incubation in 2 μg/mL puromycin for at least 7 days.

Quantitative reverse transcription‐PCR (RT‐qPCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted from ECA‐109 cells using the TRIzol Plus RNA purification kit (Invitrogen, Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Bio Inc.) was used for reverse transcription. Reverse transcription was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reaction mixtures were prepared using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc.), and quantitative reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR) was performed using the ABI7500 PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCt method. 13 All primers (GenePharma) are listed in Table S1.

Xenograft assays

Nude mice (nu/nu; 6 weeks old) were used for in vivo studies. ECA‐109 cells with and without OE‐MUC20 in logarithmic growth phase were inoculated subcutaneously into nude mice. Then, the mice were treated with mTOR inhibitors by intraperitoneal injection twice weekly for 4 weeks. The bodyweight and tumor volume of the mice were measured weekly using a weighing machine and a Vernier caliper. Tumor volume was calculated by using the equation V = (W2 × L)/2, where V is the volume of the tumor, W is the width of the tumor, and L is the length of the tumor. Mice were sacrificed at the indicated time points, and tumor tissue from each mouse was weighed and photographed using a camera.

Statistical analysis

All data presented were obtained from three independent experiments and are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 6. The significance of differences was assessed using a student's t test. A significance level of 0.05 was selected, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference in all comparisons.

RESULTS

Paclitaxel mediates apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells by activating the mTOR pathway, accompanied by upregulation of MUC20

ECA‐109 esophageal cancer cells were treated with different concentrations of paclitaxel (0, 1, 10, and 100 μM and 1, 10, and 100 nM) for 48 h, and cell viability was measured by a CCK‐8 assay. Paclitaxel caused a concomitant dose‐dependent decrease in the viability of ECA‐109 cells (Figure 1a). Previous studies have shown that paclitaxel can induce apoptosis in several types of cancer cells. 14 , 15 To investigate whether the cytotoxicity of paclitaxel in ECA‐109 cells was correlated with the induction of apoptosis, Annexin V‐FITC/PI staining was performed after treatment with 10 μM paclitaxel for 48 h. As shown in Figure 1b, c, the apoptosis rate of ECA‐109 cells treated with 10 μM paclitaxel for 48 h was 18.6%, indicating significant induction of apoptosis compared with the control group (p < 0.05). We found that MUC20 was significantly upregulated after PTX treatment by qPCR and western blotting, suggesting that paclitaxel may induce apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells by targeting MUC20 (p < 0.05; Figure 1d–f).

The mTOR pathway is one of the most important signaling pathways in physiological cellular processes. Aberrant mTOR pathway activation regulates autophagy, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, apoptosis, chemoresistance and metastasis in many human cancers. 16 By western blotting, we found significant activation of mTOR signaling and upregulation of its downstream target pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) (Figure 1e, g–i). PKM2 can interact with mitofusin 2 (MFN2), a key regulator of mitochondrial fusion, to promote mitochondrial fusion and OXPHOS and attenuate glycolysis. 17 Thus, activation of mTOR signaling may attenuate glycolytic activity in esophageal cancer through upregulation of PKM2. The above results demonstrate that paclitaxel may activate mTOR signaling‐mediated apoptosis and glycolysis through upregulation of MUC20 expression.

Overexpression of MUC20 promotes apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells by triggering mTOR signaling

To confirm whether paclitaxel triggers mTOR signaling‐mediated apoptosis by increasing MUC20 expression, we constructed the pIRES‐eGFP‐MUC20 plasmid and the corresponding control plasmid and successfully transfected these plasmids into ECA‐109 cells (Figure 2a, b). The viability of MUC20‐OE cells was significantly reduced (p < 0.05; Figure 2c). We treated the MUC20‐OE cell line with rapamycin, an mTOR‐specific inhibitor, and as expected, rapamycin treatment reversed the decrease in cell viability caused by MUC20 overexpression (p < 0.05; Figure 2c). Flow cytometry assays revealed a significantly higher apoptosis rate (6.33%) after MUC20 overexpression compared to that in control cells and a significant decrease in ECA‐109 cell apoptosis after rapamycin treatment (p < 0.05; Figure 2d, e). In conclusion, our data suggest that MUC20 can lead to apoptosis in ECA‐109 cells through mTOR signaling activation.

MUC20 is an influential regulatory molecule upstream of mTOR signaling

Previously, we determined that apoptosis in ECA‐109 cells is suppressed by inhibition of mTOR signaling, and we next sought to further confirm the molecular mechanisms acting upstream and downstream of mTOR. We further confirmed the expression of MUC20 and PKM2 in the OE‐MUC20 and OE‐MUC20 + inhibitor groups by qPCR and western blotting. As expected, PKM2 expression was significantly increased after MUC20 overexpression (p < 0.05; Figure 3a). MUC20 mRNA and protein levels were increased after MUC20 overexpression and decreased after mTOR inhibition (p < 0.05; Figure 3b–e). Similarly, PKM2 was upregulated after MUC20 overexpression and downregulated after mTOR inhibition (p < 0.05; Figure 3c, d, f–h). Thus, these results further confirm that MUC20 strongly activates mTOR signaling and promotes PKM2 expression.

In vivo experiments confirm that MUC20 activates mTOR signaling to inhibit esophageal cancer progression

According to the experimental steps described in the Methods section, we obtained tumor tissues from each mouse in the four established groups (NC, OE, NC‐inhibitor, and OE‐inhibitor) (Figure 4a). The growth rate of xenograft tumors in mice in the OE‐MUC20 group was significantly lower than that in mice in the negative control group, the rate of increase in the xenograft tumor volume was significantly diminished (p < 0.05; Figure 4b, c), and the volume and weight of tumor tissues were significantly increased after the mTOR pathway was inhibited (p < 0.05; Figure 4b, c). In addition, Western blot analysis of tumor tissues revealed that, in agreement with the results of the cellular assay, MUC20 overexpression promoted mTOR phosphorylation as well as PKM2 expression, while the MUC20 and PKM2 protein levels were decreased after mTOR signaling was inhibited (Figure 4d–h). These results confirmed that MUC20 could activate the mTOR pathway in vivo, thereby promoting apoptosis in ECA‐109 cells.

DISCUSSION

Despite significant advances in esophageal cancer treatment strategies, the overall 5‐year survival rate of patients remains less than 25%. 18 The treatment of EC remains challenging, and an in‐depth exploration of drug treatment‐related mechanisms is required to facilitate the development of drug targets with increased effectiveness. In the present study, we sought to elucidate the target of paclitaxel in esophageal cancer, MUC20, and provide preliminary data on its potential exploitation as a therapeutic target of paclitaxel. Upregulation of MUC20 and activation of mTOR pathway‐mediated apoptosis were observed in paclitaxel‐treated esophageal cancer cells. In addition, we found that overexpression of MUC20 directly reduced cell viability and promoted apoptosis via the mTOR signaling pathway in esophageal cancer cells.

It has been reported that paclitaxel activates various signaling pathways, including the JNK, 19 WNT/beta‐catenin, 20 and TLR4 21 signaling pathways, but the primary target of paclitaxel is undefined. According to some studies, paclitaxel can activate AMPK, which is an upstream regulator of mTOR, and inhibit mTOR signaling in breast cancer and lung cancer cells. 22 However, paclitaxel may also induce the activation of a feedback loop that increases Akt phosphorylation, which can counteract the inhibition of mTOR. 23 Therefore, paclitaxel may have a dual effect on mTOR activation depending on the cellular context and its combination with other agents. Our study revealed that in esophageal cancer, paclitaxel can directly activate the mTOR pathway and that AKT/mTOR‐dependent autophagy and apoptosis can inhibit esophageal cancer tumor growth and invasion. 24 Therefore, we suggest that paclitaxel‐induced activation of apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells through the mTOR pathway may be one mechanism by which paclitaxel reduces cancer cell viability.

Since we observed significant upregulation of MUC20 upon paclitaxel treatment, to demonstrate that MUC20 is a potential target of paclitaxel, we generated the OE‐MUC20 ECA‐109 cell line in vitro. We found that MUC20 can induce apoptosis independently by activating the mTOR pathway. MUC20 is a mucin‐like glycoprotein that is overexpressed in various types of cancers, including gastric, pancreatic and breast cancers. MUC20 may be associated with apoptosis or mTOR signaling in different ways. MUC20 variant 2 is specifically expressed in gastric cancer. It can maintain mitochondrial calcium homeostasis and increase drug resistance by inhibiting apoptosis through suppression of the AKT/mTOR pathway. 25 MUC20 can activate the HGF/MET pathway in pancreatic cancer cells and promote cell proliferation, migration and invasion. In contrast, silencing MUC20 attenuates the malignant phenotype of pancreatic cancer cells and induces their apoptosis. 25 MUC20 interacts with HER2 and regulates its downstream signaling, including signaling through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. MUC20 also regulates the expression of Bcl‐2 family proteins and affects the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to apoptosis. 10 We demonstrate for the first time the significant effect of MUC20 on esophageal cancer cell apoptosis, which is mediated through mTOR signaling.

Both mTOR and PKM2 are key regulators of aerobic glycolysis, a metabolic hallmark of cancer cells that allows them to rapidly produce energy and biosynthetic precursors. mTOR induces the expression of PKM2, which catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate, the final step of glycolysis. 26 PKM2 also regulates flux of glycolytic intermediates into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and serine biosynthesis pathway (SBP), which are important for nucleotide and amino acid synthesis. Oncogenic signaling and metabolic alterations are interrelated in cancer cells. mTOR is frequently activated in cancers and controls cell growth and metabolism. mTOR signaling regulates amino acid, glucose, nucleotide, fatty acid and lipid metabolism. Conversely, metabolic precursors (e.g., amino acids) activate mTOR. 27 Determining the mechanisms by which such metabolic changes in turn maintain mTOR signaling and tumorigenicity is anticipated to be a very interesting research hotspot in the future.

In conclusion, our results suggest that paclitaxel targets MUC20 to activate mTOR signaling‐mediated apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells. The results of in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed the therapeutic effect of MUC20 upregulation on esophageal cancer. This study further deepens our understanding of the physiological mechanisms of paclitaxel.
