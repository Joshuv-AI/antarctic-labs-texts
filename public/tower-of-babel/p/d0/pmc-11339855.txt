Spray skin protectant versus standard moisturiser in the prevention of radiodermatitis in patients with anal canal and rectal cancer: A randomised clinical trial
Authors: Fabiana Verdan Simões, Thamiris da Silva e Silva, Aline Aniceto Pires, Caio Ribeiro Muzzi França, Nathália Sodré Velasco, Valdete Oliveira Santos, Katia Moreira, Marcelle Miranda da Silva, Marcos Antônio Gomes Brandão, Beatriz Guitton Renaud Baptista de Oliveira, Rafael Celestino da Silva
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evidence on products for the prevention of radiodermatitis is limited. The primary objective was to analyse the effectiveness of the spray skin protectant ‘non‐burning barrier film’ in the prevention of radiodermatitis with moist desquamation in patients with the anal canal and rectal cancer followed in nursing consultations compared to a standardised moisturiser based on Calendula officinalis and Aloe barbadensis. Single‐blind randomised clinical trial. The study was performed in a hospital in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, with 63 patients undergoing anal canal and rectal cancer treatment, randomised into one of the following two groups: an experimental group, which used a spray skin protectant and a control group, which used a moisturiser. Data were collected using an initial and subsequent evaluation form and were assessed using descriptive and inferential analyses. Participants who used the spray skin protectant had a lower chance of presenting radiodermatitis with moist desquamation and a longer time without this outcome when compared to the control group. The overall incidence of radiodermatitis was 100%, with 36.5% being severe. Furthermore, 17.5% of participants discontinued radiotherapy due to radiodermatitis. There were no differences between the groups regarding the severity of radiodermatitis and the number of patients who discontinued radiotherapy. The skin protectant was effective in preventing radiodermatitis with moist desquamation amongst patients with anal canal and rectal cancer.Keywords: anal neoplasms, clinical trial, oncological nursing, radiodermatitis, radiotherapy, rectal neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy as a cancer treatment modality can cause a variety of adverse reactions in patients, known as toxicities, one of which is radiodermatitis, which is the focus of this study. This is defined as a group of skin changes caused by ionising radiation that occur from internal to external structures and can lead to secondary infections. 1 , 2

Radiodermatitis is classified into grades according to the toxicity of the skin assessment. According to the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) scale, it is classified as follows: Grade 0: no change from baseline; Grade 1: mild erythema, epilation and dry peeling, as well as decreased sweating; Grade 2: moderate to severe erythema, uneven moist desquamation and moderate oedema; Grade 3: confluent moist desquamation beyond skin folds and locker oedema; Grade 4: ulceration, haemorrhage and necrosis and Grade 5: death. 3 Grades 1 and 2 are considered less severe and Grades 3 and 4 are more severe. 4

Patients treated for the anal canal and rectal cancer tend to have more severe degrees of radiodermatitis, due to the presence of both intrinsic and extrinsic predictive factors, such as combined chemotherapy protocols that enhance the effect of radiotherapy; an irradiated area with folds, which cause more humidity, heat and propensity to friction and radiotherapy toxicity in the lower gastrointestinal system, which results in clinical events that favour skin breakdowns, such as diarrhoea and faecal incontinence. 2 , 5 , 6

A high prevalence of radiodermatitis with severe cases is observed in patients undergoing treatment for anal canal and rectal cancer in the results of recent research. 6 , 7 , 8 A study that determined the prevalence of radiodermatitis, the degrees of severity and the impacts of treatment interruption in patients with the anal canal and rectal cancer found that 99.1% of patients had radiodermatitis, and of the cases 34.8% were severe. Discontinuation of treatment occurred in 13% of patients and was associated with severe radiodermatitis. 6 Another study that retrospectively analysed acute toxicity of 64 patients with anal squamous cell cancer treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy identified 34 patients (53%) with severe toxicity, of whom 50.7% developed dermatological toxicity. Twenty‐three patients (36%) had treatment interruption of 1 or more days. 7

These unplanned interruptions of radiotherapy can negatively influence the clinical prognosis, reducing cure rates of the disease. 9 , 10 The greater severity of radiodermatitis contributed to the worsening of the patient's overall state of health and quality of life, with deterioration of functional capacity, intensification of emotional reactions and physical symptoms such as anxiety, depression, altered body image, pain, itching, fatigue, insomnia and lack of appetite. 11 , 12

It should be noted that the characteristic of radiodermatitis that precedes the onset of a more severe degree is moist desquamation, a rupture of the epidermis with exposure of the dermis that begins in the form of plaques, which can be present from Grade 2 radiodermatitis. As the treatment dose accumulates, the plaques increase in size, as radiotherapy further damages the basal layer of the epidermis. Thus, radiodermatitis with moist desquamation has a higher risk of progressing to more severe stages and, consequently, of having an impact on treatment and the patient's life. 2 , 13

This scenario indicates the need for interventions for the prevention of radiodermatitis, especially the more severe degrees. According to the clinical guidelines of the Oncology Nursing Society, the only recommendation for the prevention of radiodermatitis with strong evidence is the washing and skin care regimen. 1 Products with the active ingredient Calendula officinalis have already been classified by the Oncology Nursing Society as ‘capable of being effective’ but in a revision of the guidelines in 2020, the expert panel concluded they were unable to determine the desirable effects of calendula, due to lack of certainty of evidence. 1 , 13 , 14 The use of Aloe barbadensis (aloe vera) has been identified as a knowledge gap because there is limited evidence to support its recommendation. 1 , 13

The Oncology Nursing Society guidelines also recommend semi‐permeable dressings with standard washing and skin care. Semi‐permeable dressings include spray skin protectants, which are products that function as a physical barrier between the stratum corneum and irritants or moisture. 1 Skin protectors have been used in the prevention of radiodermatitis, but with low certainty of evidence. 1 , 13 , 15 The investigations into the use of these products have been directed at patients with breast and head and neck cancers, with limited knowledge of their use in the pelvic region. 15 , 16

This affirmation is ratified by an integrative review on the effectiveness of spray skin protectant and C. officinalis in the prevention and treatment of radiodermatitis, in which only two studies were found on the use of spray skin protectant and three on calendula, 03 of which were on patients with breast cancer and 01 with head and neck cancer. However, the authors considered the results to be inconclusive, due to systematic errors of conduction or analyses without statistical significance. 15

A systematic review evaluated the evidence on the effectiveness of semi‐permeable dressings in preventing radiodermatitis in cancer patients. The analysis of six randomised clinical trials indicated that semi‐permeable dressings were beneficial in controlling radiotherapy‐related skin toxicity but rigorous trials showing stronger evidence are needed. 16

This limited evidence on the use of products to prevent radiodermatitis indicates the importance of developing new research to expand the possibilities of interventions that promote patient safety. This is relevant because radiodermatitis is one of the adverse reactions to ionising radiation, therefore, this knowledge is essential for the nurse's role in preventing this complication in nursing consultations through the implementation of physical examination of the irradiated area and the planning of interventions in accordance with institutional clinical protocols. 17 , 18

In Brazil, in the national reference institution for cancer treatment, nurses in nursing consultation can manage skin toxicities caused by radiotherapy in accordance with the institutional Care Protocol for Radiodermatitis. This protocol established that the prevention of radiodermatitis from the beginning of radiotherapy should be carried out through the use of a moisturising gel cream based on C. officinalis and A. barbadensis (Dnativ Revita Derm®).

The Dnativ Revita Derm® has been recommended at the study site for approximately 25 years, and this indication is based on its composition with A. barbadensis and C. officinalis, which are anti‐inflammatory, antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, angiogenic and curative herbal therapies. 1 , 13 , 19 , 20 , 21 Dnativ Revita Derm® is presented in the form of a gel cream and is applied to the skin manually, with the main action of moisturising. 1 , 13 , 19 , 20 , 21

This protocol also recommends a skin protector spray (called ‘non‐burning barrier film’) only in cases where the skin is intact, but there is moisture due to diarrhoea, incontinence or anal or vaginal secretions. Spray skin protectants come in liquid form and are applied directly to the skin using a spray, without the need for manual friction and work by creating a physical barrier on the skin. 22 , 23

Thus, although Dnativ Revita Derm® and the skin protectant spray are used with the intention of protecting the part of the skin on which they are applied, this protection is offered in different ways. Therefore, whilst Dnativ Revita Derm® allows body fluids to come into direct contact with the area of the skin where it has been applied, the skin protectant spray creates a barrier that prevents this contact.

It should be noted, however, that research that has already evaluated the use of spray skin protectors to prevent radiodermatitis in other regions and obtained positive results has used this product since the beginning of radiotherapy treatment. 15 , 24 Given this panorama, the hypothesis was raised that such patients have a greater chance of preventing radiodermatitis by incorporating the use of a spray skin protector from the beginning of radiotherapy treatment, compared to the standardised moisturising gel cream in the care protocol.

The primary objective was to analyse the effectiveness of the spray skin protectant ‘non‐burning barrier film’ in the prevention of radiodermatitis with moist desquamation in patients with the anal canal and rectal cancer followed in nursing consultations compared to a standardised moisturiser based on C. officinalis and A. barbadensis.

METHOD

Study design

This was a single‐blind randomised clinical trial carried out in a single institution, registered on August 26, 2019, in Clinical Trials under the number NCT04067310 with the acronym PROT. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04067310?id=NCT04067310&rank=1. The report of this research was organised according to the CONSORT tool.

Study location

The study was conducted in a hospital specialised in cancer treatment located in the city of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. The investigation took place in the radiotherapy sector of this institution, where radiodermatitis was managed in accordance with the Care Protocol for the Prevention and Treatment of Radiodermatitis previously described.

Participants, selection criteria

Patients with anal canal and rectal cancer who met the following inclusion criteria were eligible to participate: indication for radiotherapy and conventional fractionation with a linear accelerator treatment; age greater than or equal to 18 years and no previous history of radiotherapy at the same treatment site (re‐irradiation). Exclusion criteria were patients who had preexisting dermatitis at the irradiated site, which would make skin evaluation difficult during radiotherapy or a history of allergic reaction to any products used in the study.

The following were the intervention discontinuity criteria: observation of moist desquamation during radiotherapy; loss to follow‐up and factors analysed by the main researcher that could compromise the research results, such as, for example, non‐adherence to nursing consultations. Following randomisation, these discontinuity criteria were evaluated during follow‐up nursing consultations. Patients who were discontinued from the intervention did not return to the research protocol and received weekly follow‐up nursing consultations and were treated according to the care protocol for radiodermatitis in patients receiving pelvic irradiation until they met the nursing consultation discharge criteria.

The criteria for discontinuation of the research were: withdrawal of the informed consent form; change by the physician of the treatment planning for hypofractionation; and at the discretion of the investigator, such as longer than expected follow‐up time, suspension of radiotherapy before starting treatment planning or carrying out radiotherapy outside the research institution. Discontinued patients from the research were also followed up in the nursing consultations and followed the institution's radiotherapy care protocol.

To recruit participants, the researcher identified patients with anal canal and rectal cancer who were scheduled for weekly care at the radiotherapy outpatient clinic. Eligible patients were referred to the nursing consultation for screening. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were evaluated in these nursing consultations, and the selected patients were given information about the research objectives, the products under study, the research procedures and the potential benefits and risks. Patients who accepted were scheduled for nursing consultation evaluation, performed on the first day of radiotherapy treatment, when the signed informed consent forms were randomised, and the research procedures began.

Sample size

The definition of the sample size was based on previous research on radiodermatitis in the same population in which a 35% prevention response to the event was obtained with the use of Dnativ Revita Derm®. 6 The use of the spray skin protectant was expected to increase the response in 30% more than that observed with the Dnativ Revita Derm® control product. With the application of Fleming's method for sample calculation, the expected response was ≥45.50% for patients using the spray skin protectant, an experimental product. 25 The alpha level was set at 0.10 and the power at 80%. The calculated sample size included 112 participants, with 56 in each study group. Considering a 10% sample loss in follow‐up, the final estimated size was 124 patients, with 62 in each group.

Randomisation

The experimental and control groups were swapped randomised blocks during the nursing consultation evaluation. Participant allocation was performed by three trained nurses through a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) application module. The randomisation list was hidden by the software itself. Due to the different sensory characteristics of the products chosen as intervention and control, it was not possible to blind the research participants.

Interventions

Experimental group: used a 3M brand spray skin protectant, Cavilon™, which is described as a ‘non‐burning barrier film’ and is composed of the following chemical compounds: hexamethyldisiloxane, acrylic terpolymer, isooctane and polyphenylmethylsiloxane copolymer. 21 The product is packaged in a 28‐mL spray bottle of a non‐cytotoxic liquid solution based on diffused polymers. In the product manufacturer's information, it is a polymeric solution used to protect the skin from aggressive agents such as body fluids, adhesives and friction; to protect sensitive and fragile skin and to protect skin injured by small cuts or scratches. It reduces pain without causing burns and protects for up to 72 h. Because it is washing‐resistant, there is no need to remove and reapply it with each sanitisation. 22

After patients were allocated to this group in the nursing consultation evaluation, they were instructed as to the product's application, which included a demonstration by the nurse and training of the companion/family member. In addition, the patient received an information leaflet with the following guidelines: the product should be applied to the anterior and posterior regions of the pelvis (pubis, groin, perineum, intergluteal region and around the anus and the lower fold of the buttock. For women, this includes the labia minora and labia majora, and for men, the penis and scrotum). Before putting on underwear, patients should allow the product to dry for 90 s following administration. The instructions called for daily application, once post‐irradiation and on weekends and holidays at home, as well as after intimate hygiene, once the area is dry.

Control group: used Dnativ Revita Derm® gel with ecossomes, brand Triasil Indústria Química e Farmaceutica Ltda, a moisturiser that contains vitamins A, E and provitamin C, the purified and stabilised active fraction of A. barbadensis, ceramides and C. officinalis in its composition. Ecossomes are biovectors that increase product absorption. In the product manufacturer's information, this element is registered with the Brazilian Health Surveillance Agency as a cosmetic, that is, it can only be used on the whole skin for hydration purposes.

The instructions and training given to the patients/families regarding the use of this control product were provided in the evaluation consultation and through an explanatory leaflet with the following information: apply, it at least twice a day, with reapplication after any intimate hygiene in the anterior and posterior regions of the pelvis. The moisturiser should be applied with circular gentle movements until absorbed by the skin.

Before performing radiotherapy, the skin should be clean, without applying the control product or experimental product. Participants in both groups received the required amount of experimental and control products for home use. These products were used until reaching the primary outcome of the study (incidence of moist desquamation) or nursing discharge. Additionally, the patients also received intimate hygiene products for home care.

Variables, data collection and instruments

The outcomes evaluated were as follows: primary outcome – incidence of moist desquamation in the experimental and control groups and secondary outcomes – occurrence of temporary interruption of radiotherapy due to radiodermatitis and occurrence of radiodermatitis at severe degrees.

This evaluation began with data collection via the initial evaluation and subsequent evaluation forms. The initial evaluation form was applied in the nursing consultation evaluation, before starting radiotherapy. Data regarding the following variables were collected: sociodemographic variables (age, sex, colour and education); life habit variables (alcohol use and smoking) and clinical and treatment variables (oncological diagnosis, the presence of colostomy, histological type, total and daily irradiated dose in centigrays [cGy] and concomitant chemotherapy).

The evaluation form was then used every week during the nursing consultation follow‐up during radiotherapy. Data were collected on the following variables: attendance, clinical characteristics, classification of radiodermatitis according to the original RTOG scale, 3 temporary interruption of treatment with the number of days interrupted, radiotherapy fraction and reason and adverse events related to the products or those that could potentially interfere with the study outcomes.

Follow‐up nursing consultations occurred once a week (every five applications of ionising radiation) and, in exceptional cases, twice. The evaluation of the participant's skin in the region of radiotherapy application was performed in the first nursing consultation evaluation and during treatment in the follow‐up nursing consultation, until nursing discharge, aiming to observe the evolution and the presence of radiodermatitis. The nursing discharge occurred on the last radiotherapy application or shortly after, based on skin assessment within RTOG grades 0 or 1. 3

Patient follow‐up took place for 68 days. During the intervals between weekly consultations, the team was available in the study sector every day to answer patients' questions or address any other research‐related concerns. During the weekly follow‐up consultation, the participant's adherence to the research protocol was also assessed by asking the patient questions about the correct use of the products according to the previous instructions, as well as about the adoption of self‐care measures, such as water intake, protection against heat and personal hygiene.

The main researcher provided nursing consultations, and the skin was evaluated by two nurses on the research team who had been trained by the main researcher. The outcome assessments were single‐blinded since the nurse in charge of the assessment did not know which group the participants belonged to.

Data analysis

After organising the database and double‐checking the input, the data were processed using IBM SPSS software, Version 23. Intention‐to‐treat analysis was performed.

Descriptive statistics based on absolute (n) and relative (%) frequency were applied to analyse the sociodemographic, clinical and radiotherapy treatment‐related variables of the total sample of participants and their distribution between the experimental and control groups. In the bivariate analyses that included qualitative variables, Pearson's chi‐square and Fisher's exact and odds ratio tests were applied. Initially the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to quantitative variables to determine whether the sample followed normal distribution. Because they did not present a normal distribution, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was chosen to analyse these variables. A significance level of 10% was established for all bivariate analyses (p < 0.10). This value was chosen because it was considered that small sample sizes have greater variability, which could make it difficult to identify the effects of interventions if a lower level of significance was adopted.

Binary logistic regression was chosen to test the hypothesis of association between exposure and outcome based on the odds ratio and respective 90% confidence intervals (90% CI). The multivariate model was composed of all the variables that in the bivariate analyses presented p ≤ 0.10 and which, according to the literature, are known to be associated with the studied outcome and/or exposure.

Finally, the estimated survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and the logrank test to verify the differences between the experimental and control groups as a function of the time between the beginning of the experimental treatment (exposure) and the occurrence of moist desquamation (outcome) based on their survival curves. The logrank test compared the two curves, with statistical significance set at a p‐value ≤0.10.

Ethical considerations

The present study was approved on 15 August 2019 by the ethics committee of the José Alencar Gomes da Silva National Cancer Institute, Brazil, with resolution number 3596987 and Certificate of Presentation for Ethical Consideration number 19153319.6.0000.5238, in accordance with all national ethical standards. The participants signed an informed consent form and those who did not want to participate followed the institution's radiodermatitis care protocol.

However, because of the low number of eligible patients as a result of COVID‐19, the data collection period was extended for an additional 10 months to reach the expected sample size. Even with this extension, the recruitment of participants ended in October 2022 with 63 patients because it was not possible to reach the calculated sample size.

DISCUSSION

Patients with anal canal and rectal cancer had high percentages of incidence of radiodermatitis (100%) and moist desquamation (71.8%). Multivariate analysis showed that, when compared to the Dnavit Revita Derm® moisturiser, the acrylic terpolymer‐based spray skin protectant was more effective in preventing radiodermatitis with moist desquamation, with the experimental group having more time free of this outcome.

Clinical trials testing products for preventing and treating radiodermatitis are commonly found in head and neck and breast cancers. 13 , 26 Regarding the use of spray skin protectant, a randomised controlled trial compared the effectiveness of 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film and mometasone furoate cream (Elomet®), a synthetic corticosteroid, in preventing radiodermatitis in breast cancer patients who underwent radiotherapy treatment and who underwent mastectomy or breast‐conserving surgery. 23

Thirty‐nine study participants were randomly allocated into three groups: (1) 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film vs. no treatment (n = 13), (2) Elomet® vs. no treatment (n = 9) and (3) Elomet® vs. 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film (n = 17). The Elomet® group took 53.4 days to develop Grade 2 radiodermatitis compared to the 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film, which it reached in 44.5 days. 23

Another randomised clinical trial compared the effectiveness of 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film and Sorbolene cream, composed of water and oils with 10% glycerin, for the prevention of moist desquamation in mastectomized patients undergoing radiotherapy treatment. 27 Sixty‐one participants were randomly allocated into two groups: (1) medial application of 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film and lateral film of Sorbolene cream (n = 31) and (2) lateral application of 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film and medial application of Sorbolene cream (n = 30). The intention‐to‐treat analysis showed that 46 patients obtained an RTOG score = 2 in the areas treated with Sorbolene versus 33 in the areas treated with 3M™ Cavilon™ No Sting Barrier Film. 27

Other topical barrier products (Mepilex® Film; Strata XRT®), which do not come in spray form and protect the skin by forming a film, have shown promising results in patients with breast and head and neck cancers. 19 , 28 , 29

One study examined the use of the barrier film in reducing the severity of radiodermatitis in breast cancer patients, as well as the patients' perceptions of the product's results. 28 The study included 101 Danish patients who were randomised based on whether the Mepitel film was applied to the lateral or medial part of the breast, with the patients acting as their own control. Patients reported significantly less pain, itching and burning, in addition to less oedema and reduced sensitivity in the area of the skin covered by the film. When compared to standard treatment, patients treated after mastectomy had less severe radiodermatitis at the end of radiotherapy with the film. 28

No clinical trials were found on the effectiveness of the control product, Dnativ Revita Derm®. However, despite this gap in knowledge, Aloe vera is currently recommended by a clinical trial in the Oncology Nursing Society guidelines. C. officinalis has been shown in previous studies to be more effective than essential fatty acids and Trolamine in patients with breast and head and neck cancers. 1 , 19 , 20 , 21

One highlight is research that also evaluated the effect of cream on preventing radiodermatitis, which was composed of nanoparticles and vitamin E as an antioxidant. It was a pilot, triple‐blind, randomised study, which was developed with 40 women with breast cancer undergoing radiotherapy, who were divided into: Intervention Group: received the cream with nanoparticles and vitamin E; Control Group 1: received the cream without nanoparticles or vitamin E and Control Group 2: received the cream with nanoparticles without vitamin E. The results showed that all patients had radiodermatitis with no differences in the degrees between the groups. There was a protective effect of the cream with nanoparticles and vitamin E in relation to the time of onset of radiodermatitis in patients who did not receive a booster dose of radiodermatitis. 30

The prevalence of severe radiodermatitis was 36.5%, and statistical analyses did not support the superiority of one product over another in reducing severe radiodermatitis. However, the experimental group had a higher incidence of Grade 1 radiodermatitis, whilst the control group had a higher incidence of the more severe grades (grades 3 and 4). This indicates that the experimental product protected against moist desquamation better than the control product, thus preventing the effects of recurrent moisture in patients undergoing treatment for anal canal and rectal cancer.

Studies cite humidity and skin folds as risk factors for radiodermatitis. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 In the case of patients with anal canal and rectal cancer, due to the region affected, exposure to sources of humidity from bodily fluids is more common, as well as friction from body folds and increased temperature in the region. These factors, combined with the repetitive damage of ionising radiation to the skin, which reduces the number of functional stem cells, alters endothelial cells and causes inflammation, help to explain the high prevalence of severe radiodermatitis in this population. 19

In a study with 112 patients with anal canal and rectal cancer, 34.8% of patients reached severe grades of radiodermatitis using Dnativ Revita Derm®, the control product in this study. 6 This percentage is considered high and reveals the magnitude of severe grades in this specific population, since studies in patients with head and neck, breast and gynaecological cancers revealed lower percentages of 11.4%, 18%–20% and 6.6%, respectively. 10 , 19 , 35 , 36

Regarding treatment interruption, 17.5% of the participants interrupted radiotherapy due to radiodermatitis, with no difference between the groups. This interruption ranged from 3 to 15 days, with an average of 6 days. Higher percentages were discovered in an Italian study of 84 anal cancer patients treated with IMRT, in which treatment was interrupted 63% of the time due to cutaneous toxicity; the median interruption in these patients was 7 days. 8

Patients with anal canal and rectal cancer have higher percentages of interruptions when compared to other regions. A radiodermatitis prevalence study in a sample of 167 patients with head and neck cancer showed that 2.4% of the patients suspended treatment. 10 In another study with 566 breast cancer patients who underwent radiotherapy, 3.2% discontinued treatment due to radiotherapy side effects. 37 This emphasises the importance of research into radiodermatitis prevention and management in this population.

It should be noted that the temporary interruption of treatment due to the severity of radiodermatitis is directly related to moist desquamation. This is because extensive moist desquamation is related to local hypersensitivity, itching, pain due to exposure of nerve endings and loss of the protective barrier, risk of infection and poorer quality of life. 38 , 39

A 7‐day interruption can result in a 3%–25% loss of local disease control, whilst 14–16‐day interruptions have a negative impact on treatment results. 40 Best clinical practices determine that radical courses of RT should not be interrupted; however, if interruptions are inevitable, a compensatory treatment should be performed by the radiation oncologist. 40

One of the limitations of the research was the number of participants because although the researchers extended the data collection period, it was not possible to reach the calculated sample size. This aspect limited the generalisation of the research conclusions. Another limitation was the small number of other clinical trials that also evaluated the use of the spray skin protector or the moisturising cream used in the research, as this restricted the comparison of the results obtained and limited the interpretation of the research findings.

Multivariate analysis showed that the spray skin protectant resulted in greater chances of preventing radiodermatitis with moist desquamation in comparison with the control product, the moisturiser DNAtiv Revita Derm®. There was a significant difference in the occurrence of radiodermatitis with moist desquamation between the groups studied as a function of the time for the occurrence of this event, with the experimental group having a longer time free of moist desquamation. These results support the claim that the skin protectant spray is effective in preventing radiodermatitis with moist desquamation.

The findings allow the recommendation of including skin protectant sprays amongst the product options for the prevention of radiodermatitis from the start of radiotherapy. Thus, the evidence produced serves as a basis for the organisation and/or revision of care protocols for the management of radiodermatitis, with the application of this technology within the scope of nursing care.

Knowledge about the effectiveness of tested technology for the care of patients with anal canal and rectal cancer can be translated into the clinical practice or nurses in radiotherapy nursing consultations, thus supporting the planning of their preventive actions to minimise radiodermatitis and reduce radiodermatitis discomfort.
