Iron Dysregulation and Vascular Diseases: A Contemporary Review
Authors: Golam Iftakhar Khandakar, Wa Du, Chenran Wang, Matthew Corriere, Yanbo Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iron is essential for the cardiovascular system to maintain its function. Disruption of iron balance, in the form of either iron deficiency or overload, has been increasingly recognized to be linked to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. The consequences of iron dysregulation in specific vascular cell types, such as endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and macrophages, contribute to critical processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular remodeling. In this review, we focus on the role of iron imbalance in the pathogenesis of vascular diseases, in particular atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysm. We provide an overview of cellular and systemic iron homeostasis, explore the impact of iron dysregulation on vascular cell biology, and discuss mechanistic insights into iron imbalance–induced vascular pathology. We also summarize potential therapeutic approaches targeting iron metabolism, review ongoing clinical trials, and outline future research directions needed to translate these findings into clinical interventions for vascular disease.

Iron is indispensable for the growth and development of all living organisms. It plays a central role in key physiological processes, including energy production, oxygen transport, and enzymatic reactions. However, unbound free iron can produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induce oxidative stress, which triggers cell damage and contributes to various diseases. Under physiological conditions, systemic and cellular iron metabolism is precisely regulated to maintain normal cell function. 1 In contrast, iron metabolism is dysregulated in response to various pathological stimuli. A better understanding of the impact of iron dysregulation on disease development and progression, and exploring the underlying mechanisms, may facilitate the development of novel strategies to improve disease management. 2

Accumulating evidence suggests that iron homeostasis is essential for maintaining cardiovascular health. Iron dysregulation (iron overload and iron deficiency) can induce inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are linked to cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Recently, ferroptosis, an iron‐dependent form of cell death, has been recognized as an important contributor to vascular dysfunction. While growing research has focused on the role of iron in heart biology, its effects on blood vessels remain less explored. In this review, we examine how disturbances in iron homeostasis affect vascular cell function and contribute to vascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysms, and peripheral artery disease (PAD). Moreover, we discuss molecular mechanisms, highlight therapeutic approaches aimed at regulating iron metabolism, and outline current clinical trial efforts.

Iron Regulation and Dysfunction

Iron Regulators Maintain Iron Homeostasis

Although multiple systemic and cellular mechanisms contribute to iron homeostasis, iron regulatory proteins (IRPs), specifically IRP1 (encoded by ACO1) and IRP2 (encoded by IREB2), represent the critical intracellular iron sensors that directly regulate iron metabolism–related genes. 3 Cellular iron homeostasis is meticulously regulated by IRP1 and IRP2, which modulate the abundance of iron transporters and storage proteins, including TFR1 (transferrin receptor 1), divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), ferroportin‐1 (FPN1), and ferritin. 3 Mechanistically, IRPs bind to the stem‐loop structure of the iron‐responsive element located in the 3′ untranslated region of TFR1 and DMT1 mRNAs in conditions of low cellular iron levels. 4 This binding stabilizes TFR1 and DMT1 mRNAs and enhances iron absorption. In contrast, when the iron levels are elevated, IRP1 acquires a [4Fe‐4S] cluster, which converts the protein into a cytosolic aconitase and loses its RNA‐binding capacity. 5 In parallel, in iron‐replete conditions, IRP2 undergoes iron‐ and oxygen‐dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation by E3 ubiquitin ligase F‐box and leucine‐rich repeat protein 5 (FBXL5). 6 Reduced IRP activity under high intracellular iron results in destabilization of TFR1 and DMT1 mRNAs, suppresses iron uptake, and prevents excessive intracellular iron accumulation. 4 The IRP/iron‐responsive element system likewise limits translation of ferritin and FPN1 via iron‐responsive elements located in their 5′ untranslated regions in low iron conditions, thereby restricting storage and export. 4 The mechanisms by which IRP1 and IRP2 regulate iron metabolism‐related genes are illustrated in Figure 1. Animal models support the critical role of IRPs in maintaining cellular and systemic iron homeostasis. Irp1 −/− mice exhibit a marked transient polycythemia, characterized by reduced HIF‐2α mRNA, increased erythropoietin (Epo) mRNA in kidneys, and inappropriately elevated serum erythropoietin levels. Irp1 deletion results in extramedullary hematopoiesis, splenomegaly, polycythemia, reticulocytosis, increased hepcidin production, pulmonary hypertension, and premature death. 7 , 8 In contrast, Irp2‐deficient mice display elevated ferritin levels, reduced TFR1 expression, impaired iron uptake, and progressive adult‐onset neurodegeneration. 9 Overall, while IRP1 and IRP2 share overlapping roles in regulating iron metabolism, they work through different regulatory mechanisms, and the loss of either of them exhibits distinct physiological outcomes. In addition, as a critical ferroxidase, ceruloplasmin catalyzes iron oxidation from ferrous iron (Fe2+) to ferric iron (Fe3+). 10 This process enables iron binding onto transferrin and facilitates cellular iron efflux via ferroportin‐1. Ceruloplasmin dysfunction impairs iron export and leads to tissue iron overload and oxidative injury, which contribute to neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson disease. 11 In mice, ceruloplasmin knockout induced iron accumulation in multiple tissues and organs (liver, spleen, pancreas, and duodenum), indicating its essential role in systemic iron homeostasis. 12

IRP1‐ and IRP2‐mediated regulation of iron metabolism–related genes.

In iron‐deficient cells, IRP1 and IRP2 bind to IREs in the 3′ untranslated regions of TFR1 and DMT1 mRNAs, stabilizing these transcripts and promoting iron uptake. When iron levels are sufficient, IRP1 incorporates a [4Fe–4S] cluster and functions as cytosolic aconitase, while IRP2 undergoes iron‐ and oxygen‐dependent degradation via FBXL5, resulting in loss of IRE binding. Reduced IRP activity leads to destabilization of TFR1 and DMT1 mRNAs, preventing excess iron accumulation. IRP binding also suppresses translation of FPN1 and ferritin under low‐iron conditions, limiting iron export and storage. Collectively, the IRP–IRE system ensures precise regulation of iron import, export, and sequestration to maintain cellular iron homeostasis. Iron Overload

Iron overload is the condition of excessive accumulation of iron in various organs resulting from increased intestinal absorption, parenteral administration (eg, blood transfusions), or excessive dietary consumption. 13 It is primarily caused by a genetic defect in iron homeostasis, referred to as hereditary hemochromatosis (HH), that can lead to liver cirrhosis, diabetes, arthritis, endocrine dysfunction, and cardiomyopathy. 14 Clinical indicators of iron overload include elevated serum ferritin levels, increased serum iron, and high transferrin saturation. 15 In iron overload conditions, iron levels in the circulation exceed the iron‐binding capacity of serum transferrin, resulting in non–transferrin‐bound iron (NTBI) uptake. Iron overload plays a positive role in the development of atherosclerosis. 16 It negatively impacts cardiovascular health in patients with hemochromatosis, exhibiting increased morbidity and death. 17 Table 1 summarizes 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 the commonly used screening and diagnostic criteria for iron overload, particularly in the context of hereditary hemochromatosis (American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases 2011 Practice Guideline, Diagnosis and Management of Hemochromatosis). 18 Criteria include elevated transferrin saturation 19 , 20 and serum ferritin levels, 21 , 22 followed by confirmatory testing to rule out secondary causes and assess tissue iron stores. In addition to the conventional measures above, emerging biomarkers of iron dysregulation, including labile plasma iron, 23 NTBI, 24 and ferritin isoforms (H‐ and L‐ferritin), 25 can provide additional insights into oxidative stress and vascular injury.

Iron Deficiency

Iron deficiency commonly arises from inadequate dietary intake, impaired intestinal absorption, increased iron loss, or enhanced physiological demand. It negatively affects physical function, cognitive performance, and the quality of life. 26 Iron deficiency is prevalent in patients with CVD, including chronic heart failure (HF), acute HF, pulmonary arterial hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), and aortic stenosis. 26 , 27 The prevalence of the condition is more frequently observed in women. 27 To evaluate the therapeutic potential of iron supplements, it is necessary first to see the impact of iron deficiency on cardiovascular function. Recent research has shed light on the roles of hepcidin and IRPs in maintaining iron homeostasis in this context. 26 Hepcidin production is reduced in iron deficiency, hemorrhagic/hemolytic anemia, and hypoxic conditions, enhancing intestinal iron absorption and increasing iron release from the body’s iron stores. However, in case of iron overload and an inflammatory state, hepcidin is upregulated in response to inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin‐1β, interleukin‐6, and interleukin‐22. 28 Increased hepcidin leads to iron sequestration in macrophages and hepatocytes and reduces iron exports from enterocytes. At the posttranscriptional level, the iron‐saturated IRPs lose their capacity to bind to the 5′ untranslated region of ferritin mRNA, resulting in decreased ferritin abundance and impaired iron storage. 29 Table 2 outlines the stages of iron deficiency in humans, key diagnostic indicators, and their respective reference ranges (laboratory measurements commonly used in the evaluation of iron status (Institute of Medicine [US] Panel on Micronutrients, 2001). 30 , 31 These stages progress from early iron depletion to iron deficiency anemia, identified by distinct biochemical and hematologic markers.

Iron Dysregulation in Vascular Diseases

Given the crucial role of iron in various enzymes and physiological functions, dysregulation of iron metabolism (deficiency and overload) is a pathophysiologic mechanism for a wide range of diseases. 2 In contrast with other forms of classical unprogrammed cell death, ferroptosis is predominantly associated with disruptions in iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and imbalances in the antioxidant system, as discussed in other reviews. 32 Recent studies have demonstrated the role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of various CVDs. 32 Here, we review the literature to elucidate the complex relationship between iron dysregulation and vascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, aneurysm, and PAD (Figure 2).

Iron Dysregulation in Endothelial Cells

Endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction caused by hemodynamic changes or detrimental stimuli has been linked to atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, PAD, and aortic aneurysm. Iron overload leads to endothelial dysfunction through pro‐oxidant and proinflammatory effects, increasing monocyte adhesion, transmigration, and apoptosis, and increasing hyperpermeability, while reducing nitric oxide bioavailability. 33 Recent investigations have elucidated the influence of iron dysregulation on EC function and atherosclerosis. 2 When systemic iron levels exceed the transport capability of transferrin, NTBI circulates throughout the body and deposits in vascular cells, potentially causing endothelial oxidative damage. Epidemiological and experimental studies have demonstrated that iron overload in vascular cells promotes atherosclerosis by inducing oxidative stress, EC apoptosis, and inflammation. 1 Notably, iron chelation can mitigate the endothelial dysfunction through suppression of inflammatory response by inhibition of intercellular adhesion molecule 1, vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, E‐selectin, and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP‐1), suggesting that iron‐induced endothelial dysfunction is reversible. 34 Deferoxamine, an iron chelator, improves endothelium‐dependent, nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation in patients with coronary artery disease. 35

Ferroptosis has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. A recent report suggests that inhibition of ferroptosis reduces endothelial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation in mouse aortic ECs and alleviates atherosclerosis in mice. 36 In Apoe −/− mice, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) overexpression reduces the development of atherosclerosis and delays its progression. Overexpression of GPX4 inhibits the expression of adhesion molecules in ECs, and the adhesion of monocytes to ECs, as well as endothelial necrosis and apoptosis. 36 Additionally, blocking ferroptosis by the inhibitor ferrostatin‐1 prevents lipid peroxidation and reduces atherosclerosis in the aorta of Apoe −/− mice fed a high‐fat diet. In vitro studies suggest that blocking ferroptosis inhibits oxidized low‐density lipoprotein (LDL)‐induced lipid peroxidation and endothelial dysfunction in mouse aortic ECs. 37 Another recent study reported that overexpression of sestrin 1, a stress‐inducible protein that protects cells against oxidative stress and other stressors, attenuates atherosclerosis in the Apoe −/− mouse model and suppresses inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in human umbilical vein ECs treated with oxidized LDL. 38 A recent study revealed that 59 genes associated with ferroptosis were identified in human atherosclerotic plaques by reanalysis of Gene Expression Omnibus data sets. Through functional annotation, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, and construction of protein–protein interaction networks, 5 hub genes (mitogen‐activated protein kinase 1[MAPK1], tumor protein p53[TP53], signal transducer and activator of transcription 3[STAT3], prostaglandin‐endoperoxide synthase 2[PTGS2], and heme oxygenase 1[HMOX1]) were identified. 39 Nevertheless, the role of ferroptosis in atherosclerosis and specific molecular mechanisms remains to be fully explored. Premenopausal women typically have lower iron stores due to menstrual blood loss and estrogen‐mediated suppression of hepatic hepcidin. In contrast, men and postmenopausal women tend to accumulate higher tissue iron, partially through testosterone‐driven hepcidin downregulation and increased erythropoiesis. 40 Serum ferritin levels are positively and independently associated with carotid atherosclerosis in Asian women but not in Asian men. 41 Additionally, intravenous saccharated ferric oxide significantly increases iron and malondialdehyde, a marker of lipid oxidation, and impairs flow‐mediated vasodilatation, an index for EC function, in male nonsmokers. 42

Iron Dysregulation in Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells

Dysfunction of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), a major cell type in the vessel tunica media, contributes to atherosclerotic plaque formation and aortic aneurysm development. VSMCs exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity, readily phenotypically switching to macrophage‐like or mesenchymal stem cell–like cells, which contributes to vascular remodeling and disease progression. 43 Iron overload induces malfunction in VSMCs, including proliferation, apoptosis, ROS production, phenotypic switch, and calcification. 44

Iron overload leads to extensive accumulation of iron in the arterial media layer (rich in VSMCs) in Apoe −/− /Fpn1 wt/C326S mice, a genetically engineered double‐mutation model combining Apoe deficiency with Fpn1 gain of function. The collagen content in atherosclerotic plaques in Apoe −/− /Fpn1 wt/C326S mice was reduced, even though there was no significant change in the quantity of VSMCs in the plaques. 16 Accumulation of NTBI in VSMCs creates a pro‐oxidant environment, promoting foam cell formation and increasing plaque vulnerability. Treatment with ferric ammonium citrate, an iron supplement delivering a bioavailable source of ferric iron, induces apoptosis, inflammation, and MCP‐1 expression, as well as monocyte adhesion and migration. 16 Moreover, iron chelation using deferoxamine significantly reduces VSMC proliferation. 45 Although elevated ferritin levels, reflecting iron dysregulation, are positively associated with cardiovascular risk in patients with chronic kidney disease, 46 clinical studies that directly link iron overload to vascular calcification remain limited. Cell culture studies indicate that iron‐induced calcification in human aortic smooth muscle cells is mediated by interleukin‐24, which is increased during calcification. 47 Consistently, in vivo studies demonstrated that iron overload promotes aortic calcium deposition and interleukin‐24 expression in rats, which further supports interleukin‐24’s role in pathogenic vascular calcification. 48 Moreover, both solute carrier family 39 member 14 and solute carrier family 39 member 8 are upregulated in VSMCs during vascular calcification. Overexpression of solute carrier family 39 member 14 enhances calcification and increases intracellular iron accumulation in VSMCs. 49 Zip14 is also upregulated in macrophages in response to lipopolysaccharide treatment. Silencing of Zip14 in macrophages significantly reduced the production and release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α). 50 Taken together, solute carrier family 39 member 14 promotes iron and other metal uptake, enhances vascular calcification, and induces inflammation in diverse cell types. However, the precise mechanisms by which iron contributes to vascular calcification remain to be fully elucidated.

Iron‐induced inflammation and oxidative stress contribute to the pathophysiology of AAA. The iron‐accumulated area positively correlates with the macrophage infiltration area in both human and murine AAA walls. Iron restriction decreases the incidence of AAA with a reduction of oxidative stress and inflammation. 51 Smooth muscle cell–specific deletion of Smad4 increases the expression of inflammatory cytokine interleukin‐1β, which promotes vascular inflammation and AAA formation in adult mice. 52 Recent work by Ren et al. revealed the critical role of ferroptosis in the formation and rupture of AAA, and the association of ferroptosis with inflammatory and oxidative responses through TNF and the NOD‐like signaling pathways. GPX4 was identified as a critical anti‐ferroptosis gene and a potential therapeutic target for AAA treatment. 53 Supporting this, recent investigations in Apoe −/− mice found that activation of the solute carrier family 7 member 11–GPX4 signaling pathway by ferrostatin‐1 inhibits lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in mouse VSMCs and improves structural integrity of the aortic wall. 54 The mechanisms by which iron dysregulation in VSMCs contributes to atherosclerosis and AAA are illustrated in Figure 2. Currently, few clinical studies have confirmed the effects of ferroptosis on AAA progression, underscoring the opportunity and need to clarify its therapeutic potential.

Iron Dysregulation in Macrophages

Macrophages are involved in early atherosclerotic lesion formation and advanced plaque stability by inflammatory and lipid‐handling pathways. Iron dysregulation, both iron deficiency and iron overload, induces ROS and lipid peroxidation in macrophages, thereby exacerbating vascular inflammation. Iron overload induces macrophage dysfunction by promoting foam cell formation and, as recently reported, by altering glycolysis and macrophage polarization. 55

Notably, the iron chelator deferoxamine decreases intracellular iron levels in Apoe −/− mice and reduces proinflammatory factors, including MCP‐1, and markers of macrophage infiltration. This intervention effectively attenuates atherosclerotic lesion formation. 56 Similarly, treatment with deferiprone, another iron chelator, reduced lipopolysaccharide‐induced M1 phenotypic expression and decreased oxidized LDL uptake. 57 Hepcidin deficiency is associated with increased serum iron and decreased intracellular iron in macrophages. Hepcidin‐ and LDL receptor–deficient (Hamp −/−/Ldlr −/−) mice fed a high‐fat diet for 21 weeks showed attenuated atherosclerosis and reduced iron content and inflammatory response in macrophages. 57 Macrophage‐specific Fpn1 deficiency (Fpn1 LysM/LysM/Apoe −/−) was shown to exacerbate atherosclerosis by increasing ROS and systemic inflammation and altering plaque lipid composition. Mechanistically, Fpn1 deficiency significantly downregulates ABC transporters (ABCA1 and ABCG1) via reducing the expression of liver X receptor‐ α. 58 In contrast, another study using Apoe −/− mice with macrophage‐specific Fpn1 deletion showed iron accumulation in macrophages without a significant effect on overall atherosclerotic lesion development. 59 A separate investigation using Apoe −/−/Fpn1 wt/C326S mice demonstrated increased macrophage content and lipid burden within atherosclerotic plaques. Additionally, this study suggests that intraplaque VSMCs may adopt a macrophage‐like phenotype through a phenotypic switch under conditions of iron dysregulation. 16 Collectively, these findings indicate that the relationship between macrophage iron metabolism and atherosclerosis progression is still incompletely understood and needs more studies. 16 , 33 Single‐nucleotide polymorphisms in the hemochromatosis gene Hfe (homeostatic iron regulator) have been identified to be associated with reduced LDL cholesterol in human plasma on the basis of analysis of the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium dataset. In Apoe −/− mice, Hfe deletion reduced atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, Hfe not only acts as a negative regulator of LDL receptor expression in hepatocytes but also controls the Kupffer cell uptake and transfer of LDL cholesterol to hepatocytes. 60 The study links iron availability, Kupffer cell function, cholesterol homeostasis, and atherosclerosis.

Macrophages mediate iron accumulation in AAA tissues, and chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix degradation contribute to disease progression. 51 , 61 Recalcati et al. demonstrated that iron‐loaded macrophages upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) under proinflammatory conditions. 62 Inhibiting macrophage accumulation and reducing inflammation may protect against AAA development. 61 Kitagawa et al revealed that AAA formation in Apoe −/− mice induced by angiotensin II positively correlates with iron accumulation in both vascular macrophages and ECs, a feature absent in control mice. 63 Similarly, Sawada et al. demonstrated that iron accumulation and macrophage infiltration occur in human and murine AAA tissues. Iron restriction decreases the incidence of AAA formation by suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress. Aortic expression of TFR1 was increased in human and murine AAA walls, and the TFR1‐positive area correlates with regions of iron accumulation and F4/80‐positive macrophage infiltration. 51 Moreover, iron restriction attenuates the activities of c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase, aortic MMP‐2, and MMP‐9 in mouse AAA walls. 51 Single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis of human AAA tissues revealed significant upregulation of the ferroptosis‐related pathway in macrophages compared with normal tissue. In murine AAA models, treatment with liproxstatin‐1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor, reduces aortic diameter, suppresses proinflammatory cytokine expression, and limits macrophage infiltration. At the mechanistic level, liproxstatin‐1 mitigates ferroptosis and lowers macrophage MMP‐9 expression. 64 How macrophage iron dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and AAA is illustrated in Figure 2. In summary, correction of iron dysregulation in macrophages is a potential therapeutic approach to slow AAA progression.

Iron Dysregulation in Other Immune Cell Types

In addition to macrophages, iron metabolism also modulates the activity of other immune cells that contribute to vascular injury. In neutrophils, iron is crucial for antimicrobial function and for the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). 65 Neutrophils significantly contribute to atherosclerosis by secreting interleukin‐1β. Using the murine model of atherosclerosis, cholesterol crystals triggered neutrophils to release neutrophil extracellular traps. These neutrophil extracellular traps prepare macrophages to release cytokines, activate T‐helper 17 cells, and promote sterile inflammation in atherosclerosis. 66 The research indicated that hyperlipidemia in the Apoe‐deficient mouse model leads to neutrophilia, which is associated with the early development of atherosclerotic lesions. Neutrophils are also present at sites of plaque rupture and in individuals experiencing acute coronary syndromes. 67 Iron availability is essential for T‐cell activation and effector function. Iron influx supports interleukin‐2 receptor signaling, mitochondrial respiration, and proliferation of CD4+ T cells. Consistently, iron restriction during T‐cell stimulation negatively impacts mitochondrial function. 68 The role of ferroptosis in T cells has been explored primarily in the field of tumor immunology. Regulation of ferroptosis in T cells, including how iron availability, lipid peroxidation, and GPX4 influence T‐cell fate and function, remains incompletely understood. 69 Direct evidence linking iron dysregulation in T cells to vascular diseases is currently limited. Natural killer cells have been implicated in a range of vascular diseases, mainly through their effects on immune surveillance and vascular inflammation. Current studies show that iron availability modulates natural killer metabolism and cytotoxicity, mainly in viral infection models. 70 Direct evidence that natural killer cell–specific iron dysregulation causes vascular disease remains limited. Collectively, accumulating data suggest that iron dysregulation in immune cells may promote proinflammatory polarization and potentially facilitate vascular pathology, which warrants further investigation.

Systemic Iron Dysregulation and Vascular Diseases

The direct effects of iron dysfunction on vascular cells, associated with atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysms, have been increasingly well recognized; however, its effects on vascular cells in PAD are less well understood. PAD is a progressive vascular disease characterized by impaired blood flow and ischemia, primarily caused by atherosclerosis. 71 Indeed, systemic iron overload or deficiency is implicated in the progression of PAD.

Iron overload promotes oxidative stress and vascular inflammation. Excess reactive iron facilitates ROS formation via Fenton chemistry, resulting in endothelial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and VSMC dysfunction. 72 Elevated serum ferritin has been associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic disease, including PAD. 73 NTBI promotes oxidative stress and disrupts cellular membrane lipids, thereby impairing cell function. In patients with PAD, the NTBI pool shifts from low‐molecular‐weight complexes to higher‐molecular‐weight forms compared with healthy controls. Most NTBI is bound to serum albumin, and this NTBI–albumin complex can be isolated and may serve as a potential biomarker. 74 Sex differences in PAD have been well recognized. Women tend to present later in the disease course with greater functional impairment and more rapid decline than men. Although men generally have higher rates of lower‐limb amputation, women often experience poorer recovery and quality of life. 75 Notably, a recent study found that increased ferritin within physiological limits is associated with clinically significant PAD in women, suggesting a possible role of iron (directly or indirectly) and highlighting the sex differences in PAD. 76 Iron chelation or reduction of iron stores can attenuate vascular lesions and improve endothelial function. 35 Conversely, iron deficiency, often observed in chronic inflammation and kidney disease, is associated with impaired EC angiogenesis and proliferation. 77 Iron is required to maintain mitochondrial energy production and endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity. 78 Iron deficiency can worsen ischemia in PAD by inhibiting endothelial function recovery and neovascularization. 79 In the mouse ischemic hind‐limb model, deferoxamine pretreatment accelerates endothelial progenitor cell senescence, suppresses endothelial progenitor cell angiogenesis, and inhibits blood perfusion in ischemic tissue. 80 The impact of endothelial iron dysfunction on PAD is depicted in Figure 2. Recent evidence shows increased expression of ferroptosis‐related genes, including prostaglandin–endoperoxide synthase 2, heme oxygenase 1, acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4, ELAV‐like RNA‐binding protein 1, and beclin‐1, in the calf muscle of patients with critical limb ischemia PAD compared with controls without PAD and patients with intermittent claudication, impying that ferroptosis contributes to myopathy at an advanced stage of PAD. 81 Overall, these findings underscore the importance of precise iron regulation to support effective vascular repair in ischemic conditions and highlight the need to dissect the mechanisms by which iron dysregulation in vascular cells drives PAD pathogenesis.

Anemia is a common condition in patients hospitalized for PAD and is independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular and limb events, amputation, and death. Both amputation and mortality risk increase significantly at low levels of hemoglobin. 82 Iron deficiency anemia is highly prevalent worldwide. Iron deficiency, particularly transferrin saturation levels, may also exacerbate the severity of PAD, and correcting iron deficiency may improve outcomes, suggesting that iron deficiency could be a therapeutic target. 83 , 84 Anemia increases the risk of perioperative morbidity and death among patients with PAD, who have an increased prevalence of iron deficiency anemia. Patients with critical ischemia, the most severe category of symptomatic PAD, also have a higher prevalence of anemia, as well as high near‐term rates of amputation and death. 84 , 85 More research is needed to fully understand the relationship among anemia, iron deficiency, and PAD, which could help determine the best strategies for managing iron deficiency in PAD. 85

Iron Status in Acute Cerebral Diseases and CVDs

Iron balance is increasingly recognized as a determinant of injury severity and recovery in acute stroke and myocardial ischemia. A Mendelian randomization analysis showed that genetically higher serum iron, ferritin, and transferrin saturation are positively associated with stroke. 86 In contrast, iron deficiency has emerged as a prognostic factor in acute cardiovascular events. A meta‐analysis of acute coronary syndrome cohorts found that iron deficiency is independently associated with a worse long‐term prognosis. 87 Furthermore, a recent prospective multicenter cohort demonstrated that low ferritin at admission, defined as <100 or 100 to 299 ng/mL with transferrin saturation <20%, was associated with worse 90‐day functional recovery in acute ischemic stroke. 88 These findings support that both iron overload and deficiency impair tissue resilience, underscoring the importance of maintaining iron homeostasis in acute cerebrovascular disease and CVD.

Potential Iron Therapies and Clinical Trials

Therapeutic Agents Targeting Iron‐Related Pathways in Atherosclerosis

Growing evidence suggests that therapies targeting iron metabolism may offer a promising strategy for treating atherosclerosis and other CVDs. Excess iron triggers multiple cellular disturbances, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis, which in turn contribute to endothelial dysfunction, VSMC calcification, and foam cell formation. Deferoxamine, deferiprone, deferasirox, and dexrazoxane are Food and Drug Administration–approved iron chelators widely used in clinical practice to manage iron overload disorders. 89 In parallel, ferroptosis inhibitors, including ferrostatin‐1 and liproxstatin‐1, have emerged as potential agents to prevent the progression of atherosclerosis. Those inhibitors scavenge lipid peroxides, reduce labile iron pools, and limit ROS accumulation, thereby targeting the iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation pathways. 90 Beyond traditional iron chelation and supplementation, several new strategies can potentially modulate iron handling more precisely in vascular disease. Modifying the hepcidin–ferroportin‐1 axis with hepcidin agonists or antagonists would redistribute iron from macrophages and vascular cells, thereby reducing inflammation and inhibiting atherogenesis. 57 Radical‐trapping antioxidants ferrostatin‐1 and liproxstatin‐1, both ferroptosis inhibitors, reduce iron‐induced lipid peroxidation and attenuate vascular injury in preclinical models of atherosclerosis and ischemia–reperfusion injury. 37 , 91 Emerging approaches that target ferritinophagy, lysosomal iron regulation, and mitochondrial iron handling further highlight iron metabolism as a viable therapeutic pathway to mitigate vascular injury in atherosclerosis, aneurysm, and PAD.

Clinical Trials Evaluating Oral and Intravenous Iron Therapy

Clinical trials evaluating iron therapy in CVD, particularly HF, have been conducted, and some have shown beneficial outcomes for patients. Several studies have demonstrated the benefits of intravenous iron therapy in HF, particularly in improving exercise and functional capacity. The FERRIC‐HF (Ferric Iron in Heart Failure) trial (n=35) showed improved exercise tolerance, especially in anemic patients receiving intravenous iron. 92 The FAIR‐HF (Ferinject Assessment in Patients With Iron Deficiency and Chronic Heart Failure) trial was the very first randomized controlled trial to show a functional benefit of intravenous iron in patients with HF, conducted by a large double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study, randomized 459 patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction and iron deficiency to receive ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) or placebo, showing improved symptoms and exercise capacity. 93 CONFIRM‐HF (A Study to Determine the Efficacy and Safety of Finerenone and SGLT2i in Combination in Hospitalized Patients With Heart Failure; n=304) further demonstrated that FCM significantly improved 6‐minute walk distance, reduced hospitalizations, and enhanced quality of life. 94 In EFFECT‐HF (Effect of Ferric Carboxymaltose on Exercise Capacity in Patients With Iron Deficiency and Chronic Heart Failure; n=172), FCM maintained peak VO2 over 24 weeks, but the control group declined. 95 The AFFIRM‐AHF (A Randomized, Double‐Blind Placebo Controlled Trial Comparing the Effect of Intravenous Ferric Carboxymaltose on Hospitalizations and Mortality in Iron Deficient Patients Admitted for Acute Heart Failure) trial evaluated intravenous FCM in patients with iron deficiency hospitalized for acute HF. While FCM reduced recurrent hospitalizations, it did not significantly lower cardiovascular death over 1 year. 96 In the IRONMAN (Effectiveness of Intravenous Iron Treatment Versus Standard Care in Patients With Heart Failure and Iron Deficiency) trial (2022), 1137 patients with chronic HF and iron deficiency were followed up for up to 7 years. Although intravenous iron was associated with fewer HF hospitalizations and cardiovascular deaths, the differences were not statistically significant. 97 The PIVOTAL (Proactive Intravenous Iron Therapy in Hemodialysis Patients) trial, conducted in hemodialysis patients, showed that high‐dose intravenous iron significantly reduced the risk of first and recurrent HF events. 98 In this population, these clinical benefits may result from correction of functional iron deficiency and enhancement of systemic oxygen delivery, which could outweigh potential proatherogenic effects of iron supplementation. The impact of iron supplementation likely varies with the clinical context, baseline iron status, and patient comorbidities. Conversely, in the TACT (Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy), disodium EDTA‐based iron chelation therapy was evaluated in patients with atherosclerosis and was shown to reduce adverse cardiovascular events in stable patients following myocardial infarction. 99

The HEART‐FID (Treatment for Heart Failure With Iron Deficiency) trial (2023; n=3065) demonstrated that intravenous FCM modestly improved 6‐minute walk distance and reduced HF‐related hospitalizations and deaths over 12 months. 100 The recent FAIR‐HF2 trial found that patients with transferrin saturation <20% showed comparable benefits regardless of transferrin saturation levels and no differential response based on ischemic status. Importantly, higher cumulative intravenous iron doses were well tolerated, addressing prior safety concerns. However, the overall results did not meet predefined statistical criteria for efficacy. 101 Recent clinical trials indicate that treating iron deficiency in patients with HF reduces the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, but optimal intravenous iron dosing strategies remain to be established. Intravenous iron therapy appears more effective than oral supplementation for improving functional capacity in HF. Current Challenges and Future Directions

Iron imbalance leads to vascular dysfunction and vascular disease, with ROS and inflammatory processes being key components. Understanding the role of iron dysregulation in vascular diseases might yield efficient strategies for treating them. 2 Targeting iron dysregulation has shown potential in mitigating the inflammatory aspects of cardiac disease. For example, iron chelator deferiprone effectively attenuates inflammatory responses in rats with dilated cardiomyopathy by reducing nuclear factor κ‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells and cyclooxygenase‐2 transcript and protein levels. 104 Beyond iron chelation and supplementation, additional strategies aimed at modulating systemic and cellular iron balance include targeting the prolyl hydroxylase domain protein–hypoxia‐inducible factor pathway, regulating the hepcidin–ferroportin‐1 axis, and using ferroptosis inhibitors. 1 Modulating systemic or cellular iron levels through hepcidin regulation, iron chelation, or ferroptosis inhibition may provide new avenues to inhibit oxidative vascular injury. Further studies are needed to evaluate therapeutic approaches that restore iron homeostasis to preserve vascular function. Combining such strategies with metabolic or anti‐inflammatory interventions may provide synergistic protection against vascular injury.

In atherosclerosis, current investigations focus on elucidating multiple aspects of iron biology. However, the effects of various iron sources on vascular pathology, the prognostic value of NTBI and transferrin‐bound iron, and the impact of ferroptosis on vascular dysfunction remain to be fully investigated. 33 In addition, AAA and atherosclerosis share common risk factors and pathological features. It is therefore plausible that preventing iron accumulation may warrant investigation as a therapeutic strategy for AAA. Indeed, iron restriction has been shown to suppress AAA formation by reducing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress and by improving endothelial function. 51 , 61 Moreover, sex differences in iron status have been reported in vascular diseases, including atherosclerosis and PAD. The mechanisms underlying sex‐dependent regulation of iron handling and differential cellular responses remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Integrated advanced approaches combining analyses of human samples with preclinical animal models and appropriate alternative in vitro systems will provide a robust framework to uncover novel mechanisms of iron dysregulation in vascular injury. Single‐cell and single‐nucleus RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics can delineate cell‐type–specific responses and explore intercellular communication among different cell types in target vascular tissues. Comparative analysis of diseased versus control tissues helps elucidate the mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of vascular diseases. A major knowledge gap exists between iron dysregulation and PAD. While iron overload has been linked to oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction in atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysm, mechanistic studies in ischemic PAD skeletal muscle and microvasculature are limited. Future work is needed to define how iron handling and ferroptosis in ECs, VSMCs, and immune cells, and their intercellular interactions, contribute to disease severity and limb outcomes. Translational efforts can integrate iron‐related molecular signatures with functional outcomes. Vascular organoids are powerful models for investigating vascular development, disease mechanisms, and regenerative medicine. They support the study of physiological and pathophysiological processes and provide a platform for exploring mechanisms and screening drugs. Moreover, clinical trials could assess whether correcting iron overload or deficiency improves vascular cell function and reduces recurrent cardiovascular events. Despite the encouraging results, additional studies and more preclinical research are needed to refine these strategies and reduce the risk of adverse effects associated with iron modulation. Integrating circulating biomarkers with imaging‐based iron quantification may enhance trial design and enable more precise patient selection.

Conclusions

Iron homeostasis is critical for maintaining cellular function and various physiological processes. Excessive or deficient iron is detrimental to living organisms. The pathogenic role of iron overload in vascular diseases has been widely recognized in recent years. Indeed, both iron overload and iron deficiency can impair vascular cell function, underscoring the indispensability of a balanced iron status for vascular homeostasis. Thus, the dual impacts of iron dysfunction and the underlying molecular mechanisms warrant further elucidation. Although animal models of iron overload or deficiency are used for in vivo studies, how well current animal models can replicate human vascular pathology needs careful evaluation. Over the past decade, accumulating experimental data suggest that ferroptosis contributes to the pathophysiology of CVDs. However, the specific mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in various vascular diseases remain to be fully investigated. The significance of ferroptosis inhibition needs to be evaluated in clinical trials. Identification and validation of sensitive and specific biomarkers beyond conventional iron indices are needed for quantification of iron dysregulation, prognosis, and translational applications. Exploring the impact of iron dysregulation and underlying mechanisms will help identify potential therapeutic targets and develop novel approaches to treat CVD.
