Irregularity of visual motion perception and negative symptoms in schizophrenia
Authors: Yi Fan, Yunhai Tao, Jue Wang, Yuan Gao, Wei Wei, Chanying Zheng, Xiaotong Zhang, Xue Mei Song, Georg Northoff
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Schizophrenia (SZ) is a severe psychiatric disorder characterized by perceptual, emotional, and behavioral abnormalities, with cognitive impairment being a prominent feature of the disorder. Recent studies demonstrate irregularity in SZ with increased variability on the neural level. Is there also irregularity on the psychophysics level like in visual perception? Here, we introduce a methodology to analyze the irregularity in a trial-by-trial way to compare the SZ and healthy control (HC) subjects. In addition, we use an unsupervised clustering algorithm K-means + + to identify SZ subgroups in the sample, followed by validation of the subgroups based on intraindividual visual perception variability and clinical symptomatology. The K-means + + method divided SZ patients into two subgroups by measuring durations across trials in the motion discrimination task, i.e., high, and low irregularity of SZ patients (HSZ, LSZ). We found that HSZ and LSZ subgroups are associated with more negative and positive symptoms respectively. Applying a mediation model in the HSZ subgroup, the enhanced irregularity mediates the relationship between visual perception and negative symptoms. Together, we demonstrate increased irregularity in visual perception of a HSZ subgroup, including its association with negative symptoms. This may serve as a promising marker for identifying and distinguishing SZ subgroups.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a complex syndrome, and heterogeneity is particularly evident in the early and developmental stages of the illness1–3. Characteristically, symptoms of SZ can be classified as “positive”, “negative”, and “cognitive”4. Of these, negative symptoms have been reported as one of the most common early symptoms manifestation of schizophrenia5–7, manifesting as a relative lack of affective, cognitive and goal-directed behaviors8,9. Diagnosis and treatment for SZ are currently based on the patterns of symptoms and their course, rather than objective markers, though10,11. A key problem in identifying clinically relevant markers is the high inter-individual’s variability among SZ participants12–16. This raises the question whether such high inter-individual variability may, in part, be physiological, rather than just being methodological noise in the data, and thus reflect high intraindividual variability within the schizophrenia subjects themselves. In that case, intraindividual variability may by itself serve as potential objective marker for distinguishing different individuals or subgroups of SZ on psychological or neural grounds.

Impairments in visual motion perception offer the opportunity for identifying potential markers in multiple neuropsychiatric disorders17–19, including SZ20–22. A well-known visual paradigm, motion center-surround interactions, can evoke spatial suppression in high contrast moving gratings, i.e., patterns become harder to perceive as stimulus size increases23,24. This paradigm reflects visual motion processing in human middle temporal visual cortex (MT), and weak spatial suppression is associated with decreased GABA concentrations in MT area25. Tadin et al. discovered that SZ patients exhibited elevated duration thresholds and abnormally weak center-surround suppression (e.g., motion suppression index (SI)) with the latter being most pronounced in SZ with negative symptoms26. The commonly used index for duration threshold in the paradigm is usually the duration required to reach the set steady-state accuracy rate obtained by fitting multiple trials, i.e., the static index. However, this neglects the changes in the intra-subjects’ visuo-behavioral responses from trial to trial, i.e., their variability. Such trial-by-trial variability may be key in SZ as recent studies using other tasks on both behavioral27,28 and neural29,30 levels observed higher variability between trials in SZ thus featuring them by temporal irregularity and imprecision.

We here raise the question whether such higher variability and irregularity are also manifest on the psychophysical level of visual perception in SZ. In view of this, the aim of this study was to quantify the variability of visual motion perception in patients with schizophrenia, to explore the heterogeneity of SZ subgroups driven by visual motion perception, and to identify a putative objective marker for SZ subgroups. For that purpose, we developed a method for dynamic analysis of the visual motion suppression task, using a staircase method to analyze the variability of visual-behavioral perception in a dynamic way on a trial-by-trial basis. We hypothesized higher variability and thus irregularity on a trial-to-trial basis in SZ compared to HC. Next, based on the variability of durations across trials, we identified subgroups of SZs, i.e., high and low irregularity groups (HSZ and LSZ), by using the unsupervised cluster analysis method. Moreover, we associated those subgroups with different symptoms like negative and positive. Together, we show abnormally high temporal irregularity in visual perception in SZ which allows to define two different SZ subgroups. Therefore, our dynamic variability measures may serve as a promising marker for differentiating SZ subgroups, which carries important scientific and clinical implications.

Differences in static measures and variability indices between SZ and HC

The differences between the SZ and HC groups in the traditional static measures were analyzed based on the fitting stable value across all trials24,26. In addition, when calculating differences based on the variability between trials, we found that the SZ group showed higher mean values (3.90 ± 2.30 ms) than the HC group (2.64 ± 0.79 ms) (t = 4.07, P < 0.001) and higher variability in SD values (8.02 ± 1.97 ms) compared to the HC group (6.97 ± 1.48 ms) (t = 3.36, P = 0.003) (Fig. 2d, e). Together, these findings clearly show higher variability and thus more irregularity in the trial-to-trial durations in SZ when employing the dynamic analysis of the visual motion task.

Principal component analysis on trial-by-trial durations

Figure 2a, b shows significant differences between the HC group and the schizophrenic patients in small stimulus thresholds, but no significant differences were found in time thresholds for large stimuli, and these results are consistent with previous findings26,31. Therefore, our subsequent analyses focus on the motion perception data of the small gratings. We extracted the raw data for the 80 consecutive durations contained in the small stimuli which was done for each subject in both groups, such each single trial from each subject was included in the subsequent analyses. These features were then subjected to Principal Component Analysis, resulting in the extraction of four principal components (PCs) from a cohort of 60 SZ. These four PCs accounted for 47.08%, 62.56%, 71.85%, and 78.32% of the cumulative variance in the data, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

SZ subgroups determination based on unsupervised clustering

Following K-means + + clustering analysis, two different clusters were identified: Cluster 1 (SZ1) with a sample size of 24, and Cluster 2 (SZ2), consisting of 36 samples (Fig. 3a, b). The evaluation of the number of clusters was determined by the elbow method32 and the gap statistic method33 (details in Supplementary Method and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Cluster comparison

The difference between the two subgroups was further analyzed by their static and variability indices. The small threshold of the SZ1 group was significantly higher (4.81 ± 2.25 ms) than that of the HC group (3.88 ± 0.74 ms), (F (2,120) = 4.13, P = 0.016). The Hedges’ g effect sizes for the comparisons between the groups were as follows: HC vs. SZ1, 0.781; HC vs. SZ2, 0.392; and SZ1 vs. SZ2, 0.272. The suppression index (SI) value of SZ1 group (0.12 ± 0.15) was significantly lower than that of the HC group (0.20 ± 0.12) (F (2, 120) = 3.46, P = 0.033). In contrast to the variability measures, the static parameters did not differ between the SZ2 group and the other two groups. The Hedges’ g effect sizes for SI as follows: HC vs. SZ1, 0.613; HC vs. SZ2, 0.317; and SZ1 vs. SZ2, −0.274 (Fig. 3c, d).

Next, we compared the variability features between the two subgroups. The mean values were significantly higher in the SZ1 group (mean: 5.41 ± 2.86 ms) compared to the HC group (mean: 2.74 ± 0.81 ms) and the SZ2 group (mean: 2.89 ± 0.98 ms), (F (2, 120) = 30.36, P < 0.001). Similarly, the standard deviation (SD) values were also significantly higher in the SZ1 group (SD: 9.58 ± 1.38 ms) compared to the HC group (SD: 6.88 ± 1.72 ms) and the SZ2 group (SD: 6.98 ± 1.59 ms) (F (2, 120) = 26.13, P < 0.001), whereas no such differences were observed between HC and SZ2 (Fig. 3e, f). The Hedges’ g effect sizes for the mean were: HC vs. SZ1, −1.617; HC vs. SZ2, −0.173; and SZ1 vs. SZ2, 1.272. The Hedges’ g effect sizes for the SD were: HC vs. SZ1, −1.641; HC vs. SZ2, −0.062; and SZ1 vs. SZ2, 1.700. The results excluding the outliers in Fig. 3c–f are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, which shows consistent differences in the variability indices.

The enhanced variability in the SZ1 subgroup can be explained by the increased fluctuations in the durations across trials (details see Supplementary Method). We therefore termed it as hyper irregularity in that schizophrenia subgroup (HSZ). In contrast, the variability index of the SZ2 subgroup had no significant differences from HC. This was further confirmed when, after extracting the principal components from the visual data of HC and the two SZ subgroups together, we again confirmed that there is a significant overlap between HC and SZ2 (Fig. 3g). Therefore, we referred to SZ2 as low irregularity schizophrenia (LSZ).

Cluster assessment

To validate the optimal two-cluster solution identified by the K-means + + algorithm, we employed an independent validation approach using the Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) algorithm with a K-fold procedure. With 3-fold cross-validation, the mean training accuracy was 97.50% and the mean testing accuracy was 93.33% ± 11.55; with sixfold cross-validation, the mean training accuracy was 96.67% and the mean testing accuracy was 95.00% ± 8.37; finally, with ninefold cross-validation, the mean training accuracy was 96.46% and the mean testing accuracy of 96.83% ± 6.30. Overall, the algorithm showed a stable performance across training-testing partitions, indicating robustness and consistency in its classification capability (Fig. 3h). To improve the reliability of the results, we introduced the leave-one-out (LOO) method and conducted the experiments accordingly; the confusion matrices for all test sets are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Relationships between subgroups and psychopathological symptoms

Linear correlations between visual indices and clinical symptoms

In HSZ and LSZ subgroups, we revealed distinct patterns of association between visual indices (small threshold, suppression index, standard deviation and mean) and clinical symptoms measured by PANSS (Fig. 4a). Notably, the HSZ group exhibited significant correlations of PANSS Total scores (PANSS-T) with visual static index SI, and the two visual variability indices (mean, SD). Furthermore, the HSZ group exhibited pronounced associations of these visual indices with specifically the negative symptoms. Conversely, in the LSZ group, the association was observed between mean values and positive symptoms. When considering all SZ individuals, a general linear regression analysis revealed a trend toward a negative correlation between the SI index and PANSS-T score (r = −0.25, P = 0.05). However, further regression of the cluster results indicated that this negative correlation was primarily driven by the HSZ subgroup (r = −0.493, P < 0.014), while no such correlation trend was observed in the LSZ subgroup (r = 0.017, P = 0.922) (Fig. 4b). Mediation analysis

In HSZ subgroup, we observed significant correlations between SD and SI (r = 0.52, P = 0.009) as well as between SD and PANSS-T (r = −0.60, P = 0.002) (Fig. 4c). To further investigate the potential role of the three variables, we applied a mediation analysis. In this analysis, SD values (mediating variable Mi) are related to both independent variable SI (X) and the dependent variable PANSS-T score (Y). The results revealed a complete mediation effect, with SD (Mi) accounting for 40% of the observed relationship between SI (X) and PANSS-T (Y). The significance of this mediation effect was further demonstrated by the bootstrapping test, which revealed a significantly indirect effect (c = −7.56, [BootLLCI, BootULCI] = [−19.93, −1.59]) (Fig. 4d). Whereas the direct effect (c’= −11.39, [LLCI, ULCI] = [−24.87, 2.08]) was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the relationship between SI and PANSS-T is primarily mediated by SD, highlighting the important role of visual perceptual variability in SZ subjects in contributing to generate their symptom severity.

Furthermore, notable negative correlations were observed between SI and PANSS Negative scales (PANSS-N) (r = −0.43, P = 0.034), as well as between SD and PANSS-N scales (r = −0.59, P = 0.003) (Fig. 4e). In the mediation analysis among the three variables of SI, SD and PANSS-N scales, there was strong evidence that SD (mediating variable Mi) entirely mediated the association between SI (X) and PANSS-N (Y), with a mediation percentage of 47%. The bootstrapping test showed a significant indirect effect (c = −4.49, [BootLLCI, BootULCI] = [−10.98, −1.17]) while the direct effect (c’ = −5.13, [LLCI, ULCI] = [−13.18, 2.93]) was by itself not statistically significant (Fig. 4f).

In addition, we probed another variable, with SI (Mi) as the mediating variable between SD (X) and clinical symptoms (Y). No significant mediation effect on either PANSS-T (c = −1.65, [BootLLCI, BootULCI] = [−3.52, 0.16]) or PANSS-N (c = −0.74, [BootLLCI, BootULCI] = [−2.10, 0.42]) was observed (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). These findings suggest that the relationship between SI and symptom scores of PANSS-T and/or PANSS-N is indirect only and mediated by the variability marker, e.g., SD. Moreover, in the analysis of symptom-driven behavior (symptoms as independent variable), we did not obtain any significant mediation results (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). Together, these results demonstrated that the variability index SD value is pivotal in mediating the impact of visual perception (SI) on psychopathological symptoms severity (PANSS-T and/or PANSS-N) in the HSZ group.

Differential symptom patterns in subgroups

In the total SZ sample, the number of patients with negative symptoms is approximately equal to the number of patients with positive symptoms (Supplementary Fig. 6a). After K-means + + clustering in this work, it is noteworthy to remark that the predominant proportion of patients (66%) exhibited negative symptoms in the HSZ subgroup. In contrast, the predominant proportion of patients (62%) exhibited positive symptoms in the LSZ subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Furthermore, we compared the N-5 score (where N-5 is the single symptom from PANSS negative) between the HSZ and LSZ groups and found that there is a significant difference in (P < 0.009), e.g., compared to the LSZ group, the HSZ group suffered a more significant impairment in abstract thinking difficulty (N-5 scores, shown in Supplementary Fig. 7). Additionally, a noteworthy correlation between mean values and PANSS-negative scales was observed in the HSZ group (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Conversely, in the LSZ group, a significant relationship emerged between mean values and PANSS-P scales (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

To justify and control for the specificity of the clustering results, particularly the association of visual perceptual irregularity with specific psychopathological symptoms, we conducted the reverse analyses. We now classified SZ subjects primarily according to their symptoms, e.g., negative, and positive, rather than according to their dynamic visual measures. This classification was then subjected to secondary comparison with respect to the visual indices (static and variability indices) between SZ patients with predominantly negative symptoms and those with predominantly positive symptoms as two groups. The results showed that there were no significant differences in static indices and variability indices between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b).

Together, our results suggest that, although there were no differences in the PANSS scale scores (Positive, Negative, General and Total) and disease duration across the two subgroups when analyzed independently of their visual perception variability measures (see Supplementary Table 2). This, albeit indirectly, further supports that the occurrence of negative and positive symptoms is specifically related to the dynamic changes in visual perception rather than just reflecting the distribution and severity of positive and negative symptoms by themselves independent of the visual dynamics.

Discussion

We here, for the first time, investigated the variability of visual motion perception in SZ. This allowed us to distinguish a high and low irregularity group which were associated with negative and positive symptoms, respectively. Moreover, high variability (SD values) mediates the relationship of visual perception (suppression index, SI values) with total and negative symptom severity in the HSZ subgroup. Together, our findings show the potential of visual perception irregularity serving as a promising biomarker of SZ subgroups. This carries both clinical diagnostic and therapeutic implications.

In all SZ group, we found the reduced surround suppression (lower SI values) compared to HC subjects (see Fig. 2c). These results are consisted with previous findings in other domains of visual perception26. Weaker surround suppression (lower SI values) corresponds to the reduction of perception abilities, including figure-ground segmentation, contour detection, and perception of three-dimensional structures34–37, and also the lower performance in complex cognition38,39. A deficit in the GABAergic system is well documented in the reduced surround suppression25,40,41. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that visual perception deficits in SZ patients are typically associated with negative symptoms rather than positive symptoms26,42,43.

Although PANSS scores did not significantly differ between the two subgroups (Supplementary Table S2), we found that their variability indices differed between them (Fig. 3e, f). This suggests that the dynamic indices may provide a more fine-grained resolution and differentiation than the more coarse-grained psychopathological scoring. More generally, our results are consistent with previous research, which suggest that clinical symptoms and their biological underpinnings may not always be in perfect agreement44,45. Moreover, our study suggests that HSZ and LSZ may arise from different underlying mechanisms. In the HSZ subgroup, negative symptoms are mediated by visual variability indices (SD, Fig. 4c–f). While in the LSZ subgroup, visual variability indices are associated with positive symptoms (Fig. 4a). Our findings in especially the high irregularity subgroup extend these observations by showing that visual perceptual variability is enhanced in SZ which may thus mediate the impact of the decreased surround suppression (SI values) on negative symptoms: due to the increased variability, e.g., irregularity, SZ subjects may remain unable to synchronize with and thus align (e.g., temporo-spatial alignment46) their own inner dynamics to the outer motion dynamics of the visual stimulus. They consequently may perceive the visual stimulus’ motion dynamics in a highly variable and probably temporally fragmented way47,48, which, albeit tentatively, may lead to social withdrawal, cognitive decline, deficits in perceptual reasoning38,39, and other negative symptoms49.

Since the motion suppression task is known to involve the higher visual area middle temporal (MT)24,25, one would assume abnormal activity in this region of SZ patients. This is indeed the case as shown in previous studies50,51. We hypothesize that specifically the dynamics of MT like its neural variability is abnormally high and temporally irregular in SZ, which remains to be tested. Though orientation-specific surround suppression is also associated with the in primary visual cortex (V1) neurons40,52, only a minority of these cells show motion sensitivity53. Moreover, whether such abnormal neuronal dynamics in MT is related to the increased variability and entropy of phase-related processes in SZ, as typically observed in EEG54 remains to be investigated. Finally, applying unsupervised clustering algorithms, we identified a particular subgroup with high variability in visual motion perception and high negative symptoms as different from another subgroup with lower visual variability and predominantly high positive symptoms. There are multiple research strands suggesting that schizophrenia symptoms stem from impaired predictive coding mechanisms55,56. Converging data suggest a diametric predictive strategy from autism to schizophrenia. Previous research reported that the perceptual inference in autism is rigidly shaped by incoming sensory information, while the schizophrenia population is prone to overestimate the precision of their priors’ models57,58. The motion discrimination paradigm had been applied to the study of schizophrenia26 and autism18. The research results show that in the type of schizophrenia patient, they exhibit perceptual impairment (Tadin’s study and the results in this work), while autism patient, they exhibit hypersensitivity18. Hence, we speculate that in autism, disorganized and variable coding of external stimuli might result in the development of less stable predictive models about the environment, while, in schizophrenia patient, predictive models fail to update due to the imprecision of the encoded sensory information.

Cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia could serve as confounds. For instance, attention is a compromised ability in schizophrenia. The study of Fuller et al.59 indicated that the primary impairment of attention in schizophrenia lies in the control of attention and not in the selection processes that operate once attention has been directed to an object. Martinez et al’s study showed that the attention control system of SZ patients possibly is intact, though their sensory processing is impaired60. Furthermore, the brief duration (about 100 ms) in the visual motion paradigm precludes most top-down attentional effects38,61. In this work, we found there is significant higher values in N-5 score (abstract thinking difficulty) of HSZ group compared to LSZ group. Abstract thinking is considered a function of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)62. Our recent research found that the sensory cortex can form strong connections with DLPFC, and jointly contribute to cognitive function63. Therefore, we propose that abnormal perception dynamics and cognitive impairment are closely linked in SZ.

Methodological limitations

Some methodological issues are necessary to be taken into consideration. First, we here specifically focused on the well-established motion suppression paradigm while leaving aside other visual perception capacities and paradigms, e.g., form perception64,65. Second, all the patients in our sample were undergoing pharmacotherapy, which might have affected the results. In line with recent recommendations and standards, we examined the potential impact of the psychotropic medication load—the number and dosage of different medications—on state and variability measures (ST, SI, mean, SD) in SZ. This was done by converting antipsychotics into chlorpromazine dose-equivalents66,67. We then used the codes 0, 1, 2, and 3 to indicate no medication, and dose-equivalents below, equal, or above the mean effective daily dose (chlorpromazine = 400 mg), respectively68. All patients with schizophrenia in this study were on antipsychotic medication, to further control for the eventual effect of pharmacotherapy on indicators in the psychophysical paradigm, we used one-way ANOVA to compare ST, SI, mean, SD values in samples of antipsychotics medications below (n = 27), equal to (n = 5), or above (n = 28) mean effective daily dose. No significant differences were found between the three groups in terms of static and variability indices. In addition, we investigated the potential effects of drugs on experimental data by correlating total psychotropic load with indices extracted from the psychophysical paradigm. The results showed that we did not find significant differences between antipsychotic load and SI (r = 0.05, P = 0.71), with small stimulus threshold (r = 0.05, P = 0.70), with mean value (r = 0.14, P = 0.30), and with SD (r = 0.21, P = 0.11). Together, there were no significant drug-related effects on our variability measures of visual perception.

Murray et al.69 reported significant differences in duration thresholds for motion discrimination between males and females. This study exclusively included male participants to avoid the influence of sex variables on the experimental results4,70. Therefore, future research should investigate whether the findings can be generalized to female participants. It will be important to include both genders in subsequent studies to determine if there are any gender-specific differences in visual motion perception and to enhance the generalizability of the results.

In conclusion, we here investigated the visual perceptual dynamics in SZ and HC by developing an analysis method for capturing the dynamics in a well-established visual perception task, the motion suppression task. SZ subjects show higher variability and thus more irregularity in their trial-to-trial durations of the motion suppression task. Employing clustering methods on the variability visual data, we subdivide our total SZ subjects into two subgroups, that is, with high irregularity and low irregularity, respectively. The SZ group with high irregularity exhibits high negative symptom severity while the one with low visual irregularity is more related to high positive symptom severity. Together, our findings demonstrate the importance of abnormal trial-to-trial dynamics, e.g., irregularity, in the visual perception of SZ, including its association with specific psychopathological symptoms. That lends further support to the recent assumption of temporal imprecision and instability being a basic disturbance of SZ14,30,71.

Methods

Participants

A total of 60 patients (all males, aged between 18 and 45 years) who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria for SZ were recruited from an inpatient treatment facility in Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital, from March 2019 to December 2022. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital and all research protocols complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The symptoms of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) were used to assess patients’ positive, negative, and general symptoms72. The definition of Negative and Positive symptom patients and Exclusion criteria are described in Supplementary Method. Age- and education-matched healthy male subjects (n = 63) assessed with Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.)73 to exclude the history of any psychiatric disorder, were recruited from the local community and compensated for their time. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, with visual acuity greater than 1.0, and provided informed consent before the start of the study.

Measurement of motion spatial suppression

Stimulus patterns were generated in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) with the Psychophysics Toolbox74. The experiment was conducted using a portable computer for the visual motion paradigm. Subjects were required to be in a dark environment, sitting at 47 cm away from the LCD screen, with their heads supported on a chinrest. The stimuli consisted of vertically drifting sinusoidal gratings with a contrast of 50%, a spatial frequency of 1 cycle/°, and velocity of 4°/s. Stimuli were 1 cycle per degree drifting vertical sine wave gratings surrounded by two-dimensional Gaussian envelopes. Stimulus size (defined as twice the SD of the envelope) was either 1° or 5°. Gratings were ramped on and off with a Gaussian temporal envelope, with a total duration defined as 8 standard deviations. In this study, duration thresholds were represented as one standard deviation of the Gaussian function.

At the start of each trial, a cross appeared in the center of the screen for 500 ms, instructing the participants to focus their gaze on it and keep fixating at the cross throughout the trial. In each trial, a grating of either large or small size was randomly presented at the center of the screen. The grating drifted either leftward or rightward, and participants were asked to judge the perceived moving direction by a key press. Auditory feedback was provided in each incorrect response. The detailed procedure for measurement is presented in Supplementary Methods. Correct rates for different stimulus durations were calculated separately for each participant and then fitted into a cumulative Gaussian function to estimate the duration threshold corresponding to 75% accuracy on the psychometric function for each stimulus size (Fig. 1c). Stimulus demonstration and practice trials were presented before the first run. Auditory feedback was provided for each wrong response. 3-down/1-up adaptive staircase method

In this study, we used the 3-down/1-up staircase method to determine stimulus duration by adjusting the stimulus strength based on participants’ responses. Both large and small stimuli had two distinct staircases to cover a range of perceptual intensities. Specifically, if a response was incorrect, the step size increased. If the previous two trials and the current trial were correct with minimal changes in stimulus strength, the step size decreased. Otherwise, the step size remained unchanged. Further details on the adaptive step size are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Unsupervised clustering for visual perception

Clustering identification

Visual perceptual heterogeneity in schizophrenia was characterized by clustering subtypes, using the small stimulus grating “durations” variable. The study employed the sequence of 80 “Durations” of small stimulus gratings as the visual feature, with minimal differences in scale and uniform magnitude across all dimensions. No standardization was applied here to maintain more degree of the original features’ variability. PCA was applied to identify visual features for the motion perception task in schizophrenia patients. K-means + + is a straightforward but efficient clustering algorithm suitable for exploring and understanding the structure of clusters in data, and usually performs well in low-dimensional data. The vision-based PCs were then used to run clustering analysis using the K-means + + algorithm (Fig. 2a, b). To determine the optimal estimate of the number of clusters (K), the Elbow method32 and the Gap Statistic method proposed by Tibshirani33 were used, the number of K was set from 1 to 10 to assess the most suitable number in the data. Validation of the cluster analysis

To evaluate the reliability of the optimal two-cluster solution, we used the Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) algorithm. LDA maximizes the ratio of inter-class variance to intra-class variance, providing robust results even with limited data. Using LDA to validate clustering outcomes ensures consistency with the distance-based approach of K-means + +. The validation involved a K-fold cross-validation procedure, aimed at assessing the consistency of classification across various training-testing partitions. The participants were divided into three configurations, with threefold, sixfold, and ninefold cross-validation in separate trials. In each configuration, the dataset was partitioned into K subsets, where K is the number of folds. Each subset was used once as the testing set while the remaining K-1 subsets formed the training set. This procedure was repeated K times (folds), ensuring that each subset was used as the testing set exactly once. The training and testing accuracy of the LDA algorithm were evaluated under these different configurations, ensuring the robustness of the clustering outcome by examining classification performance over multiple iterations and varying sizes of training and testing sets.

In addition, we cross-validated the stochastic validation procedure with the leave-one-out (LOO) method, which maximizes the use of the data and reduces bias by using one sample from each iteration as the validation set and the remaining samples as the training set. We applied the LOO method to all participants, recorded the training and testing accuracies for each iteration, and calculated the overall average accuracy and standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) in this study. Data are all expressed as mean ± SD; Student’s t test was used to examine differences in psychophysical indices between HC and SZ, the P values were corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control for false discovery rate (FDR) of multiple comparisons; to compare the static and variability indices among HC and SZ subgroups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by correcting for multiple comparisons using the Tukey test. In addition, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to test the correlation between variability measures and PANSS clinical symptoms. Differences or correlations were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 and are denoted with one asterisk (*).

The mediation model was analyzed using PROCESS V4, a toolbox in SPSS75. The prerequisite for mediation analysis is that the independent variable should be a significant predictor of the mediator, and the mediator should be a significant predictor of the dependent variable. We examined whether the association between SI and PANSS score was mediated by variability measure in psychophysical experiments. In addition, a bias-corrected bootstrap approach was employed to assess the significance of direct and indirect effects in the mediation model (details see Supplementary method).
