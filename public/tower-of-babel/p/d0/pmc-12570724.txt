Dietary curcumin prevents hypercholesterolemia by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α and reducing intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression in high-fat diet-fed hamsters
Authors: Zhuo Cao, Jie Yang, Haiyan Mai, Ting Hong, Xiaobing Chen, Dan Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNiemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1) mediates cholesterol absorption and plays major roles in cholesterol homeostasis. Our previous study showed that curcumin reduced NPC1L1 expression and cholesterol absorption in Caco-2 cells. This study aimed to investigate whether curcumin could prevent hypercholesterolemia by transcriptionally suppressing intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression via inhibition of sterol regulatory element binding protein-2 (SREBP-2) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1α (HNF1α) transcriptional activity.MethodsMale hamsters were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) with or without 0.1% w/w curcumin for 12 weeks. Additionally, Caco-2 cells and HepG2 cells were treated with 6, 12, 25, or 50 µmol/L curcumin for 24 h. The cholesterol absorption was determined with 22-NBD-cholesterol assay, the promoter activity of NPC1L1 and the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α were determined with luciferase assay, and the gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α and the nuclear abundance of SREBP-2 and HNF1α were measured by RT-qPCR and Western blotting, respectively.ResultsCompared to HFD-fed hamsters, curcumin supplementation significantly lowered cholesterol levels in the serum (20.2%,) and liver (26.1%), and increased fecal neutral sterol excretion (114.5%). In addition, curcumin significantly reduced the gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α and the nuclear abundance of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver of HFD-fed hamsters. Furthermore, the dual luciferase assay showed that the promoter activity of NPC1L1 and the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells were dose-dependently inhibited after 24 h curcumin treatment, and maximally inhibited by 50 µmol/L curcumin. Additionally, the gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α and the nuclear abundance of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells were consistently suppressed by curcumin. Curcumin also significantly reduced the cholesterol uptake in both Caco-2 and HepG2 cells.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that curcumin prevents HFD-induced hypercholesterolemia by transcriptionally suppressing intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression and cholesterol absorption via inhibition of SREBP-2 and HNF1α transcriptional activity. Our research provides evidence for curcumin as a potential nutraceutical for hypercholesterolemia treatment by regulating NPC1L1 and enterohepatic circulation metabolism of cholesterol.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01031-x.

Introduction

Hypercholesterolemia is a major risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, which is the leading cause of death in developed countries and a large burden on society [1]. Cholesterol metabolism in the body is precisely regulated by multiple processes, including cholesterol synthesis, absorption, and excretion. The disorder of cholesterol metabolism can cause elevated cholesterol levels, leading to hypercholesterolemia [2]. Cholesterol metabolism in the body is the same as that of bile acids and involves enterohepatic circulation [3]. The enterohepatic circulation of cholesterol includes dietary and bile cholesterols, which are excreted from the liver via the biliary pathway, absorbed by the small intestine into the bloodstream, and returned to the liver through the portal vein. Enterohepatic circulation of cholesterol is important for maintaining cholesterol homeostasis in the body. Excessive intestinal cholesterol absorption and increased hepatobiliary cholesterol reabsorption can disturb cholesterol metabolism and cause hypercholesterolemia [4, 5].

The Niemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1) protein is primarily responsible for intestinal cholesterol absorption [6]. NPC1L1 is located mainly in the brush border membrane of the small intestine and mediates cholesterol absorption in the small intestine. It is also expressed in the hepatic bile duct membranes of humans, rats and hamsters and mediates the hepatic reabsorption of biliary cholesterol [4, 7]. Research has demonstrated that in transgenic mice specifically expressing human NPC1L1 protein in the liver, the cholesterol content in the bile significantly reduces while the cholesterol content in the liver increases, indicating that NPC1L1 in the liver participates in hepatic reabsorption of biliary cholesterol [5].

Transcription factors sterol regulatory element binding protein-2 (SREBP-2) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1α (HNF1α) have been identified as transcriptional activators of NPC1L1 and are involved in the regulation of NPC1L1 expression. The binding site to SREBP-2 has been identified in the NPC1L1 promoter, the promoter of human NPC1L1 (−1570/+137) contains a fragment of the SREBP-2 binding site (GAAGGGGAGG, −91/−81 bp) [8, 9]. In addition, transfection of SREBP-2 in HepG2 cells induces transcriptional activity of NPC1L1, indicating that SREBP-2 is involved in the regulation of NPC1L1 promoter activity [9]. Similarly, HNF1α has been shown to transcriptionally regulate NPC1L1 expression, and its binding site with the NPC1L1 promoter has been determined. The promoter of human NPC1L1 (−1570/+137) contains a fragment of the HNF1α binding site (GGTGGATCATTAACC, −158/−144 bp) [9].

Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from turmeric rhizome [10], possesses many biological functions, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-lowering effects [11, 12]. Besides these well-known effects, curcumin was also found to have the hypocholesterolemic effects in vivo by attenuating circulating concentration of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [13–15]. Our previous study showed that supplementation with 0.1% w/w curcumin for 12 weeks exhibited lower serum and liver TC and triglyceride (TG) concentration in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed hamsters and ApoE−/− mice [13, 16, 17]. Furthermore, curcumin treatment reduced NPC1L1 expression and cholesterol absorption in Caco-2 cells in a dose-dependent manner [18]. We also found that supplementation with 0.1% w/w curcumin for 12 weeks significantly inhibited the gene and protein expression of NPC1L1 and SREBP-2 in the small intestine of HFD-fed hamsters and ApoE−/− mice [13, 16]. Dietary intake of 500 and 1000 mg/kg curcumin or curcumin combined with piperine for 8 weeks was also shown to suppress the gene and protein expression of NPC1L1 and SREBP-2 in the small intestine of lithogenic diet-fed C57BL6 mice [19]. However, the effects of curcumin on the promoter activity of NPC1L1 and the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α, as well as whether curcumin can prevent hypercholesterolemia by transcriptionally suppressing intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression via inhibition of SREBP-2 and HNF1α transcriptional activity, remain unclear.

Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that curcumin prevented HFD-induced hypercholesterolemia by transcriptionally inhibiting intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression and suppressing intestinal cholesterol absorption and hepatic biliary cholesterol reabsorption. We further aimed to elucidate whether curcumin inhibited intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression through suppressing the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α. To test our hypothesis, we used HFD-fed hamsters, whose lipid metabolism pattern and NPC1L1 expression distribution (both expressed in the small intestine and liver) are most similar to those of humans [20], as well as Caco-2 and HepG2 cell models, to investigate the specific transcriptional regulatory mechanism by which curcumin inhibits intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression and prevents hypercholesterolemia.

Methods and materials

Animals and treatment

Five-week-old male Syrian golden hamsters were purchased from Baineng Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). They were raised in a specific pathogen-free environment at 22 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Following a week of acclimation, 24 hamsters were randomized into three groups: low-fat diet (Control, MD12014), high-fat diet (HFD, MD12015, including 41% energy from fat and 0.15% cholesterol), and HFD supplemented with 0.1% w/w curcumin (HFD + CUR). Curcumin was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (purity ≥ 98%; St. Louis, MO, USA). This dosage of 0.1% w/w curcumin was chosen for in vivo experiments based on our and other previous studies that showed the specific effects of curcumin on cholesterol metabolism and atherosclerosis [13, 16, 17, 21]. A dose of 0.1% w/w curcumin has been reported to effectively improve HFD-induced dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis [21]. Our dose of 0.1% w/w curcumin of hamster is approximately equivalent to consumption of 200 mg curcumin per day by a 60 kg human. To calculate this, we assumed that a 110 g hamster from HFD + CUR group consumed an average of 5 g food or 5 mg curcumin per day, which is about 45.45 mg per kg of hamster bw. By multiplying this value by 3/37 km factors [22], it would be 3.69 mg/kg bw in human or 200 mg curcumin intake by a 60 kg human.

The constituents of MD 12,014 and MD 12,015 are listed in Table S1. After a 12-week feeding period, the hamsters were fasted for 12 h followed by intraperitoneal anesthesia using pentobarbital. Blood was collected from the orbital venous sinus, and then centrifuged to separate the serum. The small intestine and liver tissues were excised and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and the remaining tissues were promptly frozen at −80 °C. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Sun Yat-sen University (No. 2019-011) and was conducted in accordance with the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals Guide.

Biochemical indexes analysis

Serum TG, TC, LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations were measured using different test kits (Catalogue # A111-1-1, Catalogue #A110-1-1, Catalogue #A112-1-1, Catalogue #A113-1-1, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). The liver tissue was excised and homogenized with lysis buffer. After standing for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred to an eppendorf tube, and a portion of the sample was taken for calibration. Subsequently, the supernatant was heated in a 70 °C water bath for 10 min, then centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting supernatant was used for liver lipid determination. The test kits (Catalogue #A111-1-1, Catalogue #A110-1-1, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) were used to measure the lipid contents in the liver, specifically determining the concentrations of TC and TG in the liver according to the instructions provided in the test kits.

Fecal neutral sterol assay

Three days before being euthanized, the hamsters were kept separately in metabolic cages and fecal samples were collected from each hamster. The feces were dried under negative pressure in a vacuum lyophilizer (700401040, Labconco, Kansas, MO, USA) and then ground to a powder, weighed to approximately 100 mg per hamster, sealed, and stored at −80 °C. 1 mg/mL 5-α-Cholestane (Catalogue #481-21-0, Sigma, Saint Louis, MO, USA) was added as an internal standard, and the sample to be tested was extracted by saponification with 1 mol/L NaOH at 100 °C. N, O-bis-(trimethyl) trifluoroacetamide (containing 1% trimethylchlorosilane) and pyridine (1:2, vol/vol) were added and reacted with the sample at 60 °C for 1 h. Fecal neutral sterol, including coprostanol, cholesterol and dehydrocholesterol, was then analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph (7890B, Agilent, California, USA) equipped with an HP-5 (0.25 μm × 0.32 mm × 30 mm) column. The operating conditions of the gas chromatograph were as follows: carrier gas was nitrogen, flow rate 1 ml/min, splitting ratio 20:1, inlet temperature 270 °C and detector temperature 280 °C. The initial temperature of the column chamber was 80 °C and was increased to 220 °C at 20 °C/min, then to 290 °C at 3 °C/min and maintained at 290 °C for 8 min. Process the standard and sample using the same method, including saponification and derivatization. And measure the standard curve by configuring standard samples of different concentrations. Using peak area to create a standard curve for determining sample concentration.

Cell culture and treatment

Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (Catalogue #11965092, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin was used to culture Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. When the cell density was between 60% and 70%, the cells in the various groups were treated with 6, 12, 25, or 50 µmol/L curcumin for 24 h, whereas the control group received only 1‰ dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Catalogue #ST038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

The effect of curcumin on cell viability was assessed by 3-(4,5-dimethy1-2-thiazolyl)−2,5-dipheny1-2-H-tetra-zolium bromide (MTT) assay. In brief, Caco-2 and HepG2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were incubated with different concentrations (1.5, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50 or 100 µmol/L) of curcumin for 24 h, then 50 µL 1x MTT (Catalogue #C0009S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added into each well and incubated in 37℃ for 4 h to make MTT revert to formazan. Next, the supernatant was taken out and the precipitated formazan crystals per well were dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO. After gentle mixing, the absorbance of each well was assayed at 570 nm using an ELx-800 instrument (ELx-800, BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The survival rate of cells was calculated as follows: (optical density of experimental sample/optical density of control) × 100%.

The doses of 6–50 µmol/L curcumin were chosen for cell experiments based on previous studies and our MTT assay results. According to the results of the cell viability test, curcumin, at a concentration of 50 µmol/L, had no harmful effects on Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. As a result, 6–50 µmol/L curcumin was chosen for the cellular experiments (Fig. S1).

Plasmid construction

A luciferase reporter plasmid for the NPC1L1 promoter was created as previously described [8, 9]. The NPC1L1 promoter was inserted into pGL3-Basic luciferase reporter vector. The promoter of human NPC1L1 (−1570/+137) contains a fragment of the SREBP-2 binding site (GAAGGGGAGG, −91/−81 bp), which has been validated in HuH7 and Caco-2 cells [8, 9]. We chose a total of 212 bp containing this fragment and inserted it into the luciferase reporter plasmid. Similarly, we obtained a fragment of the binding site of NPC1L1 for HNF1α (GGTGGATCATTAACC, −158/−144 bp) according to previous study [9], and we chose a total of 212 bp containing this fragment and inserted it into the luciferase reporter plasmid. In addition, the SREBP-2 luciferase reporter plasmid contained the entire sequence of the SREBP-2 coding sequence (3440 bp). Likewise, the HNF1α luciferase reporter plasmid containing the entire sequence of the HNF1α coding sequence (1910 bp) was constructed. All promoter, transcription factor, and Renilla reniformis luciferase reporter plasmids were constructed by Wuhan GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Transient transfection and luciferase assay

Caco-2 and HepG2 cells (2 × 106 cells) were seeded into 24-well plates. When the cell density reached 70%−80%, the cells were transfected with the NPC1L1 promoter luciferase plasmid. Alternatively, the cells were co-transfected with the SREBP-2 or HNF1α luciferase plasmid and the NPC1L1 promoter luciferase plasmid using LIPO3000 reagent (Catalogue #L3000075, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The Renilla reniformis luciferase plasmid was used as an internal reference. Cells were co-transfected with the NPC1L1 reporter luciferase plasmid, SREBP-2 (or HNF1α) luciferase plasmid, and the Renilla reniformis luciferase plasmid for reporter assays. After 24-h transfection, the cells were rinsed with PBS and treated with the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Catalogue # E1960, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The levels of firefly and Renilla reniformis luciferase were determined using an ELx-800 instrument (ELx-800, BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Each condition was assayed in triplicate for every experiment, and each experiment was repeated at least three times. Luciferase activity was expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla reniformis luciferase activity in each sample.

RNA extraction and real-time RT-qPCR analysis

Using the TRIzol reagent (Catalogue #15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), total RNA was isolated from cells and tissues, then reverse transcribed into cDNA using Takara Prime Script™ RT kit (Catalogue # RR074A, Takara Bio Inc., Dalian, China). RT-qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems Q6 system (ABI Q6, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Catalogue # 4309155, Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The expression level of each gene was analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the expression level of β-actin. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Nuclear protein extraction and Western blotting analysis

The nuclear protein was extracted from Caco-2 and HepG2 cells as well as small intestine and liver tissues using a Nucleoprotein Extraction Kit (Catalogue # C500009, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The BCA Protein Assay kit (Catalogue # P0009, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to determine the protein concentration. 30 µg protein lysates were subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Catalogue #IPVH00010, Milipore, Massachusetts, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with 1:1000 diluted primary polyclonal anti-Histone H3, anti-SREBP-2, and anti-HNF1α antibodies (Catalogue #SC-517576, Santa Cruz, CA, USA; Catalogue #Ab30682, Abcam, MA, USA; Catalogue #22426-1-AP, Proteintech, Chicago, USA) overnight at 4℃. After that, the membrane was incubated with 1:10000 dilutions of goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP secondary antibody (Catalogue #ab205718, Abcam, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature, and the band was detected using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence Reagent Kit (Catalogue #32209, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The luminescence intensity of the band was quantified using Image J software (NIH, USA). Histone H3 was used as an internal control for equal protein loading.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Caco-2 and HepG2 cells (2.5–3.0.5.0 × 105) were cultured in a confocal dish and then treated with 12 µmol/L curcumin for 24 h when the cell density reached 70%–80%. Next, the cells in the confocal dish were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, washed twice with ice-cold phosphate buffer saline and incubated with phosphate buffer saline plus 0.1% Triton X-100 (Catalogue #ST-1723, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min at room temperature, and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 60 min. After that, the cells were incubated with 1:200 diluted primary polyclonal anti-SREBP-2 and anti-HNF1α antibodies (Catalogue #Ab30682, Abcam, MA, USA; Catalogue #22426-1-AP, Proteintech, Chicago, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by the incubation with Alexa Fluor™ 647 Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody (1:1000 dilutions, Catalogue #A21235, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:1000 dilutions, Catalogue #C1002, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to stain the nuclei of cells in the dark for 15 min at 37 °C. Finally, the cells were washed 5 min for 3 times and were photographed using Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (LSM 900, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Cholesterol absorption assay

Caco-2 and HepG2 cells were treated with 6, 12, 25, or 50 µmol/L curcumin for.

24 h when the cell density reached 60%−70%, and then incubated for 4 h with medium containing 4 µmol/L 22-NBD-cholesterol (Catalogue #N1148, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were photographed using an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity of cells was measured at excitation and emission wavelength of 469 and 537 nm, respectively, using an ELx-800 instrument (ELx-800, BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The cholesterol uptake of Caco-2 and HepG2 cells was corrected using cellular protein concentration.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). All results were expressed as mean ± SEM. Three independent cell experiments were performed, and each experiment was repeated at least three times. For the experimental data, we analyzed the average from three independent experiments. All cell studies were performed in the same way. One-way ANOVA test was used for comparison between groups. Bonferroni correction was employed for post-hoc analysis. P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for graph plotting.

Results

Curcumin improved basic and biochemical parameters and increased fecal neutral sterol excretion in HFD-fed hamsters

After 12 weeks of feeding, the body weight gain of hamsters in the HFD group was higher than that of the control group, and the body weight gain of curcumin-treated hamsters was lower than that of hamsters in the HFD group; however, the food intake among the three groups was not significantly different. In addition, serum TG, TC, and LDL-C concentrations were higher and serum HDL-C concentrations were lower in HFD-fed hamsters than in control and curcumin-treated hamsters. The liver TC and TG concentrations in curcumin-treated hamsters were consistently lower than those in HFD-fed hamsters (Table 1).

Hamsters in the HFD group excreted considerably more neutral sterols in their feces when supplemented with curcumin. The excretion of neutral sterols in hamsters treated with curcumin was approximately twice that in hamsters fed HFD. Furthermore, hamsters treated with curcumin considerably excreted coprostanol, a key component of neutral fecal sterols (Table 1).

Curcumin inhibited NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α gene expression in the small intestine and liver of HFD-fed hamsters

The gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver tissues of hamsters was evaluated by RT-qPCR assay. The hamsters fed a HFD exhibited higher gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver than did the control hamsters. However, the hamsters treated with curcumin exhibited lower gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver than did the HFD-fed hamsters (Fig. 1A-F).

Curcumin reduced the nuclear abundance of HNF1α in the small intestine and liver of HFD-fed hamsters

SREBP-2 and HNF1α are nuclear transcription factors that function actively in the nucleus and influence related genes expression. Therefore, we extracted the nuclear protein and further investigated whether curcumin influenced the nuclear abundance of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver of HFD-fed hamsters. As shown in Fig. 1, the hamsters fed a HFD exhibited higher nuclear abundance of HNF1α in the small intestine compared with the control hamsters; however, the hamsters treated with curcumin exhibited lower nuclear abundance of HNF1α in the small intestine compared with the HFD-fed hamsters (Fig. 1G-H). In addition, the hamsters fed a HFD exhibited higher nuclear abundance of pSREBP-2 and HNF1α in the liver tissues than did the control group (Fig. 1I-L) and curcumin supplementation exhibited lower nuclear abundance of HNF1α in the liver compared with that in the HFD group (Fig. 1I-J); however, curcumin had no influence on the nuclear abundance of pSREBP-2 in the liver (Fig. 1K-L).

Curcumin inhibited the promoter activity of NPC1L1 by suppressing the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells

To further understand whether curcumin inhibited NPC1L1 expression at the transcriptional level via regulation of the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α, we first performed a luciferase activity assay on the NPC1L1 promoter to determine the effect of curcumin on NPC1L1 promoter activity and verify the specific promoter regions of curcumin suppressing NPC1L1. The NPC1L1 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid, which carries a 212 bp fragment containing the SREBP-2 binding site (−91/−81 bp) or the HNF1α binding site (−158/−144 bp), was transfected into Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. The cells were then incubated with different doses of curcumin (6, 12, 25, or 50 µmol/L) for 24 h. After treatment with different doses of curcumin for 24 h, the luciferase activity assay showed that the promoter activity of NPC1L1 in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells was significantly inhibited by curcumin (Fig. 2A-D). These results suggest that the binding site of SREBP-2 at the NPC1L1 promoter regions (−91 to −81 bp) and the binding site of HNF1α at the NPC1L1 promoter regions (−158 to −144 bp), which are responsible for the basic transcriptional activity of the NPC1L1 promoter, are the cis-responsive elements of curcumin that suppresses the NPC1L1 promoter activity in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells.

Next, we further examined the effects of curcumin on the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. The transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α was evaluated using a dual luciferase assay after 24-h curcumin treatment. Discussion

This study provides new evidence that curcumin prevented HFD-induced hypercholesterolemia by transcriptionally inhibiting intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression and suppressing intestinal cholesterol absorption and hepatic biliary cholesterol reabsorption. Furthermore, curcumin transcriptionally inhibited intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression by suppressing the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α. Our study provides a novel insight into the anti-hypercholesterolemic mechanism of curcumin.

Hypercholesterolemia is a critical risk factor for the development and progression atherosclerosis. The disorder of cholesterol homeostasis has been demonstrated as the main cause of hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis [2]. Intestinal absorption of cholesterol and hepatic reabsorption of biliary cholesterol, known as the enterohepatic circulation of cholesterol, are the major factors influencing cholesterol homeostasis. Cholesterol absorption transporter NPC1L1 plays a key role in cholesterol metabolism by regulating cholesterol absorption in enterocytes [4, 6]. Reducing the intestinal absorption of cholesterol via NPC1L1 inhibition can effectively lower blood cholesterol concentration and decrease the risk of cardiovascular disease [23, 24]. In addition, the liver can reabsorb cholesterol from bile via the NPC1L1 protein in the bile duct membrane [4, 25]. Hepatic NPC1L1 regulates biliary cholesterol content, and overexpression of hepatic NPC1L1 results in a significant decrease in biliary cholesterol concentration, which is accompanied by a 30%–60% increase in plasma cholesterol [5]. Therefore, inhibiting the expression of intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 can reduce both intestinal cholesterol absorption and hepatic reabsorption of biliary cholesterol, thereby significantly decreasing plasma cholesterol concentration. Ezetimibe, a typical NPC1L1 inhibitor, has been widely applied to lower cholesterol concentration and treat hypercholesterolemia in humans [26, 27]. NPC1L1 deletion has also been shown to reduce cholesterol absorption and effectively prevent hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis development [28, 29].

It has been reported that curcumin can reduce serum TC and LDL-C concentration, resulting in a cholesterol-lowering effect [16, 30, 31]. Our previous studies also found that curcumin supplementation significantly reduced serum TC and LDL-C concentration by reducing intestinal cholesterol absorption via the downregulation of intestinal NPC1L1 in HFD-fed hamsters [13]. Curcumin has also been shown to decrease plasma cholesterol concentration and inhibit the development of atherosclerotic lesions in HFD-fed ApoE-/- mice, which has been linked to its inhibitory effects on intestinal NPC1L1 expression and cholesterol absorption [16]. In line with these results, our current study showed that curcumin exerted a strong hypocholesterolemic effect and prevented hypercholesterolemia in HFD-fed hamsters, which was accompanied by a significant reduction in intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression and enhancement of fecal cholesterol excretion. And the analysis of NBD-cholesterol absorption showed that curcumin also significantly inhibited cholesterol uptake in both Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. Collectively, these results suggest that curcumin prevents HFD-induced hypercholesterolemia by suppressing NPC1L1 function in both the small intestine and liver, as well as inhibiting cholesterol absorption and promoting cholesterol elimination from feces. Previous studies revealed that the mechanisms underlying the anti-hypercholesterolemic effects of curcumin were likely mediated by upregulation of the hepatic LDL receptor and inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase [32, 33]. Our current study demonstrates a new anti-hypercholesterolemic mechanism of curcumin, which is achieved by regulating the intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 function and the enterohepatic cholesterol metabolism. Likewise, the NPC1L1 inhibitor ezetimibe has been found to reduce plasma cholesterol by inhibiting NPC1L1 function in both the intestine and liver, and thus regulating the enterohepatic circulation of cholesterol [5]. Therefore, the regulation of intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 function may be a new effective target for the prevention and treatment of hypercholesterolemia and cardiovascular disease.

Considering the regulatory mechanisms of NPC1L1 expression, several transcription factors such as SREBP-2 and HNF1α are reported to be involved in NPC1L1 regulation. SREBP-2 is a major regulator of the human NPC1L1 promoter, and two SRE cis-elements have been identified in the NPC1L1 promoter [8]. NPC1L1 promoter activity and gene expression were observed to be increased by SREBP-2 in a dose-dependent manner in HuH7 cells [9]. HNF1α exhibits the capacity to transcriptionally regulate the NPC1L1 promoter, with its binding sites having been identified within the promoter of human NPC1L1 [9]. The transcriptional activity of HNF1α was found to be positively correlated with the promoter activity and gene expression of NPC1L1 [9]. Furthermore, our previous study revealed that curcumin suppressed the gene and protein expression of NPC1L1 and inhibited cholesterol absorption in Caco-2 cells [18]. Similarly, in HFD-fed hamsters and ApoE−/− mice, dietary supplementation with curcumin significantly suppressed the gene and protein expression of NPC1L1 in the small intestine [13, 16]. Based on these results, we further investigated the role of curcumin in NPC1L1 transcriptional regulation in the small intestine and liver.

The dual luciferase reporter assay was used to test the transcriptional activity of transcription factors and promoters, by which we verified whether curcumin could influence the NPC1L1 promoter activity through regulating the transcriptional activity of SREBP-2 and HNF1α, subsequently inhibiting NPC1L1expression and cholesterol absorption. Previous study showed that curcumin decreased the transcriptional activity and expression of SREBP-2 in the hepatic stellate cells by suppressing the specificity protein-1 activity [34]. Our current study demonstrated an inhibitory effect of curcumin on SREBP-2 transcriptional activity in Caco-2 cells and then decreased NPC1L1 promoter activity. Moreover, we also found that curcumin inhibited the promoter activity and gene expression of NPC1L1 via downregulation of SREBP-2 transcriptional activity in HepG2 cells, along with a decrease in cellular cholesterol absorption. More innovatively, we further observed that curcumin suppressed the transcriptional activity of HNF1α in both Caco-2 and HepG2 cells, subsequently inhibited the promoter activity and gene expression of NPC1L1, as well as the cellular cholesterol absorption. The changes in gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α at the cellular levels were further validated in vivo. Using a HFD-fed hamster model, we found that the gene expression of both NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α in the small intestine and liver were suppressed by curcumin. Consistent with our results, dietary curcumin or curcumin combined with piperine has also been shown to suppress NPC1L1 and SREBP-2 gene expression in the small intestine of lithogenic diet-fed C57BL6 mice [19]. In addition to the inhibition of intestinal NPC1L1 and SREBP-2 gene expression, we innovatively found that curcumin inhibited the gene expression of NPC1L1, SREBP-2, and HNF1α in the liver tissue of HFD-fed hamsters, as well as the HNF1α gene expression in the small intestine.

Nuclear transcription factors SREBP-2 and HNF1α exhibit active functions within the nucleus and influence the target gene expression. Therefore, we further extracted the nuclear proteins to determine the influences of curcumin on nuclear SREBP-2 and HNF1α. At low cholesterol levels, SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) can transport the precursor form of SREBP-2 (pSREBP-2) from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, where it is processed by two membrane-associated proteases (site 1 protease and site 2 protease), releasing from the precursor protein to the mature form of SREBP-2 (mSREBP-2), which has the NH2-terminal bHLH transcription factor structural domain and operates as a transcription factor. Mature SREBP-2 translocates to the nucleus and binds to SRE motif and E-box in the promoter of the SREBP-2 target gene, and then stimulates the promoter activity and expression of the target genes [35]. In this study, we found that curcumin treatment significantly reduced the nuclear abundance of pSREBP-2 and mSREBP-2 in both Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. However, we did not detect nuclear mSREBP-2 in the small intestine or liver tissues of HFD-fed hamsters. We speculated that SREBP-2 protein hydrolysis might be inhibited because the free cholesterol levels are not low in HFD-fed hamsters. Similarly, Field et al.. have indicated that it is challenging to identify the mature form of SREBP-2 in the intestines and livers of hamsters fed a cholesterol-enriched diet or control chow, but the mature form of SREBP-2 is evident in the intestines and livers of hamsters fed a cholesterol-deficient diet [36], and a sufficient cholesterol diet has been reported to drastically reduce the nuclear abundance of SERBP-2 in the livers of hamsters owing to the inhibition of SREBP-2 hydrolysis [37]. In addition, our study demonstrated that curcumin reduced the nuclear abundance of HNF1α in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells. Consistent with our results, Tai et al.. showed that curcumin reduced the nuclear abundance of HNF1α in HepG2 cells, leading to reduced interaction with the PCSK9 promoter and downregulation of PCSK9 expression [32]. Most importantly, curcumin also reduced the nuclear abundance of HNF1α in the small intestine and liver tissues of HFD-fed hamsters. It indicates that the inhibition of HNF1α.

activation is implicated in the transcriptional suppression of intestinal and hepatic NPC1L1 expression by curcumin, thereby reducing cholesterol levels in hamsters.

However, one limitation of our study was that only male hamsters were included. Due to the periodic changes in physiology, metabolism, and hormone levels in female mice, there may be potential impacts on the experimental results. And most previous studies have used male hamsters as experimental models [13, 20, 36, 37].

Therefore, male hamsters were selected as the research subjects in this study. But gender is known to have an effect on hypercholesterolemia; therefore, both sexes need to be considered in further studies. Another limitation of our study was that only a single dose of curcumin was used in in vivo experiments, and in the future, multiple doses will be used to observe the dose-response relationship of curcumin. Finally, the anti-hypercholesterolemic effect of curcumin needs to be further validated in human.

Conclusion

In summary, our present study has demonstrated that curcumin effectively prevents HFD-induced hypercholesterolemia through the transcriptional inhibition of intestinal and hepatic Niemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1) expression, thereby suppressing intestinal cholesterol absorption and hepatic biliary cholesterol reabsorption. Furthermore, curcumin transcriptionally suppresses the expression of NPC1L1 in both intestinal and hepatic tissues by inhibiting the transcriptional activities of SREBP-2 and HNF1α. These findings establish the foundation for the potential of curcumin in the prevention and treatment of hypercholesterolemia, positioning it as a promising nutraceutical for hypercholesterolemia prevention and treatment.
