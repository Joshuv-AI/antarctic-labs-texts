Identification and characterization of stem cells in three different oral structures by immunohistochemistry. An in vitro study
Authors: J Dharshini Devi, Kavitha Bottu, Ramya Sekar, J Logeswari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Stem cells are undifferentiated cells with the capacity for self-renewal and differentiation, playing a key role in tissue repair and regeneration.Aim:The study aims to assess and compare the expression pattern of stem cell markers like CD44, CD34, and OCT4 in the gingival mucosa, palatal mucosa, and dental pulp from both permanent teeth and shedding primary teeth.Materials and Methods:Sixty tissue samples were taken from the patients without any medical history undergoing standard dental operations. We performed immunohistochemical staining of CD44, CD34, and OCT4 antibodies in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections. The staining intensity and distribution were measured using a semiquantitative immunoreactive score. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) analysis was conducted to compare the levels of marker expression between various tissues.Statistical Analysis:Data were analysed using Microsoft Excel and IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics, with Chi-square and one-way ANOVA tests applied and P < 0.05 considered significant.Results:CD44 demonstrated strong positivity predominantly in the basal and suprabasal epithelial layers, with higher staining intensity observed in the palatal mucosa compared to other tissues. CD34 expression in dental pulp samples exhibited distinctive vascular-associated staining patterns, while gingival and palatal lamina propria showed prominent endothelial cell positivity. OCT4 expression was negative across all four tissue groups.Conclusion:CD44 showed significant epithelial expression, its widespread distribution limits, and its specificity as a stem cell marker. CD34 proved reliable for identifying vascular components but may not represent all hematopoietic stem cells. The absence of OCT4 expression suggests tissue stability without evidence of pluripotent stem cell activity.

INTRODUCTION

Stem cells possess unique capabilities which enable them to create new tissues because they retain their potential to become any type of specialized cell. The body uses their functions to maintain tissue integrity, while they proceed to repair any injuries that occur.[1] We generally classify stem cells as embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and adult stem cells. Adult stem cells encompass two main types which are mesenchymal stem cells and dental-derived stem cells that medical professionals can obtain from various locations such as bone marrow, fat tissue, umbilical cords, and different areas from the mouth, respectively.[2] In oral tissues, stem cells from exfoliating deciduous teeth (SHED), dental pulp, periodontal ligament, and gingival demonstrate their rapid multiplication abilities and their potency to develop different cell types and their effectiveness in minimizing immune responses.[3]

In the oral mucosa, stem cells in the basal cell layer maintain steady tissue turnover by self-renewal, guided differentiation, and surface replacement through asymmetric divisions that preserve their number.[4] Any disruption in these regulatory mechanisms leads to risk issues like malignancy; on the other side, their repair network makes them ideal for grafts and healing.[5] Gingival mesenchymal stem cells exhibit high growth potential and immunoregulatory functions, which aids in soft-tissue healing and continuous regeneration.[6] Stem cells derived from palatal mucosa have abilities of restoring epithelium and modify the extracellular matrix.[7] Each of these oral stem cell groups comes with signature molecular markers that highlight their potential for regeneration.

Markers are mostly proteins on the cell surface or inside the cells that are used to recognize, separate, and profile stem cells, distinguishing them from fully differentiated cells.[8] Immunohistochemistry is a technique used to detect and localize specific antigens or proteins in tissue sections by using antigen–antibody reactions, thereby helping to identify the distribution and intensity of marker expression within different tissues. In dental and oral research, various immunohistochemical markers are used to identify stem cells specific to different tissue lineages such as CD45 for hematopoietic tissues, Nestin and SOX1/SOX2 for neural and neuroectodermal tissues, and CD73 and CD105 for mesenchymal and stromal tissues. In the present study, CD44, CD34, and OCT4 were used as markers for multipotency, hematopoietic origin, and pluripotency to assess stemness traits in oral tissues.

CD44, a cell surface glycoprotein, involves in adhesion, movement, and hyaluronic acid binding, so we used it to identify multipotent stem cells and assess their self-renewal and differentiation potential.[9] CD34 helps in evaluating the vascular density and used to identify endothelial or progenitor cells, reflecting the tissue’s angiogenesis and repair potential.[10] OCT4, the pluripotency guardian transcription factor, conforms the undifferentiated nature of the cells.[11] Together, these markers offer a better understanding on the nature of the stem cell, their function, and their role in tissue regeneration.

Thus, this study aims to identify and characterize stem cells of gingival mucosa, palatal mucosa, and dental pulp from permanent and deciduous teeth, by using CD44, CD34, and OCT4 IHC markers. Also, it aims to compare the expression of CD44 for multipotency, CD34 for hematopoietic stem cells, OCT4 for pluripotency, and to correlate their combined expression to define tissue-specific stem cell patterns in oral tissues.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study was designed as a observational cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology and Oral Microbiology, Meenakshi Ammal Dental College and Hospital, Chennai. Ethical clearance for the research was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee (MADC/IEC/II/35/2023).

Selection of participants

Altered immunoexpression of CD44, CD34, and OCT4 has been reported in various pathological conditions of oral tissues, including oral epithelial dysplasia, oral squamous cell carcinoma, oral submucous fibrosis, inflammatory gingival lesions, periodontitis, and regenerative pulpal conditions, where these markers are associated with stemness, angiogenesis, cellular proliferation, and tissue repair.[121314151617] Therefore, only clinically healthy oral tissues were included in the present study. Subjects with tobacco habits were excluded, as tobacco exposure may induce inflammatory and molecular alterations that can affect stem cell marker expression. Similarly, subjects with pulpal, periapical, or periodontal pathologies were not included, since these conditions may alter the immunoexpression profile of the studied markers. Pulpal tissues were harvested only from healthy teeth extracted for orthodontic or impaction-related reasons, including impacted third molars, without evidence of caries, pulpitis, periapical lesions, or periodontal disease. Gingival and palatal tissues were obtained only from clinically normal tissues during graft-related procedures, and exfoliating deciduous teeth were collected only during physiological shedding without associated pathology. Accordingly, subjects with systemic illness, tobacco habits, oral mucosal lesions, gingival or palatal pathology, pulpal or periodontal disease, or any local or systemic condition likely to influence CD44, CD34, and OCT4 immunoexpression were excluded from the study.

Sample collection

Gingival mucosa, palatal mucosa, and dental pulp from both permanent teeth and exfoliating deciduous teeth were obtained. Permanent premolars and third molars were extracted under aseptic conditions and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hours. Dental pulp tissues from both permanent teeth [Figure 1a] and exfoliating deciduous teeth [Figure 1b] were retrieved through longitudinal sectioning of teeth using a sterile chisel and further fixed in formalin. 0.5 cm to 1 cm of free gingiva and palatal mucosal 2–3 mm below the attached gingiva were collected during minor surgical interventions, and written informed consent was obtained from all the participants involved in study subsequently fixed in formalin, and processed into paraffin-embedded tissue blocks for histological and immunohistochemical analysis.

Study size and sample distribution

The sample size for this in vitro immunohistochemical study was determined based on feasibility of tissue procurement and in reference to the study by Shetty et al.[29] on immunohistochemical characterization of stem cell and differentiation markers in the dental pulp of human natal teeth. A total of 60 specimens were included, with 15 specimens in each group to allow balanced comparison across the four tissue groups.

Immunohistochemical assessment

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to evaluate the expression, and localization of CD44, CD34, and OCT4 in gingival mucosa, palatal mucosa, and dental pulp from permanent and exfoliating deciduous teeth. Sections of 3–4 μm thickness, prepared from archived formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections, were treated using heat-induced epitope retrieval with Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) in a pressure cooker at 95–100°C for 15 minutes, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 0.03% H2O2 in PBS.[18] Sections were incubated with mouse monoclonal anti-CD44 (HCAM/918, PathnSitu), rabbit monoclonal anti-CD34 (EP88, PathnSitu), and rabbit monoclonal anti-OCT4 (EP143, PathnSitu), antibodies at 1:100 dilution followed by HRP-linked secondary antibodies, and DAB chromogen with haematoxylin counterstain. Positive controls were tonsil for CD44, kidney for CD34, and seminoma for OCT4, while negative controls were excluded from the primary antibody. Slides were analysed at different magnifications, and immunohistochemical scoring was performed using a semi-quantitative immunoreactive score (IRS) system for CD44, CD34, and OCT4. For each slide, five representative high-power field were selected, and the immunopositive cells were assessed by three blinded observers. The percentage of positive cells was scored as follows: 0 = no positive cells, 1= <10% positive cells, 2 = 10–50% positive cells, 3 = 51–80% positive cells, and 4= >80% positive cells. The staining intensity was scored as 0 = absent, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = strong. The final IRS was obtained by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score, giving a total score from 0 to 12. Based on the IRS, expression was categorized as 0–1 = negative, 2–3 = weak, 4–8 = moderate, and 9–12 = strong. Any discrepancy between the observers was resolved by consensus and the final score was included for analysis to ensure reliability.[19] In gingival and palatal mucosal samples, cases were classified as positive when immunoreactivity was detected in epithelial cells, cells within the connective tissue, endothelial cells, or endothelial lined blood vessels. In dental pulp samples, all positively stained cells were included in the evaluation, regardless of their specific location within the tissue.

Statistical analyses

All the data were obtained and entered in Microsoft Excel Spread sheet and was subjected to statistical analysis which was carried out using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 26, IBM, Chicago, USA). Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical data were expressed as n and %. The Chi-square test was carried out for categorical comparisons. A P value less than 5% was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Sixty tissue samples in total were analysed that comprised gingival mucosa (n = 15), palatal mucosa (n = 15), pulp tissue from permanent teeth (n = 15), and pulp tissue from exfoliating deciduous teeth (n = 15). All specimens were determined to be suitable for inclusion and none were excluded as a result of inadequate fixation or tissue loss at this stage of sample preparation. Samples were collected from patients aged between 8 and 40 years undergoing routine extractions or minor oral surgical procedures.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on all four tissue samples [Figures 2a, 3a, 4a and 5a] to confirm the histological features and tissue integrity. CD44 immunoexpression demonstrated predominantly membranous and cytoplasmic staining localized within the basal and suprabasal epithelial keratinocytes of the gingival and palatal mucosa. Gingival epithelium [Figure 2b] exhibited moderate positivity in 46.7% (7/15) and strong positivity in 33.3% (5/15) of cases. In comparison, palatal epithelium [Figure 3b] demonstrated significantly higher strong positivity in 86.7% (13/15) of cases. Additionally, strong CD44 immunoreactivity within the spinous layer was observed in 60% (9/15) of palatal mucosa compared with only 6.7% (1/15) of gingival mucosa samples, indicating greater proliferative and progenitor cell activity within palatal epithelium. Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in CD44 expression between gingival and palatal epithelium in the basal layer (P = 0.007), suprabasal layer (P = 0.003), and entire epithelial thickness (P = 0.02), while no significant differences were observed in the spinous layer (P = 0.35) and superficial layer (P = 1.0), confirming significantly enhanced CD44 expression in palatal mucosa compared to gingival epithelium [Table 1].

Within dental pulp tissues, CD44 positivity was localized predominantly to the cell membrane and cytoplasm of pulpal stromal cells distributed around perivascular regions, suggestive of mesenchymal stem cell niches. Exfoliating deciduous pulp tissue [Figure 4b] showed predominantly moderate CD44 positivity in 93.3% (14/15) of cases, whereas permanent dental pulp tissue [Figure 5b] exhibited predominantly negative expression in 80% (12/15) and only mild positivity in 20% (3/15) of cases. Comparative analysis among all tissue groups demonstrated statistically significant variation in CD44 expression (P = 0.001), with exfoliating deciduous pulp and palatal epithelium showing significantly greater expression compared to permanent dental pulp. These findings indicate increased stem cell-associated activity and regenerative potential within exfoliating deciduous pulp tissue [Table 2 and Figure 6a].

CD34 immunostaining demonstrated membranous positivity predominantly localized to endothelial cells lining blood vessels within the lamina propria and pulpal vasculature. Gingival mucosa [Figure 2c] exhibited strong endothelial positivity 100% in all cases, while palatal mucosa [Figure 3c] demonstrated strong positivity in 93.3% (14/15) and moderate positivity in 6.7% (1/15) of samples, indicating rich vascularity and active angiogenesis within oral mucosal tissues. In exfoliating deciduous pulp [Figure 4c], CD34 expression was comparatively reduced, with mild positivity in 46.7% (7/15), moderate positivity in 20% (3/15), and negative staining in 33.3% (5/15) of cases. Conversely, permanent dental pulp tissue [Figure 5c] demonstrated greater vascular marker expression, with moderate positivity in 66.7% (10/15) and strong positivity in 33.3% (5/15) of cases. The difference in CD34 expression among the study groups was statistically significant (P = 0.002), indicating significant variation in vascular distribution among oral tissues [Table 2 and Figure 6b].

OCT4 immunoexpression was completely absent in all examined tissue groups, including gingival mucosa, palatal mucosa, permanent dental pulp, and exfoliating deciduous dental pulp [Figure 2d, 3d, 4d and 5d]. No nuclear staining was observed in any of the 60 examined cases (100% negative expression), and no statistically significant difference was identified among the groups (P = 1.0). Since OCT4 is a pluripotency-associated transcription factor that normally demonstrates nuclear localization when positive, the absence of staining indicates lack of detectable pluripotent stem cell populations within the investigated tissues [Table 2 and Figure 6c].

When combined, these findings suggest significant differences in the expression of stem cell markers across various oral tissues. Stronger CD44 signals were found in the deciduous pulp and palatal mucosa, suggesting distributed multipotent stem cells. Strong CD34 expression in the gingival and palatal regions is supported, but their rich vascular density indicates greater distribution of hematopoietic stem cells, while the absence of OCT4 rules out pluripotency, indicating stable differentiation.

DISCUSSION

In the world of research, stem cells have recently gained high attention. Stem cells are undifferentiated cells with the ability to self-renew and differentiate into specialized cell types. They are present in both in embryos and adult cells. Based on their origin and differentiation potential, stem cells are broadly divided into three categories: embryonic stem cells, adult (somatic) stem cells, and iPSCs.[20] In addition to this, they are also classified based on their differentiation potential into totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent, and unipotent stem cells. Totipotent stem cells have the capacity to give rise to all cell types, including extraembryonic tissues; pluripotent stem cells can differentiate into all cell types of the body; multipotent stem cells can develop into a limited range of cell types within a specific lineage; and unipotent stem cells which are restricted to a single cell type. Embryonic stem cells have pluripotency as they are derived from the inner cell mass of blastocysts (5–7-day embryos) and they can differentiate unlimitedly, but the major disadvantage is that they have more ethical barriers, risk of teratoma formation, and difficulty in controlling differentiation. In contrast to embryonic stem cells, adult stem cells are multipotent cells found in various tissues after birth and are responsible for tissue maintenance and repair. They are found in various organs such as bone marrow, brain, gut, lung, liver, skin, muscle, and adipose tissue, where they exist as a rare population within specialized niches and play a crucial role in tissue maintenance and repair.[120] Although primarily multipotent with limited differentiation potential, adult stem cells, particularly mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived from bone marrow and adipose tissue, exhibit a degree of plasticity, enabling differentiation across multiple lineages. Due to fewer ethical concerns and better clinical safety, they are widely utilized in regenerative medicine. iPSCs are adult cells that have been reprogrammed to a pluripotent state, enabling them to differentiate into all cell types similar to embryonic stem cells, without associated ethical issues.[21]

Stem cells from oral cavity offer a more accessible way to generate a huge amount of mature cells. By the process of self-renewing, stem cells maintain consecutive cell growth and specialization. The ability of stem cells to recapitulate is determined by stem cell markers, which are nothing but protein products.[22]

This study’s objective was to use histological and immunohistochemical technique to characterize the stem cellness of different oral tissues (dental pulp, gingival, and palatal tissues), so that it provides a baseline for stem cells and regeneration potential patterns. As far as my knowledge, no study has compared the expression patterns of exfoliating deciduous dental pulp, permanent dental pulp, gingival, and palatal tissues. The results of this study offer a baseline for identifying and characterizing stem cells as hematopoietic, pluripotent, and multipotent stem cells.

CD44 is a glycoprotein found on the cell surface that can take on several structural shapes and serves as a marker for cell adhesion, cell–matrix interaction, and cell migration across space. CD44 begins to show up at different stages of embryonic development where extracellular matrix is being created.[23] CD44 supports the mineralization process in tooth pulp.[24] In this study, CD44 showed intense immunoexpression across the palatal epithelium and exfoliated deciduous dental pulp, reflecting high cell density with regenerative and adhesive roles. Moderate-to-strong expression was observed in the gingival epithelium, indicating active stem cell division. Pulp expression was restricted to perivascular niches, consistent with prior multipotent stem cell locations.[25] Thus, palatal epithelium and deciduous pulp have more active multipotent stem cell niches than permanent pulp or gingiva. The epithelial distribution of CD44 expression pattern on various layers of gingiva and palatal epithelium is consistent with the work of D.T. Oliveira et al.(1997)[26] where they used CD 44 variants expression in normal oral epithelium such as tongue, the floor of mouth, gingiva, and hard palate, while positive expression of CD44 in all the study groups which included normal gingival tissue was reported by Lucarini et al. (2009).[27] High expression in normal mucosa when compared to oral cancer was noted by Thankappan et al. (2022),[17] these compared the normal mucosa with diseased cases. Zhiwei Jiang and Na Li et al. (2022)[28] found positive CD 44 expression in palatal-derived mesenchymal stem cells. The CD44 expression in the pulp of deciduous teeth almost aligns with findings by Shetty et al. (2018),[29] who reported 100% moderate expression. Chen et al. (2013)[24] and Umemura et al. (2016)[30] showed positive CD44 expression of dental pulp.

A transmembrane glycoprotein commonly used as a marker of hematopoietic stem cells marker which is present in small percentage in human bone marrow cells is CD34. The bone marrow’s CD34-positive enhanced cell population serves as the primary source of hematopoietic activity in this tissue. It is regarded as one of the most important HSC indicators.[31] In this study, CD34 immunoexpression primarily targeted endothelial cells lining gingival and palatal tissues, indicating rich vascularization and endothelial stem cell support for regeneration. Permanent and exfoliated deciduous pulps showed distinct vascular/stem cell patterns. Gingiva has highest microvessel density, then palate, permanent pulp, and deciduous pulp highlighting superior vascular repair capacity in gingival/palatal sites. Findings align with Desai et al. (2010),[32] who reported CD34 positivity in normal oral mucosa and Shetty et al. (2018)[29] reported contrasting outcome CD34-negative expression in dental pulp of natal and deciduous teeth, while Tenyi et al. (2023)[33] study aligns with the our result in expression of CD34 in dental pulp tissue.

OCT4 is an embryonic stem cell marker that functions as a DNA-binding transcription factor. It recognizes octamer DNA sequences and regulates gene transcription, helping maintain pluripotency and self-renewal in embryonic stem cells. Because of this role, OCT4 is widely used as a marker of pluripotent stem cells.[34] In this study, the absence of OCT4 in normal adult somatic tissues shows stable differentiation and absence of pluripotency. The lack of OCT4 expression in oral and dental tissues confirms their committed and differentiated status, indicating a limited potential for pluripotency. Shetty et al. (2018)[29] reported similarly to our study outcome that there is OCT4 negative expression in the dental pulp of natal and deciduous teeth. Contrastingly, Widera et al. (2009)[35] reported positive OCT4 expression in palatal tissue using immunocytochemical techniques and reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction in his in vivo study. Thankappan et al. (2022)[17] reported low-level OCT4 positivity in normal oral mucosa in his study.

We also found that dental pulp samples, particularly from deciduous teeth, vacuolations were observed within the connective tissue stroma, which corroborated with the observations of Bhuyan et al. (2020)[36] and likely reflecting physiological remodelling, minor inflammatory responses, or fixation artefacts. We also observed positive expression of CD44 in lymphocytes of inflamed shedding pulp. Also, spindle-shaped cells in lamina propria of gingiva and palatal mucosa showed CD44 expression. However, stress related to malocclusion and crowding may alter the normal histological architecture of the pulp, even in teeth that appear clinically healthy.[37]

The majority of earlier literature analysed healthy and diseased tissues; however, there is a very limited comparison between various normal oral tissues to determine stemness. In our study, the results demonstrate that gingival and palatal mucosa contain substantial amounts of multipotent stem cells in comparison to stem cells in exfoliated deciduous dental pulp, thereby proving their potential as reliable stem cell sources for use in regenerative medicine. The high vascularization present in these tissues makes them ideal for application in both tissue repair and regenerative purposes. OCT4 was absent across all tissue groups, suggesting a differentiated state and stable tissue-specific identity.

The limited availability of pulp from exfoliating deciduous teeth led to research into oral epithelial tissues, which showed similar stem cell expression. This suggests that oral epithelium, although unexplored, may be a promising source of stem cells for regenerative therapy.

The study has two main limitations that is small sample size and relied only on immunohistochemical evaluation which shows marker expression but fails to measure stem cell functionality. The current research needs to confirm and extend its findings through studies that use both molecular and functional assays, including flow cytometry and gene expression profiling and differentiation studies.

CONCLUSION

CD44 is widely expressed in normal epithelial, stromal, and mesenchymal cells, limiting its specificity as a definitive stem cell marker; however, comparatively stronger membranous staining observed in the basal and suprabasal epithelial layers indicates a higher concentration of stem cells in these regions when compared to other regions, suggesting that CD44 may serve as a marker for regional enrichment and aid in the potential isolation of progenitor cell populations. Vascular-associated progenitor zones became visible through CD34, but this marker cannot serve as a complete hematopoietic stem cell indicator because some stem cells that remain inactive do not express CD34. OCT 4 expression remained absent in all differentiated tissues which created an expected and positive outcome that demonstrated effective control of pluripotency through epigenetic processes. While most studies have focused on dental pulp tissue and have demonstrated successful regenerative outcomes, other oral tissues, such as gingival and palatal mucosa, also possess regenerative potential and may serve as an alternative source of progenitor cells. Overall, these findings suggest that CD44 and CD34 can help in identifying progenitor or stem cell associated areas, but their limited specificity as standalone markers and the absence of OCT4 indicate differentiated tissues; nevertheless, oral tissues, such as palatal mucosa, gingival mucosa, and dental pulp, may represent accessible sources of multipotent stem cells.
