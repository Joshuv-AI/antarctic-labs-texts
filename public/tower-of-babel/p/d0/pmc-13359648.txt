DICER-driven regulation of lipid metabolism and macrophage-adipocyte crosstalk in partial lipodystrophy

ABSTRACT
Partial lipodystrophy is characterized by abnormal fat distribution, impaired thermogenesis, systemic inflammation, and diminished DICER1 expression in adipose tissue. Using adipocyte-specific Dicer1-deficient murine models to mimic lipodystrophy, we identified early metabolic and inflammatory alterations associated with the onset of partial lipodystrophy. DICER deficiency in adipocytes impaired lipid metabolism, leading to increased release of saturated lipids and triglycerides into the medium while reducing the biosynthesis of anti-inflammatory lipids. These changes promoted a pro-inflammatory environment by inducing macrophages to release IL-1β. In parallel, both adipocyte-autonomous DICER deficiency and the resulting inflammatory environment impaired adipocyte thermogenic capacity. Lipidomic analyses revealed reduced levels of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and other polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in Dicer1-deficient adipocytes, accompanied by increased expression of genes encoding CYP450 enzymes linked to lipid degradation, and reduced DHA biosynthesis. Co-culture experiments confirmed that Dicer1-deficient adipocytes exacerbate macrophage-driven pro-inflammatory profile and thermogenic gene suppression. Notably, DHA supplementation mitigated the pro-inflammatory response triggered by conditioned media from Dicer1-deficient adipocytes. These findings reveal a critical role for adipocyte DICER in regulating lipid metabolism, macrophage-adipocyte interactions, and thermogenic function, offering new insights into molecular mechanisms driving metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation associated with partial lipodystrophy.

Introduction

Lipodystrophy is a group of metabolic disorders characterized by abnormal fat distribution and metabolic complications such as insulin resistance, diabetes, and dyslipidemia [1-4]. These disorders include congenital (genetic) or acquired forms, which can be further classified as generalized and partial. Partial lipodystrophy involves selective fat loss in specific body regions, leading to metabolic disturbances. Although traditionally considered rare, recent evidence suggests that lipodystrophy may be more prevalent, especially among populations such as patients with HIV, and is often underdiagnosed [5-7].

Partial lipodystrophy manifests as lipoatrophy, affecting areas like the face, buttocks, arms, and legs, and lipohypertrophy, with fat accumulation in truncal regions such as the abdomen or neck (buffalo hump), resulting in insulin resistance, hypertriglyceridemia, and increased cardiovascular risk [8-10]. Its pathophysiology involves a complex interplay between adipocytes and immune cells, including accumulation of adipose tissue (AT) macrophages, contributing to chronic low-grade inflammation [11-13]. While dysfunctional adipocytes release molecules that activate macrophages and exacerbate inflammation [14], recruited macrophages can reduce thermogenic capacity of AT [15,16], yet another feature of lipodystrophy [17-19].

Reduced DICER1 expression in AT has been linked to the pathophysiology of partial lipodystrophy [17,18]. DICER performs several molecular functions, most notably the processing of precursor micro-RNAs (miRNAs) into their mature forms [20]. Consequently, reduced DICER levels impair miRNA biogenesis, contributing to adipocyte dysfunction [17,18,21]. Adipocyte-specific Dicer1 knockout (AdicerKO) mice have been used as a model of partial lipodystrophy, as they recapitulate several features of the syndrome, including reduced intra-abdominal fat, whitening of brown adipose tissue (BAT), insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and premature aging [18,21,22], making them a valuable tool for studying the mechanisms underlying partial lipodystrophy.

Here, we used the AdicerKO mouse and differentiated murine adipocytes with Dicer1 deletion to investigate the mechanisms underlying DICER-associated partial lipodystrophy, particularly focusing on lipid metabolism, inflammation, and thermogenic capacity. Our findings reveal that DICER deficiency in adipocytes is sufficient to disrupt oxidative metabolism, alter lipid accumulation and release, and create a pro-inflammatory environment within AT. DICER deficiency results in adipocytes releasing more palmitate and triglycerides in the medium while producing less polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which acts as an anti-inflammatory molecule suppressing macrophage-intrinsic production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β. In parallel, both cell-autonomous DICER deficiency and macrophage recruitment contributed to the suppression of adipocyte thermogenic capacity. These mechanisms lead to impaired energy utilization and dysfunctional lipid handling in adipocytes, triggering inflammation and contributing to AT abnormalities characteristic of lipodystrophy.

Methods

Animals

Eight-week-old male adipocyte-specific Dicer1 knockout AdicerKO mice and their respective Lox controls were generated via Cre-Lox recombination by crossing Dicerlox/lox mice with transgenic mice expressing Cre recombinase under the adiponectin promoter [18]. The animals were obtained from the Multidisciplinary Center for Biological Research (CEMIB) at UNICAMP, Brazil. All procedures were approved by the UNICAMP Animal Ethics Committee protocol 4749–1/2017.

Mice were assigned to three temperature conditions: A) thermoneutrality (30°C ± 2°C), where animals were maintained for five days in a climate-controlled chamber (HPP750life Memmert) with controlled humidity and a 12/12-hour light-dark cycle [23,24]; B) intense cold exposure (4°C ± 2°C), where mice were housed individually in mini-isolators for five days in a cold room at 4°C [18]. Rectal temperature was monitored and mice showing hypothermia below 28°C were transferred to a 37°C heating plate; C) mild cold exposure (room temperature, 21°C ± 2°C), where mice were housed under standard facility conditions. After five days, mice were euthanized using ketamine (360 mg/kg) and xylazine (36 mg/kg). Each group contained three to eight mice, depending on the analysis.

Cell isolation and flow cytometry analysis

Fresh inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) was minced and digested in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing collagenase VIII (1 mg/mL) at 37°C for 30 min under agitation at 120 rpm. Cells were filtered through a 100-μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 200 × g for five minutes. The stromal vascular fraction (SVF) was incubated with erythrocyte lysis buffer (ACK, Lonza) for five minutes, washed, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) [25]. Cell count was determined using a Neubauer chamber.

For immunodetection of cell surface proteins, cells were incubated with fluorescently-labeled antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated in vitro with PMA (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (500 ng/mL) for 60 min to induce IL-1β followed by brefeldin A (1 μg/mL) for an additional four hours. Cells were then fixed using Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD), permeabilized with Perm Wash (BD), and stained. The following antibodies were used: cell surface markers - F4/80 (BV421, 1:200), CD11b (APC Cy7, 1:200), CD11c (PE Cy7, 1:200), CD206 (APC, 1:200) (BioLegend); intracellular marker-pro-IL-1β (PE, 1:100) (eBioscience); Live/Dead (AQUA, 1:500). A minimum of 100,000 events was acquired using BD FACSCanto II BD Biosciences and data were analyzed using FlowJo 9.5.3 with gating strategies available in Figure S1A-E.

Cell culture

Primary pre-adipocytes were isolated from the SVF of subcutaneous (SAT) and brown (BAT) adipose tissues of Lox and AdicerKO mice using collagenase IV (1 mg/mL). Cells were cultured in 10 cm plates with DMEM (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) until reaching 80% confluence. Cells were then trypsinized, seeded onto 24-well plates, and upon reaching 100% confluence, induced to differentiate for 2 days with DMEM containing 20 nM insulin, 1 nM T3, 0.5 mM IBMX, 1 μM dexamethasone, 2.8 μM rosiglitazone, and 0.125 mM indomethacin. After induction, cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 20 nM insulin and 2.8 μM rosiglitazone for an additional 6 days [26].

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) were isolated from the femur and tibia of 8-week-old C57BL/6 male mice (UNICAMP CEUA protocol 5183–1/2019). After red blood cell lysis, cells were plated at 1.0 × 106 cells/mL in RPMI medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% pyruvate, 1% MEM vitamins) supplemented with 10% L929-conditioned medium [27]. On day 5, the medium was replaced, and on day 6, macrophages were polarized for 24 h into different phenotypes: inflammatory (100 ng/mL LPS + 20 ng/mL IFN-γ), or repair/resolution (20 ng/mL IL-4). Co-culture experiments were performed by adding 2 × 104 macrophages to differentiated adipocytes derived from the SVF of AdicerKO or Lox adipocytes, followed by incubation for 6 h with 10 μM isoproterenol.

Macrophage cell line (NR-9456) was cultured at 5 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates using RPMI medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). Repair/resolution polarization was induced with 20 ng/mL IL-4 for 24 h.

Tamoxifen Inducible Dicer KO cells:

Loss-of-function experiments were performed using immortalized white subcutaneous pre-adipocytes derived from the stromal vascular fraction of Dicer1 flox mice expressing a tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase (Cre-ERT2). This system enables conditional knockout of Dicer1 upon treatment with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT). To immortalize the cells, they were transduced with the pBABE-neo large TcDNA vector (Addgene #1780), which expresses the SV40 T antigen. For Dicer1 knockout induction, cells were treated with 10 μM 4-OHT or ethanol (vehicle control) for 24 h, followed by washing and a 24-hour incubation in DMEM.

Adipocyte differentiation and conditioned media treatment:

Pre-adipocytes were cultured to 100% confluence (day 0) and, after 2 days, differentiated using DMEM (10% FBS, 20 nM insulin, 2.8 μM rosiglitazone, 1 nM T3, 0.5 mM IBMX, 1 μM dexamethasone, 0.125 mM indomethacin) for 2 days (day 2). Cells were then maintained in DMEM with 20 nM insulin, 2.8 μM rosiglitazone, and 1 nM T3 for 2 more days (day 4), followed by DMEM with 20 nM insulin and 2.8 μM rosiglitazone for 2 additional days (day 6) [26]. On day 6, Dicer1 knockout was induced with 4-OHT for 24 h, and cells were incubated for 6 h with 25% conditioned medium from BMDM activated with 100 ng/mL LPS + 20 ng/mL IFN-γ (inflammatory), or 20 ng/mL IL-4 (repair/resolution), along with 10 μM isoproterenol to stimulate beige adipocyte differentiation.

Levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in macrophage culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA MAX™ Standard Sets from BioLegend: Mouse TNF-α ELISA MAX™ Standard (Cat# 430904), Mouse IL-6 ELISA MAX™ Standard (Cat# 431304), Mouse IL-1β ELISA MAX™ Standard (Cat# 432604), and Mouse IL-1β ELISA Set BD OptEIA BD Biosciences (Cat# 559603). Macrophages were stimulated under the indicated conditions, and culture supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. Cytokine concentrations were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol, using a standard curve generated from recombinant protein standards. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and values were normalized to total protein content where applicable.

In Vivo DHA Supplementation and Tissue Collection Protocol

Mice were allocated into four experimental groups: Lox control (n = 5), AdicerKO control (n = 5), Lox + DHA (n = 5), and AdicerKO + DHA (n = 5). The DHA-supplemented groups received a high dose of 1 g/kg body weight per mouse of Ultra Concentrated Mega DHA (Vita-for), providing 1500 mg DHA and 300 mg of EPA per dose, administered daily via micropipette-guided administration each morning for five weeks, while control groups received water.

At the end of the supplementation period, SAT was collected under sterile conditions, weighed, and suspended in 500 μL of culture medium to prevent dehydration. After all tissues were collected, 1 mL of medium per 0.05 g of tissue was added. Tissues were finely minced using sterile scissors, transferred to a 6-well plate, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. On the experimental day, supernatants from the incubated SAT were collected and centrifuged at 450 g to remove tissue debris. The conditioned medium (1%) was used to treat WT BMDM cells for 2 h under conditions including SAT-derived medium from AdicerKO or Lox mice, with or without DHA supplementation.

Oxygen Consumption Analysis Using Seahorse Flux Analyzer

Oxygen consumption analysis was performed as previously described in [28]. To measure oxygen consumption, 5000 cells from differentiated DICERKO pre-adipocyte cell line were cultured in 24-well plates. The day before the experiment, the Seahorse XF cartridge was hydrated with XF Calibrant solution (Agilent Technologies) and incubated overnight at 37°C without CO2. Adipocytes were treated with 10 μM isoproterenol for 6 h and incubated for 1 h in Seahorse XF DMEM (Agilent) supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 2 mM pyruvate, and 1 mM glutamine prior to measurement.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was assessed through sequential injections of oligomycin (1 μM), carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP, 2 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (1 μM). Results were normalized to protein level and measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™ BCA, Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Mitochondrial Superoxide Detection (MitoSOX Assay)

WT and DICERKO adipocyte cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 10 min at 37°C in the dark with MitoSOX reagent (5 μM, Molecular Probes, Thermo Scientific). Antimycin A (2 μM) was used as a positive control, as it inhibits mitochondrial complex III and increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Cells were washed three times with PBS, and fluorescence was detected using a spectrophotometer (510–580 nm). ROS quantification was normalized to protein content, determined by BCA assay (Pierce™ BCA, Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was assessed using CellTiter Glo and MTT assays according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the CellTiter Glo assay, luminescence was measured as an indicator of cellular ATP levels. For the MTT assay, cells were incubated with MTT reagent, and the resulting formazan crystals produced by metabolically active cells were solubilized and quantified by measuring absorbance. Cell viability was expressed relative to vehicle-treated controls.

LDH release assay

Cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in culture supernatants. After treatment, supernatants were collected and incubated with LDH reaction mixture according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a plate reader, and percent cytotoxicity was calculated relative to spontaneous LDH release from untreated cells and maximum LDH release induced by DMSO treatment. Percent LDH release was calculated as: 100 × (experimental – low control) / (high control – low control).

qPCR analysis

RNA was extracted using Trizol (Life Technologies), and RT-qPCR was performed as previously described [28]. Reverse transcription used 1 μg RNA (High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, Applied Biosystems). qPCR reactions contained 250 nM primers, 25 ng cDNA, and 1 × SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) in 10 μL total volume. Gene expression was analyzed on a CFX384 (Bio-Rad) with 18S or 36b4 as the endogenous control. Primer sequences are in Table S2.

RNA sequencing

DICERKO adipocyte cells were treated with 10 μM isoproterenol for 6 h, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Life Technologies). RNA quality and integrity were assessed using Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) following poly(A) mRNA enrichment. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating paired-end 150 bp reads. RNA-seq data processing was performed using Galaxy [29]. Paired-end RNA-seq reads underwent an initial quality control check with FastQC (Andrews, FastQC), followed by the removal of low-quality sequences and adapters using Trimmomatic [30]. The processed reads were aligned to the mouse genome mm10 using the HISAT2 aligner [31] with the default settings provided by Galaxy.

After alignment, read counts were generated using featureCounts [32] to quantify reads mapped to genomic features. The generated counts were subjected to low-abundance filtering, removing genes with fewer than 4 reads across all samples, followed by the removal of genes with variance lower than 5. Then, differential expression analysis was conducted using the online tool NetworkAnalyst 3.0 [33], employing the edgeR package [34] for statistical modeling. Data normalization was performed using the TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) method to adjust for sequencing depth differences between samples [35]. Differentially expressed genes were identified based on variance adjustment and TMM normalization, with an FDR cutoff of 0.05 used to select significantly regulated genes.

Lipid extraction and LC/MS

Lipids were extracted using a modified version of the Bligh-Dyer method [36]. Cell pellets were lysed in 500 μL of PBS. 450 μL were diluted with 450 μL of PBS and used for lipid extraction. For media samples, 900 μL of cell line or primary cell culture media were used. Samples were manually shaken in a glass vial (VWR) with 1 mL methanol and 2 mL chloroform containing internal standards (d31-palmitic acid, d7-Cholesterol) for 30 s. The resulting mixture was vortexed for 15 s and centrifuged at 2400 x g for 6 min to induce phase separation. The organic (bottom) layer was retrieved using a Pasteur pipette, dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen, and reconstituted in 2:1 chloroform: methanol for LC/MS analysis.

Lipidomic analysis was performed on a Vanquish HPLC online with a Q-Exactive quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ion source (Thermo). Data were acquired in positive and negative ionization modes. Solvent A consisted of 95:5 water:methanol, solvent B was 70:25:5 isopropanol:methanol:water. For positive mode, solvents A and B contained 5 mM ammonium formate with 0.1% formic acid; for negative mode, solvents contained 0.028% ammonium hydroxide. An XBridge (Waters) C8 column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 50 mm) was used. The gradient was held at 0% B between 0 and 5 min, raised to 20% B at 5.1 min, increased linearly from 20% to 100% B between 5.1 and 55 min, held at 100% B between 55 min and 63 min, returned to 0% B at 63.1 min, and held at 0% B until 70 min. Flow rate was 0.1 mL/min from 0 to 5 min, 0.3 mL/min between 5.1 min and 55 min, and 0.4 mL/min between 55 min and 70 min. Spray voltage was 3.5 kV and 2.5 kV for positive and negative ionization modes, respectively; S-lens RF level was 65. Sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gases were 50, 10 and 1, respectively. Capillary temperature was 325°C and auxiliary gas heater temperature was 200°C. Data were collected in full MS/dd-MS2 (top 10). Full MS was acquired from 150 to 1500 m/z with resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 1 × 106 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. MS2 was acquired with resolution of 17,500, a fixed first mass of 50 m/z, AGC target of 1 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 200 ms. Stepped normalized collision energies were 20, 30 and 40%.

Lipid identification was performed with LipidSearch (Thermo). Mass accuracy, chromatography and peak integration of all LipidSearch-identified lipids were verified with Skyline [37]. Peak areas were used in data reporting, data was normalized using internal standards.

Polar extraction and LC/MS

Polar metabolites were extracted from samples according to Yuan et al., 2012 [38]. Briefly, polar metabolites from AdicerKO and Lox primary adipocyte-conditioned media were extracted with 500 μL of 80% methanol (cooled to −80 °C). Samples were sonicated, vortexed, and incubated for 4 h at −80 C. Samples were centrifuged (14,000 x g, 4 C, 10 min), and supernatants were transferred to new tubes and dried in speed vac. Samples were reconstituted in 45 μL water prior to injection.

Polar metabolites were analyzed on a Vanquish HPLC coupled to a Q-Exactive quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo) fitted with an XBridge Amide HILIC column (3.5 μm, 100 ×4.6 mm i.d., Waters). The following LC solvents were used: solution A, 95:5 water:acetonitrile, 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, 20 mM ammonium acetate, solution B, 100% acetonitrile. The following gradient was utilized: at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with a gradient consisting of 85–60% B in 2 min, 60–50% B in 8 min, 50–2% B in 2 min, 2% B for 4 min, up to 85% B in 1 min and reequilibrate at 85% B for 7 min, for a total run time of 24 min. The injection volume was 10 μL, the column oven temperature was set to 25 °C and the autosampler kept at 4 °C.

Spray voltage was 3.5 kV and 2.5 kV for positive and negative ionization modes, respectively; S-lens RF level was 65. Sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gases were 30, 10 and 1, respectively. Capillary temperature was 325°C and auxiliary gas heater temperature was 200°C. Data was collected in full MS/dd-MS2 (top 5). Full MS was acquired from 60 to 900 m/z with resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 3 × 106 and a maximum injection time of 200 ms. MS2 was acquired with resolution of 17,500, a fixed first mass of 50 m/z, AGC target of 1 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 200 ms. Stepped normalized collision energies were 20, 30 and 40%.

D5-ALA incorporation and DHA synthesis analysis

Differentiated WT adipocytes were incubated with D5-labeled α-linolenic acid (D5-ALA, C18:3) at 50 μM, 10 μM, and 1 μM, as well as heavy ALA at 10 μM, in media containing 0.9% BSA without FBS. Cells were collected after 4, 8, and 24 h. A negative control (50 μM SC26196) was included for 24 h to inhibit PUFA synthesis.

Following the initial test, subsequent analysis was performed at 10 μM D5-ALA for 8 h. Lipids were extracted using the Bligh-Dyer method and analyzed by LC/MS as described. DHA Standard (Cayman Chemical, Cat# 26414) was used for quantification. D5-labeled DHA and intermediates were identified by detecting mass shifts corresponding to deuterium incorporation, with peak integration validated using LipidSearch and Skyline.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired t-test, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group analyses, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when necessary. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 11.

Results

Dicer1 deficiency in mature adipocytes induces a pro-inflammatory environment and impairs the thermogenic response in AT of young mice prior to the development of partial lipodystrophy

To explore how metabolic disruptions emerge in partial lipodystrophy, we used young, 8-week-old AdicerKO mice. This approach allowed us to investigate the phenomena preceding the onset of partial lipodystrophy, which develops later in life in this model [18], thereby uncovering potential mechanisms driving its pathogenesis while avoiding confounding effects from secondary phenotypic manifestations. Thermogenic adipocytes contribute to lipid oxidation and the buffering of circulating fatty acids, while lipodystrophic states are associated with impaired thermogenesis and increased accumulation of inflammatory macrophages [1,39-42]. Therefore, to account for adipocyte thermogenic status as an experimental variable, we exposed mice to thermoneutrality (30°C), mild cold stress (room temperature, 21°C), or intense cold stress (4°C), and assessed thermogenic and inflammatory markers in AT.

Cold exposure increased the abundance of macrophages (F4/80+CD11b+) in SAT of young mice, with this effect being more pronounced in AdicerKO mice at room temperature (Fig. 1A-C and Fig. S1). Inflammatory macrophages (F4/80+CD11b+CD11c+) were more abundant in SAT of AdicerKO mice compared to the LoxP-flanked “floxed” littermates (Lox) at room temperature (mild cold stress) and thermoneutrality, while repair/resolution macrophages did not show a statistical difference at these conditions. Notably, during intense cold stress, repair/resolution macrophages were less abundant in AdicerKO mice than in Lox controls (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that adipocyte Dicer1 deficiency skews macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype in SAT, an effect particularly evident under mild cold stress.

Consistent with this pro-inflammatory state, SAT macrophages in AdicerKO mice showed increased pro-IL-1β expression at room temperature (Fig. 1D), mirrored by higher Tnf, Cd86, and Ccl2 mRNA levels in whole SAT (Fig. 1E-H), supporting the role of adipocyte DICER in regulating AT inflammation before lipodystrophy becomes apparent (Fig. S1F). Notably, these effects were influenced by the ambient temperature.

Changes in ambient temperature control thermogenesis, and thermogenesis can be influenced by the inflammatory status of the AT [43-46]. Cold exposure increased Ucp1 expression in SAT of both Lox and AdicerKO mice (Fig. 1I). Under mild cold stress (21 °C), Ucp1 levels were significantly lower in AdicerKO mice compared with Lox littermates. As expected for SAT, Ucp1 expression spanned several orders of magnitude across thermoneutrality, mild cold stress, and cold exposure, and is therefore shown with axis breaks to permit visualization across conditions.

Dicer1 deficiency induces pro-inflammatory markers and impairs metabolic function of adipocytes in a cell autonomous manner

To determine if DICER is required cell-autonomously to maintain the thermogenic potential of adipocytes and trigger inflammatory signals, we differentiated preadipocytes from the SVF of BAT and SAT of AdicerKO and Lox mice into mature brown and beige-like adipocytes, respectively. Since in this model Dicer1 deletion is driven by the adiponectin promoter, preadipocytes still express functional DICER, making this an adipocyte-specific Dicer1 knockout model. We observed a marked decrease in thermogenic markers, such as Ucp1 and Elovl3, in both primary brown and beige-like adipocytes differentiated from BAT and SAT SVF of AdicerKO mice compared with Lox mice, with no change in the white adipocyte marker Hoxc9 (Fig. 1J and K). Additionally, Dicer1 deficiency led to increased levels of genes encoding cytokines and chemokines, including Il10 and Ccl2, in beige adipocytes (Fig. 1L). These results show that Dicer1 deficiency in adipocytes reduces thermogenic gene expression cell autonomously and triggers pro-inflammatory signals.

Next, we investigated transcriptional changes occurring in mature adipocytes following Dicer1 depletion. We used an immortalized subcutaneous preadipocyte cell line derived from Dicer1lox/lox mice, allowing Dicer1 deletion after adipocyte differentiation (DICERKO) (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A-C). Following knockout induction with 4-hydroxytamoxifen and isoproterenol stimulation to mimic cold exposure, RNA sequencing analysis identified 39 downregulated and 42 upregulated genes compared to controls (Fig. 2B). Downregulated genes were associated with neurotransmitter signaling, lipid metabolism, and cytokine signaling pathways, while upregulated genes were related to metabolic and inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2C-G). These transcriptomic changes suggest that Dicer1 deficiency reprograms beige adipocytes towards a pro-inflammatory and metabolically impaired state.

Consistent with the primary cell data and the alteration in genes involved in metabolic pathways, we observed decreases in thermogenic markers, such as Ucp1, Dio2, and Elovl3 in differentiated DICERKO adipocytes, with no changes in Ppargc1a or Prdm16 levels (Fig. 3A). Moreover, baseline oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (Fig. 3B and C), ATP-linked OCR (Fig. 3D), and mitochondrial maximal respiratory capacity (Fig. 3E) were reduced in DICERKO adipocytes. Although proton leak-related OCR showed a slight reduction, it was not statistically significant (Fig. 3F), and no differences in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production were observed (Fig. 3G). These results show that Dicer1 deficiency impairs mitochondrial respiration in beige-like adipocytes.

Crosstalk between macrophages and adipocytes is influenced by the abundance of DICER in adipocytes

While Dicer1 deficiency impairs adipocyte energy utilization cell-autonomously, increased macrophage accumulation could represent yet another stimulus to reduce thermogenic capacity in AdicerKO mice. To test this, we co-cultured primary beige-like adipocytes from AdicerKO or Lox mice with wild-type BMDMs for 6 h in cold-mimicking conditions (T3 + isoproterenol) (Fig. 4 A). Consistently, Dicer1 deficiency reduced Ucp1 expression (Fig. 4B). Co-culture with IL-4-polarized (repair/resolution) or LPS+IFNγ-polarized (inflammatory) macrophages also reduced Ucp1 in Lox adipocytes, regardless of macrophage phenotype (Fig. 4B and S3A-H). Interestingly, macrophage co-culture did not further reduce Ucp1 in AdicerKO adipocytes, suggesting that Dicer1 deficiency and macrophages suppress thermogenesis via convergent mechanisms. Similarly, conditioned media (CM) from activated macrophages reduced Ucp1 and Prdm16 in Lox, but not in AdicerKO adipocytes (Fig. 4 C-D).

We also found higher expression of inflammatory markers (Tnf, Il1b, Nos2, Il10) in AdicerKO adipocytes co-cultured with activated macrophages (Fig. 4E-H). Notably, Tnf was elevated in AdicerKO adipocytes even in the absence of macrophages (Fig. 4E). These data suggest that Dicer1 deficiency and macrophage activation similarly suppress thermogenic genes and promote inflammatory gene expression in adipocytes.

Normal levels of Dicer1 in adipocytes allow the release of molecules that suppress inflammatory markers in macrophages

To investigate how DICER controls adipocyte-macrophage crosstalk under cold exposure, we tested whether CM from primary AdicerKO-derived differentiated adipocytes modulate macrophage inflammatory status under cold-mimicking conditions. Given that repair/resolution macrophages are more prevalent in SAT and tend to accumulate during cold exposure — a process attenuated in AdicerKO mice — we opted to polarize macrophages toward the repair/resolution phenotype to more closely replicate the cold exposure condition. We first determined the CM concentration that would alter inflammatory markers without affecting macrophage polarization. We used 1% CM in subsequent experiments as it did not increase Cd206/Mcr1, a repair/resolution marker (Fig. 5A). Since AdicerKO mice maintained at room temperature displayed elevated levels of pro-IL-1β+ macrophages in AT, we investigated IL-1β as an indicator of a pro-inflammatory state. CM from AdicerKO adipocytes (CM KO) increased IL-1β mRNA and protein in IL-4-activated BMDMs (Fig. 5B-C), an effect confirmed in NR-9456 macrophages (Fig. S3I-J). CM KO also elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels (Fig. S3K-L), demonstrating that Dicer1-deficient adipocyte CM counteracts IL-4-induced repair/resolution polarization by promoting pro-inflammatory cytokines.

To pursue the immunomodulatory molecules present in adipocyte CM, we fractionated the CM by molecular size and exposed IL-4-activated NR-9456 macrophages to the resulting fractions. Specifically, CM from adipocytes was first passed through a 100 kDa cut-off column to separate molecules larger than 100 kDa from those smaller than 100 kDa. The < 100 kDa fraction was subsequently filtered through a 3 kDa cut-off column, generating two distinct fractions: molecules between 3 and 100 kDa (retained by the second filter) and molecules smaller than 3 kDa (flow-through). Small lipid mediators and PUFA-derived eicosanoids would be expected to be found in the < 3 kDa fraction, whereas lipids bound to protein or lipoprotein carriers would be expected to be found in the > 100 kDa fraction. We observed that all fractions of CM from Lox control adipocytes suppressed Il1b mRNA levels, except for the intermediate fraction (3–100 kDa), which failed to suppress Il1b expression (Fig. 5D). In contrast, CM KO failed to suppress Il1b regardless of fraction, suggesting that Dicer1 deficiency leads to loss or reduction of molecule(s) that suppress IL-1β production in macrophages. In addition, we denatured the CM using heat (Fig. 5E) or proteinase K treatment (100 μg/mL at 37 °C for 2 h) (Fig. 5F). Heat treatment abrogated the differences, whereas proteinase K treatment only partially reduced them, indicating that the active factor is thermolabile but unlikely to be exclusively protein dependent, consistent with a lipid-mediated mechanism.

Lipids and other metabolites meet these biochemical criteria as they can be freely available in the low molecular weight fraction or bound to high molecular weight components. To identify DICER-dependent immunomodulatory metabolites, we performed untargeted LC-MS/MS metabolomics on CM from AdicerKO and Lox differentiated adipocytes (Table S1). This analysis revealed 14 metabolites decreased and 6 increased in AdicerKO samples (Fig. S4A), including reductions in prostaglandins such as prostaglandin G2 and the thromboxane derivative 11-dehydro-TXB3 (Fig. S4B-E). These results, together with the fractionation and denaturation profiles, suggested that Dicer1 loss alters the release of metabolites consistent with a lipid-like mediator, prompting a broader lipidomics analysis to define how Dicer1 deficiency remodels lipid composition and release into the extracellular medium.

Dicer1 deficiency alters lipid composition and release by adipocytes

To expand our analysis to other potential metabolite mediators, including lipids, we continued our search for DICER-dependent immunomodulatory metabolites by performing global lipidomics analysis on CM from primary differentiated SAT-derived adipocytes using LC-MS/MS. Our results revealed changes in a wide range of lipid profiles upon Dicer1 ablation (Fig. 6A). Specifically, we observed an increase in palmitic acid (FA 16:0) (Fig. 6B), accompanied by reduced levels of longer, saturated fatty acids containing 18, 20, and 22 carbons in AdicerKO CM (Fig. 6C).

Our results also showed that the knockout of Dicer1 leads to marked shifts in the composition of unsaturated fatty acids in the CM. Particularly, we found an increase in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), such as palmitoleic acid (FA 16:1) and oleic acid (FA 18:1) (Fig. 6D). Conversely, we observed a decrease in PUFAs (Fig. 6E), where docosapentaenoic acid (DPA, FA 22:5 ω3) decreased by 54% and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, FA 22:6) decreased by 37% in DICERKO adipocytes (Fig. 6E). These findings demonstrate that DICER regulates the levels of fatty acids released by adipocytes and maintains the balance of fatty acid saturation among these extracellular lipids. Notably, DHA can exist both as a free PUFA and within carrier-associated lipid species, which aligns with our molecular fractionation data, in which the CM activity is distributed into both the < 3 kDa and > 100 kDa fractions.

Additionally, DICERKO CM had lower phosphatidylserine, lysophosphatidylglycerol, and lysophosphatidylcholine levels, and higher lysophosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylglycerol (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, total triglyceride levels in the CM from differentiated primary AdicerKO SAT adipocytes were 47% higher (Fig. 6G). Given this increase, we examined the incorporation of omega-3 fatty acids into triglycerides. We found a significant reduction in triacylglycerides containing alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), DPA, and DHA in the AdicerKO group (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these results indicate that Dicer1 deficiency shifts the adipocyte secretome toward increased release of saturated fatty acids and triacylglycerides, together with reduced levels of PUFAs, including omega-3 species such as DHA.

To confirm these findings using an independent cell line, we conducted lipidomic analysis of the CM derived from Dicer1-deficient adipocytes differentiated from the immortalized DICERKO preadipocyte cell line (Fig. S5A). While in this cell line we did not observe changes in the levels of saturated free fatty acids, we found a reduction in PUFAs in the CM of the DICERKO group compared to controls (Fig. S5B). Notably, omega-3 PUFAs were particularly affected, with EPA (FA 20:5) levels decreased by 12% and DHA (FA 22:6) levels reduced by 19% in DICERKO adipocyte CM (Fig. S5B), agreeing with the primary cell data. In both systems, adipocyte viability and differentiation were preserved (Fig. S2A-I), indicating that the extracellular lipid changes did not arise from cell lysis.

Dicer1 deficiency disrupts fatty acid metabolism and alters lipid profiles in adipocytes

To further explore the impact of Dicer1 deficiency on lipid accumulation, we performed global lipidomics analysis on whole-cell lysates from immortalized DICERKO differentiated adipocytes (Fig. 7A). Similar to our findings in CM from AdicerKO SAT-derived differentiated adipocytes, DICERKO cells showed reductions in PUFAs, with EPA decreased by 19% and DHA by 23% (Fig. 7B). We also observed decreased hydroxylated fatty acids derived from EPA and DHA, which promote anti-inflammatory responses and repair/resolution macrophage polarization [47-50], as well as reduced hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid levels, a metabolite of arachidonic acid (Fig. 7C). Consistent with our primary differentiated adipocyte lipidomic analysis, total phosphatidylserine levels were reduced in DICERKO cells, while lysophosphatidylglycerol and phosphatidylglycerol increased (Fig. 7 D).

In assessing whether these lipidomic alterations were reflected in AT lipid composition, we analyzed free fatty acids in SAT from 6-month-old AdicerKO and Lox control mice using LC-MS/MS lipidomics. The results revealed a 2.5-fold increase in palmitic acid levels (Fig. 7E), accompanied by a reduction in DHA levels in AdicerKO mice (Fig. 7F), agreeing with the data in vitro. These experiments underscore a consistent decrease in DHA levels across multiple experimental approaches, evidencing a pivotal role of DICER in regulating the accumulation and release of DHA by adipocytes.

DICER regulates the biosynthesis of DHA in adipocytes

Given that DHA is the most abundant PUFA released by adipocytes in our dataset and has anti-inflammatory properties, we investigated whether Dicer1 deficiency affects DHA synthesis. We optimized the tracing conditions using varying concentrations of cold ALA and d5-ALA and included a delta-6 desaturase inhibitor as a negative control (Fig. S6). Using deuterium-labeled ALA tracing, we found that DICERKO adipocytes differentiated from the immortalized preadipocyte model showed reduced incorporation of labeled EPA (Fig. 7G), DPA (Fig. 7H), and DHA (Fig. 7I), with heavy DHA levels decreased by 30% compared to controls. Although RNA sequencing did not show changes in canonical DHA biosynthetic enzymes, we observed a 7-fold increase in Cyp2f2, a 5-fold increase in Cyp27b1, and a 50% increase in Cyp2e1 expression (Fig. S7A-E). Notably, these genes belong to the cytochrome P450 family, members of which have been implicated in lipid oxidation processes, including the metabolism of PUFAs [51].

DHA supplementation reduces macrophage pro-inflammatory markers induced by CM of DICERKO adipocytes

Reduced levels of DHA and other anti-inflammatory lipids in AT could contribute to the inflammatory profile observed in AdicerKO mice. To test this hypothesis, we investigated whether DHA supplementation could attenuate the pro-inflammatory profile of macrophages exposed to LPS and nigericin (used to induce IL-1β production) or to CM from AdicerKO SAT-derived differentiated adipocytes. DHA supplementation reduced both IL-1β release and TNF-α secretion in NR-9456 macrophages treated with CM from AdicerKO adipocytes (Fig. 7J and K), indicating that DHA mitigates this pro-inflammatory condition. Importantly, these data suggest that DHA is the released molecule from adipocytes that suppresses IL-1β in macrophages in a DICER-dependent manner.

We also conducted in vivo experiments to assess the effects of DHA supplementation on AdicerKO mice. After 5 weeks of oral DHA supplementation, the animals were euthanized, and SAT was collected, minced, and used to prepare CM, which was subsequently applied to treat BMDMs. Our results showed that CM from AdicerKO fat explants led to higher IL-1β levels in BMDMs (Fig. 7L). In contrast, CM from AdicerKO mice supplemented with DHA did not result in such an increase (Fig. 7L), showing that DHA supplementation in vivo effectively suppressed the pro-inflammatory condition elicited by loss of Dicer1 in adipocytes.

Discussion

Partial lipodystrophy is characterized by abnormal AT distribution accompanied by metabolic dysfunction and reduced adipose DICER1 expression, with consequent downregulation of several miRNAs implicated in disease pathogenesis [18,21,42,52,53]. Consistent with the role of adipocyte DICER in disease pathogenesis, mice with adipocyte-specific Dicer1 deletion develop intra-abdominal fat loss, severe insulin resistance, and BAT whitening, in part due to reduced levels of miRNAs required for maintaining adipocyte identity [18,21]. Similarly, individuals with HIV-associated partial lipodystrophy exhibit decreased DICER1 expression in AT, along with reduced circulating miRNAs, diminished thermogenic markers, subclinical inflammation, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance [17,18,21,53]. Together, these observations support DICER as a mechanistically relevant regulator of AT homeostasis in both experimental models and human partial lipodystrophy [42,54].

Building on this framework, the present study identifies adipocyte DICER as a key regulator of lipid remodeling, macrophage activation, and thermogenic capacity in AT. Dicer1 deficiency in adipocytes leads to a cell-autonomous reduction in the expression of Ucp1, Dio2, and Elovl3, indicating that impaired thermogenic capacity is established independently of macrophage input. Although the presence of macrophages also suppresses Ucp1 expression in adipocytes, the lack of additional repression when Dicer1-deficient adipocytes are co-cultured with macrophages suggests a convergence between intrinsic DICER-dependent mechanisms and inflammatory signaling pathways. While the molecular basis of this convergence was not directly addressed in the present study, it is plausible that it involves DICER-derived products, such as miRNAs known to favor thermogenesis [55]. Importantly, macrophage Dicer1 levels were not experimentally manipulated in our models, as Dicer1 deficiency was induced selectively in adipocytes, indicating that the inflammatory responses observed here reflect adipocyte-derived signals rather than macrophage-intrinsic Dicer1 deficiency.

To define the mechanisms underlying these phenotypes, we next examined lipid composition and adipocyte-derived signaling pathways. Together, our findings support a model in which adipocyte Dicer1 deficiency alters lipid composition and reduces the availability of anti-inflammatory omega-3 PUFAs, particularly DHA, leading to changes in the adipocyte lipid secretome that promote macrophage inflammatory activation and contribute to impaired thermogenic capacity. Reduced levels of DHA and related omega-3 PUFA species were accompanied by a relative increase in saturated fatty acids, a lipid profile known to favor inflammatory activation of AT macrophages [56-58].

The sustained nature of Dicer1 loss in our model is central to its biological relevance, as transient knockdown approaches do not fully recapitulate the chronic miRNA depletion observed in human lipodystrophy. Consistent with this distinction, siRNA-mediated Dicer1 knockdown in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes does not significantly affect lipid accumulation or expression of canonical lipogenic markers such as PPARγ and FAS [59]. In contrast, the adiponectin-Cre-mediated AdicerKO model used here captures sustained miRNA depletion and tissue-level dysfunction that acute knockdown systems are unlikely to reproduce, consistent with the chronic DICER1 reduction observed in human lipodystrophy [21,53]. Moreover, to our knowledge, this is the first study to interrogate the role of DICER in lipid metabolism at high resolution using lipidomics, revealing selective alterations in specific lipid classes rather than a global disruption of lipid storage. Accordingly, despite robust Dicer1 deletion in our models, Oil Red O staining is not altered, and triglyceride release into the medium is, in fact, increased, supporting a model in which DICER regulates lipid composition and signaling rather than bulk lipid accumulation.

As observed for the thermogenic gene program, these lipid changes were detectable in isolated Dicer1-deficient adipocytes, indicating that remodeling of the adipocyte lipid secretome is a cell-autonomous consequence of DICER loss rather than a secondary response to immune remodeling. Consistent with this interpretation, depletion of DHA, which has been shown to restrain macrophage inflammatory signaling [57,58,60,61] and induces thermogenesis and oxidative metabolism programs in adipocytes [62], provides a mechanistic link between adipocyte Dicer1 deficiency, the early inflammatory phenotype observed in AT, and associated impairment of thermogenic function.

Reduced incorporation of ALA into EPA, DPA, and DHA indicates impaired omega-3 PUFA biosynthetic flux in Dicer1-deficient adipocytes but does not distinguish whether this reflects reduced synthesis or altered downstream processing. Conversion of ALA to DHA [63] requires sequential desaturation by FADS2 (Δ6-desaturase) [64,65] and FADS1 (Δ5-desaturase) [64], elongation by ELOVL5 [66,67] and ELOVL2 [68], and final chain shortening through peroxisomal β-oxidation in the Sprecher pathway [69,70], and disruption at any of these steps could limit DHA availability [63]. An additional possibility is altered lipolytic selectivity, because preferential mobilization of unsaturated fatty acids depends on ATGL [71] and HSL activity [72], and reduced release of PUFA species could shift the adipocyte lipid profile toward saturated fatty acids independently of biosynthetic defects [73,74]. Distinguishing among these mechanisms will require step-resolved isotope tracing together with direct measurement of desaturase, elongase, peroxisomal, and lipolytic enzyme activities.

In addition to alterations in biosynthetic pathways, Dicer1-deficient adipocytes exhibit upregulation of genes encoding cytochrome P450 enzymes, including Cyp2f2 and Cyp2e1. Cytochrome P450 enzymes are broadly involved in lipid oxidation and metabolic remodeling, and CYP2E1 has been reported to metabolize PUFAs such as EPA and DHA [75,76]. In our model, increased expression of these enzymes occurred alongside altered lipid composition and reduced DHA levels. Rather than implying a direct causal relationship, these observations are consistent with broader metabolic reprogramming that accompanies Dicer1 loss. Given the established roles of CYP enzymes in fatty acid metabolism and thermogenic regulation [56,75,77-79], their upregulation may reflect downstream adaptations within a disrupted lipid regulatory network. Because DICER regulates the maturation of miRNAs and other small RNAs [80], increased CYP expression could arise from loss of RNA-dependent regulation of these enzymes or their upstream regulators. Consistent with this possibility, Cyp2f2 and Cyp2e1 transcripts have been associated with miRNA-mediated control in other contexts [81-84]. Given the complexity of long-chain PUFA biosynthesis [85] and turnover [86], the reduction in DHA observed in Dicer1-deficient adipocytes is unlikely to result from disruption of a single regulatory interaction. Instead, it more likely reflects coordinated remodeling of lipid metabolic pathways downstream of DICER deficiency. Further studies will be required to clarify these relationships and determine their functional relevance.

In addition to acting directly on inflammatory signaling pathways, DHA serves as a precursor for specialized pro-resolving mediators such as resolvins and protectins [87,88], which actively promote resolution of inflammation. Whether production of these mediators is altered in Dicer1-deficient adipocytes remains to be determined. Notably, both DICER and DHA have been independently linked to AMPK-dependent metabolic regulation [89,90], and reduced DHA availability in Dicer1--deficient adipocytes may therefore represent one mechanism connecting DICER loss to impaired mitochondrial adaptation and inflammatory signaling [91]. Whether AMPK activity is altered downstream of DICER in this context has not been addressed.

In agreement with a functional role for DHA depletion, supplementation experiments attenuated inflammatory responses induced by CM from Dicer1-deficient adipocytes, supporting the interpretation that reduced availability of omega-3 lipid mediators contributes to macrophage activation [92]. However, these experiments do not establish that restoration of DHA alone is sufficient to rescue the broader metabolic phenotype associated with DICER loss.

Consistent with these mechanisms, the lipid remodeling and inflammatory signatures identified here resemble transcriptional changes reported in SAT from patients with familial partial lipodystrophy type 2, where pathways related to mitochondrial function and lipid metabolism are reduced alongside increased expression of inflammatory genes across adipocytes and macrophages [93]. These observations support the interpretation that altered adipocyte lipid composition may contribute to immune remodeling of AT in lipodystrophy rather than representing a secondary consequence of systemic metabolic dysfunction. In addition to regulating intracellular lipid metabolism, adipocyte DICER contributes to the production of circulating and exosomal miRNAs that mediate intercellular communication within AT [21], raising the possibility that disruption of RNA-dependent signaling further shapes macrophage activation via extracellular vesicle-containing miRNAs. Because DICER also regulates macrophage polarization and mitochondrial metabolism in immune cells [94], reduced macrophage-intrinsic DICER activity may represent an additional contributor to AT inflammation in human lipodystrophy. Whether the reduction in DHA and other anti-inflammatory PUFAs observed in this model also occurs in human lipodystrophy remains unknown, as AT PUFA composition has not been systematically examined in patient cohorts. If present in humans, reduced availability of omega-3 lipid mediators could represent a metabolically relevant contributor to persistent AT inflammation and impaired thermogenic adaptation in this syndrome.

In summary, these findings support a central role for DICER in maintaining adipocyte homeostasis, including regulation of lipid metabolism, thermogenic capacity, and macrophage–adipocyte crosstalk. The reduction in DHA levels and the associated pro-inflammatory phenotype support a functional link between adipocyte Dicer1 deficiency and immunometabolic dysfunction. The molecular mechanisms connecting DICER loss to reduced DHA availability remain unresolved and will require identification of the RNA species and regulatory pathways controlling PUFA metabolism in adipocytes. Given the anti-inflammatory properties of DHA, supplementation may represent a strategy to mitigate inflammatory outputs associated with DICER deficiency, although whether restoration of DHA improves thermogenic capacity or systemic metabolic function in vivo remains to be determined. Because this study was performed exclusively in male mice, evaluation in females will be required to determine whether these effects are conserved across sexes. More broadly, therapeutic approaches aimed at restoring RNA-dependent regulatory balance at the level of DICER or downstream, rather than targeting individual metabolic enzymes, may provide a more integrated strategy to improve adipocyte function and reduce metabolic inflammation.
