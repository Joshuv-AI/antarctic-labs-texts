GPR30-mediated NETs degradation via Trex1 in heart failure of aged female mice
Authors: Shuaishuai Zhang, Zilin Li, Xiaoya Wang, Xiaowu Wang, Jun Du, Jiaqi Liu, Zhirong Cheng, Diancai Zhao, Weixun Duan, Jipeng Ma, Jincheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe occurrence of cardiovascular diseases increases dramatically in postmenopausal aged women. Accumulating evidence has indicated that estrogen protects hearts from cardiovascular diseases. However, the underlying mechanisms was not fully elucidated. This present study was designed to investigate the function of GPR30 in pathological heart failure of aged female mice with the focus of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).MethodsTransverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery was performed to induce heart failure in aged female mice. RNA-seq and flow cytometry were employed to study neutrophils activity during heart failure of aged female mice. Heart function and cardiac fibrosis as well as NETs level were assessed. Reduction of NETs by DNase I administration、 G1 treatment and Trex1 overexpression in macrophage were conducted to elucidate the role of NETs in this pathological process. Co-culture of RAW264.7 macrophages and neutrophils were used to examine the function of Trex1 in macrophage.ResultsOur bulk RNA-seq analysis showed that neutrophil migration and neutrophil chemotaxis were markedly enhanced at the early stage of pathological cardiac hypertrophy in aged female hearts. We further demonstrated that NETs generated by these activated neutrophils in aged female myocardium at the late stage were significantly increased following TAC surgery accompanied with the reduction of GPR30 expression. GPR30 agonist G1 treatment preserved cardiac function and reduced myocardial fibrosis in aged female mice with heart failure. To further validate the key role of NETs, DNase I administration markedly enhanced cardiac performance and attenuated cardiac fibrosis with the overall neutrophil reduction in the myocardium. Our in vitro results showed that overexpression of Trex1 in RAW264.1 macrophage enhanced neutrophil NETs clearance, thus indicating that GPR30 activation could increase the exonuclease three prime repair exonuclease 1 (Trex1) expression which may be associated with the reduction of NETs level in hypertrophied hearts.ConclusionNETs generated by neutrophils exacerbate pressure overload–induced heart failure in aged female mice. GPR30 activates Trex1 signaling in macrophage to enhance NTEs degradation and thus attenuates TAC-induced cardiac dysfunction, providing an avenue for the novel therapeutics against cardiac dysfunction in postmenopausal women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08088-z.Keywords: GPER1 protein (GPR30), Postmenopausal, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Three prime repair exonuclease 1, Transverse aortic constriction, Macrophages

Introduction

The risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in postmenopausal women was significantly increased compared to premenopausal women and age-matched men [1]. The key findings from clinical trials show that hormone replacement therapy (HRT) reduces the incidence of CVD and benefits postmenopausal women when the HRT starts at the early stage of menopause [2, 3]. It is reasonably speculated that estrogen and its receptors might exert the protective roles against cardiovascular diseases among premenopausal women. The subsequent studies showed that the both nuclear estrogen receptors ERα, ERβ and non-nuclear estrogen receptor G protein coupled receptor 30 (GPR30) participated in the protection of hearts in postmenopausal women [4, 5]. Unlike hormone replacement therapy (HRT), which is associated with increased risks of breast cancer and endometrial hyperplasia via ERα, selective activation of GPR30 may confer beneficial effects on cardiovascular protection, neuroprotection, and metabolic regulation without promoting proliferation in reproductive tissues [6]. Furthermore, our primary results indicated that activation of GPR30 mitigated transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced cardiac fibrosis and preserved cardiac function in aged female mice [7]. However, the mechanistic involvement of innate immune cells in this process remains unclear.

Neutrophils, as key innate immune cells, contribute to host defense by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—complexes of extracellular chromatin and bactericidal proteins [8]. The accumulating studies have also shown that this process can be activated not only by various pathogens and their components but also by ischemia and aseptic inflammation which are key features in diverse CVD [9]. The study on experimental atherosclerosis demonstrated that inflammasome activated by NLRP3 in myeloid cells increased neutrophil accumulation and NETs in atherosclerotic plaques [10]. The extracellular chromatin in NETs aggravated myocardial ischemia reperfusion (MI/R) injury which could be a novel strategy to combat ischemia-induced cardiac injury [11]. Since sex hormones, mainly estrogen, dominate immune response more profoundly in females [12], we hypothesize that estrogen receptor-mediated regulation of neutrophils and NETs contributes to CVD progression in postmenopausal women. However, the role of NETs in heart failure in aged females remains poorly defined.

Notably, three prime repair exonuclease 1 (Trex1) as the major intracellular DNA exonuclease is an enzyme to degrade single- and double-stranded DNA to prevent the activation of the interferon response in mammalian cells [13]. It was shown that Trex1 knockout mice displayed inflammatory myocarditis, indicating its important role in maintaining organ homeostasis, thus preserving cardiac function [14]. However, whether this effect was associated with NETs has yet been explored. Furthermore, Trex1 in macrophage was shown to degrade chromatin which is the main component of NETs [15]. Although emerging evidence indicates that the GPR30/Trex1 axis may protect heart by clearing NETs in aged females, the pivotal question of whether GPR30 safeguards cardiac function via Trex1-mediated suppression of NETs deposition remains undefined. Thus, our present study was designed to explore the role of Trex1-mediated NETs clearance in the GPR30 protection against heart failure in aged females.

Materials and methods

Reagents

GPR30 agonist G1 (10008933) was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). DNase I was obtained from Roche (10104159001, Roche, USA). AAV-GPR30 and its control vector were purchased from the GeneChem Company (Shanghai, China). The anti-H3Cit antibody (ab281584), anti-MPO antibody (ab208670), anti-PAD4 antibody (ab214810), anti-GPR30 antibody (ab37742), anti-ANP antibody (ab225844), anti-β-MHC antibody (ab172967), anti-TGF-β1 antibody (ab27969) and anti-COL I antibody (ab260043) were purchased from Abcam company (Cambridge, MA, USA). The anti-COL III antibody (22734-1-AP) was purchased from Proteintech company. The anti-Trex1 antibody and anti-GAPDH antibody were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (CA, USA). The anti-Ly6G (551459) antibody was purchased from BD Biosciences. The anti-MPO (AF3667) antibody for immunofluorescent staining was obtained from R&D system. The goat anti-Rat FITC (ab6840), goat anti-Rabbit Cy3 (ab6939) and donkey anti-Goat Cy3 (ab6949) antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). The goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse HRP secondary antibodies for western blot were purchased from Zhongshan Company (Beijing, China). DAPI was obtained from Roche Molecular Biochemicals (Bayer, Mannheim, Germany). ECL reagent was purchased from Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA). NETosis Assay kit was obtained from Cayman Chemical (No. 601010). Trex1 expression plasmid was synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The Lipofectamine LTX reagent was obtained from Invitrogen company (CA, USA).

In Vivo experimental design and treatment

This study complies with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and all animal experiments were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fourth Military Medical University (No. 20200308). The sample size was chosen based on the scale of previous studies in the field that reported significant effects for similar phenotypic outcomes. The sample size justification is therefore inherently retrospective and primarily based on the observed effect sizes and statistical significance, which constitutes a potential limitation of this study. Female C57BL/6 mice at the age of 8–10 weeks old and 18 months old were purchased from Experimental Animal Center of Fourth Military Medical University. Mice were fed with normal diet and drinking water and raised in cages under a 12:12-hr light/dark cycle.

To investigate the NETs generation during the progression of pathological cardiac hypertrophy, female young mice (YO) at the age of 8–10 weeks and the aged female mice (AG) at the age of 18 months were used in the first in vivo experiments. Mice in the YO group and the AG group underwent the sham surgery while 18-month-old mice in AG/TAC group underwent transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery. TAC model was established as previously described to mimic pathological myocardial hypertrophy and even heart failure in postmenopausal women [7]. Heart function and histological assessment in AG/TAC-1 W and AG/TAC-12 W groups were performed at 1week and 12 weeks respectively following the operation.

For the second in vivo study, mice were divided into the following four groups. Mice in the aged (AG) group were injected with 5 × 1010 empty adeno-associated virus (AAV) for each mouse as the control vector via a tail vein while mice in AG+GPR30/G1 group were administered with GPR30 agonist G1 once a day with the dose of 35 µg/kg body weight intraperitoneally and a tail intravenous injection of 5 × 1010 adeno-associated virus genome particles carrying GPR30 (AAV-GPR30). After two weeks, sham operation was performed in both AG and AG+GPR30/G1 groups. Mice in AG/TAC group received a tail injection of empty AAV and underwent a TAC surgery 2 weeks after AAV injection while mice in AG/TAC+GPR30/G1 group were injected AAV-GPR30 via a tail vein and G1 administration and TAC surgery was conducted 2 weeks after injection. To explore the log-term effects of GPR30 activation on cardiac function and fibrosis, heart function and histological assessment were performed at 12 weeks post the operation.

For the third in vivo study, to clarify the role of NETs, mice were treated with DNase I (0.05 mg/kg) or PBS twice daily via intraperitoneal injection after TAC surgery. Then cardiac function and fibrosis were assessed at 12 weeks post the operation.

For the in vivo study, mice were divided into two experimental groups to investigate the necessity of macrophage Trex1 in GPR30-mediated cardioprotection. All mice first received a tail vein injection of AAV-GPR30 (5 × 1010 genome particles per mouse) and concurrent daily intraperitoneal administration of GPR30 agonist G1 (35 µg/kg body weight). Two weeks after this initial treatment, mice were administered intraosseously an AAV vector (1 × 1011 genome particles per mouse) utilizing the F4/80 promoter, with one group receiving a control vector encoding scramble shRNA and the other group receiving a vector encoding Trex1-shRNA for macrophage-specific knockdown. One week following the intraosseous injection, all mice underwent transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery. Cardiac function and histological assessment were performed at 6 weeks post-TAC to evaluate the long-term functional and structural consequences of macrophage-specific Trex1 knockdown under GPR30 activation.

All mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups and the investigator performing surgeries and treatments was unaware of group allocation until the time of intervention.

RNA-seq and data analysis and qPCR verification

Heart tissues were obtained from both groups and cDNA libraries were constructed following RNA isolation and quality check. RNA sequencing was conducted by using Illumina HiSeq 2500/4000 system (Gene Denovo Biotechnology). Clean reads were filtered by fastp (version 0.18.0) and mapped to mouse reference genome (GRCm38 Ensembl release100) using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie [4], and gene-level expression abundances (raw counts and FPKM) were calculated using RSEM. Quality control metrics included assessment of mapping rates (average unique mapping > 85%), principal component analysis (PCA) and sample correlation heatmaps based on gene expression data. For identification of significantly altered transcripts, we employed the DESeq2 algorithm implemented in R software, applying selection criteria of absolute log2 fold change > 1 and false discovery rate-adjusted p-value < 0.05. Subsequent functional characterization of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) incorporated both Gene Ontology classification and KEGG pathway examination on the Omicsmart platform (https://www.omicsmart.com). The RNA-seq data were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), accession number GSE322841.

Total RNA is first isolated from myocardial tissue 5 days after surgery by homogenization in TRIzol reagent, followed by phase separation with chloroform. The RNA from the aqueous phase is then precipitated with isopropanol, washed with ethanol, and dissolved in RNase-free water. After quantifying the RNA concentration and quality, it is reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcriptase enzyme and random primers (MF012, Mei5Bio, Beijing, China). Subsequently, quantitative PCR is performed using a SYBR Green master mix and gene-specific primers (MF306, Mei5Bio, Beijing, China). The relative expression of the target gene is finally determined by normalizing its quantification cycle (Cq) value to that of a housekeeping gene using the 2^(-ΔΔCq) method.

Echocardiography

Mice were slightly anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane mixed with oxygen and placed on a heating pad to maintain the body temperature. Heart rates of mice from different groups were maintained in 400–500 bpm. The echocardiography was performed by using VisualSonics Vevo 2100 apparatus with a 40-MHz transducer. The M-mode images were recorded for further analysis. The ejection fractions (EF)%, fraction shortening (FS)%, interventricular septal thickness at end diastole (IVSd), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end diastole (LVPWd), left ventricular internal systolic dimension (LVIDs), and left ventricular mass (LV Mass) were calculated by the Vevo Lab 5.8.1 software (FUJIFILM VisualSonics. Toronto, ON, Canada). All echocardiographic measurements were performed by an operator blinded to group identity.

Histological staining of cardiac tissues

The heart tissue samples were collected at 1week and 12 weeks respectively after TAC surgery and the heart weight as well as body weight was obtained. The Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining was conducted to evaluate morphological change and Masson Trichrome staining was performed to assess cardiac fibrosis. The cardiac hypertrophy was assessed by wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining. All the protocols were followed with routine procedures. The cardiac fibrosis and cross-sectional area of cardiomyocyte were calculated based on the images from Masson staining and WGA staining respectively.

Flow cytometry

To obtain single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis, freshly isolated mouse hearts were first perfused with PBS to clear blood cells, then carefully minced into approximately 1-mm³ fragments. The tissue fragments were subjected to enzymatic digestion in a 37 °C water bath for 60 min using a cocktail containing 0.25 mg/ml of Liberase TH (Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 µg/ml DNase I (Worthington), with gentle agitation to ensure complete dissociation. The resulting cell suspension was sequentially filtered through a 40-µm nylon mesh (Falcon), washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in staining buffer at a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/ml. Before staining, cell viability was examined by using cell counter (RWD, Shenzhen, China) and Only samples with viability greater than 90% can be used for subsequent experiments. For immunophenotyping, cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with fluorescently conjugated antibodies against CD45 (APC/Cyanine7, BioLegend 103115), CD11b (FITC, BioLegend 101205), and Ly6G (APC, BioLegend 127613) with appropriate isotype controls included for gating. After antibody staining, cells were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in 500 µL of cold PBS, and immediately acquired on a BD FACS flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). All flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Version 10.4, TreeStar, Ashland, OR). Gating strategies shown in Fig. S1 were established using fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls and isotype-matched antibodies. The analyst was blinded to group identity during data acquisition and analysis.

Isolation of primary neutrophils from mouse bone marrow and cell culture of primary neutrophils and RAW264.7 macrophages

The mouse neutrophil isolation kit was purchased from TBD Science (Tianjin, China). The aged female C57BL/6 mouse was sacrificed and femur as well as tibia were collected. Medullary cavity was exposed by cutting the edge. The femur and tibia were washed three times from both ends of the bone with RPMI 1640 and the bone marrow cells were collected. Primary neutrophils were isolated by density gradient centrifugation following the manufacture’s instruction. Then neutrophils were further cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Solarbio, China) supplemented with 10% FBS at a concentration of 106 cells/ml pre-coated with 0.2% gelatin.

RAW264.7 macrophages were purchased from cell bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and were routinely cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS (Gibco, NYS, USA) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

In vitro cell experiments and treatments

The in vitro study included two experiments. The first experiment was to investigate the function of G1 on RAW264.7 macrophage clearance of NETs. Neutrophils were mixed with RAW264.7 cells with a ratio of 1:5 and were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium. Cells in the angiotensin II (Ang II) group were treated with 0.1 nM Ang II for 4 h while cells in Ang II + G1 group were incubated with 0.1 nM Ang II and 5 nM G1 at the same time for 4 h. At the end of experiment, cells were either fixed for immunostaining or collected for western blot or in vitro NETs measurement.

The second experiment was designed to study the role of Trex1 overexpression in RAW264.7 cells on NETs degradation. When cell confluence reached 40–50%, RAW264.7 cells were firstly transfected with Trex1 overexpression plasmid or empty control plasmid (Tsingke Biotechnology, Beijing, China) using Lipofectamine LTX in the indicated groups followed by incubation at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 6 h. Then the cells were co-cultured with neutrophils at a ratio of 1:2 following 5 nM G1 incubation for 4 h or together with 0.1 nM Ang II treatment for 4 h.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cardiac sections from myocardial tissue and the cells following different treatments were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Then tissue sections or the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocked with 10% goat serum or 1% bovine serum albumin. Then the sections or the cells were incubated with Ly6G, H3Cit or MPO primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with the proper secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dye at room temperature for 2 h. The nuclei were visualized with DAPI staining. The images were observed by using ZEISS LSM 900 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (ZEISS, Microscopy, Germany) and analyzed by Image J software.

Neutrophils NETs measurement in vitro

Neutrophils NETs were determined by measuring neutrophil elastase (NE) activity via a commercial NETosis assay kit (Cayman Chemical) following manufacture’s instruction. In brief, unbound NE in the cell supernatant was washed away and the bound NE in the NETs was cleaved by S7 nuclease. The substrate was then added and detected at 405 nm. The NE content was calculated via a standard curve.

Western blot

The protein samples from cardiac tissues and cells were obtained by using RIPA buffer with freshly-prepared protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Millipore). The protein mixtures were separated by 8%-12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h and probed with anti-GPR30, anti-PAD4, anti-H3Cit, anti-MPO, anti-TGF-β1, anti-COL I, anti-COL III, anti-ANP, anti-β-MHC or anti-GAPDH antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing for 5 times with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The blots were visualized using super enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (36208ES60, Yeasen, China). The quantification of each band was conducted by using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± S.E.M. The statistical analysis was performed by using GraphPad Prism software 9.0. The statistical analysis among multiple groups was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparison. The P value was indicated in the corresponding figures.

Results

Neutrophils were greatly infiltered and activated following pressure overload in hearts of aged female mice

Our RNA-seq data showed that the difference between aged hearts and aged pressure overloaded hearts was effectively distinguished by principal component analysis (PCA) shown in Fig. 1B. Then DEGs were identified by R package DESeq2 algorithm and demonstrated in the volcano plot of Fig. 1C. GO enrichment in Fig. 1D indicated that the most abundant DEGs were clearly associated with immune response processes, particularly with neutrophil migration and chemotaxis. Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis showed that the top pathways were involved in cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways in Fig. 1E. Additionally, GSEA analysis in Fig. 1F and G revealed that neutrophil activity was significantly induced by TAC surgery in aged female mice. The expression of genes related neutrophil activation and chemotaxis including s100a8, s100a9, cxcl1, and cxcl2 were detected by qPCR to further validate our RNA-seq results. The results shown in Fig. S2 demonstrated all these genes were significantly increased following TAC surgery, indicating the central role of neutrophil activity in aged female hearts. Thus, our RAN-seq data clearly demonstrate that neutrophil is activated during the early stage of cardiac hypertrophy in aged female mice.

Cardiac function and fibrosis were deteriorated following TAC surgery in aged female mice

We further assessed cardiac function at 1 week and 12 weeks following TAC surgery in aged female mice. Compared to young mice group, LVEF and LVFS of 18-month-old female mice was slightly decreased, implying the decline of cardiac systolic function in Fig. 2B-D. However, there was no significant alteration of IVSd, LVPWd, and LV Mass between two groups except the enlarged LVIDs in Fig. 2E-H. LVEF and LVFS of mice at 1 week post TAC was further decreased compared with aged female mice. Moreover, cardiac systolic function as evidenced by LVEF and LVFS were markedly worsened at 12 weeks after TAC surgery in Fig. 2B-D. We also observed the significant increase of IVSd, LVPWd, LVIDs, and LV Mass, indicating the pressure overload- induced cardiac hypertrophy (Fig. 2E-H). Additionally, pressure overload led to significantly myocardial fibrosis and cross-sectional area enlargement of cardiomyocytes following TAC surgery in Fig. 2I-L. These data above clearly showed that cardiac systolic function was slightly reduced with aging alone and markedly declined following TAC surgery while cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy were markedly aggravated in aged female mice during pressure overload.

NETs were gradually increased in response to pressure overload-induced heart failure in aged female mice accompanied by the decline of GPR30 level

To clarify the function of neutrophil activation following TAC surgery in aged female mice from RNA-seq data, the neutrophil infiltration in myocardium was assessed by flow cytometry and tissue immunofluorescence. The number of neutrophils in myocardial tissues markedly elevated at 1week after TAC and maintained relatively high level at 12 weeks after TAC as evidenced by Ly6G positive cells in myocardium and flow cytometry in Fig. 3A-C. Considering NETs is the critical activity for activated neutrophils, we further evaluated cardiac NETs levels. The H3Cit/Ly6G double positive cells were increased at 1 week post TAC and reached higher level at 12 weeks after TAC compared with sham-operated groups in Fig. 3B-D. The cardiac expression of MPO was upregulated with aging and reached highest level at 12 weeks post TAC while H3Cit expression was mildly altered but was greatly increased at 12 weeks after TAC surgery in Fig. 3E-H. These results together suggested that neutrophils were infiltrated with age and greatly aggravated post TAC surgery while pathological cardiac hypertrophy caused significant NETs production at 12 weeks after TAC. Interesting, GPR30 expression was downregulated in aged female mice compared to young mice and even lowered post TAC surgery in Fig. 3E and F, which was contrary to myocardial NETs production.

GPR30 overexpression combined with G1 treatment improved cardiac function and reduced myocardial fibrosis post TAC surgery in aged female mice

To demonstrate the effects of GPR30 activation on cardiac function in aged female mice, we established the pressure overload-induced heart failure model by TAC surgery and examined cardiac function and fibrosis at 12 weeks following TAC surgery. To fully activate GPR30, the G1 agonist and AAV-GPR30 were applied. As shown in Fig. 4A-C, LVEF and LVFS were markedly decreased in AG/TAC group while GPR30 activation alone hardly alter cardiac function of aged female mice. Furthermore, cardiac hypertrophy manifested by IVSd, LVPWd, LVIDd, and LV Mass were significantly increased following TAC surgery as demonstrated in Fig. 4D-G. However, these deteriorative effects were partly reversed by GPR30 activation, indicating a protective role of GPR30 activation against cardiac dysfunction induced by pressure overload in aged female mice. We further observed that GPR30 activation reduced the expressions of β-MHC and ANP in Fig. 4H-J, indicating the delay of progression of heart failure. This result was consistent with our previous study [7], showing a long-term beneficial role of GPR30 activation in pressure overload-induced heart failure for female hearts.

GPR30 activation reduced neutrophils NETs level in pressure overload-induced heart failure of aged female mice

Since the decrease of GPR30 protein level was accompanied with neutrophils NETs generation as mentioned in above data, whether estrogen receptor GPR30 activation could regulate NETs generation is worthy to be further clarified. Notably, the expression of GPR30 was greatly increased in myocardium of AG/TAC+ GPR30/G1 group and AG+ GPR30/G1 group in Fig. 6A and B. The expressions of H3Cit and MPO were significantly increased at 12 weeks following TAC surgery while GPR30/G1 treatment inhibited the H3Cit and MPO expressions but not PAD4 in Fig. 6A, C-E, which indicated that the reduction of NETs by GPR30 might not attribute to the direct inhibition of NETs production. Notably, PAD4 expression remained unchanged despite NETs reduction (Fig. 6C), indicating GPR30-mediated NETs clearance is dissociated from synthesis inhibition and operates via degradation pathways. The co-localizations of MPO/Ly6G and H3Cit/Ly6G were dramatically increased in AG/TAC group while these effects were alleviated following GPR30/G1 treatment shown in Fig. 6G-K. Furthermore, TGF-β1 expression in Fig. 6A and F were elevated following TAC surgery in aged female mice, which were decreased in AG/TAC+ GPR30/G1 group indicating the beneficial role of GPR30 in cardiac fibrosis. These results show that neutrophil NETs are involved in the pathogenesis of TAC-induced heart failure and fibrosis and GPR30/G1 reduces NETs level in myocardium of aged female mice.

Discussion

The novel finding of this study is that chronic GPR30 activation by G1 treatment combined with GPR30 overexpression inhibited myocardial fibrosis and preserved cardiac function in aged female mice subjected to pressure overload. This finding was consistent with previous studies and indicated that activation of GPR30 protected hearts from pathological stress [16–19]. Although it was shown that cardiac hypertrophy was attenuated via directly activation of GPR30 in cardiomyocytes [20], in present study our results further demonstrated that this protective effect was partly attributed to the enhancement of neutrophil NETs clearance via macrophage activation based on our in vivo and in vitro results. Identifying the GPR30/Trex1/neutrophil NETs pathway for pressure overload-induced heart failure (HF) in aged female mice reveals the pathological molecular mechanism and provides therapeutic targets for treating HF in aged postmenopausal women.

It is well recognized that the incidence of cardiovascular deaths increases sharply in postmenopausal women, especially during menopause transition [21]. Early menopause is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, implying the vital role of estrogen in the reduction of CVD risks and protection of cardiac function [22]. Although estrogen replacement therapy (ERT) displayed beneficial effects on prevention of cardiovascular diseases among postmenopausal women [2], the adverse side effects including the increased risk of cancers should be considered when ERT was initiated [23]. Thus, it is worthy to explore the molecular basis of cardiovascular diseases to develop the safe and effective treatments for aged postmenopausal women. In order to simulate the pathophysiological condition of postmenopausal women, the following animal model and treatment were employed in our present study. Firstly, TAC surgery in the 18-month-old female mice was performed to establish a model so as to fully mimic the condition of cardiovascular disease in postmenopausal women. Secondly, to fully activate GPR30 in the elderly female mice, we used adeno-associated virus to upregulate GPR30 expression and GPR30 agonist G1 to activate its activity.

Estrogen as the primary female sex hormone exerts its protective function in diverse cardiovascular diseases mainly through its three receptors including ERα, ERβ and GPR30 [24]. Among them, GPR30 is a novel membrane-bound receptor and mediates rapid non-genomic signaling response in cardiovascular system [25]. G1 is a potent agonist [26] and ample studies including our previous data clearly demonstrated that GPR30 activation by G1 mitigated myocardial injury and further improved heart function in myocardial infarction, diabetic cardiomyopathy and myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury in estrogen-deficiency female mice [19, 27–29]. In addition, in our previous study, G1 administration combined with GPR30 overexpression inhibited cardiac hypertrophy and ameliorated cardiac systolic function in aged female mice [7]. Mechanically, GPR30 suppressed ERK phosphorylation to reduce MMP-9 expression, thus inhibiting cardiac fibrosis. However, whether inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages participate in this pathological process of cardiovascular hypertrophy in aged female mice was still unknown.

Accumulating evidence demonstrated that inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages in myocardium play key roles in the pathogenesis of HF [30]. Notably, neutrophils as the most abundant cells of innate immune system can produce neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) to eliminate the pathogens such as bacteria [31, 32]. NETs comprises of the backbone of DNA fragments from decondensed chromatin coated with neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) which can resemble a net and release to the extracellular space [31]. The primary function of NETs was to entrap and kill the invading microbial pathogens including bacteria, fungi, and parasites. However, NETs production is also a key driver in the non-infection-related inflammation such as cardiovascular diseases [33]. In experimental atherosclerosis, numerous studies demonstrated that NETs participated in the whole stages of atherosclerosis progression. Oxidative stress and inflammasomes as the pathological stimuli could induce NETs to promote atherosclerosis [10, 34, 35]. Moreover, NETs were observed in rat myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (MI/R) model and DNase I could degrade NETs to alleviate MI/R injury [36]. Recently, KLF2 in neutrophils inhibited NETs generation to reduce thrombosis, which improved cardiac function in response to chronic angiotensin II infusion [37]. Very recently, it was shown that estrogen could regulate macrophage function to attenuate cardiac inflammation via GPR30 in response to hypobaric hypoxia [38]. In accordance with these studies, our results showed that NETs level in myocardium measured by H3Cit and MPO was gradually increased and reached the highest level at 12 weeks after TAC surgery while GPR30 expression was reduced following aging and even decreased after TAC in aged female mice. Immunofluorescent staining data further demonstrated the Ly6G positive cells and H3Cit/Ly6G double positive cells in cardiac tissues dramatically rose following TAC. Regarding cardiac function, LVEF and LVFS as well as cardiac fibrosis were slightly reduced and greatly worsened following TAC in aged female mice. The above data clearly showed that impairment of cardiac function was accompanied with the increased NETs level and reduction of GPR30 expression in aged female hearts.

Notably, we noticed that GPR30 expression declines with aging and in response to pressure overload. However, the upstream regulator of GPR30 expression is still lacking. Since aging and pressure overload are associated with increased oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, how these pathological factors result in the decrease of GPR30 expression is an interesting question. Due to the markedly reduction of estrogen level and GPR30 level in aged female mice, we thus assessed the effects of GPR30 activation in TAC-induced heart failure of aged female mice. Immunofluorescent staining results showed that GPR30 activation reduced NETs level in TAC-induced heart failure. Cardiac function was also preserved and cardiac hypertrophy was inhibited following GPR30 activation. Interestingly, although PAD4 was a key enzyme for NETs generation [39], there was no alteration after GPR30 activation, which may indicate that NETs reduction may be caused by its degradation regulation by GPR30. Previous study demonstrate that macrophage is vital for NETs degradation [40] and GPR30 activation regulates macrophage phenotype switch [41]. Therefore, we investigated the role of G1 in in vitro co-culture of primary neutrophils and macrophage. As expected, NETs were reduced while Trex1 expression was increased following G1 treatment. To further verify this result, Trex1 was overexpressed in RAW264.7 macrophage when co-culture of neutrophils and macrophage. Consistently, NETs were reduced when overexpression of Trex1 in RAW264.7 macrophage. These data imply that GPR30 activation increased Trex1 expression to degrade NTEs in the failing hearts of aged female mice.

While our study provides novel insights into the protective role of GPR30 activation in aged female heart failure models, there are some limitations in the present study. Although the use of aged female mice subjected to TAC surgery effectively mimics certain aspects of postmenopausal heart failure, species-specific differences in immune responses, hormonal regulation, and cardiac physiology may limit the direct translatability of these findings to humans. Thus, human validation is critical to assess GPR30 expression, Trex1 activity, and NETs accumulation in cardiac tissue or peripheral blood from postmenopausal women with heart failure, ideally stratified by disease stage and estrogen status. Secondly, while G1 is a widely used and selective GPR30 agonist in preclinical models, potential off-target effects cannot be entirely ruled out, particularly in chronic dosing regimens. Furthermore, while our DNase I intervention studies provide strong causal evidence for the role of NETs in cardiac dysfunction, the absence of direct correlation analysis between NET burden and cardiac function parameters within the same animals as a limitation. The correlating individual NET levels with echocardiographic indices would have further strengthened the translational relevance of our findings. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate this relationship and establish NETs as a potential biomarker.

The beneficial effects of DNase I, an enzyme that degrades extracellular DNA, provide strong construct validity for the pathogenic role of extracellular chromatin in our model. It is important to note that DNase I can target chromatin from various sources, including but not limited to NETs. Most importantly, while our data point towards a critical role for Trex1 in macrophages, the in vivo experiments were not performed with a macrophage-specific conditional knockout model. Therefore, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that Trex1 expression in other cell types contributes to the observed phenotypes. Future studies employing neutrophil-specific genetic models to attenuate NETosis including Padi4−/− mice or macrophage-specific Trex1-deficient mice will be invaluable to conclusively establish causality and rule out potential contributions from other forms of extracellular chromatin to fully validate the mechanistic model proposed in our experiments.

In summary, in aged female hearts, our results clearly illustrate that GPR30 activation mitigated pressure overload-induced cardiac fibrosis and improved cardiac function in aged female mice. Furthermore, our in vivo and in vitro data showed that NETs was increased in pathological hypertrophied myocardium and G1 administration could increase Trex1 expression and further improve NETs clearance. To advance these findings toward clinical translation, we propose a defined translational roadmap: First, validation in human samples through quantification of plasma NETs biomarkers (e.g., cf-DNA, MPO-DNA complexes, and H3Cit) and myocardial TREX1 expression in postmenopausal women with heart failure. Second, comprehensive preclinical development of GPR30-targeted therapeutics, including the evaluation of biased GPR30 agonists with improved cardiac selectivity and favorable safety profiles, alongside assessment of Trex1 activators as potential adjunct therapies. As a selective GPR30 agonist, G1 offers a potential safer alternative to conventional hormone replacement therapy by activating beneficial non-genomic estrogen signaling pathways without the proliferative effects on breast and endometrial tissues associated with classical ER activation. Finally, well-designed early-phase clinical trials to establish the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and preliminary efficacy of GPR30 modulation in postmenopausal populations. These strategic steps will critically determine the potential of GPR30 agonism as a novel therapeutic approach for precision cardiology in aging women, addressing a significant unmet clinical need in cardiovascular medicine.
