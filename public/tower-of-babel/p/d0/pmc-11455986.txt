Comprehensive analysis of PFAS presence from environment to plate
Authors: Maria-Eleni Dimitrakopoulou, Manos Karvounis, George Marinos, Zacharoula Theodorakopoulou, Eleni Aloizou, George Petsangourakis, Mihalis Papakonstantinou, Giannis Stoitsis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) pose an emerging environmental risk impacting food products and ecosystems. This study analyzes over 150,000 entries from food safety authorities and scientific publications from 2017 onwards. Our findings show that fish & seafood, and biota have the highest PFAS concentrations due to environmental contamination and bioaccumulation. Surface water samples also frequently contain PFAS, raising concerns about long-term ecological and human health effects. Comprehensive strategies are essential to mitigate these risks.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Climate-change ecology

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) constitute a group of synthetic chemicals that find extensive application in both industrial and consumer products. They are commonly used in firefighting foam, non-stick cookware, waterproof fabrics, and food packaging, among others. One of the concerning characteristics of PFAS is their persistence and bioaccumulative nature. These chemicals do not readily degrade in the environment, leading to their long-lasting presence, and they can accumulate in the food chain. The persistence of PFAS means that they do not break down easily over time, even in the face of environmental degradation processes. As a result, PFAS can persist in soil, water, and air for extended periods, leading to widespread environmental contamination. The bioaccumulative nature of PFAS raises concerns about potential health risks for both humans and wildlife. Studies have linked exposure to certain PFAS compounds with adverse health effects, including reproductive issues, developmental problems, liver and kidney damage, and an increased risk of certain cancers1–5.

The available information on PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances) health impacts is currently incomplete and fragmented, leading to difficulties in prioritizing which PFAS should be of greater environmental and health concern. There is an ongoing debate about whether to assess PFAS as a collective group from a health perspective or to evaluate individual PFAS based on their specific potential health impacts. PFAS can be categorized into two primary families: polymer and non-polymer PFAS5. Health concerns have predominantly been associated with non-polymer PFAS, which includes perfluorinated carboxylic acids (PFCAs) and perfluorinated sulfonic acids (PFSAs)6.

The determination of PFAS in food products and the environment involves a series of analytical techniques, including sampling, extraction, and analysis. Conventional methods, such as LC-MS/MS, are effective in PFAS analysis but are often expensive and not widely accessible. On the other hand, emerging low-cost methods offer the advantage of affordability but lack sensitivity compared to conventional techniques, sometimes falling short of meeting regulatory detection limits. The development of low-cost sensors has the potential to be a significant change in PFAS analysis, as they can aid in identifying and prioritizing samples for further investigation and swift intervention7.

Given the widespread occurrence of PFAS in various environmental media and their presence in a wide array of products, it is essential to address this issue comprehensively. Continuous monitoring, research, and regulatory efforts are necessary to minimize human exposure to PFAS and mitigate their potential health and environmental impact. Therefore, the objective of this review is to compile existing data on the occurrence of PFAS, including measured max and mean values, geographical origin, sampling strategies, contamination sources, and recovery values of the applied analytical methods. By gathering this comprehensive information, we aim to gain insights into the prevalence and distribution of PFAS compounds in various regions. This review aims to enhance our understanding of the worldwide prevalence of PFAS and their potential sources of contamination, explore the presence of PFAS compounds in food and environmental samples, and gain insights into the processes of bioaccumulation from the environment to the food chain through an in-depth analysis of publicly available data from public authorities and peer-reviewed literature.

Literature search

Search criteria and search strings

The search protocol used for identification of the relevant scientific publication and reports in Pubmed and ScienceDirect on PFAS in food, limited to the papers published in the last 5 years, contained the following strings:

(“polyfluoroalkyl” OR “PFAS” OR “perfluoroalkyl”) AND “sample” AND (“food” OR “feed” OR “supplements” OR “drinking water”).

On the other hand, the search protocol used for identification of the relevant scientific publication on PFAS in environmental sources, limited to the studies published in the last 5 years, was:

(“polyfluoroalkyl” OR “PFAS” OR “perfluoroalkyl”) AND “sample” AND (“soil” OR “water” OR “aquatic environment” OR “land”) AND (“concentration” OR “occurrence” OR “exposure”) NOT (“thyroid”[Title]) NOT (“allergy”[Title]) NOT (“cancer”[Title]) NOT (“biomarker”[Title]) NOT (“clinical”[Title/Abstract]) NOT (“breast milk”[Title]).

Publications that do not include data in any format (table, supplementary material, links, data sources references) were not considered as relevant for this study. Publications that include a meta-analysis of other studies were not considered as relevant, but they were used to identify references to data sources. Search process

The process of selecting studies for inclusion in the review was rigorously evaluated by three independent reviewers (M.P., M.K., and M.D.). Mendeley was instrumental in identifying and eliminating duplicate publications, ensuring that each article was considered only once. The initial screening involved assessing titles and abstracts, guided by predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles deemed potentially relevant progressed to the subsequent stage for more comprehensive evaluation. In this phase, a second round of selection took place based on a thorough examination of the full text of the publications included. Each author independently reviewed the potentially relevant studies, adhering to predefined eligibility criteria, to determine the final set of studies to be incorporated into the review.

Data collection process

The information extracted from the selected full-text articles and relevant databases for inclusion in the study covered various key parameters. These included details on the analytical method employed, the percent recovery, the sampling strategy, the samples’ geographical origin, the matrix under examination, the maximum resulting concentration of PFAS, the mean value of PFAS, and the identified source of contamination.

Data for PFAS occurrence in foods have been extracted from the existing data platform of Agroknow, literature review, and external data sources. Data from external data sources were identified for PFAS and extracted datasets were selected as the ones that fit best with this study’s objectives. Collected data covers high-level food categories, environmental media, 125 PFAS, and a time of more than 20 years from 2000 to 2021.

The most prevalent PFAS compounds found in environmental samples include PFOS, PFOA, PFHxs, PFUnA, PFDA, PFHpA, PFNA, PFPea, PFHxA, and PFTrDA. Out of these, six belong to the category of long-chain PFAS, while four fall into the short-chain category. Table 7. provides comprehensive information on PFAS compounds detected above the LOQ within the two categories exhibiting the highest number of records (surface water) or the highest concentration (biota). The highest number of records >LOQ observed in the category of surface waters is primarily attributed to the PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS compounds. One significant source can be industrial discharges, as certain industries have historically released PFOS, PFOA, and other PFAS chemicals into the environment as part of their manufacturing processes. Another notable source is aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF), which contains PFOS and can contribute to soil contamination when used during firefighting training exercises or in emergency response situations. Additionally, the disposal of products containing PFOS and PFOA in landfills poses a risk, as these chemicals can leach into the surrounding soil over time. Lastly, PFOS and PFOA can be transported through the air and deposited onto soil surfaces through atmospheric deposition.

Geographical distribution of PFAS in food products and environment

Table 9 provides information about the geographical origin of the total samples analyzed. It is important to note that this data is not representative of the contamination level in each country, but rather reflects the monitoring programs for PFAS in those regions. Records from Australia have been excluded due to Australia’s extensive mapping efforts on PFAS contamination, accessible at the following link: https://pfas.australianmap.net/. Increased awareness and monitoring efforts in France and in Italy might contribute to a higher number of reported samples compared to other countries. The high presence of PFAS in the United States, compared to some other countries, can be attributed to a combination of historical factors and proactive monitoring efforts. The US has a legacy of substantial PFAS usage across various industries and manufacturing facilities, dating back decades. Moreover, the use of PFAS-containing firefighting foams at military installations has been a notable source of contamination. A robust regulatory framework and an extensive system for monitoring environmental contaminants have led to increased awareness and testing in the United States, potentially contributing to higher detection rates. Nevertheless, it is essential to recognize that PFAS contamination is a global concern, and many nations worldwide are grappling with similar challenges that require coordinated efforts to address effectively. Italy’s National Research Council (CNR) initiated comprehensive monitoring of PFAS presence within the country in 2013. This proactive approach likely contributes to the robust detection of PFAS contamination in Italy. Norway, despite its renowned pristine landscapes, is not exempt from the presence of PFAS compounds. These contaminants find their way into the environment through various sources, including industrial activities, firefighting foams, and the use of PFAS-containing products. Elevated PFAS levels have been detected in some drinking water supplies, prompting regulatory measures to limit exposure. Norwegian authorities are actively engaged in monitoring, research, and remediation efforts to comprehend the extent and impact of PFAS contamination in soil, water, and wildlife46–48. Germany’s rigorous approach to monitoring PFAS contamination in food is driven by its status as the largest food retail sector in the European Union49–51. PFAS can enter the food chain from a variety of sources, including contaminated soil, water, and packaging materials. As a response, German authorities conduct systematic testing and monitoring of a wide range of food items, including fish, meat, dairy, and vegetables, to assess PFAS levels and potential health risks to consumers. Germany adheres to European Union (EU) regulations, which establish maximum residue levels (MRLs) for PFAS in certain food categories, ensuring that food products available in the market comply with established safety standards. Additionally, attention is given to sites with historical PFAS contamination, as they can represent potential sources of PFAS in the food chain, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive monitoring and safety measures to safeguard food quality and public health.

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) represent a group of water-soluble chemical compounds widely employed for various applications over the past six decades. Notably, perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) are among the most extensively studied within this group52–55. Human exposure to PFAS primarily occurs through dietary intake, although in specific instances, drinking water can also be a significant source, along with minor contributions from dust and indoor air exposure56. These chemicals have attracted significant attention due to their widespread use across various industrial and consumer applications, raising concerns about their environmental persistence and potential health risks. One of the key areas of focus has been on understanding the environmental behavior of PFAS, particularly their persistence and tendency to biomagnify in food chains. PFAS have been found to resist degradation processes, leading to their accumulation in ecosystems over time. Thus, the collective efforts of researchers worldwide have resulted in a substantial body of literature on PFAS, covering a wide range of topics, including their environmental fate, exposure pathways, toxicity mechanisms, and potential mitigation measures57–59. While a majority of the available data originates from studies conducted in the European Union, the United States, Australia, and China, there are still considerable information gaps for many regions worldwide60.

Analyzing the geographical distribution of PFAS from existing available data, an interesting pattern emerges. The results demonstrate that within the European Union, France, Italy, Germany, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway exhibit a significant number of samples exceeding the LOQ for PFAS contamination. This observation is consistent with prior studies examining contamination sites across Europe and within scientific literature61,62. This geographic disparity offers intriguing insights into the sources and pathways of PFAS contamination. However, a monitoring campaign for chemical contaminants was conducted in France in 2021, while other countries, such as Sweden, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and Norway, have established National Monitoring Programs for chemical contaminants. Therefore, the number of records exceeding the LOQ in these specific countries can be attributed to these programs. This underscores the presence of PFAS in both the environment and food, highlighting the necessity for all European countries to implement a comprehensive approach to address this issue16–64. The presence of PFAS in the United States, when compared to certain other nations, can be attributed to a combination of historical factors and proactive monitoring endeavors. The United States has a historical legacy of extensive PFAS usage across a range of industries and manufacturing facilities, for many decades. Additionally, the utilization of PFAS-containing firefighting foams at military installations has significantly contributed to environmental contamination65. Moreover, in more industrialized nations, fluorochemical production plants (FPPs) have played a significant role in the direct discharge of long-chain legacy PFAS into surface water and the atmosphere66–68. Notably, the United States’ commitment to comprehensive monitoring and research initiatives has fostered a deeper understanding of PFAS contamination, shedding light on the scope of their presence in various environmental and food matrices. The United States (through the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey or NHANES), Canada (via the Canadian Health Measures Survey), Germany (with the Human Biomonitoring Commission of the Federal Environmental Agency), and France (under the French Ministries of Health and Environment), have instituted national biomonitoring programs69. Thus, besides environmental pollution, also EU’s proactive approach to investigation and monitoring plays a significant role in detecting and addressing PFAS contamination in food49–62,64–75.

The presence of PFAS in surface water and drinking water samples analyzed, underscores the potential contamination of water sources, emphasizing the pervasiveness of these compounds in the environment76,77. However, it’s notable that PFAS were typically found at low concentrations in these samples. This indicates that while contamination is prevalent, the levels detected are generally minimal. Despite their low concentrations, the presence of PFAS in numerous samples remains concerning due to their enduring presence in the environment, prompting worries about potential long-term effects on ecosystems and human health. Additionally, the high mean values of PFAS detected in food samples indicate a significant concern within this study. PFAS compounds, which have entered water sources, tend to accumulate in the tissues of organisms over time, creating a reservoir of these chemicals. As organisms consume contaminated water and prey on one another, PFAS concentrations increase up the food chain. Consequently, top-level consumers, such as fish and seafood, exhibit much higher PFAS concentrations in their tissues than in the surrounding environment. Apart from the biomagnification of existing PFAS compounds, there were also indications of precursor substances accumulating in the food chain, indicating the possibility of their transformation into more persistent PFAS compounds in higher trophic levels78,79.

Therefore, the detection of the highest maximum concentrations in Fish & seafood and biota categories demonstrates the bioaccumulation potential, pointing out the need for vigilance in monitoring PFAS in the food chain80,81. Thus, the high PFAS levels in the Fish & seafood category indicate widespread contamination in specific areas of the aquatic environment, originating from industrial discharges, urban runoff, or atmospheric deposition. In contrast, ticks are ectoparasites that feed on the blood of various host animals, including mammals and birds. According to literature, PFAS compounds have permeated the terrestrial food web, with ticks acting as bioindicators82,83. Elevated PFAS levels in biota are indicative of widespread environmental contamination, which can have cascading effects on wildlife health, ecosystem dynamics, and even potential risks to humans. Besides, our findings align with other studies, collectively highlighting the widespread presence and concerning levels of PFAS contamination across various environmental and food matrices, underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive mitigation strategies84–89. Moreover, it emphasizes the crucial role of establishing stringent regulations by regulatory bodies to curb PFAS contamination and safeguard public health and the environment.

In general, PFAS exhibits remarkable stability because all the hydrogen atoms in their carbon–fluorine (C–F) bonds are replaced by fluorine atoms, enhancing their overall molecular energy. The unique structure of PFAS imparts them with hydrophobic and lipophobic characteristics, as well as exceptional chemical and thermal stability, which collectively render them resistant to hydrolysis, photolysis, metabolism, and biodegradation90. Our study revealed the prevalence of PFOS and PFOA in most environmental samples, while PFHxS were found in surface water samples. Notably, these compounds were also detected in fish and seafood products, suggesting their transmission to aquatic life through contaminated surface water91–93.

Moreover, the significant detection of PFAS in drinking water samples indicates potential routes of exposure, with PFAS potentially making its way into poultry and meat products through drinking water or even into crops via irrigation practices. PFOS, PFOA, and PFOSA contain eight carbon atoms, which contribute to their enhanced stability, and this can enlighten their frequent detection in seafood, meat, and poultry products94. Additionally, in marine environments, research has shown that organisms at higher trophic levels exhibit a significantly greater ability to convert PFOSA into PFOS, as evidenced by the proportion of these compounds in their PFAS profiles and body burdens78.

Analyzing the data, it becomes clear that most PFAS compounds detected in food samples mostly belong to the long-chain PFAS category. In contrast, the environmental samples contain both long-chain and short-chain PFAS. Short-chain PFAS with hydrophilic properties exerts more significant effects on surface waters, while long-chain PFAS with hydrophobic characteristics can accumulate in sediments, posing risks to aquatic organisms95. This disparity indicates a notable difference in the distribution and prevalence of PFAS compounds in these two types of matrixes. The presence of long-chain PFAS in food samples is significant as they tend to bioaccumulate in living organisms. This is especially concerning as long-chain PFAS are typically associated with a higher potential for health and environmental risks due to their persistence and bioaccumulative properties. In environmental samples, the detection of both long-chain and short-chain PFAS compounds indicates that various sources may contribute to environmental contamination. This reflects the diverse applications and sources of PFAS in the environment, ranging from historical uses to ongoing emissions. The presence of short-chain PFAS in the environment is noteworthy as they are often considered less bioaccumulative and less persistent, but they are still of concern due to their potential to leach into water sources and impact aquatic ecosystems.

Addressing these concerning findings necessitates a multifaceted approach. First and foremost, there is a critical need to identify and investigate potential sources of PFAS contamination in marine and terrestrial ecosystems and the environment, with the aim of preventing further pollution96,97. Additionally, comprehensive health risk assessments are imperative to evaluate the potential health implications for consumers of contaminated foodstuff98,99. Furthermore, it is essential to assess the ecological impact of PFAS contamination on these ecosystems, considering potential effects on biodiversity and food chains. In parallel, regulatory measures should be contemplated, including the establishment or revision of standards for PFAS levels in food products, to safeguard both the environment and human health. Finally, the development and implementation of mitigation strategies, remediation efforts, and enhanced waste management practices are essential steps in reducing PFAS contamination in affected ecosystems.

In conclusion, given the interconnected nature of environmental challenges, understanding how factors such as PFAS presence intersect with climate change is vital for safeguarding public health and ecosystems. This necessitates a holistic approach involving research, policy, and action to mitigate their combined implications. Moreover, ensuring the availability of public data for laboratory testing of PFAS in environmental and food samples, as well as promoting data sharing, is essential for fostering transparency, collaboration, and informed decision-making in addressing PFAS contamination and its impacts on climate and public health.

The distribution of PFAS in food and the environment, as explored in our study, is subject to notable limitations that warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, while our data sources, drawn from publications and reports, provide a diverse array of information, they inherently carry biases and may overlook certain regions and food categories. Notably, our analysis revealed significant data gaps in regions like Africa and South America, limiting the broader applicability of our findings. Additionally, temporal coverage presents a challenge, with sparse data available from recent years and a scarcity of historical records prior to 2005, impacting both the comprehensiveness and temporal relevance of our analysis. Furthermore, the high contamination prevalence observed in our sample set may be influenced by targeted sampling efforts in areas known for elevated contamination risks, potentially skewing the overall distribution patterns. Methodologically, while we prioritized the inclusion of available public data and categorized samples based on their utility, it’s crucial to note that concentrations below the limit of detection in certain categories do not necessarily imply the absence of contamination but rather reflect limitations in data sources. Consequently, our study should be viewed as an in-depth exploration of publicly available data rather than an authoritative account of PFAS distribution and impact. Moving forward, addressing these limitations through refined methodologies is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of PFAS distribution dynamics and informed decision-making in environmental and food safety policies.
