Conventional and hybrid time series models for forecasting medication dispensing and errors integration in automated dispensing cabinets
Authors: Abbas Al Mutair, Kawther Taleb, Mrs. Kawthar Alsaleh, Chandni Saha, Batool Mohammed Alhassan, Mohamed Alsalim, Horia Alduriahem, Muhammad Daniyal, Zainab Almoosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Automated dispensing cabinets (ADCs) represent a critical innovation in modern healthcare, revolutionizing medication management by improving efficiency, accuracy, and security. With the increasing reliance on these technologies, optimizing their performance is paramount. This study aims to apply conventional, hybrid time series, and machine learning models to forecast three key performance indicators of ADCs: items dispensation, override occurrences, and error integration. Using monthly data from the MICU at Almoosa Hospital between January 2023 and December 2024, we employed both traditional linear time series models (e.g., autoregressive models, simple exponential smoothing, autoregressive moving average, and theta models) and advanced non-linear machine learning models (e.g., NPAR, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)) in various hybrid configurations (ARIMA-ANN, EMS-ANN, NPAR-ANN). Model accuracy was assessed using key metrics such as RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and RMSLE, with a bootstrap 95% CI to ensure the best performance for predicting future trends. The study demonstrated that the NPAR-ANN, a hybrid model combining nonparametric ARIMA and artificial neural networks, showed superior performance. The model demonstrated the best performance, achieving the lowest RMSE values. Specifically, for the number of items issued, it attained an RMSE of 71.50, for overrides an RMSE of 15.43, while for error integration, and an RMSE of 20.92 lowest among all competing models. This novel study modeled key parameters of ADCs, providing data-driven insights that can inform hospital decision-making and optimize medication management. The study showcased the application of hybrid machine learning models in forecasting critical ADC parameters, offering valuable data-driven insights for hospital administrators.

Introduction

The concept of the closed-loop electronic medication management system, automated dispensing cabinets (ADCs) are devices that control medication storage, management, dispensing, and delivery that may integrate human operations with electronic information and allow tracking of every medication-related issue throughout the medication process. Conventionally, process of delivery of medication in a hospital involves a multistep process that includes prescription, transcription and documentation, dispensing, administering, and monitoring. Through these steps, the medication moves from the pharmacy to the patient, requiring careful management at every stage. The recognition of technology and automation in pharmacy practice has become increasingly common to enhance patient well-being, improve workflow efficiency, and reduce healthcare costs1. In hospital settings, the medication process typically consists of three main phases: assembling, dispensing, and administration. Among various automation tools, ADCs have seen significant improvements2. An ADC, also known as a unit-based cabinet or automated dispensing device, is a computerized system used in healthcare environments, primarily hospitals, to store, dispense, and monitor medications and supplies. It functions as a secure, decentralized system for medication management, boosting efficiency and safety at the point of care.

Literature review

A study conducted by the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists found that 97% of hospitals in the US now use ADCs3. Similarly, in the UK, the adoption of pharmacy automation and ADC usage has grown rapidly over the past 15 years, driven by an audit commission report that recommended automation to reduce medication dispensing errors4–6. ADCs feature secure storage compartments, each dedicated to a specific medication, and nurses who interact with these systems daily play a crucial role in ensuring medication safety7. Despite their potential benefits, ADCs face challenges such as staff resistance and noncompliance with new workflows, which can limit their effectiveness. Healthcare professionals’ attitudes toward technology are key in influencing their acceptance and successful use of these systems8. Over the last decade, many hospitals have adopted computerized provider order entry systems for prescribing support and barcode medication administration systems for patient and medication documentation to minimize medication errors9. The safety benefits of ADCs have been documented in several studies, showing a reduction in medication errors in ICUs and emergency departments10–13. One study used a business-intelligence dashboard linked to ADCs across 11 cabinets, employing a pre-post implementation design to demonstrate significant reductions14. Another study modeled ADC implementation in surgical units (Taichung VGH), reporting dramatic decreases in medication delivery times and dispensing errors, along with improved nurse satisfaction15. Literature also includes studies measuring the impact of ADCs on hospitalization outcomes. For example, different studies demonstrated significant reductions in prescription, dispensing, and administration errors after introducing the ADC system16–19. A systematic review by16 presented findings suggesting that ADCs positively impacted the rate of omitted or delayed doses.

Research gap

A key aspect of implementing ADCs involves monitoring the number of items dispensed, override frequency, and integration errors. These factors can affect the efficiency and safety of medication dispensing. ADCs are used to enhance medication safety, improve drug distribution, reduce medication errors, provide 24-h exchange services, increase control over narcotics and other drugs, and track and proactively monitor the drug use process. Despite the widespread adoption of ADCs in healthcare, especially in the US and UK, limited research has examined how real-time data analytics can predict medication dispensing patterns and related errors. Most studies like16–19 focused on ADC adoption, staff attitudes, and safety improvements, but neglected predictive modeling approaches. There is a significant gap in applying modeling techniques to forecast dispensing volumes and identify potential integration errors. This predictive ability could support proactive decision-making, optimize resource use, and enhance patient safety. Filling this gap is essential for advancing the efficiency and reliability of ADCs in clinical practice.

Motivation of the study

Driven by the lack of predictive analytics in current ADC workflows, this study introduces the use of traditional and hybrid time series models to predict medication dispensing patterns and errors in integration. To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop an approach specifically aimed at modeling and forecasting ADC performance in hospital environments.

Novelty of the work

For modelling and predicting of the data collected on different time intervals, time series modeling is a commonly used predictive method. While models such as ARIMA are effective for linear trends, and artificial neural networks (ANNs) handle nonlinear patterns, hybrid models that combine both approaches provide improved predictive performance. There are different statistical and time series methods in the literature that have been extensively used to model time series data. Linear models, such as the ARIMA20, analyze a linear function between past data and past errors. As an alternative to ARIMA, if the data exhibits non-linear patterns, as is mostly found in error integration and overrides, ANNs can be considered for time series forecasting due to their flexibility in dealing with nonlinear patterns21. Presently, hybrid models have been proposed in the literature to improve the quality of forecasts, covering the combinations of linear and nonlinear models22–24. Studies have shown that health-related administrative data, particularly in medication dispensing systems, often exhibit such dual behavior due to workflow variability, human factors, and system design21.

This study will model and analyze the key factors impacting ADCs, including the number of items dispensed, override occurrences, and integration errors, using time series analysis. By applying both linear (ARIMA) and nonlinear (ANN) models, along with hybrid approaches, this study aims to capture the dual nature of patterns observed in item issues, overrides, and integration errors. These predictive models will be employed not only to forecast future trends but also to help in decision-making in hospital settings.

Methods

The dataset used in this study was collected monthly from January 2023 to December 2024 at the Almoosa Specialist Hospital, Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. It is the time series data of the ADC, and it does not involve any patient data. Almoosa Specialist Hospital (ASH) is a leading provider of healthcare services, which has a capacity of 460 beds and is the first and largest private healthcare institution in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. The integration of ADCs in the MICU (Medical Intensive Care Unit) within ASH was recently introduced as part of an initiative to enhance medication management efficiency and patient safety.

This study utilized retrospective time series data extracted from the Almoosa Specialist Hospital’s ADC system, Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. Institutional approval for accessing and analyzing the data was granted by the Almoosa Specialist Hospital Ethical Review Board with the approval number (log number ARC-23.02.03), and the study involved no experiment on humans, human participation and/or the use of human tissue samples. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, as approved by the ethics committee. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Statistical analysis

The analysis process of time series involved several stages for forecasting and modeling ADC data. Firstly, there was no missing data in the study. The data was analyzed by descriptive analysis to detect patterns and important features. Visualization was performed to see the pattern of time for various linear and non-linear time series models. The seasonality of the series was checked by seasonality ANOVA by F-test and cointegration was performed by the Johansen cointegration test. The hyperparameters were tuned for each model as follows: ARIMA parameters (p = 1, d = 1, q = 1) were selected based on AIC and stationarity diagnostics. ETS was tuned with an additive error and no trend or seasonality and a smoothing parameter α = 0.9999. For ANN, the network was optimized with one hidden layer containing 5 neurons, ReLU activation in the hidden layer, linear activation in the output layer a learning rate of 0.01, and 500 training epochs. The NPAR model was configured with a lag order of 3, using a Gaussian kernel for smoothing. The analysis and visualization was performed in R-Studio. The results were declared significant if p < 0.05.

Forecasting models for ADC data

The selection of both conventional linear models (ARIMA, ESM, Theta) and machine learning models, alongside their hybrid combinations (ARIMA-ANN, ESM-ANN, NPAR-ANN), was driven by the dual-nature characteristics of ADC data. ADC metrics, such as items dispensed, overrides, and integration errors, exhibited both linear and non-linear trends.

The selection of these models is based on the nature of the ADC data. Conventional linear models like ARIMA excel at capturing systematic patterns, while ANN and NPAR are well-suited to modeling nonlinear dynamics. Hybrid models bridge this gap by decomposing the forecasting problem: linear components (such as baseline trends) are handled by traditional methods (ARIMA, ESM, NPAR), while the residuals, which represent complex and unexplained variations, are modeled by ANN. Accordingly, our study incorporated linear, nonlinear, and hybrid models, each known for effectively capturing different structural patterns in time series data, to comprehensively analyze the behavior of the series.

The theta model

The Theta Model is a forecasting method that predicts future values based on the average change in the time series data. The theta model is known for its strong performance with relatively short time series. It involves calculating the average change between consecutive time points and extrapolating it into the future28. It typically uses 2 theta lines as its main parameter, which controls the decomposition and forecasting process. Hybridization of linear and machine learning time series models

Hybrid time series models combine the strengths of both linear models (like ARIMA) and nonlinear models (such as artificial neural networks), enabling them to capture complex patterns. While ARIMA excels at modeling trend and seasonality, neural networks handle irregularities and nonlinearity often present in ADC integration errors. By integrating these approaches, hybrid models offer superior forecasting accuracy and robustness compared to single-method models, making them particularly well-suited for predicting medication dispensing trends and operational anomalies in hospital ADC systems. A detailed systematic review by29 concluded that all investigated hybrid forecasting methods performed better than individual ones while modelling errors, particularly and other series in general.

Three hybrid models have been applied to model ADC data. The first one is the hybridization of the ARIMA and ANN models. The ARIMA-ANN model combines the ARIMA and ANN to increase time series forecasting quality by capturing both linear and non-linear trends of data30,31. ARIMA is effective in catching the linear aspects like trends and seasonality, while ANN considers the modeling of complex, non-linear relationships. In this hybrid approach, ARIMA is first applied to model the linear components of the series, and then ANN is used to capture any non-linear residuals or patterns that ARIMA may not evaluate. The final forecast is a combination of the outputs from both models, which allows for more accurate predictions by addressing both linear and non-linear components of the data. The series was combined by using the hybrid approach32, which combines and applies both the linear and nonlinear models in separate structures for modeling linear and nonlinear components.

The NPAR-ANN model first applies NPAR to smooth the data and capture its underlying structure, and then ANN is applied to model the residual for non-linearity. The combination of these techniques enhances the overall forecast accuracy by capturing both smooth and complex features in the data.

The ESM-ANN hybrid model is the combination of ESM with ANN to address time series forecasting with both trend and seasonality. In this hybrid approach, ESM was used to first model the linear components of the time series, and then ANN was applied to the residuals to account for any non-linear patterns that ESM might not capture18,20.

Results

The ADF test results showed that all three series were non-stationary in their levels and required differencing to become stationary. The number of items issued and integration errors became stationary after second-order differencing (ADF statistics: − 4.13 and − 3.90 with p-values 0.01 and 0.02, respectively), while overrides required third-order differencing (ADF statistic: − 4.17, p = 0.01). To address concerns of potential over-differencing, which can remove important structural information, the Johansen cointegration test was applied to check for long-run equilibrium relationships among the variables. The cointegration test using the trace statistic indicated no statistically significant cointegrating vectors at the 5% significance level; the null hypothesis of no cointegration (r = 0) was not rejected (test statistic = 21.43, critical value = 31.52). Model accuracy measures with 95% bootstrap CI

Bootstrap is a non-parametric statistical technique that involves repeatedly sampling with replacement from the observed validation dataset to create multiple bootstrap samples. For each bootstrap sample, the forecasting models was applied separately to the sampled validation sets, and their error metrics were recalculated. This process was repeated thousands of times to generate an empirical distribution of each error metric for each model. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these distributions are then used to derive the lower and upper bounds of the 95% CI, respectively. Performing bootstrapping in this manner allowed for a more robust and statistically valid comparison across models, providing stronger evidence to support claims that one model outperformed others.

Each model’s performance was compared using these measures to determine the most accurate forecasting method. The NPAR-ANN model showed the best performance across all series, with the lowest RMSE values. For the number of items issued, it achieved an RMSE of 71.50, followed by the LOESS model with an RMSE of 502.75, while the ARIMA model had a much higher RMSE of 2247.52. In the overrides series, NPAR-ANN again had the lowest RMSE at 15.43, with Holt-Winters ESM as the second-best at 25.27, compared to ARIMA’s RMSE of 38.32. The study results showed that among the eight models tested, NPAR-ANN consistently outperformed others across all three outcomes, achieving the lowest accuracy measures. LOESS (NPAR) and within the single models, ESM performed well across all three series. However, it did not surpass NPAR-ANN in accuracy (Table 3). The relatively narrow and consistent CIs, especially for top models like NPAR-ANN and ARIMA-ANN, indicated stable and precise estimates. Time series cross-validation

Rolling-origin cross-validation was used to assess the forecasting performance of models. This approach maintained the temporal order by sequentially expanding the training window, starting with an initial size of 15 observations from the 24-point series. While models like ARIMA and ANN handled this setup well. With only 24 data points, traditional rolling cross-validation was applied to NPAR-ANN by using a larger training set (90%) and a smaller test set to facilitate model fitting and evaluation. The cross-validation results indicated that the NPAR-ANN model performed well across all three series. For Number of Items Issued, NPAR-ANN achieved the lowest RMSE (59.30, in Overrides, NPAR-ANN also recorded the smallest errors (RMSE 12.08, MAE 8.58, MAPE 8.05%) and for Integration Errors, NPAR-ANN maintained superior accuracy (RMSE 15.23, MAE 10.27, MAPE 25.36%), outperforming all models. This model effectively combined nonparametric regression with neural networks to capture complex nonlinear patterns, resulting in notably improved forecast accuracy (Fig. 7). Discussion

The patient safety benefits of ADCs have been reported in several previous studies, including a reduction in medication errors in intensive care units (ICUs) and emergency departments2,10. However, the benefits of ADCs still need to be evaluated, given the different models19. This study introduced a novel hybrid time series model, the NPAR-ANN, and compared it with eight other models using accuracy-based metrics. The ADC data exhibited both linear and nonlinear fluctuations, necessitating various modeling strategies. This study provided a methodological approach to assess ADC parameters.

The results showed that the NPAR-ANN model outperformed all other models, and it can handle the complex features of ADC data, supporting existing research that indicates hybrid models perform better in forecasting real-world, volatile systems29. The strength of the NPAR-ANN lies in its dual architecture. It effectively modeled linear trends and adapted to structural changes without relying on strict assumptions about stationarity or linearity33. Unlike ARIMA, which risks model misspecification in non-stationary data, NPAR adaptively models trends in such data25,27. Additionally, the ANN component successfully captured nonlinear structures, addressing a key limitation of ARIMA-ANN models, where initial misspecification in the linear model can lead to residual errors in the neural network layer32. Kernel smoothing in the NPAR stage helped stabilize trend estimation in small-sample contexts and reduced overfitting, a common challenge with ANN models21,34.

The study also found that the models performed well for items issued, due to their higher volume, smoother trends, and lower noise-to-signal ratio, which align with NPAR-ANN’s strengths in trend decomposition. These findings are consistent with studies Makridakis et al.35 and Gneiting et al.36, which highlighted that hybrid and machine learning models often excel where clear patterns exist. Additionally, the present study demonstrated their dominance in modeling overrides and errors, consistent with broader literature on time-series forecasting29. The results suggested that the integration error would remain stable and decrease in the coming years, supporting findings from the Swiss study37, where ADC dispensing errors reduced from 5 to 1%, and the Taiwan study19, where errors decreased by 75% after continuous implementation of ADCs.

The observed gradual decline in overrides throughout the study period suggests that ongoing practices, such as system upgrades, user training, or protocol adjustments, are effective. If these efforts continue, the declining trend in both overrides and integration errors could be maintained. Overall, this study offers a methodological framework for forecasting healthcare operations related to ADC parameters, providing a model that can be adopted by other hospital settings to enhance their own practices.

Limitations

The main limitation of the study is that it focused on a regional hospital that recently implemented the ADC system. It is possible that the findings may not fully reflect variations in ADC performance across different hospitals and regions with different operational environments and healthcare practices. Therefore, it is recommended to gather additional data from other regions to validate the model, which can then be used to guide further decision-making policies. The unique characteristics of the hospital’s automated dispensing cabinet (ADC) system and specific drug applications may introduce biases, since certain medications or error types might be over- or underrepresented.

Another limitation of the study is the small sample size; models such as ARIMA, Holt-Winters exponential smoothing (without seasonality), and the Theta model can be applied because they are capable of producing reliable forecasts even with limited data. NPAR can also be used if smoothing parameters are selected carefully. Although ANN models generally require larger datasets for optimal performance, they can still provide valuable insights when applied thoughtfully and validated properly.

Conclusion

This novel study modeled key parameters of ADCs, providing data-driven insights that can inform hospital decision-making and optimize medication management. The study showcased the application of hybrid machine learning models in forecasting critical ADC parameters, offering valuable data-driven insights for hospital administrators. Future work can explore the integration of more advanced forecasting models, like Prophet or Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks in complex healthcare time series.
