Assessing anhedonia in Chinese healthy and depressed adolescents: reliability, validity, and clinical utility of the anhedonia scale for adolescent
Authors: Chi-Son Kuan, Yun-han He, Yu-jia Xu, Si-lu Chen, Ling-ling Wang, Ke Ni, Han-yu Zhou, Xin-li Chi, Chao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnhedonia, a core symptom of adolescent depression, is often difficult to assess, the 14-item Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) was developed to address this need. This study aimed to evaluate the reliability and validity of the ASA among healthy and depressed adolescents in China.MethodsIn Study 1, a large community sample of 1,448 adolescents (female = 855; mean age = 14.61 years) from middle schools across eastern, western, and central China were recruited between October 2022 to April 2023 to evaluate the psychometric properties of the ASA in general population. To validate the factor structure, confirmatory factor analysis was employed. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) and test-retest reliability at six-month intervals (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC) were also assessed. In Study 2, a clinical sample of 168 adolescents with major depressive disorder (MDD), 121 with subthreshold depression (SD), and 149 healthy controls (HC) were recruited between April 2023 to April 2024, to examine the construct validity, discriminative ability, and clinical applicability and divergent validity of the ASA. One-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to analysis the group difference.ResultsIn Study 1, validity assessments confirmed that a three-factor model - (1) Enjoyment, Excitement, Emotional Flattening; (2) Enthusiasm, Connection, Purpose; (3) Effort, Motivation, Drive—provided a good fit. Reliability assessments demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.733 to 0.883) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.749). In study 2, the ASA exhibited acceptable reliability and validity within the MDD group. Crucially, ANOVA results revealed significant group differences in ASA scores, with MDD patients scoring the highest, followed by SD groups, and then HCs (MDD > SD >HC, ps < 0.001), demonstrating strong discriminative ability.ConclusionsThese results indicate that the ASA demonstrates acceptable construct validity, reliability, discriminative ability and shows promise as a useful tool for assessing anhedonia in Chinese adolescents for both research and clinical settings with Chinese adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08318-0.

Introduction

China accounts for 12.9% of the global adolescent population [1]. A national epidemiological study reported that 17.5% of Chinese children and adolescents (6–16 years) experience at least one psychiatric disorder, and the prevalence rate for major depressive disorder (MDD) specifically is 2% [2], which is roughly 3.6 million after applying this rate to the 2024 census population of approximately 180 million individuals aged 10–19 years [3]. Anhedonia, a core symptom of MDD [4, 5], is defined as a condition in which individuals are disinterested in or find it difficult to experience pleasure from previous rewarding activities [6]. Among adolescents with depression, anhedonia is associated with poorer treatment outcomes, including reduced responsiveness to pharmacological interventions [7] and psychological therapies [8]. Even among healthy adolescents, anhedonia has been identified as a risk factor for later onset of mental illnesses [9]. However, accurate assessment of anhedonia in youth remains a critical gap in both research and clinical practice, particularly in the Chinese context.

Anhedonia reflects underlying dysfunctions in reward-related cognitive and affective processing. Adolescence is a critical period marked by heightened sensitivity to rewards, increased reward-seeking, and greater risk-taking compared to adulthood [10–12]. Anhedonia has been linked to dysfunctions in reward processing [13], which are more pronounced in adolescents with MDD than in adults [14–16]. Interestingly, despite these impairments, adolescents with MDD are more likely to retain consummatory pleasure responses compared to adults with depression [16, 17]. These developmental distinctions highlight the importance of age-appropriate tools to assess anhedonia in adolescents, ultimately improving diagnostic accuracy.

Currently, most existing anhedonia scales were originally developed for adult and later adapted for adolescents. For example, the Dimensional Anhedonia Rating Scale (DARS), widely regarded as the “gold standard” in clinical research, assesses anhedonia across multiple life domains [18] and has been validated in adolescent samples [19]. However, items in these scales are often rely on adult-centric scenarios, potentially limiting their ecological validity for adolescents. In measuring the rewarding process, traditional anhedonia scales, such as the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS) [20], the Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS) [21], and the adolescent version of the Anticipatory and Consummatory Interpersonal Pleasure Scale (ACIPS-A) [22], did not specifically assess motivation and reward-driven effort. These dimensions are particularly important characteristics of adolescent anhedonia. Adolescence is a period of heightened reward sensitivity with increasing demands for effortful action in pursuit of goals [10]. Empirical evidence indicates that deficits in effort expenditure and motivational aspects of reward processing are more pronounced in adolescents than in adults among depression group [23]. Moreover, adolescents with depressive symptoms exhibit blunted behavioral and neural responses during effort anticipation and effort exertion for rewards [24, 25]. Although motivation and effort are assessed in more recently developed adult scales such as the DARS [18] and the Motivation and Pleasure Scale–Self-Report (MAP-SR) [26], these instruments were not developed from adolescents’ lived experiences. Despite motivation and effort were assessed in recently developed scales, such as the DARS, and the MAP-SR, but these scales were developed based on adults.

The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA), developed by [27], addresses this gap by drawing directly from adolescents’ firsthand accounts of anhedonia. It remains the only scale specifically designed based on firsthand reports of anhedonia from adolescents with MDD in the United Kingdom. The ASA comprises three distinct factors: (1) Enjoyment, Excitement and Emotional Flattening; (2) Enthusiasm, Connection and Purpose; (3) Effort, Motivation and Drive [27]. Although recent efforts have adapted the ASA for the Chinese context and proposed a bifactor model instead of the original three-factor structure [28], several limitations remain. The clinical sample primarily comprised inpatients with moderate to severe depression, limiting the generalizability of the findings to outpatient populations. Moreover, the non-clinical participants were recruited from a single province, reducing the representativeness of the results across Chinese adolescents with diverse socioeconomic and regional backgrounds. Beyond sample characteristics, the alternative structures diverged from the theoretically derived three-domain framework grounded in adolescents’ lived experiences [29]. In addition, the original three-factor structure of the ASA has not been thoroughly examined across different clinical subgroups. Therefore, further validation of the ASA’s psychometric properties—particularly its factor structure and clinical utility—is needed in a larger and more representative sample of Chinese adolescents.

The current study aimed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the ASA in Chinese adolescents, including both healthy individuals and those with clinically diagnosed depression. To achieve this goal, we conducted two studies. Study 1 examined the factor structure, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and convergent, concurrent, divergent, and predictive validity of the ASA in a large sample of community-based adolescents. Study 2 assessed the factor structure, reliability, and discriminant validity of the ASA in a clinical sample composed of adolescents with MDD, those with subthreshold depression (SD), and healthy controls (HC).

Building on the established three-factor structure of the ASA in a UK sample, we hypothesized that: (1) the three-factor model would demonstrate a good fit among both healthy Chinese adolescents and those clinically diagnosed with depression; (2) the ASA would show acceptable internal consistency and test-retest reliability, as well as strong convergent and concurrent validity, evidenced by significant correlations with depression severity and other anhedonia-related measures; and (3) ASA scores would significantly differentiate between groups, with scores highest in the MDD group, followed by the SD group, and lowest in HCs.

Study 1

Methods

Participants and procedures

A total of 1,603 students aged 11 to 18 from four middle schools across eastern, western, and central China participated in the online survey. Following data quality screening, 155 respondents were excluded for either failing the two instructed response items (e.g., “Please select very hard-working” and “Please select sometimes”)—or completing the survey in under 10 minutes, based on the previous recommendations [30, 31]. The final sample included 1,448 participants (90.33%), with 990 of them completing a follow-up survey six months later.

Instruments

The Chinese version of the ASA was developed using standardized forward and back-translation procedures to ensure clarity and linguistic equivalence [32, 33]. With permission from the primary developer, two postgraduate students in psychology independently translated the scale into Chinese. The translation was then reviewed and refined by two bilingual psychologists. Subsequently, a postgraduate student majoring in English conducted a back-translation of the revised draft into English. By comparing the original ASA with the back-translated version, further refinements were made to ensure conceptual and linguistic accuracy, resulting in the final Chinese version of the ASA.

The Chinese version of the ASA was used to assess anhedonia levels in adolescents. The original 14-item ASA includes three subscales: (1) Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening (EEE, e.g., “Nothing made me feel excited”); (2) Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose (ECP, e.g., “I felt enthusiastic”); and (3) Effort, Motivation, and Drive (EMD, e.g., “I had no motivation to get started on things”). The ASA adapted a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = “never” to 3 = “always”.

The Dimensional Anhedonia Rating Scale (DARS) was developed by [18], a 17-item self-report questionnaire. The DARS measured state anhedonia across four life domains: Part-time Hobbies (PH), Food/Drink (FD), Social Activities (SA), and Sensory Experience (SE), with a 5-point rating scale. Lower scores indicated more severe anhedonia in the respective domains. The Chinese version of the DARS was translated and revised by [34]. Recent research has demonstrated its strong validity and reliability in Chinese adolescents [19]. In current study, the Cronbach’s coefficient for the total scale was 0.93, and ranging from 0.76 to 0.91 in subscales.

The Motivation and Pleasure Self-Rating Scale (MAP-SR) was used to measure motivational deficits and diminished pleasure across multiple domains [26]. The MAP-SR consists of four factors: social pleasure (SOC), recreational or work-related pleasure (REC), interpersonal relationships (REL), and motivation/effort to engage in activities (MOT). There were 15 items in the MAP-SR, rated on a 5-point rating scale (0–4), lower scores indicate more severe motivational and pleasure experience deficits. The Chinese version of the MAP-SR was used in the current study and it demonstrated good reliability and validity [35]. In current study, the Cronbach’s coefficient for the total scale was 0.92, and ranging from 0.72 to 0.88 in subscales.

The Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) was used to assess depressive symptoms in children and adolescents aged 7–17 years [36]. The scale consisted of 27 items rated on a 3-point scale, evaluating various emotional experiences over the past two weeks. Higher total scores indicated more severe depressive symptoms, with a clinical cutoff score of 19. The CDI measured five depression-related dimensions: Anhedonia, Negative Mood, Negative Self-Esteem, Ineffectiveness, and Interpersonal Problems. The Chinese version of CDI demonstrated good reliability and validity in a sample of Chinese adolescents [37]. In current study, the subscale Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.64 to 0.81, and the total scale Cronbach’s α was 0.94.

The Children’s Inventory of Anger (ChIA) assesses anger reactions to life events in children and adolescents aged 6–16 years [38]. There were 39-item, and four subscales: Frustration (FRUST), Physical Aggression (PHYS), Peer Relationships (PEER), and Authority Relations (AUTH), using a 4-point rating scale. Higher score indicated a greater level of anger. The ChIA demonstrated good psychometric properties in Chinese adolescents [39] under review). In current study, the subscale Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.78 to 0.84, and the total scale Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

The Social Touch Questionnaire (STQ) evaluated behaviors and attitudes related to social contact in individuals with social anxiety with 20 items [40]. The STQ assessed various emotional responses and attitudes toward social contact, using a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate more severe social anxiety problems. The Chinese version of the STQ showed good validity and reliability in adolescents [41]. In current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.70.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS statistical software version 29.0.2.0 for Mac [42] and Mplus 8.0 [43]. To evaluate the three-factor structure of the ASA, confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimator in Mplus. Model fit was assessed using the following robust fit indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI; >0.90), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI; >0.90), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; <0.08), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR; <0.08) [44–47]. Additionally, a hierarchical bifactor model was tested to determine whether the ASA structure was better explained by a general factor (representing a broad anhedonia construct) along with specific factors (capturing distinct subcomponents of anhedonia).

Validity was assessed through Pearson’s correlations between ASA scores and related constructs. For convergent validity, the correlations between the total and subscales scores of the ASA with the DARS and the MAP-SR scores were evaluated. To test the concurrent validity, the correlations between the total and subscale scores of the ASA and the CDI scores were examined. The divergent validity was assessed based on the correlation results between the total and subscales scores of the ASA, STQ and ChIA scores. To account for multiple comparisons, significance levels were adjusted using Bonferroni’s correction, which was calculated by dividing 0.05 with the number of correlation analysis [48]. The size of correlation coefficients were interpreted based on research that smaller than 0.30 is negligible; 0.30 to 0.50 is small; 0.50 to 0.70 is moderate; 0.70 to 0.90 is high; higher than 0.90 is very high [49, 50]. These correlation-based analyses collectively address construct validity by evaluating whether the ASA specifically measures the theoretical construct of anhedonia rather than unrelated or overlapping phenomena.

For predictive validity, a series of hierarchical multiple regression analyses was conducted using 6-month follow-up CDI scores as the dependent variable, to avoid multicollinearity among the highly correlated subscales of ASA. In the first step, demographic factors (age, sex ratio, attitude towards social touch, and years of education) were included as covariates. In the second step, the ASA total score or each subscale score was added separately in independent models to examine their incremental predictive utility above and beyond the demographic variables.

Finally, the internal consistency of the ASA total score and subscale scores was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. And test-retest reliability was assessed using intra-class correlation (ICC), employing a random-effects model.

Confirmation factor analysis

Building on previous research [27], the ASA was proposed to have a bifactor three-factor structure, comprising one general factor and three specific factors. To assess this structure with our data, we tested several models: the one-factor model, the two-factor model, the three-factor original model, and the three-factor bifactor model (see Table 2). The fit indices for both the original three-factor model (Robust χ2/df = 6.545, CFI = 0.946 TLI = 0.934; RMSEA = 0.062) and two factor model (Robust χ2/df = 6.389, CFI = 0.946 TLI = 0.935; RMSEA = 0.061) showed good fit. In contrast, the bifactor three-factor model and the one-factor model did not meet conventional fit standards (see Table 2, Models 2) and showed poorer fit compared to the original three-factor model. Although the two-factor solution provided statistically comparable fit, we retained the original three-factor structure—EEE, ECP, and EMD of ASA—for both theoretical and measurement reasons. This structure directly reflects the three distinct domains of anhedonia that emerged from qualitative interviews with adolescents with depression during scale development [27, 29]. Moreover, separable facets of reward processing that are particularly salient in adolescence were captured: consummatory pleasure and emotional experience were captured by EEE subscale, anticipatory/social motivation were captured by ECP subscale, and effortful motivation/drive was captured by EMD subscale [25, 29]. Retaining three factors structure provides greater conceptual clarity and clinical utility than a more parsimonious two-factor solution, while remaining fully consistent with the original theoretical framework. Validity analysis

Figure 2 A presents the convergent validity results, which showed significant negative correlations between both the total score and subscales of the ASA and most of the DARS and the MAP-SR scores. Weak to moderate correlations were found between the total and subscale scores of the ASA and the MAP-SR (rs = −0.328 to −0.568, psBonferroni < 0.05). Regarding the ASA and DARS scores, the ECP subscale of the ASA showed weak correlations with the PH sub-score, FD sub-score, and Total score of the DARS (rs = −0.317 to −0.352, psBonferroni < 0.05). Other relationships between ASA and DARS scores were negligible in strength (rs = −0.083 to −0.288, psBonferroni < 0.05). However, the correlation between the EMD subscale of the ASA and the FD and SE subscales of the DARS did not survive Bonferroni correction.

Concurrent validity was supported by significant positive correlations with CDI scores (rs = 0.289 to 0.746, psBonferroni < 0.05). Correlations between the total and subscale scores of the ASA and the ChIA and STQ were negligible (rs = 0.103 to 0.303, psBonferroni < 0.05), providing support for discriminant validity. These small correlations suggest that the ASA specifically captures anhedonia rather than broader constructs such as anger or social-touch-related attitudes. Nevertheless, since anger and attitudes toward social contact can share conceptual overlap with depressive symptoms (e.g., irritability or social withdrawal), the chosen comparison measures are not especially stringent tests of discriminant validity.

Discussion

In Study 1, the Chinese version of the ASA demonstrated good psychometric properties in a large community sample of adolescents. The three-factor model showed a better fit to others and comparable fit to the two-factor model. For greater conceptual clarity and clinical utility, we decided to keep the three-factor model. Additionally, the ASA exhibited good internal consistency, test-retest reliability, concurrent validity, and convergent validity.

Divergent validity was also supported by the negligible correlations between ASA scores, and measures of anger (ChIA) and social touch attitudes (STQ). While these results suggest a relative independence of anhedonia from anger and social touch attitudes, we acknowledge that the chosen comparison measures are not particularly stringent tests of the divergent validity of ASA. Anger and social-anxiety-related attitudes can co-occur with depressive symptomatology, potentially reducing the rigor of the test relative to completely unrelated domains (e.g., perceptual or cognitive abilities unrelated to reward processing).

In addition to strong construct validity (supported by the three-factor CFA solution, reliability indices, and appropriate convergent, concurrent, and discriminant correlations), the current findings in the community sample lay the groundwork for evaluating the ASA’s discriminative ability and real-world clinical usefulness in subsequent clinical samples. However, the suitability of the ASA for use in depressed adolescents remained uncertain. Given that the ASA was originally designed for depressed populations, Study 2 aimed to examine its validity and reliability in adolescents with depression. In advance, the study also assessed the sensitivity of the ASA in distinguishing between adolescents with MDD, those with subthreshold depression (SD), and healthy controls (HC).

Study 2

In Study 2, we aimed to further evaluate the factor structure, reliability, and validity of the ASA scale in adolescents diagnosed with clinical depression. Using sensitivity analysis, we examined score differences across different depression subgroups at various stages (acute phase, and subthreshold depression) and compared them to healthy adolescents. Additionally, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the scale’s ability to differentiate between groups.

Methods

Participants and procedures

A total of 168 adolescents with MDD (aged 11 to 22) were recruited from the Shanghai Mental Health Center, Shanghai Changning Mental Health Center, and Qiqihaer Mental Health Center. The original ASA was developed and validated for the 11–18 age range [27]. Given the limited number of adolescents with MDD in the 11–18 age range, we expanded the age inclusion criteria to 22 years to achieve a sufficiently large and representative clinical sample. This decision was guided by contemporary developmental frameworks that conceptualize adolescence as extending into young adulthood (18–24 years) [52]. It also reflects common practice in adolescent depression research, given the practical difficulties of recruiting adequately powered samples of clinician-diagnosed MDD within the narrower 11–18 window, particularly in outpatient settings [53]. MDD diagnosis was confirmed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 conducted by psychiatrists (SCID-5) [54]. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) had received modified electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), including Bilateral or Unilateral ECT, within the past 6 weeks; (2) had undergone a major medication adjustment in the past 4 weeks; and/or (3) met the diagnostic criteria for any other DSM-5 disorders.

Importantly, the SD and HC groups were not diagnosed using the same structured clinical interview as the MDD group. Additionally, 121 individuals from East China Normal University (N = 50) and part of Study 1 (N = 71), who scored ≥ 20 on the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [55, 56], and reported no prior history of MDD diagnosis, were selected as the subthreshold depression (SD) group. The MDD group was diagnosed using the gold-standard SCID-5 conducted by psychiatrists, whereas the SD and healthy control (HC) groups were classified solely on the basis of self-reported BDI cut-off scores (≥20 for SD; ≤19 for HC) and the absence of any prior MDD diagnosis. Of these, 71 participants were drawn from the community sample in Study 1 and 50 were recruited from East China Normal University. Finally, 149 participants (93 from Study 1), whose BDI scores were ≤ 19, were recruited as healthy controls (HC), with age and sex ratios matched to the SD and MDD groups. HC and SD participants were excluded if they reported (1) a diagnosis of any other DSM-5 mental disorder, and/or (2) a family history of psychiatric disorders.

Assessments

All participants completed the ASA and the DARS. Additionally, the BDI; was used to assess participants’ depression levels over the past two weeks. There were 21 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), higher score indicated more severe depression. The Chinese version of BDI had proved good validity and reliability in Chinese adolescents [57]. In current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.95.

To measure trait anhedonia, participants also completed the Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS), which evaluates anticipatory (ANT) and consummatory (CON) pleasure with 20 items and 6-point rating scale (1 = very false for me to 6 = very true for me), lower score indicated a higher level of anhedonia [21]. The Chinese version of TEPS was translated by [58], and has been validated for psychometric properties in Chinese middle school students [59]. In current, the Cronbach’s α of total scale was 0.94, and the Cronbach’s alpha for the anticipatory subscale and consummatory subscale were 0.91 and 0.89.

Finally, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S/T) [60]; was administered to assess participants’ state (S) and trait (T) anxiety levels. The 20-item scale is rated on a 4-point scale. The Chinese version of STAI-S/T was assessed with established validity and reliability in Chinese university students [61]. In current study, the Cronbach’s α of total scale was 0.96, and the Cronbach’s alpha for the STAI-S subscale and STAI-T subscale were 0.92 and 0.93.

For the MDD participants, depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), higher score indicated a greater level of depression [62], Among Chinese depressive patients, the HAMD have been proved with good reliability and validity [63]. In addition, the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) was used to evaluated the anxiety symptoms in MDD participants, higher score indicated a greater level of anxiety [64]. The reliability and validity of HAMA have been validated in Chinese patients [65].

Statistical analysis

Data in Study 2, data were analyzed using the same procedures as in Study 1, including CFA, convergent, concurrent, and divergent validity, and the internal consistency, of the ASA in adolescents with MDD, using SPSS 29.0.2.0 and Mplus 8.0. Additionally, secondary analyses were conducted to examine discriminative ability (between-group differences in the total and factor scores of the ASA using a series of one-way analyses of variance; ANOVAs). The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for the ANOVAs, and post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction. Finally, ROC curve analysis; [66, 67]; was conducted to evaluate the real-world clinical usefulness (diagnostic value) of the scale, and its ability to distinguishing between HC and MDD, and between HC and SD.

Confirmation factor analysis

The CFA was conducted in the MDD group. All factor scores were significantly correlated with each other, and the fit indices for the three-factor original model indicated a marginally acceptable fit (Robust χ2/df = 2.226, CFI = 0.900 TLI = 0.934; RMSEA = 0.085, See Table 2), especially the RMSEA value of 0.085 falls in the borderline range according to conventional guidelines [44, 68]. In contrast, the three-factor bifactor model, the one-factor and the two-factor model showed poorer fit indices, falling below conventional standards when compared to the original three-factor model. The CFA standardized loadings and between-factor correlations for the three-factor model are presented in Fig. 1B.

Reliability analysis

The ASA demonstrated good internal consistency overall (Cronbach’s α = 0.882). All subscales exhibited poor to good reliability, with Cronbach’s α values as follows: EEE (α = 0.819), ECP (α = 0.681), and EMD (α = 0.764) subscales.

Validity analysis

The ASA total score remained significantly correlated with both the DARS total and subscale scores as well as the TEPS total and anticipatory subscale scores (rs = −0.323 to −0.467, psBonferroni < 0.05). The EEE subscale of the ASA was significant correlations with the DARS total and subscale scores (rs = −0.263 to −0.396, psBonferroni < 0.05). The ECP subscale was significantly associated with the DARS total, FD, SA, and SE subscale scores, as well as the TEPS total and ANT subscale scores (rs = −0.281 to −0.501, psBonferroni < 0.05). Similarly, the EMD subscale continued to show significant correlations with the DARS total and subscale scores, as well as the TEPS total and ANT subscale scores (rs = −0.295 to −0.455, psBonferroni < 0.05, See Model B in Fig. 2. However, no significant correlations were found between the ASA scores and the MAP-SR scores after Bonferroni correction.

Concurrent validity, was supported by moderate to strong positive correlations with the BDI score (rs = 0.509 to 0.735, psBonferroni < 0.05), all of which remained significant after applying the Bonferroni correction.

Regarding divergent validity, after adjusting the significance threshold using the Bonferroni correction, no significant correlations were found between the total and subscale scores of the ASA and the total ChIA, STAI-S, and STAI-T score.

Discussion

In study 2, the Chinese version of the ASA was administrated to adolescents with clinical depression. The results showed acceptable validity and reliability in clinical sample. Confirmatory factor analyses consistently supported the original three-factor structure in both healthy and depressed Chinese adolescents. Although the two-factor model in Study 1 demonstrated acceptable fit, the three-factor solution was retained for its superior theoretical alignment and clinical interpretability. The ASA showed good internal consistency (with the exception of the ECP subscale, for which internal consistency was marginal), test-retest reliability, concurrent validity and convergent validity. Notably, no significant correlations were found between the ASA and the MAP-SR scores in the MDD group, suggesting potential conceptual distinctions between these two measures of anhedonia, which required further explanations. Divergent validity was similarly supported by the absence of significant correlations (after Bonferroni correction) with anger (ChIA) and state/trait anxiety (STAI), these non-significant associations support the specificity of the ASA in the clinical sample; yet we note that anxiety and anger inventories are not particularly stringent tests of divergent validity given their common comorbidity with depressive disorders. Taken together, these results confirm the ASA’s construct validity in the MDD group. The significant group differences (MDD > SD >HC) demonstrate sensitivity to varying levels of depressive symptom. The MDD group was defined using the gold-standard Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5), while the SD and HC groups were classified solely on the basis of self-reported BDI cut-off scores, and the absence of prior MDD diagnosis. Therefore, the between-group comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, these findings demonstrate the ASA’s strong discriminative ability across varying levels of depressive symptomatology. While the identified cut-off scores from ROC analysis suggest potential real-world clinical usefulness for screening purposes, the heterogeneity in diagnostic procedures across groups limits strong claims about diagnostic accuracy in everyday clinical settings.

Consistent with Hypothesis 3, there were significant differences in ASA scores among clinically depressed, subthreshold depressed, and healthy adolescents. These findings highlight the scale’s sensitivity in differentiating between varying levels of depressive severity in adolescents.

General discussion

The present study evaluated the psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the ASA in both healthy and depressed adolescents. Across two independent samples, the ASA exhibited good internal reliability and demonstrated concurrent, convergent, and divergent validity. Confirmatory factor analyses supported the original three-factor structure in both populations. It is important to situate our findings within the growing body of research on the ASA in China. [28] also validated a Chinese version of the ASA, proposing a two-factor structure and establishing a cut-off score for prominent anhedonia. However, their reliance on an inpatient clinical sample and a geographically limited non-clinical sample restricts the generalizability of their findings. In contrast, our study examined the original three-factor model in both a nationwide community and a clinically diagnosed MDD group from multiple centers.

The present study provides evidence across important aspects of scale evaluation. Construct validity was supported by: (a) confirmation of the original three-factor structure via CFA in both community and clinical samples, (b) acceptable to strong internal consistency and test-retest reliability, and (c) theoretically expected patterns of convergent, concurrent, and divergent correlations with established anhedonia and depression measures (while showing negligible associations with less related constructs such as anger and anxiety). Discriminative ability was demonstrated in Study 2 through robust between-group differences (MDD > SD >HC) on total and subscale scores. Although the ASA showed strong discriminative ability, between-group comparisons should be interpreted with caution because the MDD group was diagnosed using the gold-standard SCID-5, whereas the SD and HC groups were classified solely based on self-reported BDI cut-off scores and the absence of prior MDD diagnosis. The observed gradient (MDD > SD >HC) primarily reflects sensitivity to varying levels of depressive symptomatology. Finally, the identified cut-off scores and the scale’s sensitivity to symptom severity gradients offer preliminary support for the ASA’s real-world clinical usefulness as a screening and assessment tool in adolescent populations. However, because the current evidence derives from mainly cross-sectional data and groups defined with varying diagnostic rigor, additional longitudinal and treatment studies are required to establish its utility for monitoring symptom change or guiding clinical decision-making in routine practice.

In the validity analysis, the three-factor original model of the ASA demonstrated a good fit in the CFA for both healthy and depressed Chinese adolescents. This finding aligns with results from the original ASA, which was tested in a British sample [27], providing evidence for the cross-cultural applicability of the ASA across Western and Eastern cultures. Compared to previously developed anhedonia scales, the ASA includes a dimension assessing effort, motivation, and drive, which is relevant for evaluating adolescent anhedonia. Recent research highlights that reward motivation, assessed through physical effort, is a key aspect of adolescent anhedonia [24, 69]. Furthermore, heightened sensitivity in seeking and responding to rewards are characteristics behavior during adolescence [10]. Adolescents are more likely to engage in risky activities than adults in pursuit of greater hedonic experiences [70, 71]. In terms of depressive symptoms, energy loss is more prevalent in adolescents than in adults, providing evidence that motivation deficits are more severe in adolescent depressive patients compared to their adult counterparts [23]. Moreover, previous anhedonia scales either did not measure the deficit of motivation and effort (e.g., SHAPS, TEPS, ACIPS-A), or developed in adults (e.g., DARS, MAP-SR). The ASA addresses these gaps by incorporating dimensions that capture the multifaceted nature of anhedonia in adolescents. Importantly, social connections—particularly face-to-face interactions—are crucial for socioemotional development during adolescence [72], and the sense of being loved significantly influences the relationship between family communication patterns and adolescent depression [73]. These aspects are effectively assessed through the ASA’s subscales of Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose [29], making the ASA more sensitive to anhedonia in young populations.

The convergent validity analysis from Study 1 showed that ASA scores were significantly correlated with both the DARS and MAP-SR scores in healthy Chinese adolescents. These findings align with previous research showing significant associations between the ASA and the Self-Report Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia (SNS) in UK samples [27], reinforcing the ASA’s potential ability to assess deficits in motivation and hedonic capacity [26]. In Study 2, ASA total and subscale scores were significantly correlated with the DARS in adolescents with MDD, indicating that the ASA effectively captures deficits in desire, motivation, effort, and consummatory pleasure across multiple life domains [18]. Additionally, significant correlations between the ASA scores and the CON subscale of the TEPS further demonstrated the ASA’s sensitivity to consummatory pleasure deficits associated with anhedonia [21]. However, no significant correlations were found between ASA scores and MAP-SR scores in adolescents with MDD. Although anhedonia is a shared feature of both depression and schizophrenia [5], the MAP-SR primarily targets negative symptoms specific to schizophrenia [4, 26], which may not fully capture the anhedonia experiences in depression.

With respect to divergent validity, ASA scores showed only negligible correlations with anger (ChIA) and social touch/social anxiety attitudes (STQ) in the community sample. In the clinical sample, non-significant associations with anger and anxiety further support the specificity of the ASA; however, because these constructs frequently co-occur with depression and anhedonia, the chosen measures provide only a modest test of divergent validity. These findings indicate that the ASA captures anhedonia with reasonable specificity, and is not merely reflecting general negative affectivity or emotional distress. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the chosen comparison measures (ChIA, STQ, and STAI) are not especially stringent tests of divergent validity. Constructs such as anger and anxiety frequently co-occur with depression, which may limit the rigor of these tests relative to measures of entirely unrelated domains (e.g., certain cognitive or perceptual abilities). Future research should incorporate more orthogonal constructs to provide a more rigorous examination of the ASA’s specificity.

In Study 1, the first-wave ASA total score and its three subscale scores significantly predicted the second-wave CDI score, providing information on predictive validity. However, these predictive findings should be interpreted with caution. As the ASA specifically measures anhedonia—a core symptom of depression, anhedonia is also directly assessed by the CDI. Therefore, the observed prediction of later depressive symptoms may partly reflect shared symptom content rather than the ASA’s unique ability to prospectively predict new variance in depression severity independent of baseline anhedonia [6, 74]. In Study 2, ASA scores showed similar correlations with BDI scores among adolescents diagnosed with MDD. Additionally, the SD group had significantly higher ASA scores (total and subscale) compared to the HC group but lower scores than the MDD group. Although the ASA successfully discriminated the clinical groups from healthy controls, this discrimination may partly reflect the substantial construct overlap between anhedonia (as measured by the ASA) and overall depressive severity, given that anhedonia is a core diagnostic feature of major depressive disorder [6]. These patterns are consistent with findings from other anhedonia measures like the TEPS and DARS [19, 21]. Importantly, as the original ASA had not previously been validated in adolescents with MDD [27], this study examined of the ASA in a depressed adolescent population with varying levels of symptom severity. ASA is capable of capturing core features of anhedonia shared across disorders and might able to differentiate disorder-specific manifestations of reward processing deficits.

Furthermore, the reliability analysis demonstrated acceptable to strong internal consistency for both the total score and subscales of the ASA in healthy and MDD adolescents, aligning with previous findings from UK samples [27]. Test-retest reliability analysis showed ICCs ranging from 0.611 to 0.749 for the ASA total score and subscales, consistent with the UK data (ICC = 0.73 to 0.78 [27]). These results suggest that the ASA maintains stable measurement properties over a six-month period in Chinese adolescents.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the test-retest reliability and predictive validity of the ASA were not evaluated in a clinical sample, limiting the generalizability of the findings to clinical populations. Future research should consider incorporating the ASA in longitudinal studies involving clinically diagnosed adolescents to validate these psychometric properties. Additionally, applying the ASA in treatment studies could help assess its sensitivity in detecting changes in anhedonia over time. Second, while the ASA demonstrated overall acceptable to strong psychometric properties, the ECP subscale showed marginally acceptable internal consistency in the clinical sample (α = 0.681). This suggests that the items assessing enthusiasm, connection, and purpose may be somewhat less cohesive among adolescents with MDD, and findings related to this subscale warrant cautious interpretation until further replication. Third, the study did not include participants with other anhedonia-related conditions (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, eating disorders). Expanding the sample to include diverse clinical populations would allow for a more comprehensive exploration of the shared and unique characteristics of anhedonia across different disorders. Fourth, as a self-report measure, the ASA may be influenced by response biases, such as the tendency to underreport symptoms. Future studies could consider integrating more dynamic, game-like assessment methods to capture hedonic capacity in a more engaging and less biased manner. Fifth, although discriminant validity was supported by correlated to anger and anxiety measures, these instruments are not the most stringent tests of divergence; stronger evidence would require future studies with measures of more theoretically distant constructs. Sixth, although the MDD group was diagnosed with SCID-5 conducted by psychiatrists, while the SD, and HC groups were classified solely based BDI cut-off scores (≥20 for SD; ≤19 for HC) and the absence of any prior MDD diagnosis. Consequently, it is possible that some participants in the SD group may have actually met full diagnostic criteria for MDD that went undetected, while some individuals in the HC group may have had undetected subthreshold symptoms. Such potential misclassification may have introduced bias, potentially affecting the magnitude of observed group differences in ASA scores and limiting the interpretability of between-group comparisons and ROC analyses. Future studies should employ uniform structured clinical interviews (e.g., SCID-5) across all groups to strengthen the validity of these findings. Seventh, while the study demonstrates construct validity, and discriminative ability, real-world clinical usefulness (e.g., responsiveness to treatment, predictive value for functional outcomes, or cost-effectiveness in busy clinics) was not directly examined and should be evaluated in future applied studies. Finally, the age range of participants in Study 2 was broader than that in Study 1, potentially introducing developmental variability and the resulting developmental heterogeneity may have influenced the observed factor structure and validity estimates. Adolescence encompasses substantial developmental changes that may differentially affect anhedonia dimensions: deficits in effort and motivation (EMD) may be more pronounced in late adolescence, consummatory pleasure (EEE) may vary with hedonic maturation [16, 69], and social connection and purpose (ECP) may shift with evolving social reward sensitivity and identity formation [11, 72]. The broader age range in Study 2 could thus have introduced variability in reward processing profiles that obscures the factor structure seen in a more homogeneous adolescent sample. Employing the traditional adolescent age range (10–18 years) in future studies may better demonstrate the ASA’s effectiveness in measuring anhedonia compared to other scales tailored for adolescents.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that the Chinese version of the Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) possesses acceptable construct validity, demonstrated by confirmation of the original three-factor structure in both community and clinical samples, acceptable internal consistency, good test-retest stability, and theoretically expected patterns of convergent, concurrent, and divergent correlations. The scale also exhibited the ability to differentiate adolescents with major depressive disorder from those with subthreshold depression and healthy controls. These findings, together with preliminary evidence from ROC analyses, and identified cut-off scores, support the ASA as a brief, developmentally appropriate, and culturally validated tool for assessing anhedonia in Chinese adolescents across research and clinical settings. While further studies are suggested employing uniform diagnostic procedures, longitudinal designs, and treatment outcome data are warranted to fully establish its utility in routine practice, the ASA represents an addition to the repertoire of adolescent-specific anhedonia measures.
