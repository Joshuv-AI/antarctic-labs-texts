IRE1β evolves to be a guardian of respiratory and gastrointestinal mucosa
Authors: Hui Luo, Wen-Yan Gong, Yuan-Yuan Zhang, Ying-Ying Liu, Zhen Chen, Xing-Lin Feng, Qi-Bin Jiao, Xing-Wei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), a mediator of the unfolded protein response, shows the highest degree of evolutionary conservation. Vertebrates express two IRE1 paralogs: IRE1α, which is universally expressed and IRE1β, which shows specific expression within mucus secreted cells in respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts. The biological properties and regulation of the two IRE1 duplicates show evolutionary differences. As recently suggested, IRE1β-deficient mice display impairment in secreted protein expression and mucosal homeostasis. Abnormal changes in IRE1β caused by external and internal factors can disrupt mucosal homeostasis and further lead to respiratory and gastrointestinal diseases. Here, we highlight the physiological functions of IRE1β in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts in response to environmental microbes, viruses, toxins, and food components.

Introduction

Mucosal surfaces throughout organs and glands, such as the airways and gastrointestinal tract are exposed to and communicate with the external environment. Mucus, a complex viscoelastic adherent secretion, is produced by specialized mucous and goblet cells. Mucus offers a gel layer with selective permeability for gas and nutrient diffusion, absorption, and exchange in the airway and gastrointestinal tracts, with the epithelium underneath. It is also the first-line defense in the body to resist pathogenic invasion and infection. Its major components are mucins, a family of large glycoproteins synthesized by ribosomes and transported into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) for modification and folding [1,2]. Goblet cells also secrete other mucous proteins, including anterior gradient homolog 2 (AGR2), which are homologous proteins of the protein disulfide isomerase family members [[3], [4], [5]]. Dysregulated mucous protein production is associated with numerous diseases, including respiratory diseases, such as cystic fibrosis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and gastrointestinal tract diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer (CRC) [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Uncovering the regulatory mechanisms of mucus synthesis and quality control are essential for treating diseases associated with abnormal mucus synthesis.

ER, the largest membrane-bound organelle in eukaryotic cells, is composed of lamellar and tubular structures that form a network. Most of the produced membranes and secreted proteins are folded and mature within the ER lumen prior to delivery into other compartments or secretion from cells. Proteins with proper assembly can be transported from the ER to their destinations for biological functions. To ensure the fidelity of protein folding, cells have evolved a mechanism called the unfolded protein response (UPR) to regulate protein folding ability within the ER as needed [10]. The UPR in mammals includes three major signaling cascades triggered by ER transmembrane protein sensors, inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), and protein kinase RNA (PKR)-like ER kinase (PERK), which contribute to reprogramming gene transcription, protein modification, and mRNA translation, thus relieving misfolded and unfolded protein loads while restoring protein homeostasis [11,12]. The mechanism of the UPR from yeast to humans has been elucidated, providing an opportunity to understand the evolutionary aspects of the UPR.

The IRE1 branch accounts for the UPR pathway with the highest degree of evolutionary conservation within metazoans and is also the only UPR pathway in yeast. Metazoans express two IRE1 paralogs: IRE1α (coded by ERN1) and IRE1β (coded by ERN2) [13,14]. IRE1α is widely distributed within diverse cells and acts as a general ER stress sensor and mediator of UPR, and the signaling pathway of IRE1α-X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) has been relatively well studied [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. However, IRE1β expression is predominantly expressed in goblet cells that line the mucosal surfaces, including those of gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts. The highly restrictive expression of IRE1β suggests that it has a unique function in goblet cells [[19], [20], [21]]. Recently, the physiological function of IRE1β has been further revealed by analyzing the phenotypes of IRE1β knockout mice. Here, we review the evolution of IRE1β and its functions in regulating mucin production and maintaining mucosal homeostasis in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts.

Different endoribonuclease (RNase) activities of IRE1α and IRE1β

The two IRE1 paralogs consist of an N-terminal ER luminal sensor domain that monitors ER proteostasis, a transmembrane domain, and a C-terminal cytosolic effector region containing RNase and kinase domains that trigger the UPR [22,23]. Compared to human IRE1β, identity analysis of the domains of mouse IRE1β, human IRE1α, mouse IRE1α, and yeast IRE1 showed certain similarities (Fig. 1). Although IRE1α and IRE1β share some structural similarities, they have diverse activities. Determining the different functions of IRE1 isoforms and their underlying mechanisms has been an area of interest to scientists. The RNase domain of IRE1 is required for RNA cleavage via IRE1-dependent decay (RIDD) mechanism, which was first discovered in Drosophila melanogaster and is conserved in fission yeast, plants, and mammals [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Upon ER stress, IRE1α exhibits increased XBP1 mRNA cleavage. The enzymatic activity of IRE1β increases by ten times compared with IRE1α during RIDD reactions, however, it shows lower potency in XBP-1 mRNA splicing [29]. Moreover, IRE1β has increased activity in 28S ribosomal RNA cleavage to induce translational repression in response to ER stress [22,29]. A study implicated glycine 641 in IRE1β (histidine 692 in IRE1α) as a critical residue linked to reduced autophosphorylation, impaired oligomer assembly, and weakened XBP1 mRNA cleavage activity [30]. By conducting sequence alignment for IRE1α and IRE1β, researchers have put forward a paralog-selective inhibition model by interacting with non-conserved residues differentiating ATP-binding pockets in IRE1α and IRE1β, which will provide novel ideas for the design and development of specific inhibitors [31].Fig. 1Schematic diagram showing the identities of the domains of hIRE1β, mIRE1β, hIRE1α, mIRE1α and yeast IRE1. HAC1 is an ortholog of XBP1 within the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and is spliced by IRE1. Schizosaccharomyces pombe, a fission yeast, does not have a HAC1/XBP1 ortholog or a UPR-dependent transcriptional program. Nevertheless, IRE1 can trigger selective decay of BiP mRNA, which encodes a major ER chaperone required for survival during ER stress [26]. These results revealed that the IRE1 pathway evolution and RIDD are conserved mechanisms through which ER homeostasis is maintained by UPR. IRE1 sequences were analyzed in various eukaryotes to further identify the evolutionary origins of IRE1. According to the evolutionary tree shown for yeast, elegans and flies contained only one IRE1 form (Fig. 2). Remarkably, although IRE1α and IRE1β have different RNase activities, IRE1β does not evolve from diverse IRE1 ancestral forms. This suggests that the two IRE1 duplications likely occurred in the lineage preceding the ancestral vertebrate. Different selection pressures lead to two IRE1 replicators to acquire different mutations and evolve different functions; hence, IRE1β acquires a new function in goblet cells during evolution [[32], [33], [34], [35]].Fig. 2Evolutionary tree of IRE1 sequences from different organisms. IRE1 in ER stress response

At basal conditions, IRE1α bound to chaperone protein BiP remains in an inactive state [36,37]. Upon ER stress, the repressor BiP dissociates from IRE1α, which significantly increases the activity of released IRE1α (Fig. 3) [[38], [39], [40]]. IRE1 is then activated through autophosphorylation via its kinase domain. Phosphorylation of IRE1 promotes dimerization and C-terminal ribonuclease domain activation [41,42]. Upon adaptive UPR, the RNase domain in IRE1 splices XBP1 mRNA to generate its mature form, XBP1s, which encode effective transcription factors that upregulate targets for ER adaptation [43]. Extended ER stress results in ER-localized mRNA cleavage via the RNase domain of IRE1 [24,28]. Conversely, RIDD enhances ER stress while promoting apoptosis by cleaving mRNAs that encode anti-apoptotic and adaptive ER proteins and through the cleavage of miRNAs to suppress proapoptotic and proinflammatory proteins [44,45].Fig. 3Schematic diagram of dynamic regulation of IRE1α and IRE1β by chaperone proteins. In recent studies, IRE1β has been confirmed to be selectively bound by the mucin chaperone, AGR2, in the goblet cells for maintaining low activity (Fig. 3) [39,40,46]. AGR2 combines with the luminal domain of IRE1β to disrupt its dimerization. When large amount of unfolded mucin is produced, it triggers AGR2 dissociation of IRE1β, resulting in IRE1β dimerization and activation. Activation of IRE1β may cleave Mucin 2 (MUC2) mRNA to optimize the efficient folding and secretion of MUC2 and promote endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis [20]. The catalytic-dead C81S and inflammatory bowel disease-causing H117Y mutations in AGR2 disrupt the interaction with IRE1β, thereby disinhibiting IRE1β [4,39,47,48].

Several studies suggest that IRE1β is less effective than IRE1α at splicing XBP1 to alleviate ER stress [22,29]. Moreover, mice lacking IRE1β exhibit enhanced ER stress, such as increased levels of spliced XBP1 and BiP in the gastrointestinal tract [20,49,50]. Additionally, mechanistic studies have revealed that IRE1β-expressing intestinal epithelial cells exhibit decreased UPR through the suppression of IRE1α signaling. IRE1β interacts with IRE1α oligomers to inhibit its endonuclease activity for suppressing the stress-mediated XBP1 splicing [30].

Substrate preference of IRE1

Secretory and transmembrane protein synthesis initiates in the ER, followed by the cleavage of the signal peptide, glycosylation, disulfide bond generation, glycosidase trimming, and folding into mature proteins. Once ready for secretion, proteins are captured in the transport vesicles with Coat protein complex II (COP II) coating for transit to their destinations [51]. However, excessive protein influx may disturb ER homeostasis, leading to ER stress. Cells have evolved UPR to relieve pressure. IRE1β regulates Mucin 5B (MUC5B), MUC2, and microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP) protein synthesis through translational repression [20,[52], [53], [54]]. In addition, the use of a reporter system confirmed that IRE1β suppresses translation onto ER membrane rather than within cytosol, and such selective suppression is determined by the RNase activity of IRE1β (Table 1) [55]. IRE1α can target the mRNAs of ER-localized/secretory proteins and cytosolic or nuclear proteins (Table 2) [44]. To screen IRE1α cleavage targets in the whole genome, researchers have developed a specific approach by combining the in vitro cleavage and exon microarray analysis. They detected 13 new mRNAs as IRE1α cleavage candidates. The present work also mapped cleavage sites of these 13 mRNAs and analyzed the shared consensus sequence 5′-CUGCAG-3’. However, these targets need to be validated using cellular system [56]. Additionally, IRE1α cleave several precursors of miRNAs (pre-miRNAs), which can suppress the Caspase-2 mRNA translation [45]. These findings suggest that the substrate specificity of the respective RNase domains may determine their functions. Regulation of IRE1 expression

IRE1α can transcriptionally or post-transcriptionally be regulated by many stressors or proteins. Sirtuin 7 (SIRT7) de-acetylates SMAD family member 4 (SMAD4) to selectively transcriptionally activate IRE1α-XBP1 axis for promoting pro-survival ERK signaling pathway and secreting tumor-promoting cytokines in the melanoma [57]. AT-rich interactive domain-containing protein 1A (ARID1A) transcriptionally represses the IRE1α-XBP1 axis in response to ER stress. ARID1A inactivation associated with ovarian clear cell carcinoma confers sensitivity to IRE1α-XBP1 pathway inhibition [58]. Recent studies have revealed that ER-associated protein degradation (ERAD) is related to regulating protein turnover of IRE1α [59,60]. IRE1α is also degraded by selective autophagy via the receptor optineurin [49].

IRE1β is transcriptionally upregulated in cystic fibrosis and asthmatic human bronchial epithelial (HBE) cells via transcription factor SAM pointed domain–containing ETS transcription factor (SPDEF) [19]. The short chain fatty acids produced by microbiota also induces IRE1β expression, which assembles the mucus barrier and allows goblet cells to mature in the colon [61,62]. SPDEF also regulates its target genes IRE1β and Agr2 to influence mucus production in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [63]. Other extracellular and intracellular factors that activate IRE1β expression and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of IRE1β are worth exploring in the future.

Physiological role of IRE1β in mucosal epithelial cells

Loss of IRE1α in mice results in embryonic lethality caused by failure of placental development, suggesting the critical physiological effect of IRE1α on embryogenesis [64,65]. However, IRE1β knockout mice are viable [49]. In recent years, IRE1β knockout mice have been generated, which has uncovered the physiological function of IRE1β. In the respiratory tract

The respiratory tract is a complicated organ system that can be classified as the upper respiratory tract (including the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, and oropharynx), lower respiratory tract (comprising the conducting airways: trachea, bronchioles, and bronchi), and respiratory zone (alveoli and respiratory bronchioles) and functions in oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange. Specialized epithelial cells line the entire respiratory tract between the nasal cavity and the alveoli. Airway surfaces are exposed to large amounts of pathogens and particulates that can be cleared by the mucus barrier [66,67]. However, inflammation and hypersecretion of mucins contribute to the pathogenesis of numerous respiratory disorders, such as asthma, cystic fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [6,68]. Several studies have associated the inflammatory responses of HBE with respiratory diseases via activation of the IRE1-XBP1 pathway [69,70]. The airway epithelia of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cystic fibrosis show elevated production of the major polymeric mucins, MUC5B and Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC) [71]. MUC5B and MUC5AC expression is regulated by the IRE1β-XBP1 pathway [52].

IRE1β can be detected within the trachea, bronchus, and nasopharynx, organs that are rich in mucus-generating cells like goblet cells. However, it is not detected within the lung parenchyma or in human lung alveolar cells, which do not contain goblet cells [19]. IRE1β is upregulated in airway disorders, such as asthma or cystic fibrosis, with increased expression of goblet cells [19]. IRE1β−/− mice showed markedly decreased content of mucous and goblet cell numbers within the nasopharynx. After IRE1β−/− mice were exposed to ovalbumin or interleukin (IL)-13 treatment to induce allergic airway inflammation, MUC5B upregulation was significantly blunted. IRE1β overexpression enhanced the cellular mucin level and mucin production. The IRE1β-dependent mucin generation partially results from the activation of transcription factor XBP-1 and the resultant XBP-1-mediated AGR2 transcription, a gene related to the airway production of epithelial mucin [19,72].

The positive feedback bistable IRE1β–XBP1s pathway modulates MUC5B-predominant mucus blockage during idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In the basal state, IRE1β senses MUC5B protein synthesis, which activates XBP1 for regulating basal MUC5B and UPR gene levels. In response to low-intensity injury or inflammation, MUC5B expression is upregulated in the distal airway mucin-secreting cells at rs35705950 (G/G). An increased MUC5B load enhances IRE1β activity, XBP1 splicing, and relatively weak positive feedback provided by XBP1s on MUC5B transcriptional activity. Once the stimulus is removed, the cells return to basal MUC5B secretion levels. When experiencing irreversible injury or inflammation, MUC5B rs35705950 (G/T or T/T) promotes distal airway mucus secretion and triggers increased MUC5B expression, which leads to IRE1β hyperactivation and increased XBP1 splicing. This state triggers abnormal ER stress that induces injury and a pro-fibrotic microenvironment, resulting in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [52]. However, IRE1β protein is not upregulated within the house dust mite (HDM) model. Additionally, IRE1β does not participate in XBP1 signaling or mucin generation following HDM or IL-13 treatment [73].

When the respiratory tract is faced by the virus, IRE1α activate the interferon signaling pathway [74]. However, the physiological function of IRE1β in response to viruses has not been elucidated. Moreover, any one animal model cannot comprehensively encapsulate all aspects of human diseases. Further studies are warranted for revealing the role of IRE1β in respiratory diseases.

In the gastrointestinal tract

Mucus covers the inner part of the gastrointestinal tract and is produced and secreted by goblet cells. Both the colon and stomach possess a two-layer mucus system, with an inner dense and attached mucus layer as well as an outer, loose, and unattached mucus layer, whereas the small intestine has only a single unattached mucus layer [75]. MUC2 forms the mucus system in the small intestine and the colon. Mucus system in the stomach is built by MUC5AC and Mucin 6 (MUC6) [76,77]. The colonic inner mucus layer does not contain bacteria, which represents the main defense against pathogenic microorganisms [78]. MUC2 mucus barrier dysfunction due to various reasons might lead to ulcerative colitis [79,80]. Recent studies have revealed the physiological functions of IRE1β in maintaining gastrointestinal tract homeostasis.

In addition to the respiratory tract, IREβ is also expressed in the epithelia within the gastrointestinal tract [49]. When challenged with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), IRE1β knockout mice exhibit increased sensitivity to colitis and increased ER stress levels in the gastric and colonic mucosa. In addition, ER stress signals are increased, and the inflammatory response occurs earlier within the colonic mucosa in IRE1β−/− mice than wild type mice [49]. Abnormal mucin can be accumulated within the ER of goblet cells in IRE1β−/− mice. IRE1β maintains ER homeostasis in goblet cells by adjusting MUC2 expression [20].

The intestinal mucous layer is the major barrier against mycotoxins and pathogenic microorganisms that adhere to and invade intestinal epithelial cells. T-2 toxin can activate IRE1α to inhibit IRE1β, thus reducing mucin generation, causing disturbance in mucosal homeostasis, and inducing higher host infection risk [81]. In a recent study, researchers have revealed the functioning mechanism of IRE1β at the host-environment interface. IRE1β-deficient mice exhibited a decreased goblet cell number in the conventionally raised condition, indicating the necessity of IRE1β in microbiota-mediated assembly of mucus barrier and maturation of goblet cells within the colon. Mechanistically, gut microbes induce IRE1β expression to splice XBP1 mRNA, promote ER function, and avoid ER stress during the maturation of goblet cells. Genetic ablation of IRE1β in mice leads to intestinal microecological dysregulation, making it impossible to promote goblet cell growth and increases the susceptibility of colitis. IRE1β functions as a controller at the mucosal surface to mediate gut microbiota-colonic epithelium communication to drive goblet cell maturation and efficient mucus barrier development [61,62].

Chronic intestinal inflammation is a risk factor for CRC [[82], [83], [84]]. IRE1β is downregulated within human and azoxymethane/DSS-induced mouse CRC and this expression shows positive relation to MUC2 expression, suggesting the possible involvement of IRE1β in CRC tumorigenesis [85,86]. However, the detailed mechanism of the promotion of IRE1β for CRC initiation and progression needs to be further elucidated.

Chylomicron is a type of lipid droplet with a diameter of 80–500 nm synthesized by apolipoprotein B and MTP in enterocytes. It contains triglycerides, cholesterol esters, and apolipoproteins and its major function is to transport exogenous triglycerides and cholesterol into lymphatic circulation and eventually into the blood [87]. IRE1β is also involved in the regulation of chylomicron assembly. IRE1β−/− mice fed the high-cholesterol and high-fat diet showed prominent hyperlipidemia since they generated higher MTP and chylomicron levels. Mechanistically, IRE1β decreases MTP mRNA expression by increasing post-transcriptional degradation [53]. Moreover, Nuclear receptor subfamily 2, group F, member 1 (NR2F1) and IRE1β coordinate to suppress MTP expression and chylomicron production in undifferentiated intestinal cells [54]. Excess fat is a risk factor for various metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [88]. IRE1β ablation in Apolipoprotein E (APOE) deficient mice accelerated hyperlipidemia and increased atherosclerosis due to increased lipid absorption [89]. Although IRE1β is assumed to be highly expressed only in goblet cells and no expression is detected in enterocytes in the single cell analysis, researchers have observed that it affects intestinal fat absorption in enterocytes by regulating MTP [21]. In the future, cell-type specific knockout models can further reveal the expression and physiological function of IRE1β in different cell types.

The stomach possesses a two-layer mucus system built by MUC5AC, which is produced via surface pits or foveolar cells and MUC6, which is produced by the gland and neck cells [77,90]. Helicobacter pylori infection alters gastric mucin expression in epithelial cells [[91], [92], [93]]. IRE1β is also expressed in the stomach [49]. Whether the IRE1β is involved in the regulation of gastric mucus production and its role in the occurrence of gastric diseases is worth exploring in the future.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Vertebrates have evolved two IRE1 homologues: IRE1α, which retains the same function as the invertebrate ancestor and IRE1β, which is endowed with different RNase activities and special physiological functions in mucosal epithelial cells. Whether IRE1β expression can be detected within other mucosa cell types and its functions in these cells and mechanism that drives IRE1 specific expression within goblet cells are worth exploring. XBP1 associates ER stress with intestinal inflammation, resulting in a genetic risk of human inflammatory bowel disease, and several single nucleotide polymorphisms in XBP1 are significantly associated with inflammatory bowel disease [94]. Whether IRE1α or IRE1β plays a dominant role upstream of XBP1, or whether the two coordinate together is worth studying in the future.

Classical pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) is the most common differentiation state and displays high mucus secretion activity [95,96]. The transcription factor SPDEF promotes PDA growth by regulating its target, AGR2 and IRE1β, to mediate mucus production. Inactivation of SPDEF-mediated mucus production leads to abnormal tumor growth and transformation from classical to basal-like differentiation type [63]. This study expands the physiological functions of IRE1β beyond the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and also inspires further exploration of the physiological and pathological roles of IRE1β in more organs and cell types.

To maintain ER proteostasis, cells have evolved protein quality control systems by acting on diverse pathways, such as UPR, ERAD, and ER-phagy. To date, IRE1α is involved in crosstalk between the UPR, ERAD, and ER-phagy [16,60,97]. In the future, whether IRE1β coordinates with ERAD and ER-phagy to regulate intracellular protein homeostasis in mucosal epithelial cells needs to be studied.
