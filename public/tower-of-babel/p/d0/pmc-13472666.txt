Suture Tape Augmentation in Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction: A Meta-Analysis of Prospective and Matched Retrospective Studies
Authors: Jad Mansour, Michel Estephan, Matthew Nakouzi, Amer Sebaaly, Alan H. Daniels, Mohammad Daher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Previous biomechanical studies have shown that suture tape augmentation (STA) in anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) can improve the graft which can translate to reduced failure rate in a clinical setting. Previous meta-analysis did not show benefits to STA in patients undergoing ACLR. However, the included studies were retrospective and are subject to heterogeneity which can result in confounding bias. By including only prospective and matched retrospective studies, this meta-analysis will assess the benefits of STA in patients undergoing ACLR.Methods:Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar were accessed and explored until November 2025. The extracted data consisted of the risk arthrofibrosis, graft failure, return to sports (RTS), time to RTS, and postoperative functional outcomes (The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [KOOS] subscales and The International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC] scale).Results:Six studies met the final inclusion criteria including 414 patients in the STA group with age ranging from 19 to 36 years and 450 patients in the control group with age ranging from 20 to 39 years. There was no difference between the 2 groups in arthrofibrosis, graft failure, RTS, and time to RTS. In addition, there were no differences in postoperative KOOS pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sports, and quality of life subscales as well as IKDC.Conclusion:The results of this meta-analysis revealed that the systematic use of STA did not provide additional clinical benefits in patients older than 18 years undergoing ACLR.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures are one of the most frequent and debilitating orthopaedic injuries, with significant associations with both competitive and recreational athletes1-6. Contemporary management of ACL rupture has relied on surgical reconstruction. Failure of the primary graft occurs in around 4% to 17% of patients, and at long-term follow-up, defined as greater 10 years since the date of the surgery, the overall ACL graft failure was reported at 11.9%7. Young and active patients face an elevated rerupture risk, where studies have shown graft failure rates as high as 24.4% in this demographic8-10. Such limitations highlight the importance of surgical innovation to help mitigate graft failure rates. In response to these challenges, suture tape augmentation (STA), which uses an ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene tape, has emerged as a potential solution, designed to protect the biological graft during the early healing phase of ligamentization by acting as a load-sharing scaffold.11,12

Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that augmented grafts can withstand 12.2% to 73% greater load compared with a standard ACL graft, meanwhile reducing graft elongation by 17% to 60.2%13. Despite these biomechanical advantages, clinical evidence for using STA remains a topic of debate. A recent meta-analysis conducted by Tang et al. reported in their results that STA improved functional outcomes when compared with standard anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR).14 Other meta-analyses have also addressed this topic.15,16 Although these reviews have advanced the discussion on STA, they suffer from major limitations. Tang et al. pooled predominantly lower-quality case series and retrospective cohorts,14 and while Gao et al. restricted inclusion to comparative studies with a minimum 2-year follow-up16 and Wen et al. examined internal tension–relieving augmentation15, both drew on unmatched retrospective designs where baseline differences in age, sex, body mass index, graft characteristics, surgical technique, and concomitant lesions are unevenly distributed between groups and can confound the estimated effect of augmentation. To address this, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis restricted exclusively to prospective cohort studies and matched retrospective case-control studies, thereby reducing confounding and allowing the independent contribution of STA to be isolated. In addition, although these earlier reviews focused largely on patient-reported functional scores, our analysis also appraised complication and activity outcomes alongside validated functional measures, offering a more complete and methodologically rigorous assessment of STA in ACLR.

Material and Methods

Search Strategy

Following the PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar were accessed and explored until November 2025 using the following keywords “ACL”, “anterior cruciate ligament”, “suture,” and “tape” to find articles assessing the benefit of STA to in patients undergoing ACLR. Additional articles were added by going through reference lists from articles. One author extracted the data, and another confirmed the choice of the included articles17,18. The process is summarized in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart for article selection process.

Articles were included if they consisted of prospective comparative studies or retrospective comparative studies with matched cohorts, comparing patients undergoing ACLR with and without STA. Retrospective comparative articles with unmatched cohorts, as well as noncomparative articles were excluded. Skeletally mature/immature patients or type of graft were not considered in the selection of the articles. There were no restrictions on language. Nevertheless, all of the included articles were in English. Therefore, specific handling for non-English articles was not required. Eligibility of the included studies were determined by 2 reviewers independently.

These eligibility criteria were deliberately more restrictive than those of previous systematic reviews on this topic. Earlier reviews pooled heterogeneous and largely uncontrolled evidence and did not require that retrospective cohorts be matched on baseline characteristics. By contrast, we excluded all noncomparative studies and admitted retrospective comparative studies only when their cohorts were matched, retaining unmatched comparisons only if they were prospective. This criterion was applied specifically to control for the demographic and operative factors that are known to influence ACLR outcomes and that are unevenly distributed across groups in unmatched designs.

Data Extraction

Extracted data consisted of the outcomes that were assessed in the included trials, including the risk of arthrofibrosis, graft failure, return to sports (RTS), time to RTS, and postoperative functional outcomes (The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [KOOS] subscales and The International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC] scale).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies – of Interventions tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included nonrandomized studies by 2 authors independently19. This tool evaluates bias across 7 domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was judged as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias, and an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned accordingly. Studies deemed to have a critical risk of bias were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Review Manager 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration) was used for statistical analysis. We used odd ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval [CIs] for dichotomous data and means ± SD for continuous data. Q tests and I2 statistics were used to evaluate heterogeneity with the I2 measure used to describe the percentage of variation across studies being due to heterogeneity rather than chance20. When considerable heterogeneity was observed, defined as p ≤ 0.10 on the Q test or I2 ≥ 50%, we used a random-effects model; otherwise, a fixed-effect model was used. A priori, p ≤ 0.05 was chosen as a threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the Included Studies

Six studies met the final inclusion criteria including 864 patients, with 414 patients (48%) in the STA group and 450 patients (52%) in the control group with 5 studies using hamstring graft for ACLR and 1 study using a mix of hamstring, quadriceps, and patellar tendon21-26. All studies used the following Internal Brace (Arthrex, Naples, FL) ST for augmentation. Concomitant meniscal procedures were reported in 4 of the 6 included studies and included meniscal repair or partial meniscectomy. The age of the patients in the STA group ranged from 19 to 36 with a follow-up range of 24 to 33 months while it ranged from 20 to 39 in the control group with a follow-up range of 24 to 49 months (Appendix).

Arthrofibrosis and Graft Failure

Three studies including 274 patients reported data about arthrofibrosis (119 in the STA group and 155 in the control group), and 6 studies including 864 patients reported data about graft failure (414 in the STA group and 450 in the control group). There was no difference in arthrofibrosis (OR = 1.06; 95% CI: 0.46-2.47, p = 0.89, Fig. 2A) or graft failure (OR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.26-1.26, p = 0.17, Fig. 2B) between the 2 groups.

Two studies including 168 patients reported data about RTS and time to RTS (66 in the STA group and 102 in the control group). KOOS and IKDC

Three studies including 322 patients reported data about all the KOOS subscales (160 in the STA group and 162 in the control group), and 3 studies including 274 patients reported data about IKDC (119 in the STA group and 155 in the control group). Discussion

Although STA has been examined in several recent evidence syntheses, their conclusions diverge, and these differences are best understood because of study design rather than of any true inconsistency in the underlying biology. Tang et al. performed a meta-analysis that pooled predominantly noncomparative case series and retrospective cohorts and reported improved patient-reported outcomes with STA. However, the differences they identified in single assessment numeric evaluation and visual analog scale pain fell below the minimal clinically important difference, and the absence of a required comparator group left their pooled estimates vulnerable to selection effects14. As for Conde et al., they restricted their pooled failure analysis to hamstring autografts augmented with FiberTape and reported a reduction in graft failure, whereas Dhillon et al., synthesizing comparative studies qualitatively across mixed grafts and devices, found insufficient evidence that STA reduced failure or improved clinical outcomes27,28. This divergence could be explained by several characteristics. First, Conde et al.'s signal was concentrated in a single, homogeneous graft-device construct and rested on an OR whose confidence interval only narrowly excluded the null, and when other constructs such as FiberWire were analyzed, the difference disappeared. Second, Dhillon et al.'s search predated much of the recent comparative literature and did not pool data quantitatively, limiting its power to detect an effect that Conde et al.'s focused, contemporary meta-analysis could report. Critically, none of these 3 reviews required that retrospective cohorts be matched, so each remained susceptible to confounding by the demographic and operative factors known to influence ACLR outcomes. Our analysis was designed specifically to address this gap by including only prospective and matched retrospective studies, thereby isolating the independent effect of STA. The resulting conclusion, that STA confers no additional benefit, aligns with the reports of Dhillon et al., but it is not a simple restatement of their work: Several of the comparative studies we pooled were published after their 2023 search, and our matching criterion removes a source of bias that their qualitative synthesis could not. In our view, this makes the present estimate the more valid of the available syntheses and suggests that the favorable signals reported by Tang et al. and Conde et al. are more plausibly explained by residual confounding and construct-specific selection than by a true treatment effect of STA.

Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that independent reinforcement of soft-tissue grafts with suture tape results in significantly reduced elongation and higher ultimate failure load of the soft tissue graft29,30. These findings offer biomechanical support for the use of suture tapes in ACLR grafts. However, our study did not show a difference in the rates of arthrofibrosis or graft failures between the 2 groups. This is a novel finding as Tang et al. did not specifically look at the rate of these complications.14 Of note, the biomechanical studies demonstrating the benefits of STA are time-zero analyses are typically performed on small numbers of cadaveric specimens, and they characterize the construct only at the moment of implantation.29,30 They cannot capture ligamentization nor the clinical variables governing that process, such as graft positioning, rehabilitation protocol, and patient activity. Such factors may exert a greater influence on graft survival than the addition of a suture tape. Furthermore, the suture tape is a static structure that under repeated cyclical loading can be subject to creep, such that load is gradually transferred back to the maturing graft and its early protective, load-sharing effect is progressively lost.31,32 Because graft failure and reinjury typically occur late in the postoperative course, often after return to sport, the mechanical contribution of the tape may be negligible by the time it would matter most, offering a plausible explanation for the absence of any reduction in graft failure in our analysis.

In addition, we did not show any difference in patient reported outcomes measures (PROMs), translating clinically to no difference in rates and time to RTS. In their meta-analysis, Tang et al. reported improved PROMs in patients receiving STA with their ACLR14. They reported a mean difference of 0.17 for pain assessed using the visual analog scale, and 3.26 for the single assessment numeric evaluation score. However, both of these differences did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference making these difference statistical and not clinical14,33. This is further supported by their study not reporting a difference in RTS rates14. Furthermore, they did not limit their inclusion criteria to matched retrospective criteria making their analysis susceptible to confounding bias such as age, sex, obesity, graft characteristics, surgical technique, and associated lesions34-39. Our meta-analysis only included retrospective studies matching for those characteristics or prospective studies, which can contribute to isolating the impact of STA on the outcomes of ACLR. This makes the results of our meta-analysis more reliable.

Strengths and Limitations

This meta-analysis has several potential limitations. Only prospective and matched retrospective studies were included which limited the number of studies included in the overall analysis. However, by limiting our inclusion to only such studies, this article ends up reducing for confounding bias and thus providing a higher level of evidence. Nevertheless, these types of studies remain subject to biases inherent to their nature. Furthermore, most of the patients included in this study were skeletally mature especially since young patients face an elevated risk of reinjury after ACL reconstruction. However, the absence of patients younger than 18 years of age reflects the characteristics of the included studies rather than a restriction imposed by our methodology. Finally, we could not assess for publication bias because of the low number of included studies. However, if publication bias was present, it would inflate the apparent benefit of STA, which already was seen here as not being effective.

Conclusion

The results of this meta-analysis revealed that STA of ACL reconstruction did not have additional benefits compared with no augmentation. STA did not reduce failure rates or improve PROMs.
