Introduction

S. apiospermum (the asexual form of Pseudallescheria boydii) is a saprophytic filamentous fungus commonly found in temperate climates, typically inhabiting nutrient-rich, poorly ventilated environments such as soil, sewage, and decaying organic matter [1]. As an opportunistic pathogen, S. apiospermum produces abundant airborne conidia, which facilitates widespread infection in high-risk populations, including cystic fibrosis patients and lung transplant recipients. The fungus can colonize various human tissues and organs, including the lungs, nervous system, skin, joints, digestive tract, thyroid, and kidneys, with a mortality rate ranging from 40% to 95% [2]. Pulmonary infections caused by S. apiospermum often present with nonspecific symptoms such as cough and expectoration. In this case report, we detail the diagnostic and therapeutic course of a patient admitted for recurrent hemoptysis. This case was first identified based on morphological abnormalities in BALF, followed by confirmatory microbial culture and sequencing. Our findings aim to enhance laboratory personnel’s morphological recognition of S. apiospermum and to explore novel methods for utilizing lavage fluid morphology for early diagnosis and evaluation of treatment efficacy.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old woman initially presented with hemoptysis four years ago and was diagnosed with bronchiectasis complicated by infection at a local hospital. She experienced recurrent hemoptysis thereafter. One week before admission, she developed recurrent episodes of coughing up fresh blood with clots, accompanied by fatigue and chest soreness and discomfort. Upon hospitalization, her infection was empirically treated with cefmetazole sodium and bromhexine hydrochloride for expectoration, but the therapeutic response was suboptimal. She had no history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, heart disease, genetic disorders, nor did she have a family history of smoking or alcohol consumption.

Physical examination: The patient was conscious but mildly lethargic. Facial edema was absent, with no jugular vein distension. Coarse breath sounds were noted bilaterally, without obvious dry or moist rales. Heart rhythm was regular, with normal heart sounds and no murmurs. The abdomen was soft and flat, with no tenderness or rebound tenderness. No edema was observed in the lower extremities, and pathological reflexes were absent.

Case summary

The patient was diagnosed with bronchiectasis complicated by infection four years ago, followed by recurrent episodes of hemoptysis. One week before admission, she experienced renewed episodes of coughing up fresh blood, accompanied by fatigue and chest discomfort, with no improvement after self-administering traditional Chinese medicine. The patient had no underlying diseases, no signs of immunosuppression, and no history of trauma, drowning, or exposure to contaminated environments; her immunological function was normal. We conclude that long-standing bronchiectasis with recurrent infection and hemoptysis served as high-risk factors predisposing the patient to S. apiospermum infection. Morphological abnormalities in BALF were first identified, followed by confirmatory microbial culture and mNGS, which detected a mixed infection involving S. apiospermum and Bordetella species. Immediate treatment with voriconazole was associated with a favorable prognosis.

Discussion

S. apiospermum is a conditionally pathogenic saprophytic fungus with strong invasiveness and pathogenicity. While it generally does not cause disease in humans or animals under normal circumstances, it is prone to infecting immunocompromised individuals, such as those with AIDS, hematologic malignancies, organ transplants, or those on prolonged courses of glucocorticoids or immunosuppressants. It can also affect immunocompetent individuals with predisposing factors such as trauma, osteomyelitis, pneumonia, arthritis, sewage-related drowning, or ocular infections. The infection rate has shown a gradual upward trend [3], particularly among drowning patients. Fisher et al. [4]. first described a drowning syndrome characterized by brain abscesses caused by S. apiospermum infection after drowning. Mursch et al. [5] reported a case of multiple brain abscesses in a drowning patient, likely due to secondary infection from fungal exposure in contaminated water. Thus, some researchers propose drowning as an increasingly common cause of S. apiospermum infection [6–8]. In this case, the patient had no history of drowning, and the potential etiology warrants further exploration, possibly involving environmental exposure (e.g., contact with contaminated materials or inhalation). The fungal morphology and clinical features of S. apiospermum infections closely resemble those of fusariosis, aspergillosis, and other hyalohyphomycete infections. Additionally, its clinical manifestations vary widely depending on the host and infection site, to frequent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment [9]. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis requires comprehensive methods, such as fungal culture (colony characteristics), post-staining microscopic morphology, mass spectrometry, and genetic sequencing. In immunocompetent individuals, S. apiospermum pneumonia typically occurs in patients with underlying pulmonary conditions (e.g., bronchiectasis, old tuberculosis, sarcoidosis). The fungus can cause localized deep infections, spread to adjacent tissues, disseminate to distant organs, or even result in systemic involvement [10]. Notably, S. apiospermum infection may exacerbate hemoptysis in this population [11].

The other pathogen involved in this mixed infection, Bordetella, is an emerging opportunistic pathogen primarily transmitted via respiratory droplets. It can cause severe invasive diseases in immunocompromised patients [12, 13]. In healthy individuals, it may induce pertussis-like respiratory symptoms, as well as bacteremia, meningitis, endocarditis, pericarditis, pneumonia, and arthritis. Bordetella can be detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), 16 S rDNA sequencing, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assays [14], as well as mNGS. Among these, mNGS enables early and accurate screening of the pathogen, maintaining high sensitivity even in the middle-to-late stages of infection.

BALF serves as a critical diagnostic and therapeutic tool for pulmonary infections and other lung diseases. Collected from the deep lung parenchyma, BALF reflects the true pathological state and is widely used to diagnose respiratory disorders. Bronchoscopy combined with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) plays a decisive role in diagnosing and alleviating respiratory symptoms [15]. Previous studies suggest that Gram stain smears, Wright-Giemsa staining, and microscopic examination of BALF in clinically suspected patients can act as frontline tools for identifying fungal co-infections in pneumonia. Integrating next-generation sequencing, microbial culture, and relevant biomarkers may further facilitate early diagnosis. In this case, morphological abnormalities were first detected in BALF, followed by confirmation by culture and sequencing. In China, morphological analysis of body fluids has gained increasing attention in recent years. However, there remains a scarcity of specialized technicians capable of performing such analyses, including lavage fluid morphology, highlighting an urgent need to address this research gap. In a reported case of S. apiospermum infection secondary to trauma [16], cultures on Columbia blood agar and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) at 25 °C and 5% CO₂ demonstrated rapid fungal growth: initially white cotton-like colonies gradually turned brown at the center and expanded outward over days. Similarly, we cultured bronchoscopic brush specimens on Columbia blood agar and SDA plates. After three days, white colonies appeared on Columbia blood agar, while white flocculent growth was observed on SDA, consistent with findings from prior cases.

In histopathological specimens, all hyphae are non-pigmented, slender, thick-walled, and septate, with a diameter of 2–4 mm [17]. Scedosporium species are indistinguishable from other hyaline hyphomycetes, including Aspergillus spp. and Fusarium spp. Radiologically, infections caused by S. apiospermum lack specific imaging features and resemble other infections [18], often leading to misdiagnosis as pulmonary Aspergillosis or tuberculosis. Chest CT findings can be diverse, including bronchiectasis, nodules, consolidations, and cavities. These imaging manifestations often coexist, making it difficult to distinguish them radiographically from Aspergillomas [19]. Therefore, they cannot be differentiated clinically or histopathologically except by fungal culture [20]. Some literature suggests immunological cross-reactivity between Aspergillus and S. apiospermum [21], but research in this area remains limited. Healthcare professionals are insufficiently familiar with the clinical manifestations and morphological characteristics of infections caused by this fungus, and laboratory identification is time-consuming. Additionally, its resistance to multiple antifungal agents poses significant therapeutic challenges for clinicians. Due to the lack of clear consensus criteria and well-defined treatment options for most respiratory fungal diseases, clinicians face the challenge of distinguishing between harmless respiratory colonization and destructive fungal infections. In the former scenario, initiating treatment may impose unnecessary burdens on patients, including potential drug side effects, while in the latter, delayed intervention could lead to irreversible pulmonary damage. It is imperative to better characterize the clinical manifestations of respiratory fungal diseases and identify patient populations that would benefit from targeted therapies [23]. In pharmacological studies by Wang Hong et al. [24], analysis of in vitro susceptibility data for voriconazole, amphotericin B, flucytosine, fluconazole, itraconazole, and ketoconazole revealed that voriconazole exhibits the highest sensitivity, followed by posaconazole. Itraconazole demonstrates frequent resistance mutations, while amphotericin B shows the poorest efficacy [25, 26]. However, research by Sian Bentley BPharm et al. [27] further demonstrated that newer triazoles-voriconazole and posaconazole-exhibit synergistic effects with human polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) after 24 h. These synergistic or additive effects, combined with the intrinsic antifungal activity of PMNs, suggest that combination therapy with two or three agents may outperform monotherapy. Additionally, voriconazole’s low minimum inhibitory concentration and high bioavailability make it a promising candidate for combination therapy, even in immunocompromised hosts refractory to most antifungals for treating S. apiospermum infections [26–28]. However, treatment remains challenging, as many isolates exhibit intrinsic resistance to most antifungals, particularly amphotericin B. When available, antimicrobial susceptibility testing of cultured specimens is critical for determining optimal antifungal regimens. Although voriconazole is effective in some patients, clinical failures with this agent has been reported [22].

In summary, infections caused by S. apiospermum are often missed or misdiagnosed due to their non-specific clinical manifestations, leading to delayed treatment and poorer prognosis. Our case highlights the value of identifying cytomorphological abnormalities in BALF first, aiming to enhance laboratory personnel’s morphological recognition of S. apiospermum during routine examinations, improve detection rates, accelerate the diagnostic process, facilitate timely and effective treatment, and ultimately reduce patient mortality.
