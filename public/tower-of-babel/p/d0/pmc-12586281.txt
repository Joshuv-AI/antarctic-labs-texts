Moisturising Gloves as a Solution for Occupational Skin Health: Advances and Challenges
Authors: Sher Wei How, Janarthanan Supramaniam, Thet Thet Htar, Bey Fen Leo, Sivakumar Manickam, Bey Hing Goh, Siah Ying Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtended glove usage is crucial in various occupational settings to safeguard workers and maintain hygiene standards. However, prolonged wear creates an occlusive environment that disrupts normal skin evaporation, leading to temporary overhydration. This reversal of the diffusion gradient facilitates the penetration of residual soaps and alcohol from hand hygiene practices, which can deplete skin moisture and cause irritation. Moisturising agents have been integrated into gloves to address occupational dry skin, as conventional lotions often suffer from low compliance and incompatibility with latex. Their selection requires careful optimisation to ensure effective skin penetration without compromising glove integrity or introducing manufacturing complexities. Additionally, variations in activation stimuli among individuals must be taken into account to ensure consistent efficacy. This review discusses advancements in moisturising glove technologies, including interlayer coatings, direct surface applications, matrix integration, microencapsulation and hydrogel‐based controlled‐release systems. Despite these innovations, research gaps remain in standardising hydration assessment methods, evaluating real‐world performance and comparing the efficacy of moisturising gloves with conventional skincare methods. Addressing these challenges through further clinical studies is crucial for refining the design and effectiveness of moisturising gloves in occupational applications.Keywords: dry skin, functional gloves, hydration, moisturising gloves, occupational skin health

Background

Occupational skin diseases are a significant yet often underrecognized health concern, particularly among workers exposed to hazardous substances or environments. According to the 2015 U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, skin diseases accounted for approximately 15% of the 187 900 reported non‐fatal occupational illnesses, ranking second only to the broad ‘all other illnesses’ category, which excludes skin, respiratory, poisoning and hearing‐related conditions. Notably, the education and health services (20.5%) and manufacturing (14.8%) sectors were the most affected, likely due to frequent exposure to irritants, allergens and prolonged wet work [1]. Wet work, which encompasses frequent hand washing, repeated use of sanitiser and extended glove wearing, is a key contributor to skin barrier disruption in occupational settings.

This was particularly evident during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when frontline healthcare workers (HCWs) experienced heightened exposure to these practices. A questionnaire‐based study in China reported that 74.5% of frontline HCWs experienced hand skin damage, with dryness or tightness being the most common symptom (70.3%), followed by tenderness, itching and pain [2]. Similarly, an Irish study found that 75.4% of frontline HCWs reported dry skin, underscoring the widespread impact of glove‐related skin conditions [3]. These symptoms reflect epidermal barrier dysfunction, which facilitates the percutaneous penetration of irritants and allergens, increasing susceptibility to contact dermatitis [4].

Skin dryness is often the earliest and most prominent indicator of barrier disruption, preceding the onset of contact dermatitis. Preventing dryness is therefore essential to preserving skin integrity, ensuring wearer comfort and mitigating disease progression. While emollients are the standard recommendation for managing occupational skin dryness, Soltanipoor et al. reported a low usage rate among 501 HCWs, with 80% never applying hand cream before or during shifts, primarily due to concerns about interference with glove use [5, 6]. Greasy residues from creams can hinder the donning process, reduce grip and impair manual dexterity, which are critical in many occupational settings. Moreover, emollients containing petrolatum or mineral oil may diffuse into the glove matrix, leading to polymer swelling that reduces the glove's tensile strength and increases its permeability to chemicals and pathogens [7, 8]. This degradation is most pronounced in latex gloves; however, nitrile gloves, while more resistant, may still be affected by repeated or prolonged exposure [8].

To address the limitations of conventional moisturisers in occupational settings, moisturising gloves have emerged as a promising solution that incorporates hydrating agents through various technologies, including multilayer infusions, humectant‐loaded coatings, microencapsulation systems and hydrogel layers [9, 10, 11, 12]. These gloves are engineered to provide sustained hydration, reduce transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and support skin barrier function during wear, without compromising glove performance. This review examines the physiological impact of glove occlusion on skin health, outlines critical design considerations for the development of effective moisturising gloves and evaluates current technologies in terms of their mechanisms, benefits and limitations. It concludes by discussing key challenges and future directions to improve the efficacy, usability and adoption of moisturising gloves in occupational environments. While this review centres on disposable gloves used in healthcare settings, the underlying principles of skin barrier disruption and the challenges associated with moisturising glove use are broadly applicable across other occupational and non‐occupational settings.

Impact of Glove Occlusion on Skin Health

Gloves are typically made from hydrophobic materials such as natural rubber latex, nitrile butadiene rubber and polyvinyl chloride. These materials effectively repel aqueous solutions, including blood, bodily fluids and various chemicals, thereby providing protection against biological contaminants and hazardous substances [13]. Under typical physiological conditions, skin moisture constantly evaporates into the surrounding air due to the water gradient between the hydrated epidermis and the relatively dry external environment. However, glove occlusion interferes with this natural TEWL process by retaining both moisture and body heat within the glove.

The accumulation of evaporated moisture, along with sweat, results in an increase in skin pH and excessive hydration of the stratum corneum. This hyperhydration disrupts the normal water gradient, potentially facilitating the diffusion of residual chemicals into deeper layers of the skin [14]. Additionally, the increase in skin temperature caused by heat retention can expand skin pores, further enhance permeability and promote the penetration of chemicals. However, this diffusion primarily impacts lipophilic substances, as they can more readily penetrate the lipid‐rich cell membranes of keratinocytes within the epidermis [15, 16].

In addition to chemical absorption, prolonged glove occlusion can also affect the skin's microbiome. The elevated skin pH and temperature can disrupt the balance of resident microflora, which play a critical role in the skin's defence against pathogens. Notably, individuals with hand eczema have been found to show a 1.72‐fold increase in Staphylococcus aureus colony‐forming units following glove occlusion, emphasising the potential impact on skin health and infection risk [17]. Wetzky et al. investigated both the immediate and prolonged effects of glove occlusion on TEWL. Their findings showed that TEWL peaked 30 min after a single occlusion event but returned to baseline within 3 h. Moreover, prolonged glove use, 4 h daily over seven consecutive days, did not result in a sustained increase in TEWL, indicating that occlusion alone does not progressively impair skin barrier function [18]. However, when occlusion is combined with hand hygiene practices, its impact becomes more pronounced. Antonov et al. reported that handwashing with an antibacterial cleanser followed by glove occlusion led to a cumulative increase in TEWL, significantly greater than either intervention alone [19]. Similarly, Angelova‐Fischer et al. demonstrated that repeated exposure to n‐propanol and/or sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS), especially under occlusion, not only increased TEWL but also markedly depleted natural moisturising factors (NMFs) [20]. This NMF depletion under occlusive conditions highlights an elevated risk of skin barrier disruption and moisture loss.

These findings highlight the role of occlusion in increasing skin permeability, thereby amplifying the effects of residual chemicals on the skin. Given the necessity of both glove use and hand hygiene in occupational settings, implementing strategies to mitigate their cumulative impact is essential. Current guidelines from the European Society of Contact Dermatitis recommend strategies such as minimising glove‐wearing duration, using cotton liners during extended use, reducing handwashing frequency and opting for alcohol‐based hand sanitisers when hands are not visibly soiled [21]. In this context, moisturising gloves infused with barrier‐repairing and hydrating agents may offer a convenient, complementary approach to maintaining skin health during glove use.

Key Considerations in the Design of Moisturising Gloves

Moisturising gloves are engineered to deliver hydrating agents to the skin during glove use. These agents are either coated onto the glove's inner surface or embedded within the glove material. They are typically activated by physiological stimuli such as body heat, perspiration or mechanical friction. For these gloves to be both functionally effective and safe for occupational use, several design considerations must be addressed: skin permeability, material compatibility and the variability of stimulus‐responsive release mechanisms.

Skin as a Barrier

The stratum corneum comprises corneocytes embedded within a lipid matrix rich in ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids, collectively forming a highly selective barrier that regulates water loss and protects against external agents [22]. Effective moisturising agents must overcome this barrier without compromising skin integrity. There are three main skin permeation routes: intercellular, intracellular and follicular (Figure 2). The intercellular route is the most pertinent for moisturising gloves, as active ingredients primarily diffuse through the lipid layers between corneocytes. Hydrophilic compounds pass through polar head groups, while lipophilic molecules penetrate via lipid chains [23]. In contrast, the intracellular route requires active diffusion through keratinised cells and presents greater resistance to penetration. The follicular route contributes minimally in palm skin, where hair follicles are scarce [24].

Three mechanisms of active agent permeation across the stratum corneum. The intercellular pathway involves diffusion through the lipid matrix, the intracellular pathway requires transport within and between corneocytes and the follicular pathway utilises hair follicles as entry points.

For optimal passive diffusion, moisturising agents should exhibit optimal lipophilicity and a molecular weight of less than 500 Da [25]. Yao et al. demonstrated that nanosuspensions of 250 nm penetrated up to 60 μm in the skin after 1 h, while 1000 nm particles were restricted to superficial layers [26]. Similarly, Liu et al. reported that 299 nm nanoparticles exhibited a 1.84‐fold higher permeation flux compared to 906 nm particles when delivered in gel formulations [27]. Penetration can be further enhanced by incorporating chemical enhancers that act via ‘push’ (creating a solubility gradient) and ‘pull’ (modifying the lipid matrix to reduce resistance) mechanisms [23, 28, 29, 30]. The selection of safe enhancers is especially important under occlusion, where prolonged exposure may intensify their effects. Propylene glycol (a humectant), linoleic acid (an emollient) and urea (a keratolytic and humectant) provide added skin benefits, whereas, harsher solvents like ethanol should be avoided due to their potential to dry the skin under occlusive conditions [23, 31].

Compatibility With Glove Materials

Moisturising agents can be incorporated into gloves either by embedding them within the polymer matrix or by applying them as a surface coating. Each method presents distinct advantages and challenges related to material compatibility, formulation stability and release performance. Matrix embedding simplifies integration into existing manufacturing lines without significantly increasing production costs. However, certain hydrating agents possess reactive functional groups that may interfere with polymer crosslinking, potentially compromising the mechanical strength and elasticity of the glove or forming secondary interactions that limit active release to the skin [32]. Furthermore, vulcanisation temperatures exceeding 100°C can degrade thermolabile compounds, such as proteins and botanical extracts, while post‐curing leaching may remove water‐soluble moisturising agents, reducing the hydrating efficacy of the final product [33]. Uniform dispersion throughout the glove matrix also increases the risk of unintended migration to the outer surface, which may alter tactile properties or raise concerns about contamination.

Surface coating offers a more controlled alternative, allowing moisturising agents to be applied selectively to the skin‐contacting surface after glove formation. This approach preserves the integrity of heat‐sensitive compounds and avoids disruption of the polymerisation process. However, adhesion between the coating and glove substrate must be sufficiently robust to prevent premature detachment or migration before use. Formulations often incorporate polymers that are compatible with the glove substrate to enhance mechanical stability and minimise cracking during wear. Controlled‐release systems such as microencapsulation and hydrogel matrices support sustained hydration while preserving grip and wearer comfort [34]. By encapsulating oil‐based moisturisers, these systems prevent direct interaction with the glove material, mitigating risks of swelling, brittleness or degradation [35, 36]. Alternatively, chemically inert non‐polar emollients, such as dimethicone or ceramides, can be employed to ensure effective skin hydration without compromising glove integrity.

Limitations of Stimulus Dependency

Most moisturising gloves rely on water as a trigger for the release of incorporated hydrating agents. During manufacturing, these agents are typically dehydrated to enhance adhesion and then rehydrated upon glove wear through TEWL and perspiration. This rehydration generates a concentration gradient that enables passive diffusion into the skin. However, interindividual variability in TEWL and sweating, due to factors such as age, sex and skin condition, may influence activation efficiency. For example, TEWL is generally higher in women than in men until midlife, and further elevated in individuals with impaired skin barriers, such as those with atopic dermatitis or palmar hyperhidrosis, potentially enhancing the release of hydrating agents [37, 38, 39]. In contrast, individuals with normal TEWL and minimal perspiration may experience limited activation, reducing the glove's hydrating efficacy.

In microencapsulated systems, mechanical forces such as pulling, pressure or friction are used to rupture capsule walls during donning or glove use. However, release efficiency depends on task intensity and may be inconsistent across users. Some technologies also employ thermoresponsive or pH‐responsive systems. For instance, the occlusive environment of gloves can raise hand temperature above 34°C, triggering the release of actives from temperature‐sensitive hydrogels [40]. However, skin temperature fluctuates with age, gender, and ambient conditions, while skin pH (typically 4.1–5.8) is modulated by intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including cosmetic use [41, 42, 43, 44, 45].

To address these challenges, expanding activation thresholds may help accommodate diverse users and usage environments. Integrating multiple triggers, such as combining mechanical and pH‐responsive mechanisms, can further enhance reliability and performance. However, careful formulation and encapsulation design are crucial to prevent premature release due to environmental stressors such as humidity, temperature fluctuations or mechanical impact during storage and handling. A well‐balanced activation profile ensures consistent hydration upon glove use while maintaining product stability prior to application.

Lotion‐Centred Glove

The first medical gloves incorporating a moisturising function, patented in 1994, feature a three‐layer design comprising a non‐porous latex outer layer, a lotion‐containing middle layer and an inner layer with micropores that facilitate the transfer of lotion to the skin (Figure 3). To prevent premature leakage of the lotion, a temporary sealing layer is applied to the inner layer, designed to rupture in response to stimuli such as temperature, pressure or perspiration during glove wear. Additionally, a protective layer is included to safeguard the outer layer and act as a cushioning element for the moisturising glove [9].

Layers of the moisturising surgical glove invented by DeFina. The blue background highlights the three core layers, with the middle layer saturated with lotions. The grey‐coloured layers represent additional components that enhance the glove's functionality (adapted from [9]).

Despite enabling sustained hydration, this multilayer design increases glove thickness, potentially compromising flexibility and dexterity, critical attributes in healthcare settings [56]. Premature rupture of the sealing layer during transport or storage is also a risk due to heat, pressure or moisture. Additionally, excessive lubrication of the inner surface may impair grip, while prolonged contact between oil‐based lotions and latex or nitrile may degrade the glove materials [57]. These limitations suggest that such designs may be more suitable for non‐occupational or cosmetic use.

Direct Application of Moisturising Coatings

Most of the identified patents employ direct application of hydrating agents to the glove's skin‐contacting surface. This design limits the exposure of bioactive compounds to the external environment, thereby reducing the risk of inducing irritation or allergic reactions in patients during contact with gloved HCWs, while also preserving grip and friction. However, it may still present a risk of irritant or allergic responses for the glove wearer, depending on individual sensitivity and the specific formulation used. The hydrating layer typically contains one or more active compounds that function as humectants (drawing moisture from the environment), occlusives (reducing TEWL) or emollients (filling intercellular gaps to improve skin smoothness). These agents may be naturally derived, synthetically produced or formulated as combinations thereof.

Aloe vera is widely used in moisturising formulations for its hydrating and skin‐soothing properties. Chou developed a glove by coating the inner surface with a 20% aloe vera solution, which was fully dehydrated during processing and rehydrated upon glove wear via skin moisture [46]. However, as this mechanism depends on the wearer's hydration status, Loo et al. proposed a modified design incorporating a moist interior film composed of aloe vera, glycerol and water, enabling continuous hydration independent of individual skin moisture levels [51].

Freeze‐dried aloe powder has also been used as a substitute for cornstarch in powdered gloves, reducing the risk of skin irritation. Beyond its moisturising properties, the powder acts as a sweat‐absorbing agent and a donning lubricant, improving user comfort [48]. However, following the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ban on powdered medical gloves in 2016, glove technologies have since shifted towards powder‐free alternatives to reduce allergen exposure [58].

In addition to aloe vera, other botanicals such as chamomile and nopal have been incorporated for their hydrating effects. Common humectants (e.g., glycerin) and emollients (e.g., dimethicone) are frequently used to enhance water retention and improve the skin's barrier function. Some formulations also include antioxidants (e.g., vitamin C), exfoliants (e.g., salicylic acid) or skin‐softening agents (e.g., cyclomethicone) to provide additional dermatological benefits. Multi‐component blends, such as those by Shao and Howe et al., demonstrate improved skin conductance and comfort, highlighting the potential of synergistic combinations [52, 53].

Proteins, such as sericin derived from silkworms ( Bombyx mori ), have been explored for moisturising glove coatings due to their multifunctional skin benefits. As a NMF mimic, sericin also exhibits antimicrobial, anti‐ageing and wound‐healing properties. Hasegawa employed ultrasonic oscillation to extract sericin and applied it to the inner glove surface via spraying or dipping. This method enabled uniform adhesion without the need for additional binding agents [54].

While certain proteins adhere well to glove surfaces, polymers are more commonly used to improve coating stability, adhesion and overall performance. Silicone, polyurethane and acrylic polymers serve as carriers for moisturising agents and enhance glove properties such as durability, waterproofing and ease of donning [10, 59, 60]. For instance, Eng et al. combined glycerol and sodium lactate with a silicone emulsion, while Modha et al. incorporated petrolatum, shea butter and glycerol into an acrylic polymer [10, 60].

Additional ingredients such as preservatives, emulsifiers and surfactants are commonly incorporated to enhance coating stability and physical performance. For example, propylparaben, a commonly used preservative in personal care products, helps prevent microbial contamination. It is one of several additives, including thickeners, emulsifiers and non‐ionic surfactants, in the formulation developed by Williams to ensure stability and prolong shelf life [61].

Direct coating is widely adopted for its simplicity, cost‐effectiveness and scalability. By applying moisturising agents to the glove's inner surface, this approach ensures immediate skin contact and promotes hydration during wear. Despite these advantages, the effectiveness of direct coatings can be inconsistent, as the release of bioactive compounds relies on moisture stimuli such as perspiration and skin hydration levels. Additionally, plant‐derived ingredients may exhibit batch‐to‐batch variability due to differences in botanical source and extraction methods, and carry risks of microbial or chemical contamination, necessitating stringent quality control [62, 63]. Protein‐based coatings, such as sericin, are prone to degradation under adverse storage and transportation conditions. Formulation optimisation is therefore essential to improve the long‐term stability and efficacy of these coatings.

Microencapsulation and Hydrogels

Some glove technologies employ microencapsulation to entrap moisturising compounds before applying them to the glove surface. This strategy enables controlled release during wear and protects bioactive ingredients from premature degradation or interaction with the glove matrix [64]. It is particularly useful for incorporating oil‐based agents, which offer enhanced skin penetration but may compromise glove materials if left unencapsulated [11].

One patented formulation uses polyoxymethylene urea microcapsules embedded in a polyurethane matrix to deliver Panalene (emollient), vitamin A palmitate, vitamin E acetate and fragrance. Upon glove wear, the microcapsules attached to the inner glove surface rupture, releasing the active contents while also improving both wet and dry donning [47]. Schaller further developed melamine‐formaldehyde microcapsules containing a blend of chemical and botanical agents including coconut triglycerides, avocado oil, jojoba oil and olive oil. These are engineered to undergo gradual rupture under mechanical stress, allowing for sustained hydration [11].

Hydrogel‐based coatings offer another approach to sustained moisturisation. Janssen et al. designed a glove with a hydrogel layer formed via the catalytic reaction of hydroxyethyl acrylate and acrylic monomers with melamine‐formaldehyde. Active ingredients can be loaded either during the polymer crosslinking phase or post‐synthesis by swelling the hydrogel with an aqueous solution of actives, followed by drying to trap the compounds. These hydrogels respond to moisture stimuli, such as sweat or TEWL, by expanding their pores and gradually releasing the encapsulated agents [12].

Existing Gaps in Moisturising Glove Research

Despite progress in moisturising glove technologies, there are still considerable research gaps in assessing their effectiveness and real‐world applications. While current innovations aim to alleviate glove‐induced dry skin, there is a lack of standardised methods for evaluating hydration benefits, a shortage of comparative studies against traditional skin care approaches and unresolved issues concerning the long‐term effects and potential unintended consequences. Filling these gaps through comprehensive clinical studies and standardised testing protocols is essential to ensuring that moisturising gloves deliver effective skin protection in occupational settings.

Limited Clinical Data

Despite increasing interest in moisturising gloves, there is a lack of comprehensive clinical data confirming their effectiveness in preventing occupational dry skin, especially over the long term. Most of the available research is found in patents, which offer limited information on formulation specifics and efficacy testing, as these details are often regarded as proprietary trade secrets by glove manufacturers. One of the few long‐term studies conducted by West and Zhu evaluated the effectiveness of aloe vera‐coated latex gloves in reducing occupational dry skin among factory assembly‐line workers. Participants wore the gloves for 8 h per day over 30 days, followed by a 30‐day break and an additional 10 days of repeated use. The study reported favourable outcomes, including improved skin integrity, reduced redness and a decrease in fine wrinkles [65]. However, its reliance on qualitative assessments rather than quantitative measurements highlights a broader limitation in moisturising glove research, where subjective evaluations may raise concerns about accuracy and reproducibility.

Challenges in Assessing Hydration

A significant challenge in evaluating the efficacy of moisturising gloves is the transient overhydration effect that occurs immediately after glove removal, resulting from the accumulation of TEWL and sweat [66]. This temporary moisture buildup can create a misleading perception of enhanced skin hydration, making it difficult to distinguish between short‐term occlusive effects and genuine improvements in skin barrier function when assessed qualitatively. To ensure accurate evaluations, standardised quantitative measurements using a corneometer and tewameter are recommended. Furthermore, a 20–30 min acclimatisation period before and after glove removal should be observed to minimise transient hydration effects, thereby providing more reliable data on the true impact of moisturising gloves on skin health [67]. Establishing standardised methodologies would enhance the scientific rigour of moisturising glove studies and strengthen the evidence supporting their clinical efficacy.

Sustainability of Hydration in Real‐World Applications

Another important research gap is investigating whether the hydration effect of moisturising gloves persists over time, especially with frequent hand hygiene practices. Repeated handwashing with soaps or alcohol‐based sanitisers may remove moisturising agents, potentially diminishing the benefits provided by the gloves. Additional research is required to determine whether combining moisturising gloves with mild cleansers improves their effectiveness or if certain formulations are more resistant to wash‐off. Moreover, in professions like healthcare, where gloves are frequently changed between tasks and patients, it remains uncertain whether the moisturising agents have enough time to fully absorb and provide skin benefits before a new pair is worn [68]. Determining the minimum duration of wear necessary for clinically significant hydration is crucial for optimising their effectiveness.

Transfer Risks and Safety Concerns

A critical yet underexplored concern regarding the use of moisturising gloves in healthcare is the potential transfer of their formulations to contact surfaces and patients. Micropunctures from prolonged glove use raise concerns about the migration of moisturising agents or additives to the outer surface, potentially compromising aseptic procedures or triggering allergic or irritant reactions in patients. In food and manufacturing industries, the transfer of glove‐based formulations may result in product contamination, potentially leading to foodborne illness or undesirable chemical interactions that compromise product safety and efficacy [69].

Simultaneously, glove wearers remain in continuous contact with the moisturising formulation. While such coatings may reduce direct exposure to glove‐associated irritants and sensitizers (e.g., latex proteins or chemical accelerators), they may also introduce new risks. Many formulations incorporate botanical extracts containing phytoconstituents known to elicit ACD [70]. Protein‐based coatings, such as sericin, may trigger immediate‐type hypersensitivity reactions, including contact urticaria and protein contact dermatitis [71]. Fragrances, commonly included in patented formulations to enhance user appeal, belong to one of the most common allergen families implicated in ACD [72]. Additionally, the presence of penetration enhancers such as fatty alcohol ethoxylates, Teric N100 and limonene may further increase the risk by promoting deeper dermal absorption of these allergens [10, 11, 51]. These enhancers can also facilitate the entry of external irritants, such as soaps or disinfectants applied post‐glove removal, thereby potentially exacerbating skin barrier disruption.

Without a thorough safety evaluation, moisturising gloves may represent a double‐edged sword, offering therapeutic benefits while simultaneously posing potential risks to both users and their surrounding environments. Rigorous safety assessments, including dermal sensitisation testing and real‐world contamination studies, are essential to ensure that these gloves deliver effective protection without unintended consequences.

Moisturising Glove Versus Other Prevention Strategies

There is limited comparative research evaluating whether moisturising gloves offer superior protection against occupational dry skin compared to conventional approaches, such as the use of glove liners and regular application of hand creams [21]. These conventional strategies, as recommended by the ESCD guidelines, have been shown to effectively reduce the incidence of occupational dermatitis when implemented alongside routine glove‐wearing practices [21, 73, 74, 75]. It remains uncertain whether these gloves offer a more effective and convenient solution, if their benefits are comparable to, or potentially less effective than conventional methods. Direct clinical comparisons are lacking, making it challenging to determine whether moisturising gloves should be prioritised over traditional hand care practices for individuals at risk of occupational skin conditions. Addressing this gap would offer clearer guidance for occupational health recommendations.

Need for Standardised Testing in Moisturising Gloves

Unlike antimicrobial gloves, which are required to meet ASTM D7907 standards to demonstrate a specific level of bactericidal activity, moisturising gloves currently lack a standardised testing method to evaluate their hydration efficacy [76]. There is no established benchmark to define what level of hydration is clinically significant in preventing occupational dry skin. The lack of standardised efficacy testing raises concerns regarding product claims, as manufacturers are not obligated to meet specific criteria before marketing their gloves as ‘moisturising’. Developing a standardised testing framework would facilitate objective comparisons across various formulations and assist in validating their long‐term efficacy for occupational skin health. Additionally, this approach would strengthen regulatory oversight and elevate the scientific rigour of research within this domain.

Conclusion

Moisturising gloves may offer a practical alternative to conventional skin care strategies in occupational settings by providing continuous hydration during wear and reducing workflow interruptions associated with frequent hand cream application. These gloves are designed to harness the physiological effects of occlusion, such as perspiration and elevated skin temperature, to enhance the delivery of active ingredients that support hydration, barrier repair and moisture retention. Since the first patent was filed in 1994, numerous formulations have emerged, although most of these technologies have been described in patents rather than in peer‐reviewed literature. Consequently, robust data on formulation, skin compatibility and long‐term clinical performance remain limited.

Despite increasing innovation, moisturising gloves are not yet widely adopted and their long‐term safety and efficacy remain unclear. The absence of standardised methods for hydration assessment and the scarcity of real‐world studies present significant challenges to evidence‐based implementation. To advance this field, peer‐reviewed studies must complement patent‐driven innovation by providing independent validation and clinical insight. Collaborative efforts among dermatologists, formulation scientists and industry stakeholders will be essential to close existing knowledge gaps, ensure user safety and establish moisturising gloves as a credible strategy for maintaining skin health in high‐risk occupational settings.
