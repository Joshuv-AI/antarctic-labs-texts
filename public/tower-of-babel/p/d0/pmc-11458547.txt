Role of nucleobase-specific interactions in the binding and bending of DNA by human male sex determination factor SRY
Authors: Joseph D Racca, Yen-Shan Chen, Adam R Brabender, Umberto Battistin, Michael A Weiss, Millie M Georgiadis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractY-chromosome–encoded master transcription factor SRY functions in the embryogenesis of therian mammals to initiate male development. Through interactions of its conserved high-mobility group box within a widened DNA minor groove, SRY and related Sox factors induce sharp bends at specific DNA target sites. Here, we present the crystal structure of the SRY high-mobility group domain bound to a DNA site containing consensus element 5′-ATTGTT. The structure contains three complexes in the asymmetric unit; in each complex, SRY forms 10 hydrogen bonds with minor-groove base atoms in 5′-CATTGT/ACAATG-3′, shifting the recognition sequence by one base pair (italics). These nucleobase interactions involve conserved residues Arg7, Asn10, and Tyr74 on one side of intercalated Ile13 (the cantilever) and Arg20, Asn32, and Ser36 on the other. Unlike the less-bent NMR structure, DNA bend angles (69–84°) of the distinct box–DNA complexes are similar to those observed in homologous Sox domain-DNA structures. Electrophoretic studies indicate that respective substitutions of Asn32, Ser36, or Tyr74 by Ala exhibit slightly attenuated specific DNA-binding affinity and bend angles (70–73°) relative to WT (79°). By contrast, respective substitutions of Arg7, Asn10, or Arg20 by Ala markedly impaired DNA-binding affinity in association with much smaller DNA bend angles (53–65°). In a rodent cell-based model of the embryonic gonadal ridge, full-length SRY variants bearing these respective Ala substitutions exhibited significantly decreased transcriptional activation of SRY’s principal target gene (Sox9). Together, our findings suggest that nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds by SRY are critical for specific DNA binding, bending, and transcriptional activation.Keywords: protein-DNA recognition, crystal structure, DNA bending, developmental gene regulation, SRY, high-mobility group

Male development in the embryogenesis of therian mammals is initiated by Sry (1), a transcription factor (TF) encoded by the sex-determining region of the Y chromosome (SRY). Sry proteins in mammals contain a conserved DNA-binding domain, the high mobility-group (HMG) box (2), which is the signature domain of a family of related TFs. This metazoan family, designated Sry-related box (Sox) TFs (3, 4), function broadly in developmental patterning and organogenesis. Identification of Sry as the testis-determining factor in mammals was supported by the construction of XX male mice containing Y-derived transgenes (1). Further evidence for this genetic assignment and its extension to human embryogenesis was provided by the finding of clinical mutations in SRY associated with disorders (or differences) of sex development (DSD), including 46, XY sex reversal (references in Table S1). Such mutations cluster in the HMG box (5, 6, 7) (Table S1). Homologous mutations in SOX factors are associated with diverse developmental disorders and cancer (4). In the present study, we describe the crystal structure of the human SRY box–DNA complex and interrogate the role of SRY in nucleobase-specific recognition of the minor groove of the DNA.

Expression of SRY and activation of a downstream male-specific program is time- and cell-type-specific within the developing bipotential gonadal ridge (8). In mice, Sry expression sharply increases at E10.5 days post-conception and drops drastically at E11.5 days post-conception (9). The immediate downstream target of Sry is an autosomal gene-encoding transcription factor Sox9, which contains an homologous HMG box. Upregulation of Sox9 both propagates a ramifying male-specific program and inhibits ovarian differentiation (10, 11, 12). Sry binds to enhancer elements upstream of the Sox9-coding region; to date, two such elements have been described, testis enhancer core (TESCO (13)) and enhancer region 13 (Enh13 (14)), respectively, located 13 kb and 565 kb upstream of the Sox9 transcriptional start site. These enhancer elements contain Sry- and Sox9-binding sites as well as a site for steroidogenic factor 1 (Sf1), a member of the nuclear hormone-receptor superfamily. Orthologous elements and TF-binding sites have been identified upstream of the human SOX9 gene (15). Although human SRY and murine Sry exhibit extensive differences in their non-box domains (16), similar structural principles pertain to their mechanisms of specific DNA binding and bending. In therian mammals in general, SRY-containing enhanceosomes are proposed to assemble at these control regions and activate the transcription of Sox9 in embryonic pre-Sertoli cells.

The SRY HMG box comprises 86 residues, forming an N-terminal extended region, three α-helices, and C-terminal basic tail (17). For clarity, consensus numbering scheme 1 to 86 is employed here in relation to the HMG box; this consensus corresponds to residues 56 to 141 in intact human SRY (Fig. S1). The solution structure of the human SRY box has been determined (a) as bound to a blunt-ended 8-bp DNA duplex (oligonucleotide 5′-dGTTTGTGC and complement; Protein Databank entry 1HRY) and (b) as bound to a 14-bp DNA duplex (5′-GGTGTTTGTGCAGG and complement, recognition site underlined; 1J46); these were the first structures reported for a member of the SOX family (18, 19). The latter structure provides a baseline for the present comparative analysis. The DNA site in these solution structures differed in part (bold) from the reported consensus target sequence: 5′-ATTGTT and complement (13, 20, 21). In addition, sequence variation 5′ to this consensus (5′-XATTGTT; italics) has been shown to affect the extent of specific DNA bending (22). Potential contacts to this base pair are suggested by prior crystal structures of Sox box–DNA complexes (23, 24) and by the solution structure of a Sox2–POU–DNA ternary complex (25).

The major and minor grooves of the DNA double helix differ in geometry and array of functional groups amenable to protein recognition (26). In contrast to many other TF families, which primarily target the major groove, DNA recognition by the Sox HMG box occurs in a widened minor groove. Sharp DNA bend angles have been observed both by structural methods and permutation gel electrophoresis (PGE), typically with angles >70° (27, 28). A seeming discrepancy was posed by the original SRY-DNA solution structures (18) in which a smaller DNA bend angle was described (47°)—inconsistent with DNA bend angles inferred from PGE (29, 30, 31). Indeed, several subsequent crystal structures of homologous Sox box–DNA complexes have been reported, each with larger DNA-bend angles: Sox18 (23), Sox17 (32), and Sox4 (24). In the present study, we sought both to resolve this structural discrepancy and to investigate the contributions of nucleobase-specific box-DNA hydrogen bonds to (i) DNA binding, (ii) DNA bending, and (iii) transcriptional regulation. The latter biochemical and cell-based studies have focused specifically on R7, N10, R20, N32, S36, and Y74, which directly hydrogen bond to nucleobases and are conserved among both mammalian Sry domains and the broader metazoan Sox family. Whereas prior studies have focused on nonpolar contacts in the widened minor groove by a “hydrophobic wedge” of conserved side chains (18, 19), the present Ala scan provides, to our knowledge, the first functional analysis of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds at this interface. Together, our findings define key contributions to minor-groove–specific DNA binding and bending by a conserved motif of gene regulation in metazoan development.

Results

Crystal structure of human SRY box–DNA complex

We determined a 2.48-Å crystal structure of the SRY HMG box bound to duplex DNA, 5′-CACTAGCATTGTTTGGG and complement 3′-TGATCGTAACAAACCCG (5′-nucleoside overhangs in bold, recognition site underlined; Table S2). The single-strand overhangs (bold) were introduced to facilitate packing of the DNA duplex within a three-dimensional lattice. The structure includes three SRY–DNA complexes in the asymmetric unit, providing three independent snapshots of the complex (Fig. 1A). The three SRY protein molecules (chain IDs C, F, and I) in the SRY–DNA crystal structure superimpose well with rmsd values of 0.37 Å between C and F, 0.62 Å between C and I, and 0.54 Å between F and I chains (Fig. 1B and Table S3). The conformation of the HMG box in this crystal structure is similar to its conformation in solution (PDB ID 1J46; e.g., root-mean-square differences, rmsd, 0.8 Å relative to the C chain) and to crystal structures of homologous Sox domains in respective DNA complexes (4, 17, 18) with rmsds ranging from 0.6 to 1.1 Å (Table S3).

The respective DNA duplexes are designated A/B bound to box C, D/E bound to box F, and G/H bound to box I. Although most of the duplex DNA is B-form (analyzed by program 3DNA (33, 34)), the central base pairs (dinucleotide steps 5′-CA/TG, AT/AT, and TT/AA) in each of the duplexes are identified as TA-form. The 5′-TT/AA step is the site of partial intercalation by “cantilever” side chain I13 (residue 68 in intact human SRY). Relative to the similar structures of the three protein domains, the respective DNA duplexes exhibit greater structural differences. Rmsd values on pairwise superpositioning of the three DNA duplexes range from 0.78 to 1.1 Å (with a 2.0 Å cutoff for the match) and 1.3 to 2.96 Å, including all nucleobase pairs (Fig. 1C and Table S4). However, such variation is not uniform throughout the DNA molecules. The central portions of the DNA molecules (interacting with SRY) superimpose well, whereas the DNA ends (most influenced by packing interactions in the lattice) differ among the three molecules (Fig. 1C). The corresponding DNA structure in solution (PDB ID 1J46) differs so extensively that it cannot be superimposed with the crystallographic DNA structures (Fig. 1D and Table S3).

The DNA bend angle calculated using the program CURVES (35, 36) in the prior solution structure is 46.6° as compared to 69.0°, 82.0°, and 83.6° for the three crystallographic complexes (DNA duplexes A/B, D/E, and G/H, respectively; Tables 1 and S2). Both A/B and D/E DNA duplexes pack as designed in the crystal with pairing of the overhanging dC and dG nucleotides. Despite this similarity, the DNA-bending angle of A/B is significantly smaller than for D/E, likely due to other packing interactions within the lattice that impact DNA bending. The terminal dC on each strand of G/H is disordered and so does not fully pair with the overhanging dG of a symmetry-related duplex. This somewhat looser interaction may permit a slightly larger DNA bend angle.

Given the differences in DNA-bending angles between NMR-derived models and the present in crystallo SRY complexes, we analyzed DNA bend angles in homologous Sox–DNA crystal structures. Such structures have been reported for specific DNA complexes containing the HMG boxes of Sox4 (24) 3U2B, Sox17 (32) PDB entries 3F27, 6Y6L, and Sox18 (23) 4Y60. Interactions of SRY with the DNA minor groove

The reported consensus DNA sequence for SRY recognition is 5′-ATTGTT/AACAAT-3’; however, nucleobase-specific minor groove interactions in the crystal structure involve the shifted site 5′-CATTGT/ACAATG-3’ (italics; see Fig. 2, and Tables 2 and S6). Specifically, interactions with the DNA A chain involve O2 or N3 atoms within the segment 5′-ATTGT, whereas interactions with the DNA B chain involve complement 3′-GTAACA; together, these interactions redefine the recognition sequence as 5′-CATTGT/ACAATG. A consequence of the interaction of SRY with DNA is a significant widening of the minor groove, from 10 to 13 Å on the ends to 20 to 23 Å at the site of cantilever intercalation (as calculated using 3DNA; (33)). Residues involved in nucleobase-specific interactions are located on either side of the cantilever: R20, N32, and S36 on one side and R7, N10, and Y74 on the other (Fig. 2A). These interactions involve both strands of the DNA with R7, N10, R20, and S36 contacting the DNA A chain; and R7, N32, and Y74, the DNA B chain (Fig. 2, B–E). Of these residues, R7, N10, and Y74 are located within the N- and C-terminal segments, and the remaining residues within alpha helices: R20 residues within helix α1, N32 and S36 within α2. Except in one case, the minor groove–specific hydrogen bonds are formed in all three complexes; the R20-mediated interaction occurs in only one of the complexes (Table S6).

Several of these residues are involved in networks of interactions involving hydrogen-bonding to other residues within SRY or to both chains of the duplex DNA molecule. A unique set of interactions involves R20, which is hydrogen-bonded to both S36 and N32 (Fig. 2, B and C). The S36 hydroxyl group is hydrogen-bonded to N3 of 7 dA (A chain) and to an NH2 of R20, which is also hydrogen-bonded to O2 of 8 dT (A chain) (Fig. 2B). ND2 of N32 hydrogen bonds to O2 of 10 dT and N3 of 11 dG (B chain) (Fig. 2C). Thus, R20 appears to brace the side chains of both N32 and S36 as an extended DNA-binding polar surface. A second set of interactions involves R7. NH1 of R7 hydrogen bonds to O2 of 11 dT (A chain) and to O2 of 7 dC (B chain). The aromatic ring of Y74 both stacks with the guanidium group of R7 (as a weakly polar interaction), and its para-OH group hydrogen bonds to N3 of 6 dA (B chain) (Fig. 2E). The carboxamide group of N10 also hydrogen bonds to two nucleotides, both within the A chain: ND2 hydrogen bonds to O2 of 9 dT and N3 of 10 dA (Fig. 2D).

Collectively, SRY forms 10 hydrogen bonds with nucleobase atoms in the DNA minor groove (Table 2). In contrast, only six equivalent interactions are found in the solution structure of this complex, a discrepancy that appears unrelated to the DNA sequence differences. It is possible that the fewer hydrogen bonds observed in the solution structure reflect limitations of NMR analysis, that is, incomplete observability of exchangeable proton resonances. These issues notwithstanding, NMR-detected contacts by the side chains of R7, N10, N32, S36, and Y74 are largely consistent with those seen in the present crystal structure, with the exception of R20. However, there are fewer hydrogen bonds contributed by R7, N10, and N32 in the solution structure relative to the extensive hydrogen-bonding networks observed in the crystal structure. Among homologous crystal structures, 9 of the 10 minor groove–specific hydrogen-bonding interactions are conserved within Sox18 and Sox17 domain–DNA complexes. In the Sox4–DNA complex, however, only 7 of these 10 minor-groove interactions are observed (Table 2). In each case, the residues involved in the interactions are identical to those in SRY box–DNA complexes and broadly conserved among Sox and Sry sequences (Fig. S2).

Interactions of SRY with the phosphate backbone of DNA

The two SRY complexes with DNA bend angles of 82.0° and 83.6° (F-D/E and I-G/H, respectively, with F and I referring to the protein chains and D/E and G/H the DNA chains) form similar hydrogen-bonding interactions with the DNA backbone (11 and 12 hydrogen bonds, respectively) involving residues R7, N10, R17, N32, W43, K44, Y74, and R77 (Table S6). The complex with the smallest DNA-bending angle (69°; C-A/B) forms 11 hydrogen bonds with the DNA backbone involving residues R4, K6, R7, N32, W43, and R77. Of these, R7, N10, N32, and Y74 are also hydrogen-bonded to minor-groove base atoms as described above. Most of the hydrogen-bonding interactions with the DNA backbone involve side-chain atoms; exceptions include R7, which involves both side-chain and main-chain NH interactions, and R77 with only a main-chain NH interaction to the DNA backbone. R7 forms a total of five hydrogen bonds with the DNA. N10 and N32 form three hydrogen bonds, whereas the hydroxyl group of S36 forms a single hydrogen bond and the hydroxyl group of Y74 forms two hydrogen bonds with the DNA. R20 is unique in forming a single hydrogen bond with the DNA and three hydrogen bonds with S36 and N32.

Hydrogen-bonding interactions shared by the crystal structure and solution structure of the SRY box–DNA complex involve residues R7 (NH2 but not main-chain NH), N10, N32, W43, K44, and Y74 (Table S6). Backbone hydrogen-bonding interactions unique to the solution structure involve residues K51, N62, and K81. Collectively, the SRY-DNA solution structure includes 12 backbone hydrogen-bonds, comparable to the number observed in the three SRY–DNA complexes in the crystal structure. Despite this similarity in DNA backbone contacts, the number of minor-groove nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds (as above, 10 in the crystal structure, but only 6 in the solution structure) appears to correlate with respective DNA bend angles.

Properties of the DNA bound to SRY and related Sox proteins

The structures of SRY-bound DNA (this work and NMR structure [1J46]), Sox18 (4Y60), Sox17 (3F27, 6L6Y), and Sox4 (3U2B) complexes were analyzed using 3DNA (33, 34) to identify features in the DNA structure. Minor-groove interactions involved 5′-TATTGT (and complement) in Sox4- and Sox17 domain-DNA structures and 5′-CATTGT (and complement) in the Sox18 domain-DNA structure. A striking feature of the DNA structures in the SRY domain–DNA complex and homologous Sox HMG–DNA complexes are the very large roll angles at the site of cantilever insertion, the 5′-TT/AA step, and the preceding 5′-CA/TG dinucleotide step (Fig. S3). This finding is consistent with TA-form DNA, which exhibits large positive roll angles and large inclination values (33). As first anticipated by NMR studies of DNA imino protons in this complex (28), Watson-Crick hydrogen-bonding interactions are preserved in these nucleobase pairs. Respective roll angles for the 5′-CA/TG pairs within 5′-CATTGT in the three DNA duplexes bound to SRY are 26°, 18°, and 22° and for the 5′-TT/AA pairs, 46°, 55°, and 48°. Homologous Sox-DNA structures each exhibit very large roll angles, ranging from 17 to 26° for the 5′-CA/TG (or TA/TA) step and 48°-53° for the 5′-TT/AA step. In contrast, the solution structure of SRY-DNA exhibits roll angles of 9° and 22°, respectively, for equivalently positioned dinucleotide steps, which are 5′-TG/CA and 5′-TT/AA steps (Fig. S3). Other features defining the SRY-bound DNA structure include rise, X-displacement, and inclination values (Fig. S3).

Ala substitutions perturb specific DNA binding

Our biochemical and functional studies focused on the array of six conserved residues engaged in nucleobase hydrogen bonds. To determine their contributions to specific DNA binding and bending, we characterized a systematic set of Ala substitutions: R7A, N10A, R20A, N32A, S36A, and Y74A by EMSAs and steady-state fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET). These studies employed the isolated SRY HMG box as in the crystallographic studies.

EMSAs were done at 4 °C using a 5′-fluorescently-labeled 36-bp DNA target containing a central 5′-CATTGTT-3′ recognition sequence. In each set, the DNA concentration was 4 nM (i.e., less than the expected equilibrium constants), and protein concentrations were increased from 0 to 300 nM (Fig. 3A). In accordance with past studies (37), at WT protein concentrations >50 nM, higher-order complexes were observed (designated c2 in Fig. 3); protein concentrations >100 nM gave rise to a c3 complex. EMSA data were subjected to a simplified analysis (Fig. 3B): comparison of relative intensities for the c1 complex observed for each of the Ala substituted SRYs at a common protein concentration of 20 nM (Figs. 3 and S4, and Table 3). Thus, under these conditions, the relative amount of WT c1 complex was ∼41% of total DNA, whereas the N32A variant resulted in ∼46% c1, similar within experimental error. The other variants exhibited attenuated formation of the c1 complex with values of 2 to 16% c1. R7A and R20A exhibited the lowest apparent affinities with c1 values of 2 to 3% in each case and S36A higher at ∼16%. Whereas the c2 and c3 complexes were prominent in the WT lanes, corresponding bands were not precisely defined for the Ala variants due to diffuse “smearing” of the bands. The sole exception was the R20A variant, which gave rise to no higher-order complexes (either well-resolved or smeared).

We speculate that smearing of these bands at higher protein concentrations reflects kinetic instability leading to dissociation in the course of electrophoresis. To enhance such dissociation, EMSA studies were repeated at 37 °C (Fig. S4). At this temperature, no higher-order complexes are observed for any of the samples. The WT and N32A patterns were similar. For R7A, the c1 band is strongly diminished: faint bands were visible only at the highest protein concentration (100 nM); for R20A, no detectable complex bands were observed.

To avoid kinetic effects, we performed an equilibrium-binding assay based on FRET (22, 38, 39) using a 15-bp duplex containing the consensus DNA target site. In the absence of the SRY domain, this DNA probe exhibits minimal FRET between donor (fluorescein, emission maximum 525 nm) and acceptor (TAMRA, emission maximum 558 nm) (Fig. S5A, green trace). SRY-directed DNA bending shortens the mean end-to-end distance in association with enhanced FRET efficiency (Fig. S5A, black trace). Previous analysis of the WT system by time-resolved FRET with distance-distribution analysis has verified the mobility of the fluorescent probes and provided respective distance estimates of 63 Å (free DNA) and 52 Å (specific box-DNA complex) as described (40, 41). These distances include respective mean linker lengths from the 5′-ends of the DNA duplex to the photophysical centers of the probes’ respective electric dipole moments.

In the present FRET-based characterization of specific DNA-binding affinities, the fluorescent DNA site, maintained at a single concentration (25 nM), was titrated with increasing concentrations of protein (WT or variant; Fig. S5B). Differences in donor emission (ΔFRET; relative to the free DNA probe) were analyzed as a function of protein concentration. (This approach does not require the DNA concentration to be lower than the Kd values as typically assumed in EMSA.) The present measurements of WT DNA-binding affinities (at 15 and 37 °C; Table 3) are consistent with previous such values determined by this technique (42, 43). The protein-DNA equilibrium constants of the Ala variants are given in Table 3. A broad range of perturbations was observed: in accordance with the EMSA trends, substitutions N32A, S36A, and Y74A had mild effects (twofold or smaller decreases in affinity). In contrast, R7A, N10A, and R20A were more perturbing; respective affinities at 37 °C were decreased by 3.5, 3.8, and 21-fold relative to WT.

Nucleobase-specific interactions contribute to DNA bending

DNA bend angles were assessed by PGE (Fig. 4). This approach exploits differences in electrophoretic mobility on binding of the WT or variant SRY domains to a consensus DNA site differentially placed within a longer DNA fragment (150 bp; Fig. 4A, green square in lower panel). In accordance with past studies (30), the WT DNA bend angle was found to be 79° ±2 (Fig. 4B, and Tables 4 and S7). The DNA target site (5′-GTCATTGTTAGA-3′ and complement; italics) includes three flanking base pairs derived from the Sox9 Enh13 enhancer (13). PGE-based estimates represent the mean DNA bend angles in the ensemble of >1016 complexes in each well, assumed to represent a dynamic conformational equilibrium in accordance with time-resolved FRET analysis of the 15-bp model system (31). Interestingly, the range of WT DNA bend angles (76–82°) inferred from repeated PGE experiments falls within the range of static angles observed in the three crystal complexes (structural “snapshots” with respective angles 69, 80, and 84°; see above). Whereas the latter differences in structure reflect effects of the crystal lattice, sources of experimental variation in PGE studies, which presumably underlie the range of inferred bend angles on replication of the assay, include small potential differences between assays in gel composition, running temperature, voltage, and ionic strength.

PGE studies of the Ala SRY variants demonstrated a correlation between perturbations in specific DNA affinity and changes in inferred DNA bend angles. Thus, substitutions N32A, S36A, and Y74A gave rise only to mild perturbations (Table 4); within this set, the N32A variant exhibited the largest difference in mean DNA bend angle, Δθ = 8.6(±2.2)°. The difference between N32A and WT was determined to be statistically significant; however, it was not different from S36A or Y74A (Table S7). In contrast, R7, N10, and R20 were each associated with more marked perturbations in both affinity and DNA bending. Complexes with the R7A and R20A domains exhibited an ∼20° decrease (and are not significantly different from each other (Table S7)) in mean DNA bend angle (Fig. S6), while N10A exhibited a decrease of ∼14°. It is possible that such decreases in electrophoretic mean values reflect similar maximal DNA bending but with enhanced conformational fluctuations toward less-bent complexes. In an ensemble of protein–DNA complexes, position-dependent differences in electrophoretic mobility could in principle be attenuated by broader conformational sampling of DNA bend angles.

Ala substitutions impair the gene-regulatory function of SRY

The gene-regulatory activity of SRY in embryonic development, confined to a specific tissue, cell-type, and stage (44), was probed in an immortalized pre-Sertoli cell line derived from the XY rat gonadal ridge micro-dissected just prior to its morphologic differentiation (45). Such cells, designated CH34, provide an open chromatin with activated histone marks in the Sox9 target gene (13). Unlike almost all established rodent and human cell lines, the endogenous Sox9 gene in CH34 cells is hence amenable to SRY-directed transcriptional activation. To determine the functional effects of the present Ala variants in this cell-type–specific milieu, endogenous Sox9 transcript abundances were measured by quantitative reverse-transcriptase PCR (qPCR) following transient transfection with plasmids expressing an epitope-tagged WT or variant SRY (Fig. 5, A and B; see Experimental Procedures). To avoid artifacts of TF over-expression, SRY expression was calibrated by plasmid dilution within the physiological range (103–104 mean molecules per cell (46)) as described (47); in each case, respective expression levels were monitored by Western blot (Fig. 5C). As an additional control for potential confounding perturbations in nuclear localization, cellular subfractionation assays of the WT and variant SRYs were performed (Fig. 7, C–E). These assays distinguished between accumulation of SRY in the cytoplasm (as indicated by colocalization with the housekeeping enzyme GAPDH; Fig. 7C, right) versus the nucleus (colocalization with nuclear TF Yin-Yang 1[YY1]; Fig. 7C, left). Of the present set of Ala variants, two substitutions indeed perturbed the function of NLS1: R62 (R7 in the HMG box) and R75 (R20). The former (corresponding to box variant R7A) exhibited negligible nuclear accumulation whereas the latter (corresponding to box variant R20A) impaired nuclear accumulation by more than twofold (Western blots in Fig. 7C). (Negligible nuclear accumulation of R62A SRY is consistent with prior studies of DSD mutation R62G (49)) To test whether such improper localization (rather than perturbed DNA binding or bending) might account for these variants’ attenuated Sox9 expression, we fused an exogenous SV40 T-antigen NLS sequence to the N-terminus of the present set of epitope-tagged-test SRY constructs. This exogenous NLS indeed directed robust nuclear accumulation of the expressed R62A and R75A SRY variants (“SRY + SV40” in Fig. 7C). Despite complete rescue of nuclear entry, their impaired transcriptional activation of Sox9 was essentially unchanged (Fig. 7D). This control thus demonstrates that substitutions R62A and R75A (respective box positions 7 and 20; highlighted in red in Fig. 7D) markedly perturb the intrinsic gene-regulatory activity of SRY even when nuclear entry is restored. In a plot of gene-regulatory activities in the presence or absence of the SV40 T-antigen NLS (Fig. 7E), the “rescued” variants R62A and R75A are only slightly above the diagonal line, indicating negligible restoration of activity (as expected, the remaining variants are on this line within error). In an analogous plot of gene-regulatory activities in the presence or absence of MG132, no functional rescue was observed following chemical proteasome inhibition (Fig. 7F).

Trimer-specific crystal contacts among HMG boxes are not of functional significance

Finally, having examined the functional significance of nucleobase-specific interactions via systematic Ala substitutions, we examined the potential functional significance of protein–protein interactions in the present crystal form. The three protein molecules in the asymmetric unit interact centrally with the complexed DNA on the periphery—a unique feature among Sox-DNA crystal structures. This novel interface involves hydrogen-bonding interactions among side-chain interactions involving E67 (C chain) with R29 (F chain) and Q42 (I chain). R29 is located between α1 and α2, Q42 in α2, and E67 in α3 (Fig. 1A). Because E67 is not conserved among other Sox domains, this interaction is likely unique to SRY. To test the possible biological relevance of this trimer and its associated hydrogen bonds, we introduced Ala and charge-reversal substitutions (Fig. S7A); activities were tested in CH34 cells as above. The five variants (substitutions R84E, R84A, Q97A, E122K, and E122A in intact SRY; R29E, R29A, Q42A, E67K, and E67A in the HMG box) were equally expressed (Fig. S7B). Strikingly, no perturbations were observed in SRY-directed transcriptional activation of Sox9 (Fig. S7C). This result implies that the trimer interaction likely forms only under conditions of crystallization and is not relevant to SRY’s mechanism of gene regulation in vivo. The absence of functional perturbations in essence provides an extended control for the biological specificity of the CH34-based model, highlighting the importance of the present array of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds in the same crystal structure.

Discussion

Protein-DNA recognition in the major and minor grooves of the DNA double helix differ in fundamental structural and chemical features. Whereas canonical major groove–binding proteins (such as homeodomains and classical zinc fingers) exploit B-DNA-sequence-specific patterns of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors present in the major groove, the HMG boxes of SRY and homologous Sox TFs bind within a widened and underwound minor groove to induce a distorted DNA conformation. Within the minor groove, each base pair presents hydrogen bond acceptors, N3 or O2 in structurally equivalent positions (i.e., each base pair is potentially equivalent and indistinguishable from any other base pair in the absence of other factors). In this case, sequence-specific minor groove recognition by SRY involves two important components that are conserved in homologous Sox–DNA complexes: (i) nucleobase-specific hydrogen-bonding interactions mediated by conserved residues and (ii) DNA structural features that facilitate deformability enabled by partial intercalation of I13 in the 5′-AA/TT site and thereby sharp bending. These considerations have motivated studies of a “hydrophobic wedge” of conserved nonpolar residues in the HMG box that insert within the widened minor groove (18, 39). The overarching goal of the present study was by contrast to characterize and interrogate nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds by conserved polar residues. Our investigation thus had two parts, crystallographic analysis of the specific box–DNA complex to overcome the limitations of past NMR structures (18, 19) and mutagenesis of a conserved array of hydrogen-bonding residues to assess their individual contributions to specific DNA binding, DNA bending, and gene-regulatory activity. We envision that the gene-regulatory function of the sequence-specific HMG box requires a complementary interplay between distinct structural mechanisms: wedge deformation of the bent DNA site (including partial intercalation of the cantilever side chain) and subsequent “latching” of hydrogen bonds to nucleobases and the DNA backbone.

Crystal structure redefines borders of consensus DNA target site

The present cocrystal structure suggests that the SRY-DNA recognition sequence is 5′-CATTGT/ACAATG-3′ (and complement) based on the analysis of direct minor-groove nucleobase interactions—and not 5′-ATTGTT as previously proposed (20). The SRY HMG box does not make any nucleobase interactions with the final T/A pair in the previously identified recognition motif of 5′-ATTGTT (italics) but does make interactions with the G of the preceding 5′-C/G pair (bold above). This reassignment is in accordance with previous findings that (i) the terminal position of the classical target site (5′-ATTGTX; bold) exhibits only weak sequence specificity (20) and (ii) the identity of the initial base pair in the redefined site (YATTGT; bold) influences the extent of DNA bending (30). Future biological implications of this reassessment may be investigated through CRISPR/Cas9-based mutagenesis of SRY-binding sites in the upstream testis-specific enhancer elements in the Sox9 gene. The feasibility of this approach is in principle supported by observations that developmental phenotypes can be sensitive to even minor perturbations in SRY-DNA binding (as small as two-fold changes have been observed in patients with DSD (42, 47)).

Gene-regulatory networks in vivo may in general exploit both consensus and nonconsensus DNA target sites (55). “Suboptimal” TF-binding sites (from a structural perspective) may enable biological optimization of fitness, as networks evolve collectively to implement a developmental program. In the case of SRY and Sox-related TFs, sequence variation within enhancer elements might enable functional tuning of affinities, degree of specific DNA bending, and enhanceosome assembly. These subtle variations may also distinguish among otherwise homologous SOX factors with overlapping DNA sequence specificities. An example is provided by the above variations 5′-C/G-A/T-TTGT-T/G (and complement), as respectively represented in the NMR-derived and crystal structures of the SRY box–DNA complex (18, 19), each may represent functional states. Indeed, the less favorable NMR site was derived from an SRY/SOX-binding site in the gene encoding the anti-Müllerian hormone/Müllerian inhibiting substance (12, 21). The latter hormone functions immediately downstream of Sox9 in male sex determination (56). From this perspective, it would be of future interest to undertake comparative crystallographic studies of SRY or SOX domains bound to nonconsensus DNA sites utilized in bona fide enhanceosomes. Although these general principles apply to other TF families, their biological meaning is particularly important in the context of variable DNA bending and its potential mechanistic implications.

Crystal structure defines array of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds

A primary goal of our crystallographic investigation was the analysis of protein-DNA hydrogen bonds in the DNA minor groove. A natural classification of the nucleobase hydrogen bonds is based on their position relative to the site of I13 cantilever insertion. Conserved among Sry and Sox HMG boxes, the two groups are structurally distinct; we describe each in turn.

(i)On one side of the cantilever are R20, N32, and S36. The side chain of R20 effectively organizes these residues through a network of direct side-chain/side-chain hydrogen-bonds. R20 is invariant among specific HMG-box TFs (Fig. S2), and its interactions with the side chains of N32 and S36 are broadly observed among representative Sox-DNA crystal structures (Fig. 2). Structural variation in R20-DNA contacts was observed among the three independent complexes in the present SRY co-crystal structure. In one complex, the side chain of R20 contributes a nucleobase contact whereas in the other two, it does not. Analogous variability at the DNA interface occurs in the Sox-DNA structural database. Where this DNA contact is missing in some structures, in Sox17, R20 forms two hydrogen bonds to the DNA (32).

Nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds contribute to specific DNA binding and bending

Observation of a hydrogen bond at a macromolecular interface does not in itself distinguish relative contributions to affinity and specificity. Ala scanning mutagenesis of diverse protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid complexes has defined high- and low-energy contacts (58, 59). Biochemical and FRET-based studies of the present Ala substitutions suggest that SRY’s nucleobase hydrogen-bonding array have two classes of residues: (i) those with only mild perturbation of specific protein-DNA affinity (box residues N32, S36, and Y74) and (ii) those with more marked perturbations (R7, N10, and R20). Ala substitutions in the first group were compatible with near-native EMSA patterns with modestly reduced DNA bend angles (70–73°) relative to WT (79°). In contrast, Ala substitutions of the second group resulted in KD values decreased from 4- to 21-fold at 37 °C. Despite these perturbations, residual biochemical activity is substantial relative to Ala substitution of the cantilever side chain (I13A in the HMG box and I68A in intact SRY), which abolishes detectable DNA binding (39) and detectable gene-regulatory activity in CH34 cells (48). The second group also exhibits impaired DNA bending but with significant remaining bend angles (56–65°). The following structure-activity relationships are of special interest:

(i)Severity of R7A substitution. We speculate that the marked biochemical deficits caused by R7A reflect its bidentate interactions with both DNA strands as “high-energy” contacts, reinforced by the positioning of the guanidium group’s positive charges within an expanded minor groove between the flanking negative charges of the DNA backbone. The impaired affinity of the R7A variant is associated with a greater attenuation of specific DNA bending (as inferred from PGE) than the other five Ala variants.

Changes in DNA bend angles in a protein–DNA complex may represent static or dynamic perturbations. We speculate that attenuated DNA bending, as generally observed here among our set of Ala variants, is due to asymmetric conformational fluctuations in the bound DNA leading to increased subpopulations with reduced DNA bend angles. In this model, the native array of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds may be viewed, conversely, as struts that “lock” a sharp DNA bend by delimiting conformational fluctuations at the protein–DNA interface.

The present set of Ala variants provides future probes for foundational biophysical studies of the dynamics of protein-directed DNA bending. We imagine that amplified conformational fluctuations—affected to varying degrees by the individual substitutions—might open, to corresponding degrees, “exit paths” for more efficient dissociation of the bent box–DNA complex, that is, attenuating activation free-energy energies (ΔG∗) governing off-rate constants; such biophysical perturbations might in turn promote enhanceosome disassembly in the cell nucleus, limiting target gene activation. Crystal structures of these variant box–DNA complexes might depict snapshots of such less-bent DNA conformations. This hypothesis might also be tested through subnanosecond time-resolved FRET, enabling distance-distribution analysis of narrow or broad end-to-end DNA distances (60). Such studies may provide insight into a long-standing hypothesis: that extent of specific DNA bending within enhancer elements modulates gene-regulatory activity. Although evidence in favor of this hypothesis has been proposed based on DSD mutations at box position 9 (SRY mutations M64I (19, 29) and M64R (61) described as defective in DNA bending), reassessment of these findings has demonstrated that native DNA bending is maintained ((30) and Fig. S8). The present set of Ala substitutions, associated with a range of DNA-bending defects (Table 4), may in the future provide valuable reagents to uncover molecular mechanisms relating bend angle to testis-specific enhanceosome assembly and transcriptional activation.

Nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds are critical for gene-regulatory activity

The gene-regulatory activities of variant TFs can in general be influenced by the levels of protein expression, differences in post-translational modification, nuclear localization, nucleocytoplasmic trafficking, and rates of proteasomal degradation. These factors have been considered in this and past studies of SRY variants (42, 47, 50). Of special significance is the number of TF molecules per cell. Whereas transient transfection with strong viral promoters typically give rise to >106 TF molecules per cell (>1 μM in the nucleus), developmental regulators typically are expressed at 103 to 104 TF molecules per cell. To achieve such physiological concentrations, we employed a protocol in which the expression plasmid was diluted by the corresponding parent (empty) plasmid in a 1:50 ratio (47). Thus, gene-regulatory activities may be evaluated at either low or high SRY concentration. Defects in SRY-enhancer interactions may or may not be rescued by increasing the number of TF molecules per cell. An isolated defect in specific protein-DNA affinity, for example, may be overcome by over-expression whereas a qualitative defect in the complex (such as decreased DNA bending) might be independent of TF concentration. Unlike qPCR-based measurements of Sox9 transcript abundances, ChIP-based assessment of testis-specific enhancer occupancies was only feasible at the higher level of SRY expression (i.e., without plasmid dilution). The biological fidelity of these complementary cell-based assays (in relation to general mechanisms of gene regulation) was corroborated by the clear correlation between extent of Sox9 transcriptional activation and enhancer occupancy under these conditions.

Each alanine variant at the six sites of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds exhibits markedly impaired gene-regulatory activity when evaluated at the lower level of SRY expression. Residual activities were reduced essentially to that of the empty vector control. Similar results were obtained in the presence or absence of chemical proteasome inhibitor MG132, indicating that any small differences in the level of SRY expression and rates of proteasomal degradation are not responsible for the functional defects. Likewise, the addition of an exogenous SV40 NLS to compensate for the dampened nuclear accumulation, especially residues associated with the bipartite NLS, R62 and R75, did not alter attenuated Sox9 transcript upregulation. The inherent transcriptional defect for alanine present at these six positions is not due to a lack of proper nuclear localization. The specificity of these results in relation to the minor groove–binding surface of the SRY HMG box was verified through extended control studies of five mutations at three sites on the back surface of the HMG box (originally designed as a functional test of a trimer-forming surface characteristic of the present crystal lattice). On SRY over-expression, two of the variants, N32A and S36A, regained partial activity whereas the other four again exhibited negligible activity. The former are the same two variants that, in the isolated HMG box in vitro, exhibited the least perturbations in specific DNA binding and bending. Despite this overall consistency, it is puzzling that a mere twofold decrement in specific DNA affinity (or similarly minor decrement in DNA bend angle) could not be fully compensated by a >102-fold increase in intracellular SRY concentration. We speculate that the present set of Ala variants has both qualitative and quantitative defects in biochemical DNA-binding properties critical to gene-regulatory activity in an SRY-responsive cell line. It is unlikely that these substitutions alternatively impair interactions between SRY and putative partner proteins in enhanceosome assembly (such as with transcriptional co-activators or histone-modifying enzymes), since each site in the HMG box is buried at the box–DNA interface.

Our findings likely have implications for human genetics. DSD mutations in SRY have been reported at each of the six residues engaged in nucleobase-specific contacts; indeed, four of these box positions define “hot spots” with multiple variants (R7G/P, N10D/H, R20K/M/N, and S36G/C). Observation of R20K as a DSD mutation (a charge-preserving mutations) may highlight the specific function of the R20 guanidium group at the nexus of side-chain/side-chain and side-chain/nucleobase network of hydrogen bonds. Analogous clinical mutations have been found at these box positions among SOX factors (Table S11); developmental and disease-related phenotypes reflect the specialized functions of a given factor. We propose that the structure-activity relationships described here underlie these diverse developmental disorders. Deciphering allele-specific molecular mechanisms will require interdisciplinary characterization as exemplified here.

Evolution and divergence of the HMG-box superfamily

The SRY/Sox family belongs to a broader superfamily of HMG-box factors (62). This superfamily contains two fundamental classes. The first, represented by HMG-1 (63), contain domains that recognize distorted DNA structures without sequence specificity (64). The present array of six nucleobase-specific residues (box positions 7, 10 20, 32, 36, and 74) is not well conserved among such nonspecific domains. Although these domains adopt a similar L-shaped α-helical structure (65, 66), C-terminal basic tails are uncommon (Fig. S9). The second class includes the present SRY/Sox family and a related family of sequence-specific HMG boxes as found in the Lef-1/Tcf-7 TFs. The latter also mediate lineage- and stage-specific gene regulation (67).

The NMR-derived structure of a specific Lef-1 box–DNA complex ((68); PDB entry 2LEF) exhibits similarities to and differences from SRY/Sox box-DNA structures. Although sharp DNA bending is induced in each case, the Lef-1 complex is more sharply bent as observed in its solution structure (68) and inferred from PGE (∼120°) (68). Sharper DNA bending is associated with the distinct positioning of an analogous (but divergent) C-terminal tail within the DNA major groove (Fig. S10, B and C). Wright et al. have proposed that disordered basic residues in the major-groove Lef-1 tail mitigate electrostatic repulsion between DNA chains, enabling additional compression of the major groove at the center of the bent DNA site (68). The Lef-1 tail is not anchored in the minor groove by a Y74-like residue, which is not observed among Lef-1/Tcf-7-related sequences (A74 in Lef-1; Fig. S10A). Aside from an abundance of basic residues, Lef-1/Tcf-7 tails are unrelated to those of SRY/Sox domains in length and sequence in accordance with their distinct structural roles.

Unlike their divergent tails, the L-shaped α-helical box of Lef-1 is similar in sequence and structure to those of the SRY/Sox family. These similarities include a subset of the present array of nucleobase-specific residues. Both classes of specific HMG boxes contain N10 and R20 invariant elements (Fig. S10A). Although N32 and S36 are switched (i.e., Lef-1 contains S32 and N36), the analogous Lef-1 residues retain nucleobase-specific hydrogen-bonds (68). The Lef-1/Tcf-7 family retains a basic residue at box position 7, but in this case, it is Lys (not Arg). The ε-amino group of K7 forms a salt bridge to the γ-carboxylate of tail residue D76 (68). This long-range contact may facilitate reorientation of the Lef-1 tail to insert within a compressed DNA major groove (Fig. S10F). Box position 76 is conserved as Pro in the SRY/Sox family, preventing such an electrostatic interaction. We speculate that the three “missing” nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds in the Lef-1 box-DNA structure (due to the absence of R7 and Y74 as in the SRY/Sox family) are compensated by the former’s more extensive tail-DNA contacts. In particular, the para-hydroxyl group of Y78 in the Lef-1 tail contacts dT5 (DNA B-chain), R82 hydrogen bonds with dT6 (A-chain), and K86 hydrogen bonds with dG9 (A-chain). Thus, the total number of residues engaged in nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds is the same in both families of specific HMG boxes. Presumably, these two classes reflect respective DNA-bending requirements (60–80° [SRY/Sox] versus ∼120° [Lef-1/TCF-7]) within their respective enhanceosome assemblies.

Concluding remarks

Together, the present study has integrated crystallographic, biochemical, and cell-based approaches to probe the contributions of conserved nucleobase-specific box-DNA hydrogen bonds to specific DNA binding, DNA bending, and gene regulation in an architectural transcription factor. The crystal structure of the human SRY box–DNA complex has refined its DNA target site (shifted by 1 bp relative to a prior consensus sequence) and rationalized the extent of specific DNA bending relative to homologous Sox-DNA structures. Ala scanning mutagenesis of the six residues engaged in such box-DNA hydrogen bonds has uncovered a range of biochemical perturbations in specific DNA binding and bending. Correlation of structure and biochemical function suggests that a subset of box-DNA hydrogen bonds occur within an extended network of side-chain/side-chain hydrogen bonds, such that structural variability can occur through compensating reorganization of this network. The fortuitous occurrence of three independent box–DNA complexes in the asymmetric unit of the crystallographic lattice enabled assessment of such structural variation.

We suggest that the gene-regulatory function of SRY requires functional thresholds of specific DNA affinity, DNA bending, and enhanceosome lifetime: net gene regulation is thus not a linear function of these biochemical parameters. Critical to meeting these thresholds in vivo is a conserved array of nucleobase-specific hydrogen bonds. Intriguing open questions focus on the potential relationships (i) between changes in specific DNA affinity and associated perturbations to mean DNA bend angle or precision of DNA bending and (ii) between such perturbations in DNA bending and specific gene-regulatory activity. Unraveling these relationships will require molecular characterization of SRY-directed enhanceosome assembly at testis-specific enhancer elements.

Experimental procedures

Protein expression and purification

Residues 4 to 81 of the HMG box of human SRY were cloned into a pTXB1 expression plasmid using manufacturer protocols (New England Biolabs). Subcloned human SRY HMG box contains a non-native Met residue at the N-terminus; proper sequences were verified by DNA sequencing (Genewiz Labs). Plasmids were transformed into a protein expression Escherichia coli strain (T7 express, New England Biolabs) and grown in LB broth. Target protein overexpression was induced with the addition of 1 mM IPTG during exponential growth phase of the cells (optical density ∼0.4). These cells were induced overnight at 18 °C, pelleted, and stored in buffer A (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA).

Cells were lysed by sonication, and the soluble fraction isolated by centrifugation. The pH of the lysate was readjusted to 8.5 with NaOH and applied to a chitin resin (New England Biolabs) equilibrated with buffer A. After protein binding, columns were washed with five resin bed volumes of buffer A and resins were incubated in buffer A with 50 mM 1,4-DTT added to induce intein cleavage at 4 °C overnight. Cleaved HMG protein was collected and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using an SEC70 column (BioRad) equilibrated with 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0 and 200 mM NaCl. Fractions which contained the HMG box were pooled, an aliquot was used for verification using liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy, and protein was concentrated to ∼2 mM.

Crystallization

The oligonucleotides 5′- CACTAGCATTGTTTGGG and complementary 5′-GCCCAAACAATGCTAGT purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies were dissolved in 10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl and annealed at 70 °C for 20 min. The duplex was designed with overhanging G and C to form a G:C pair between two oligonucleotide duplexes and thereby facilitate packing of the complexes in a crystal lattice. The SRY–DNA complex was prepared from 2 mM SRY in 5 mM Tris 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, and 2.5 mM SRY oligonucleotide in 10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl to final concentrations of 1.1 mM for SRY and the DNA. Crystals were grown in drops containing 0.5 μl of complex and 0.5 μl of precipitant solution, 0.2 M KCl, 0.1 M Mg(CH3COO)2, 0.05 M MES 6.5, 8% PEG 8000, in sitting drops with 100 μl of precipitant solution in the reservoir. The drops were step up using the Oryx 4 crystallization robot (Douglas Instruments, Inc).

Data collection and structure determination

Crystals were cryocooled in the precipitant solution with 20% ethylene glycol. Data were collected at the Advanced Photon Source, beamline 31-ID, LRL-CAT, on a Pilatus 3-6M detector at 100 K, and processed using XDS (69). The structure was determined by molecular replacement using PHASER (70) with the search model PDB ID 4Y60, the SOX18–DNA complex, with the overhanging unpaired nucleotides removed. The structure includes three complexes in the asymmetric unit. The model was adjusted in iterative cycles of model building in COOT (71) and refined using PHENIX (72). Coordinates have been deposited with the RCSB (PDB ID 9BVD).

DNA-binding studies

EMSAs employed a dsDNA substrate with a central SRY-binding site (italics); 5′-CATACTGCGGGGGTGATTGTTCAGGATCATACTGCG. The 36 bp DNA contains a fluorescein fluorophore linked with flexible hexanyl carbon linker to the 5′ end. Each well contained 4 nM of the dsDNA and increasing concentrations of WT or variant HMG domain (10, 20, 50, 100, 300 nM). Samples were resolved on a 10% acrylamide gel (29:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide) at 4 °C or 37 °C and in 0.5× TBE buffer. Gels were imaged on a LiCor Odyssey M imaging system and band quantitation was performed using Empiria Studios 2.3 analysis software. For each well that contained 4 nM DNA and 20 nM protein, the free and shifted bands were selected using the same box size and quantitated, a similar box was used to determine the background of the sample (Fig. S5), two independent experiments were performed at 15 °C. Percent c1 complex (Fig. 3B and Table 3) was determined by averaging measurements of band intensities from each experiment. Gels run at 37 °C were equilibrated in the running buffer at 37 °C and pre-run for 30 min. The buffer temperature was measured to ensure proper temperature was maintained.

FRET-based DNA-binding studies utilized a 15 bp double-labeled DNA substrate; 5′-TCGGTGATTGTTCAG-3′ and complement (binding site is in italics). The dsDNA site was modified to contain fluorophores attached by hexanyl linkers at the 5′-ends. The FRET pair used was fluorescein (FAM) and 5-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA). The DNA concentration was held at 25 nM and titrated with increasing protein concentrations. The donor fluorophore (FAM) was excited at 495 nm and measurements were taken between 650-500 nm. Affinity is determined by plotting the difference in donor intensity of the protein:DNA complex (relative to the DNA alone) as a function of protein concentration. Data were plotted in GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 and fit with a single binding site equation; KD (FRET KD in Table 3) reflects the average of curve fit values from two independent experiments.

PGE studies of specific DNA bending

Six DNA fragments, each 150 bps long, containing the SRY-binding site 5′-ATTGTT-3′ located at various distances from the 5′ end, were used at 25 nM. Each fragment was set in complex with WT SRY or variants at either 18 or 50 nM. Gels were made 8% acrylamide (29:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide) and run in 0.5× TBE buffer at 4 °C. The DNA fragments were visualized using Syber Gold and imaged using a Licor Odyssey M imaging system. PGE experiments were run a total of four different sets with each having a different loading pattern to account for gel-specific effects especially at terminal wells. PGE data are plotted as the relative distance of the complex band compared to the free DNA (using the well edge as the starting point of migration) versus the flexure displacement of the binding site. Flexure displacement is calculated by dividing the number of base pairs from the 5′ end of the 150 bp fragment to the central TT (site of partial intercalation) in the target sequence by the total number of bases in the fragment (150 bp). Measurements were made in ImageJ Software. Curves were fit to a second polynomial (quadratic) equation which allows for bend angle calculations (73). Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad 10.2.1 using an unpaired, two-tailed student t test.

Mammalian plasmids

Plasmids with CMV promoter expressing full-length WT and SRY variants were constructed by PCR (47). The cloning site encoded a hemagglutinin (HA) tag in triplicate to enable Western blotting (WB). Selected WT and variant constructs were designed to encode an exogenous nuclear import sequence (PRRRKV) as derived from SV40 large T antigen (74) inserted between the HA tag and the SRY ORF. Mutations of SRY were introduced using QuikChange Multi Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene). Constructions were verified by DNA sequencing.

Cell culture

Rodent CH34 cells (kindly provided by Dr P.K. Donahoe, Massachusetts General Hospital) (45, 47) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 5% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For proteosome-inhibitor studies, transiently transfected cells were treated with MG132 (proteosome inhibitor, Sigma-Aldrich) after maintenance in serum-free media for 24 h. MG132 treatment was conducted for 6 h followed by 18-h incubation in normal media (serum-containing) medium.

Transient transfections

Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 as described by the vendor (Invitrogen). After 8 h in Opti-MEM medium, cells were recovered using fresh growth medium with FBS. SRY expression was monitored by WB via its triplicate HA tag. Transfection efficiencies were determined by ratio of GFP-positive cells to untransfected cells following cotransfection with pCMX-SRY and pCMX-GFP in equal amounts (47).

Western blot

Protein concentrations were measured by BSA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific); cell lysates were subjected to 4 to 20% SDS-PAGE and WB using anti-HA antiserum (Sigma-Aldrich) at a dilution ratio of 1:5000; α-tubulin antiserum provided a loading control. WB following 4 to 20% SDS-PAGE employed HRP-conjugated anti-HA antibody (Roche). The antibodies used are as follows: HA-tag (Cell Signaling (C29F4) rabbit mAb #3724), α-Tubulin (Cell Signaling #2144), YY1 (Cell Signaling (D5D9Z) rabbit mAb #46395), and GAPDH (Cell signaling (14C10) rabbit mAb #2118). The quantification of each band intensity was analyzed by ImageJ software, and statistics were analyzed using an unpaired, two-tailed student t test.

Transcriptional activation assay

Following transient transfection, SRY-mediated transcriptional activation of Sox9 was measured in triplicate by qPCR as described (47). The mixture of 50-fold empty parent plasmid with expression plasmid encoding HA-tagged SRY variants was applied to reduce protein expression in physiological range (ca. 103–104 protein molecules/cell (47)). After transient transfection, cell lysates with the mRNA of target genes were used to convert to cDNA using the two-step qPCR protocol (Bio-Rad). SRY-mediated transcriptional activation of Sox9 was probed. Primer sequences (42, 75) for all of the tested genes were applied as described in Supporting information. The internal control was the specific 5′-TATAA DNA-binding subunit of TFIID. Data analysis included three technical replicates of each of three biological replicates. The statistics were analyzed using an unpaired, two-tailed student t test.

Cellular fractionation

Pelleted cells were resuspended in 10 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 20 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Nonidet P-46, 5% glycerol, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, and 1 mM PMSF. Cells were lysed by five passages through a 25-gauge needle and centrifuged at 2500g for 15 min at 4 °C; supernatants provided cytosolic extracts. Pellets were suspended in nuclear lysis buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.9, 0.225 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, 10 ug/ml leupeptin, 10 ug/ml aprotinin, and 1 mM PMSF), nuclei were sheared by needle passage, incubated on ice for 15 min, and centrifuged 13,000g for 15 min at 4 °C.
