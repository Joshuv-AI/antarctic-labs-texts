Statistical prediction method of inclined shaft blasting fragmentation based on dynamic damage distribution in excavated rock mass
Authors: Xin Wei, Zhan Shen, Kaiwen Song, Dengxing Qu, Xinping Li, Yi Luo, Tingting Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address pilot shaft blockage and fragmentation control issues in inclined shaft blasting excavation, this study investigated the coupling mechanism between excavated rock mass damage distribution and fragmentation gradation using the Tianchi Pumped Storage Power Station water diversion tunnel inclined shaft project. A statistical correlation between dynamic damage distribution and blasting fragmentation was established through field blasting experiments and LS-DYNA numerical simulations. Based on this correlation, a fragmentation prediction model was developed using the dynamic damage distribution of the excavated rock mass.The study analyzed the influence mechanisms of three key parameters - decoupling coefficient, blasthole spacing, and detonating delay time - on rock fragmentation and size distribution, determining optimized blasting parameters for the project. Results show the damage distribution-based prediction model achieved high fitting accuracy (R²=0.9689) with maximum field validation deviation of only 2.49%, demonstrating excellent engineering applicability and promotional value. The decoupling coefficient and blasthole spacing are primary controlling factors for fragmentation distribution. Reducing the decoupling coefficient and decreasing blasthole spacing significantly enhance rock fragmentation degree and fine particle content while suppressing oversized fragment generation. Detonating delay time has limited impact, serving primarily an auxiliary optimization function. Optimized blasting parameters effectively reduced oversized fragment percentage and improved blasting efficiency and economic viability. The proposed blasting fragmentation statistical prediction method provides a novel and implementable technical approach for fragmentation prediction and parameter optimization in complex underground engineering projects.Subject terms: Civil engineering, Energy infrastructure

Introduction

In pumped storage power station construction, the excavation of water diversion tunnel inclined shafts faces numerous technical challenges due to complex conditions including large cross-sectional dimensions, high forming precision requirements, and difficulties in debris transportation. To effectively address these issues, the reverse pilot shaft expansion method is widely adopted in engineering practice1,2which involves first excavating a small-diameter pilot shaft followed by blasting expansion to the design cross-section. This approach enables phased and refined construction, effectively improving the precision and safety of inclined shaft formation while optimizing debris removal pathways and ensuring smooth operation of the chute system, thereby significantly enhancing overall construction efficiency and economic viability. During the reverse pilot shaft expansion process, the smooth removal of rock debris from the pilot shaft becomes a critical factor determining the success of the technique. Due to the confined space of inclined shafts and reliance on gravity-driven continuous transportation, oversized rock fragments after blasting can easily cause pilot shaft blockage, increasing secondary blasting and manual clearing workload, severely affecting construction progress. Therefore, achieving precise control of post-blast rock fragmentation has become the core technical challenge for successful implementation of the reverse pilot shaft expansion method3–5.

The fundamental mechanisms governing blasting fragmentation have been extensively investigated through experimental and theoretical approaches. Early research focused on the physical processes of rock breakage under explosive loading6,7. Singh et al.8 employed digital image analysis to examine the effects of charge weight, bench height to drilling depth ratio, stemming length, and other parameters on open-pit blasting fragmentation. Liao et al.9 analyzed charge structure, explosive type, intermediate medium, and hole spacing using LS-DYNA fluid–solid coupling algorithms. Developments in charge structure design have demonstrated significant potential: Melniokov and Marchenkov10 proposed interval charging to reduce excessive crushing and improve energy utilization; Dehghani et al.11 showed that emulsion explosives achieve more uniform size distribution under wet hole conditions; Li et al.3 found water-coupled blasting produces finer, more uniform fragments than air-coupled blasting; Jhanwar et al.12 confirmed that air-decked blasting improves fragmentation in jointed rock; and Hong et al.13 used small-scale single-hole blasting tests to explore charge structure effects. Research on rock mechanical properties has also advanced understanding of fragmentation. Tao et al.14 revealed the influence of rock brittleness and geometry on crack branching and fine particle generation, while Gao et al.15 demonstrated through large-scale experiments that controlling crushing zone size and explosive performance improves efficiency while reducing fines. The role of delay time has been widely recognized: Saadatmand and Katsabanis16 analyzed initiation timing through numerical simulation, and Chen et al.17 found in tunnel blasting that fragmentation first increases then decreases with longer delay, also quantifying the effects of blasthole inclination and delay duration. Li et al.18 further applied analytic hierarchy process (AHP) and a BP neural network to propose a maximum fragmentation control model for shaft blasting. These studies together provide a solid foundation for enriching blasting fragmentation control theory and guiding engineering applications. Although considerable progress has been achieved in the prediction and control of blasting fragmentation, most existing studies have focused on bench blasting in open-pit mines or conventional tunnel blasting. In these contexts, blasting conditions are relatively open, free surfaces are sufficient, and mucking systems face fewer constraints. In contrast, inclined shaft excavation presents unique and more stringent challenges. The construction space is extremely confined, and the free surface available for blasting is severely limited. Moreover, the reverse pilot shaft expansion method commonly adopted in inclined shaft excavation relies heavily on central pilot shafts and slag chutes for mucking. Under such conditions, oversized rock fragments generated by blasting can easily interlock and block the mucking pathway, causing severe construction delays, additional secondary blasting, and higher labor costs. These issues make the control of fragmentation size distribution far more critical in inclined shafts than in conventional blasting scenarios.

Effective control of blasting fragmentation depends on accurate prediction of fragmentation distribution, making prediction methods a key foundation for blasting assessment, control, and optimization19–21. Early theoretical models include the Harries model22which attributes rock mass cracking to dynamic strain with crack spacing defining block size. Koshelev et al.23 showed that Rosin–Rammler (R-R) distribution curves match sieving data, forming the basis for particle size characterization. Building on this, Kuznetsov24 derived empirical formulas from large-scale tests, while Cunningham25 introduced the classic Kuz-Ram model assuming R-R distribution. To improve prediction in the fine particle zone, Esen et al.26 proposed the Crushed Zone Model (CZM), and Ouchterlony27 developed the Swebrec function for more accurate fine fragmentation description. With the rise of artificial intelligence, machine learning has been widely applied to fragmentation prediction. Bahrami28 used artificial neural networks with multiple blasting parameters to model iron ore fragmentation. Shi et al.29 applied support vector regression for average fragment size prediction, while Huan et al.30 developed the NRBO-CNN-LSSVM hybrid model. Yu et al.31 employed a bionic hummingbird algorithm to optimize hyperparameters, and Sui et al.32 integrated random forest and XGBoost in ensemble learning for open-pit blasting prediction. Liu33 combined kernel PCA and beetle antennae search to optimize BP neural networks for coal mine blasting. More recently, Li34 proposed a hybrid Random Forest model optimized by Atomic Orbital Search (AOS) and Logistic Mapping (LM) to estimate maximum fragment size in tunnel blasting. Based on a highway tunnel case, Li35 further introduced a hybrid multi-objective intelligent model integrating SVR and MOGJO to predict and optimize adverse responses such as maximum block size. Liu36 coupled an optimal prediction model with improved PSO and used machine learning interpretability analysis to confirm geological conditions as the dominant factor influencing blasting performance. These developments illustrate the evolution from theoretical and empirical models to advanced machine learning and hybrid approaches, providing a strong basis for further improvement of fragmentation prediction methods in underground engineering.

Traditional empirical fragmentation formulas, such as Kuz-Ram and CZM, are derived from simplified geometric assumptions and statistical regression. While convenient, these models neglect the intrinsic mechanisms of dynamic rock damage evolution during blasting, which limits their adaptability under the complex geological conditions and strict operational requirements of inclined shafts. On the other hand, emerging machine learning approaches can achieve high prediction accuracy, but their performance strongly depends on large volumes of high-quality training data and substantial computational resources—requirements that are often unrealistic in engineering practice, especially for site-specific projects with limited field data. In contrast, the dynamic damage distribution–based prediction model proposed in this study does not rely on empirical simplifications or extensive training datasets. Instead, it establishes a statistical mapping relationship between the physically simulated damage distribution of the rock mass and the resulting fragmentation gradation. By explicitly incorporating the coupling mechanism between rock damage evolution and fragmentation size distribution, the model provides a mechanistic and data-efficient framework. This enables more accurate and transferable fragmentation prediction under the confined blasting spaces and stringent mucking requirements of inclined shafts, thereby overcoming the key limitations of both empirical formulas and purely data-driven methods.

This study tackles the challenges of predicting and controlling fragmentation in inclined shaft blasting by proposing a dynamic damage distribution–based prediction model that integrates field blasting test data with ANSYS/LS-DYNA numerical simulations. Unlike traditional empirical formulas such as Kuz-Ram and CZM, which rely on oversimplified geometric assumptions and statistical regression and therefore often diverge from actual field fragmentation results, the proposed model explicitly incorporates the physical mechanism of rock damage evolution. This enables more reliable predictions under the confined spaces and complex geological conditions of inclined shafts. In contrast to machine learning approaches, which achieve high prediction accuracy but depend on large, high-quality datasets and intensive computational resources, our method is data-efficient and practical. By establishing a statistical mapping relationship between simulated dynamic damage distributions and observed fragmentation gradation, the model avoids the limitations of both empirical simplifications and data-driven dependency. Large-scale engineering applications have verified its effectiveness, and systematic parameter studies further demonstrate its ability to optimize key blasting parameters—such as decoupling coefficient, initiation delay time, and blasthole spacing—thereby reducing oversized fragments, enhancing efficiency, and improving economic performance.

Numerical simulation of blast expansion of inclined shafts

Numerical model

Tianchi Pumped Storage Power Station is located in the upper reaches of the Huangya River, controlling a watershed area of about 112.9 km². The site is approximately 45 km from Wanxi Substation and 85 km from Hongniwan Substation. The project consists of an upper reservoir, lower reservoir, an underground powerhouse system, a water diversion system, and a ground switchyard (Fig. 1). The water diversion system includes two parallel tunnels (No. 1 and No. 2) oriented at NE22.2381°, with an axial spacing of 66 m. Each tunnel is connected to the reservoirs through an upper and a lower inclined shaft with a designed inclination of 55°. The excavation adopts circular cross-sections, with excavation radius of 4.2 m for the upper inclined shaft and 4.0 m for the lower inclined shaft.

Tianchi pumped storage power station.

This study focuses on the blasting excavation of the lower inclined shaft section of the No. 2 water diversion tunnel, combining field blasting experiments and numerical simulation methods to establish an engineering-oriented blasting fragmentation prediction method. It systematically analyzes the influence patterns of blasting parameters on rock mass fragmentation effects and size distribution, aiming to provide theoretical foundations and technical pathways for blasting optimization in complex inclined shaft engineering. Detailed engineering geological and hydrogeological investigations were carried out for the lower inclined shaft section of the No. 2 water diversion tunnel. The surrounding rock is predominantly composed of fresh, massive granite with relatively high integrity and strength. Fractures and joints are very limited, and structural planes are not significantly developed, which provides favorable stability conditions for blasting excavation. The hydrogeological setting is relatively simple, with no large-scale groundwater inflow, and only minor local seepage may occur along isolated discontinuities. During the investigation, in-situ rock mass sampling was performed, and the necessary parameters were subsequently determined through basic mechanical tests. These results provide reliable data support for the numerical simulations and parameter analyses in this study.

According to field engineering geological data, the strata in the inclined shaft area mainly consist of Lower Proterozoic mixed granitic gneiss, predominantly granite, with hard and dense rock mass structure, generally presenting a fresh state. No large-scale fault structures were found along the tunnel alignment, with weak regional tectonic activity and no obvious fracture zone development. The surrounding rock classification is mainly Class II and Class III, with high rock mass integrity and overall stable structure, providing favorable geological conditions for blasting excavation.

This study relies on the Tianchi Pumped Storage Power Station inclined shaft blasting project and conducts modeling analysis of the inclined shaft blasting excavation process based on the LS-DYNA numerical simulation platform. Considering the symmetry of the inclined shaft structure in geometric configuration and charge arrangement, the model adopts a 1/2 symmetric cross-section modeling scheme, ensuring simulation accuracy while improving computational efficiency. The research focuses on rock mass response and dynamic damage distribution within the inclined shaft excavation body under blasting loads, without involving structural response and stability analysis of the surrounding stable rock mass. Therefore, the modeling object is the excavation zone itself. The geometric parameters of the inclined shaft excavation body are set according to actual construction conditions: excavation body radius of 4.0 m, inclination angle of 55°, advance direction length of 4 m (corresponding to single blast advance), and central pilot shaft (or pilot hole) radius of 1.2 m. Blasthole diameter is 42 mm with depth of 3 m, including charge length of 2 m and stemming length of 1 m, using decoupled charging with 32 mm diameter. To accurately reflect the interaction between explosive detonation gas production and rock mass, the model introduces an ALE (Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian) air layer between explosive and rock mass, enabling simulation of dynamic propagation of explosion-generated gases and fluid-solid coupling processes.

To achieve sufficiently accurate response characteristics for predicting blasting fragmentation based on dynamic damage evolution, this study employed a particularly fine mesh. Such refinement is essential for reliably capturing stress wave propagation, crack initiation and coalescence, and progressive rock mass damage. The adopted discretization is far smaller—by one to two orders of magnitude—than both the overall model dimensions (8 m) and the characteristic fragment size observed in field tests (0.1–0.3 m), thus providing more than adequate spatial resolution. Inevitably, finer meshes significantly increase computational demand and runtime. To address this, large-scale simulations were executed on a high-performance computing server. After balancing numerical accuracy against computational efficiency, a uniform Lagrange mesh with an element size of 10 mm, resulting in approximately 4.26 million elements, was ultimately selected as the most appropriate and reliable discretization, and therefore no additional mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted. Model material zoning is shown in Fig. 2, with yellow representing blasthole stemming zone, red representing explosive charge zone, and blue representing rock mass region. Numerical model parameters

In the numerical simulation of this study, the RHT (Riedel–Hiermaier–Thoma model) material constitutive model is adopted for the rock mass portion. Compared with traditional material models such as HJC (Holmquist–Johnson–Cook model) and TCK (Taylor-Chen-Kuszmaul model), the RHT model can comprehensively consider strain hardening, pressure dependence, strain sensitivity, compressive damage softening, and the influence of deviatoric stress on failure surface shape when describing rock failure processes. Therefore, the RHT model demonstrates higher fidelity in impact loading and projectile penetration studies of materials such as concrete and rock, and is particularly suitable for engineering problems involving large deformation and high strain rates such as rock (especially hard rock) blasting simulation37.

The RHT model defines maximum failure surface, elastic limit surface and residual failure surface through different dynamic factors, thereby more precisely distinguishing three stages in the rock failure process: elastic stage, linear elastic stage and damage softening stage. In the RHT constitutive model, parameter determination generally combines direct laboratory measurements, dynamic testing, and numerical calibration. Static mechanical properties such as uniaxial compressive strength, tensile strength, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio are typically derived from laboratory tests. Dynamic enhancement factors, including strain-rate coefficients, are obtained from Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) experiments, while pressure-dependent strength parameters are determined through triaxial compression tests under different confining pressures. Parameters that are difficult to measure directly, such as damage evolution and residual strength coefficients, are usually established through iterative numerical back-analysis and validation against experimental or field data.

The basic static properties, including uniaxial compressive strength, tensile strength, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio, were directly measured from our laboratory experiments. For dynamic effects, strain-rate factors were partly obtained from SHPB tests, while other parameters that could not be reliably determined through laboratory testing were adopted with reference to literature on granite of similar properties. Statistical prediction method for excavated rock mass blasting fragmentation in inclined shafts

Coupling mechanism of dynamic damage distribution and fragmentation gradation

In rock mechanics research, the damage variable (D) is widely used to quantitatively describe the evolution process of internal damage in rocks. The damage variable is a dimensionless parameter with values typically ranging from 0 to 1, corresponding to the transition of rock from intact state to complete failure state. Its physical meaning can be expressed through the relationship between effective load-bearing area and initial area42:

where D is the damage variable; is the initial load-bearing area, m; is the effective load-bearing area, m.

From a microscopic perspective, the damage variable D is an important indicator characterizing the development state of internal microcracks within elements, with its value reflecting the quantity, scale, and distribution density of internal microcracks in materials under external loading. When more and larger-scale microcracks are generated within elements, the damage variable D value increases accordingly. Conversely, if there are fewer microcracks with smaller sizes, the corresponding D value is lower. Therefore, the damage variable is essentially a quantitative representation of the microscopic crack evolution process within materials43,44.

For the entire rock system, when subjected to blasting loads, damage within rock mass elements gradually accumulates and forms interconnected fracture networks, with the final damage distribution characteristics reflecting the development density and degree of fractures within the system45,46. The higher the overall damage degree of the system, the greater the proportion of elements with large D values, indicating that the rock mass is fully fragmented and ultimately tends to form blocks with smaller fragmentation sizes. Conversely, if damage distribution is limited and concentrated, the rock mass tends to form large fragmented blocks47–49. Therefore, the fragmentation size gradation characteristics of the rock mass system after blasting are essentially the macroscopic manifestation of internal dynamic damage evolution and its spatial distribution, as shown in Fig. 6.

Coupling mechanism of dynamic damage distribution and blasting fragmentation grading.

In the numerical simulation process, the dynamic damage variable D after blasting cannot be directly matched one-to-one with specific fragment sizes. However, its distribution characteristics within the overall model system provide an important basis for assessing the extent of blast-induced crack development in the rock mass50. Through statistical analysis methods such as volume distribution statistics and cumulative distribution functions, nonlinear or probabilistic mapping relationships can be established between the spatial distribution characteristics of damage variables and block size gradation parameters51. This mapping relationship is not based on direct element-level correspondence, but forms statistical correlations at the system level, thereby revealing the coupling mechanism between microscopic dynamic damage distribution and macroscopic fragmentation size gradation52,53.

For specific rock systems, under the same blasting or loading conditions, rock fragment size distributions often exhibit relatively stable fractal characteristics54. By statistically analyzing the spatial distribution of rock damage, the resulting fragmentation grading can be effectively predicted, thus providing a theoretical basis for fragmentation control. It should be noted that fragmentation is influenced by multiple factors, such as geological conditions, rock strength, joint development, explosive properties, and blast design parameters. In the proposed approach, these factors are not treated in isolation but are incorporated into the numerical simulation inputs that determine the evolution of the damage field. As a result, the damage distribution implicitly reflects the combined effects of these variables. When blasting parameters change, the degree of rock fragmentation and the corresponding size distribution also change, and this response can be quantitatively captured by comparing the resulting damage fields under different scenarios55. In this way, the mapping relationship maintains its scientific validity while remaining adaptable to varying geological and blasting conditions. Based on this mechanism, blasting parameter optimization driven by fragmentation feedback can be achieved, providing a more generalizable theoretical and methodological framework for efficient fragmentation control in engineering practice.

To achieve efficient prediction and precise control of blasting fragmentation, there is an urgent need to deeply elucidate the quantitative coupling relationship between blasting damage distribution characteristics and post-blast fragmentation gradation in engineering rock mass systems. Based on field-measured blasting fragmentation data from inclined shafts and high-precision numerical simulation results, this paper employs statistical methods to establish a mapping model between damage variable distribution and fragmentation gradation under inclined shaft blasting conditions. Accordingly, a statistical prediction method for blasting fragmentation oriented toward engineering practice is proposed. Compared with traditional empirical formulas and artificial intelligence methods that rely on big data training, this method better reflects the physical mechanisms of damage and fragmentation formation during rock mass blasting processes, offering good interpretability and adaptability. By combining blasting experiments and numerical simulations under different working conditions, it can flexibly adapt to various types and complexity levels of blasting engineering without requiring massive historical data or high computational power as support. This approach provides reliable and practical solutions for fragmentation prediction and control. For blasting fragmentation control, this method has broad engineering applicability and promotional value.

Statistical mapping of dynamic damage distribution to blasting fragmentation grading

To construct a mapping relationship model between the dynamic damage distribution of inclined shaft excavation blasting and fragmentation parameters, this study combined field blasting fragmentation tests with high-precision numerical simulation analysis. In the field blasting tests, expanded ammonium nitrate was used as the explosive. The blasting scheme strictly followed the actual design adopted in the construction site: a total of 87 blastholes were arranged, corresponding to a blasting volume of 109.16 m³ and a total charge of 91.2 kg, with an explosive consumption of 0.84 kg/m³. The blasthole diameter was 42 mm, and an uncoupled charge method was employed with a charge diameter of 32 mm. These parameter settings were directly derived from the practical blasting design requirements of the project, ensuring engineering relevance and applicability of the test results. Using the actual construction scheme as the basis not only guarantees that the experimental conditions are consistent with real engineering practice, but also allows the numerical simulation results to be directly comparable with field performance. This approach enhances the reliability of the mapping relationship model and ensures that the research outcomes can provide effective guidance for similar engineering applications. After the blasting was completed, a high-resolution digital camera was first used to capture comprehensive images of the inclined shaft blast pile area. To ensure data accuracy and integrity, the image acquisition process strictly controlled the shooting angle, lighting conditions, and camera parameters to obtain clear images of the blast pile surface. The captured images were then imported into Split-Desktop 4.0, a professional fragmentation analysis software. Based on advanced image recognition algorithms and edge detection technology, this software automatically identifies and segments the contours of individual rock blocks within the blast pile. Through pixel calibration and size conversion, it accurately calculates the equivalent particle size of each rock block. Through statistical analysis of all identifiable rock blocks within the blast pile area, the particle size distribution curve and related statistical parameters of the on-site blast fragmentation were ultimately obtained, as shown in Fig. 7(a).

Mapping relationship between fragmentation grading and damage distribution.

In this study, the damage complement (1–D) was adopted as a statistical proxy for fragmentation size. Physically, the damage variable D describes the degree of strength degradation and failure of an element or region; when D approaches 1, the rock mass tends toward complete failure, corresponding to smaller fragment sizes, whereas larger values of 1–D indicate higher structural integrity and thus larger potential fragment sizes. Therefore, 1–D can be regarded as a “retained scale” of rock blocks in a statistical sense, providing a rational basis for establishing the quantitative mapping between damage and fragmentation distributions.

To establish a quantitative mapping relationship between dynamic damage distribution and blasting fragmentation, a three-dimensional geometric analysis method was employed for in-depth research. Two basic data surfaces were constructed in a three-dimensional coordinate system: the cumulative percentage curve of the fragmentation distribution measured in situ (red) was stretched and extended perpendicularly to the fragmentation-percentage plane to form a fragmentation distribution spatial surface; simultaneously, the cumulative percentage curve of the damage complementarity value (1-D) obtained through numerical simulation (blue) was stretched and extended perpendicularly to the damage-percentage plane to form a damage distribution spatial surface. The intersection of these two surfaces in three-dimensional space forms a unique spatial curve (purple), which mathematically characterizes the intrinsic relationship between fragmentation, damage degree, and cumulative percentage. Physically, this spatial curve reveals the statistical correspondence between rock fragments of different particle sizes and their corresponding damage states under the same cumulative percentage, as shown in Fig. 8(a). This three-dimensional mapping relationship provides an important theoretical basis for understanding the intrinsic mechanisms of rock damage evolution and final fragmentation formation during blasting. Orthogonally projecting the above three-dimensional spatial curve onto the fragmentation-damage two-dimensional plane yields its planar representation (green curve), as shown in Fig. 8(d). This two-dimensional projection curve intuitively demonstrates the one-to-one correspondence between fragmentation and damage severity, forming the core statistical mapping model of this study. This model allows for direct prediction of the corresponding blasting fragmentation distribution based on the damage field distribution obtained through numerical simulation, thereby achieving a quantitative transformation from rock mass damage state to fragmentation distribution. It is important to emphasize that this mapping relationship is not based on a point-to-point correspondence between the damage variable D of a single numerical unit and a specific particle size, but rather a holistic mapping established at the system level through statistical methods. This method fully utilizes the coupling mechanism between the evolution of rock mass damage and fragmentation distribution during blasting, providing a practical and generalizable analytical approach for predicting fragmentation distribution during inclined shaft blasting excavation.

The main sources include: discretization errors of the numerical model, as the spatial distribution of the damage field depends on mesh division and element size, potentially influencing the statistical characteristics of 1–D; sampling bias in experimental data, since the measured fragmentation distribution is affected by sampling range, sieving method, and statistical criteria, which may differ from the actual overall distribution; assumptions in the mapping method, as the present study establishes a system-level statistical correspondence rather than a point-to-point match, which may lead to deviations at the local scale; and simplifications in the dynamic process, where the model does not fully account for complex factors such as joint structures and stress wave reflections, thereby introducing additional uncertainties.

The mapping relationship in this study was established based on numerical simulations and field tests. Since the engineering site is characterized by favorable geological conditions and a high degree of rock mass integrity, joints and fractures were not explicitly considered in the numerical modeling. Therefore, the proposed mapping relationship is primarily applicable to blasting fragmentation prediction in hard rock masses with good integrity and structural continuity. In rock masses with well-developed jointing, structural parameters such as joint sets, orientations, spacing, and apertures can significantly influence stress wave propagation, crack extension paths, and the resulting fragmentation distribution56. Direct application of the present method under such conditions may lead to prediction errors. Consequently, when extending this technical approach to jointed rock masses, it is necessary to explicitly incorporate joint networks into the numerical simulations, so as to account for their effects on the dynamic response and fracture mechanisms of the rock mass. This ensures the scientific validity and adaptability of the mapping relationship, while also defining the applicability boundaries of the current study and providing directions for future research.

In summary, the proposed statistical prediction method for inclined shaft blasting fragmentation can accurately reflect actual blasting effects under different blasting parameter conditions, demonstrating good applicability and engineering promotion value, and can provide effective technical support for blasting parameter optimization and fragmentation control in similar engineering projects.

Influence of blasting parameters on blasting fragmentation control in inclined shafts

In inclined shaft blasting design, the coupling coefficient, blasthole spacing, and detonating delay time are three key adjustable parameters that directly affect rock fragmentation effectiveness, possessing clear physical significance and engineering control value. The coupling coefficient determines the degree of explosive energy coupling at the blasthole-rock mass interface, thereby affecting the transmission efficiency of blasting energy to the rock mass; the initiation delay regulates the interference and superposition pattern of stress waves in the rock mass, influencing the temporal characteristics and damage range of the wave field; blasthole spacing determines the spatial distribution of the explosion stress field and its coupling relationship with rock mass structural planes, directly relating to the spatial coverage efficiency of explosive energy and the macroscopic fragmentation morphology of rock. The combined effect of these three factors determines the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of the stress field in the blasting zone, serving as the core controlling factors affecting blasting damage mechanisms and fragmentation gradation.

Blasting parameter optimization based on blasting fragmentation

Optimization indicators

In the process of inclined shaft blasting expansion excavation and mucking, central pilot shaft blockage problems are mainly caused by two types of structural mechanisms: occlusal arch and viscous arch18,57as shown in Fig. 19. Occlusal arches are typically formed by oversized rock blocks that mutually interlock and compress within the limited space of the pilot shaft, possessing certain self-stability and serving as the initial structure for blockage formation. This structure can provide a supporting platform for subsequently falling fine materials, which under the action of mixtures such as dust and moisture, promotes bonding between fine particles and ultimately forms viscous arches with more stable structure and greater cleaning difficulty. The formation of viscous arches depends on cohesive forces between fine particles and moisture cementation effects, characterized by high blockage strength and great harmfulness. Therefore, fragmentation control during inclined shaft blasting expansion excavation is crucial. On one hand, oversized rock blocks easily form interlocking structures in the central pilot shaft, inducing blockage, affecting mucking efficiency, and delaying construction progress; on the other hand, if blasting fragmentation is too large, additional secondary crushing operations are required, increasing operational costs and reducing construction efficiency. For this reason, in actual inclined shaft engineering, blasting parameters should be optimized to achieve coordinated matching between rock fragmentation and construction processes. Under the premise of meeting the requirements for smooth mucking in the central pilot shaft and fragmentation control, while considering both economy and technicality, efficient and reliable blasting excavation schemes should be developed.

Through comparative analysis combining Figs. 14 and 17, it can be seen that compared to optimizing detonating delay or blasthole spacing individually, adopting a multi-parameter synergistic optimization strategy can significantly enhance blasting coupling effects and achieve efficient utilization and reasonable distribution of blasting energy. Through joint adjustment of detonating delay and blasthole spacing, not only are the propagation paths and interference patterns of shock waves improved, but the transmission and concentration of energy within rock mass are also optimized, thereby maximizing the enhancement of rock mass damage effects and strengthening fragmentation quality. These results fully demonstrate that multi-parameter synergistic optimization has superiority in inclined shaft blasting design and is an important means to achieve balanced rock fragmentation effects and improved construction efficiency.

Drawing a pie chart of inclined shaft blasting fragmentation distribution (Fig. 25) can intuitively demonstrate the superiority of the optimized conditions. After optimizing blasting parameters, the volume proportions of both extralarge-blocks and large-blocks in the muck pile decreased significantly: extralarge-blocks decreased from 11.23 to 8.09%, large-blocks decreased from 52.63 to 46.95%, with a total reduction of approximately 19.38%. Meanwhile, the proportion of small-blocks increased significantly to 32.23%, showing overall enhancement in blasting fragmentation degree. The key indicator boulder yield decreased from 6.33% under original conditions to 4.55%, a reduction of 28.12%, effectively meeting the engineering requirements for fragmentation control in inclined shaft blasting excavation at Tianchi Pumped Storage Power Station. This optimization scheme significantly improves fragmentation effects, enhances fragmentation distribution, and reduces boulder yield while considering blasting economy, thereby effectively reducing pilot shaft blockage risk, lowering subsequent crushing and mucking costs, and improving overall efficiency and economic benefits of inclined shaft blasting construction. The optimization results indicate that dual optimization of inclined shaft blasting fragmentation and cost can be achieved through multi-parameter synergistic adjustment, demonstrating good engineering promotion value.

Conclusion

This study is based on the inclined shaft blasting excavation engineering of the water diversion tunnel of the Tianchi Pumped Storage Power Station. Field blasting experiments and high-precision numerical simulations were combined to systematically investigate the coupling mechanism between excavated rock mass damage distribution and fragmentation gradation during inclined shaft blasting. A fragmentation statistical prediction method based on excavated rock mass dynamic damage distribution was established. In addition, an in-depth analysis was conducted on the influence of key blasting parameters on blasting fragmentation and potential optimization pathways. The main conclusions are as follows:

This study systematically compared fragmentation statistical data from field blasting experiments with LS-DYNA numerical simulation results. The analysis established a statistical mapping relationship between dynamic damage distribution and fragmentation gradation of excavated rock mass in inclined shaft blasting. Based on this relationship, a blasting fragmentation prediction model was proposed using excavated rock mass damage distribution. The model fitting accuracy is extremely high (R²=0.9689). Field blasting verification results show that the fragmentation distribution predicted by the model is highly consistent with measured data, with a maximum deviation of only 2.49% and correlation coefficients all greater than 0.998. Research results indicate that this method can not only achieve accurate prediction of blasting fragmentation, but also has good engineering adaptability and promotion prospects, providing strong technical support for scientific control and parameter optimization of blasting fragmentation in complex underground engineering.

Decoupling coefficient and blasthole spacing are core parameters for inclined shaft blasting fragmentation control. Reducing the decoupling coefficient decreases the air gap within blastholes and increases charge amount, resulting in enhanced detonation energy and stress wave loading capability, significantly promoting rock mass fragmentation. When the decoupling coefficient decreased from 1.75 to 1.05, the volume proportion of extralarge-blocks decreased from 64.96 to 2.70%, while the proportion of small-blocks increased from 5.40 to 70.06%. Meanwhile, appropriately reducing blasthole spacing helps enhance energy density and wave field superposition effects, further improving fragmentation effectiveness. When spacing was reduced from 1.12 m to 0.64 m, the volume proportion of extralarge-blocks decreased from 12.24 to 9.98%, while the proportion of small-blocks increased from 21.53 to 25.54%. Synergistic optimization of both can achieve efficient control of blasting fragmentation distribution.

Adopting delayed detonation can optimize free surface conditions in the blasting zone and improve blasting energy utilization efficiency, reducing boulder yield from 18.28 to 11.23% and increasing the proportion of small-blocks by 28.89%, effectively improving rock mass fragmentation effects. However, delay time parameters have relatively limited influence on fragmentation distribution, mainly due to the small spatial scale of inclined shafts and small delay time differences in the study. Blasting waves from adjacent blastholes overlap spatially and temporally, with limited changes in wave field interference and energy transmission patterns, making it difficult to form significant energy concentration or diversion effects. Therefore, detonating delay optimization mainly serves as an auxiliary measure and needs to be synergistically adjusted with main control parameters such as decoupling coefficient and blasthole spacing to achieve efficient and controllable optimization of blasting fragmentation.

The blasting fragmentation prediction method based on dynamic damage distribution established in this study has obvious advantages, but also has certain limitations. The main advantages include: establishing a quantitative conversion from numerical damage field to on-site fragmentation distribution, overcoming the limitations of traditional empirical formulas; systematically handling the complex correlation between damage, fragmentation and cumulative percentage through three-dimensional spatial mapping; and having clear physical meaning and engineering practicality. However, there are also limitations: the mapping relationship requires on-site calibration and high-precision numerical simulation under specific conditions, which is costly and has limited universality. Future research will focus on conducting verification tests under various rock conditions, establishing a universal mapping model, and combining machine learning technology to improve its adaptability and accuracy, and promoting its application in different underground engineering projects to provide more reliable theoretical support for precise blasting design and intelligent control.
