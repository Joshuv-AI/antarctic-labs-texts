Prognostic model for early gastric cardia cancer after endoscopic submucosal dissection
Authors: Peng Zhang, Xiaoying Ma, Zibin Tian, Zheng Cui, Zhen Zhang, Tao Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a prognostic model incorporating microRNA-1246 (miR-1246), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB-1), α1-antitrypsin (A1AT), and inflammatory markers for predicting outcomes in early gastric cardia cancer after endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 280 patients with early gastric cardia cancer who underwent ESD between January 2021 and December 2023. Patients were divided into a training set (n = 196) and a validation set (n = 84) at a ratio of 7:3. Influencing factors were screened using univariate, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and multivariate logistic regression analyses in the training set. A nomogram was constructed, and the predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calibration curves. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical value.ResultsThe incidence of poor prognosis was comparable between training (43.88%) and validation set (34.52%). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and Helicobacter pylori infection as independent risk factors for poor prognosis (All P < 0.05). The nomogram demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability, with AUC of 0.789 (95% CI: 0.701–0.877) in the training set and 0.735 (95% CI: 0.638–0.835) in the validation set. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.625 and 0.841 for the training set, and 0.481 and 0.786 for the validation set, respectively. Calibration curves indicated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities.ConclusionThe proposed prognostic model offers a practical tool for individualized risk assessment post-ESD. However, the clinical application warrants further validation in large-scale, multi-center prospective studies with long-term follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03265-1.Keywords: Early gastric cardia cancer, Endoscopic submucosal dissection, MicroRNA-1246, High mobility group box 1, Α1-antitrypsin, Prognostic model

Introduction

Early gastric cardia cancer is defined as cancerous lesions confined to the mucosa or submucosa, regardless of lymph node metastasis [1, 2]. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become a key treatment due to the minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery [3, 4]. However, some patients still experience poor prognoses, such as recurrence and metastasis [5]. Emerging evidence implicates three biomarker classes in tumor progression: (1) miRNAs (particularly miR-1246) regulating cellular proliferation, (2) HMGB-1 mediating inflammatory responses, and (3) A1AT modulating protease activity. For example, miR-1246 regulates tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis [6, 7], while HMGB-1 is linked to inflammation and cancer progression [8, 9]. A1AT, an anti-inflammatory protein, is associated with tumor prognosis [10, 11]. Beyond intrinsic tumor factors, Helicobacter pylori (H.pylori) infection is a known risk factor for gastric cardia adenocarcinoma [12, 13]. Currently, there are few studies on constructing prognostic models combined with these indicators to assess the prognosis of early gastric cardia cancer after ESD. This study aimed to develop a clinically applicable predictive model incorporating both molecular and pathological variables to guide management of early gastric cardia cancer after ESD.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

280 consecutive patients who underwent ESD for early gastric cardia cancer between January 2021 to December 2023 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria: (1) Pathologically confirmed early-stage adenocarcinoma; (2) Meeting ESD indications; (3) Written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Other malignancies; (2) Severe organ dysfunction; (3) Preoperative anti-tumor therapy. Patients were randomized into training (n = 196) and validation (n = 84) sets (7:3 ratio). All ESD cases were selected according to standardized criteria: (1) mucosal or submucosal tumors (T1a/T1b) ≤ 3 cm, (2) well/moderately differentiated histology, (3) no lymphovascular invasion on pretreatment assessment, and (4) no clinical lymph node metastasis (cN0) on endoscopic ultrasound and CT. These criteria align with JGES guidelines (2020) and were uniformly applied throughout the study period. To minimize selection bias, consecutive eligible patients were enrolled regardless of pandemic status. Our center maintained consistent ESD selection protocols throughout the study period, as verified by independent audits. Based on an estimated 15% incidence of poor prognosis (recurrence/metastasis) in early gastric cardia cancer post-ESD, we calculated that a minimum of 196 patients in the training set would provide 80% power (α = 0.05, two-tailed) to detect an odds ratio (OR) ≥ 2.0 for binary predictors, assuming 10 events per variable (EPV) for the 5 covariates in our model. This aligns with recommendations for logistic regression to minimize overfitting. Internal validation was further strengthened by bootstrapping (1,000 resamples) to estimate optimism-corrected performance metrics.

Data collection

General clinical data of patients were collected, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history and family history of tumors. The clinicopathological characteristics of patients, such as tumor location, were recorded in detail. Information related to the surgery, such as operation time and intraoperative blood loss, was recorded. The expression level of miR-1246 in the cancer tissues of patients was detected by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and the expression levels of HMGB-1 and A1AT in serum were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Inflammatory indicators, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were detected. The Helicobacter pylori infection status was detected by urea breath test or rapid urease test under gastroscopy. Meanwhile, other pathogen indicators possibly related to infection, such as Epstein-Barr virus antibodies and human papillomavirus (HPV) nucleic acid, were also detected.

All biomarkers were measured preoperatively under standardized conditions: miR-1246 was quantified from diagnostic biopsy specimens obtained during initial workup (median 7 days pre-ESD, IQR 5–10), while serum markers (HMGB-1, A1AT) were assessed from fasting morning samples (8:00–10:00 AM) collected 1–3 days before ESD after 8-hour fasts and 72-hour NSAID avoidance. Post-ESD resection specimens were used solely for validation, showing strong correlation with biopsy-derived miR-1246 levels (r = 0.92, P < 0.001). Quality control measures included: Inter-assay CVs < 10% for all biomarkers (verified monthly); Standard curves run in duplicate for each plate; Blinded duplicate samples (10% random selection) for reproducibility assessment; Use of NIST-traceable standards for ELISA assays. Biomarker measurements followed standardized protocols: miR-1246 was quantified via qRT-PCR (TaqMan 478581_mir, U6-normalized, ΔCt < 7.0 cutoff); serum HMGB-1 (Tecan ST51011) and A1AT (R&D DAPTA00) were measured by ELISA with BCA protein normalization. All assays included triplicate measurements, three-level quality controls (inter-assay CV < 9%), and 10% blinded duplicates (κ > 0.90). Optimal cutoffs were determined by ROC analysis (Youden’s index) while preserving clinical interpretability.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen tissue samples (30 mg) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat#15596026) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity (A260/A280 ratio > 1.8) and concentration were measured by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Cat#RR036A).

Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green (Toyobo, Cat#QPK-201) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche). The primer sequences were: (1) HMGB1: F 5’-ATGGCAAAAGCGGACAAGG-3’, R 5’-CTTCGCAACATCACCAATGAG-3’ (amplicon 142 bp); (2) A1AT: F 5’-GCTGGGTGTGAGGATGTCT-3’, R 5’-CAGGGTGTGGTAGAGCAGTT-3’ (amplicon 168 bp); (3) Reference genes: β-actin (F 5’-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3’, R 5’-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3’) and GAPDH (F 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’, R 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’). Cycling conditions: 95 °C 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C 5 s / 60 °C 30 s. Relative expression was calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCt method with normalization to reference genes.

HMGB-1 and A1AT ELISA

Serum levels of HMGB-1 and A1AT were measured using commercial ELISA kits: (1) HMGB-1: Human HMGB1 ELISA Kit (IBL International, Cat#ST51011), detection range 0.2–100 ng/mL, intra-assay CV 4.8%, inter-assay CV 7.2%; (2) A1AT: Human SERPINA1 ELISA Kit (Abcam, Cat#ab108799), detection range 1.56–100 ng/mL, intra-assay CV 5.3%, inter-assay CV 8.1%. All samples were tested in duplicate. Values below the detection limit were assigned as 0.1 ng/mL (HMGB-1) or 0.5 ng/mL (A1AT) for statistical analysis.

Definition of pathogen positivity

Helicobacter pylori

Positive if either (1) gastric biopsy rapid urease test (RUT, Hongshi Biotech) turned pink within 2 h, or (2) histology (Giemsa stain) showed curved bacilli in ≥ 3 high-power fields.

EBV

Positive by EBER-1 in situ hybridization (ISH, Leica Biosystems, Cat#PB0589) with nuclear staining in > 10% tumor cells.

HPV

Positive if PCR with consensus primers (MY09/MY11) yielded a band of 450 bp, confirmed by type-specific primers (HPV16/18) and sequencing.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients were followed for 24 months post-ESD to assess short-term outcomes, including recurrence, metastasis, or tumor-related death. However, this duration may be insufficient to capture late recurrences, which are critical for evaluating long-term prognosis in early gastric cancer. Future studies should extend follow-up to ≥ 5 years to validate the model’s predictive accuracy for late events.

Construction and validation of nomogram model

In this study, a standard model development and validation process was adopted. First, a nomogram prediction model was constructed based on the training set data. Each independent risk factor in the model was assigned corresponding scores according to the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Then, the fitting effect of the model on the training set was evaluated by plotting the ROC curve, calibration curve, and decision curve. These curves demonstrated the prediction ability and calibration performance of the model on the training data. Subsequently, the constructed model was applied to an independent validation set, and the generalization ability of the model was evaluated by the same evaluation methods (ROC curve, calibration curve, and decision curve). To demonstrate incremental value, we compared our nomogram with two established tools: (1) eCura system, and (2) AJCC 8th edition staging (T1a/T1b).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 and R 4.2.1 [14]. Count data were presented as the number of cases (percentage), and the χ² test was used for comparisons between groups. Measurement data conforming to the normal distribution were presented as ± s, and the independent-samples t-test was used for comparisons between groups. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression (using the ‘glmnet’ package in R) was performed to screen predictors and prevent overfitting. The optimal lambda value was selected via 10-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO model were included in subsequent multivariate logistic regression. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors. A nomogram was constructed using the “rms” package in R. Model performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Model stability was assessed via: (1) Bootstrapping: 1,000 resamples to calculate optimism-corrected C-statistics and calibration slopes; (2) Events-per-variable (EPV): Confirmed EPV >10 for all covariates to mitigate overfitting. Nomogram performance was evaluated by areas under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration plots (Hosmer-Lemeshow test), and DCA. To elucidate outcome-specific predictors, separate univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted for each component of the composite endpoint-recurrence, metastasis, and tumor-related death. For each analysis, odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Interaction terms were introduced into the multivariate logistic regression model for the composite endpoint.

Complete case analysis: The 12 patients with incomplete follow-up were excluded, and the remaining 268 patients were used for initial model construction (including LASSO variable selection and multivariate logistic regression); Multiple imputation: To reduce selection bias from exclusion, 5 imputed datasets were generated for the 12 patients using predictive mean matching (PMM). This method was selected for its suitability for both continuous variables and categorical variables, which aligned with the diverse variable types in our study.

Given that non-tumor-related deaths (competing events) may bias the assessment of the composite endpoint (recurrence/metastasis/death), we performed competing risk analysis using the Fine-Gray proportional subdistribution hazards model. This model estimates the cumulative incidence function (CIF) of poor prognosis while adjusting for competing events, with subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) and 95% CI reported for each independent risk factor.

To assess the consistency and generalizability of the nomogram’s performance across clinically relevant patient populations, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses on the combined cohort (n = 280). The discriminative ability of the nomogram was evaluated within predefined subgroups based on: age (dichotomized at the median: 62 years) and Helicobacter pylori infection status (positive vs. negative). The potential heterogeneity of the predictive performance across subgroups was formally tested by introducing an interaction term between the subgroup variable and the nomogram’s linear predictor into the logistic regression model, and comparing models with and without the interaction term using a likelihood ratio test.

Univariate analysis of risk factors for poor surgical prognosis in the training set

In the training set, 86 cases (43.88%) had poor prognosis, and in the validation set, 29 cases (34.52%) had poor prognosis. Univariate analysis showed that there were statistically significant differences in indicators such as age, miR-1246 expression level, HMGB-1, A1AT, TNF-α, and Helicobacter pylori infection between patients with poor and good surgical prognosis (All P < 0.05). In the regression model, the tolerance of each variable was > 0.1, the VIF was < 10, and the condition index was < 30. Moreover, there was no situation where the variance proportion of multiple covariates under the same eigenvalue was > 50%. Therefore, there was no collinearity among the covariates (Table 2).

LASSO variable selection

LASSO regression was used to screen 12 potential predictors (age, miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, IL-6, TNF-α, Helicobacter pylori infection, etc.). The optimal lambda (λ = 0.082) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. Variables retained with non-zero coefficients included: age, miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, TNF-α, and Helicobacter pylori infection. IL-6 was excluded due to a zero coefficient (P = 0.085) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

The surgical prognosis was used as the dependent variable, and the factors with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were used as covariates for further multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results showed that the expression level of miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, Age, TNF-α, and Helicobacter pylori infection (positive = 1, negative = 0) were independent risk factors for poor prognosis after ESD in early-stage cardiac cancer (All P < 0.05) (Table 3). Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (all VIF < 2.5) and tolerance values (> 0.4), with final model coefficients provided in Table 3. The logistic equation is: logit(P)=-3.21 + 1.188×(miR-1246) + 0.040×(HMGB-1) + 0.015×(A1AT) + 0.077×(TNF-α) + 1.016×(H.pylori). IL-6 showed univariate association (P = 0.085) but was not retained in the final multivariate model.

Multivariate analysis of poor surgical prognosis in the training set

To further explore potential synergistic effects between key biomarkers (miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, TNF-α) on prognosis, we added their interaction terms to the multivariate model. A significant synergistic interaction was observed between miR-1246 and HMGB-1 (interaction OR = 1.82, 95%CI:1.15–2.88, P = 0.011). Stratified analysis revealed: Patients with high miR-1246 (≥ 1.51) and high HMGB-1 (≥ 29.34 ng/ml) had a 68.2% risk of poor prognosis, whereas the risk was only 21.5% in patients with low levels of both biomarkers (P < 0.001). No significant interactions were detected between other biomarker pairs (e.g., miR-1246 & A1AT, HMGB-1 & TNF-α) (all P > 0.05).

Construction and validation of the nomogram prediction model

Based on the independent risk factors identified by the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a nomogram prediction model for the prognosis of early-stage cardiac cancer after ESD was constructed. Scores were assigned to each independent risk factor in the model, and the total score for predicting poor surgical prognosis was calculated, which was presented as the probability of predicting poor surgical prognosis (Fig. 1).

Nomogram representation of the prognostic model forpost-ESD early gastric cardia cancer

In the training set and validation set, the C-index values of the constructed nomogram prediction model were 0.789 (training set) and 0.735 (validation set), indicating moderate discriminative ability. While these values suggest reasonable model performance, they are not exceptionally high, reducing concerns about overfitting. Calibration curves and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests confirmed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 2). In addition, the ROC curve analysis revealed the efficacy of the nomogram model in predicting the poor prognosis after ESD of early-stage cardiac cancer. The AUC values of the training set and validation set were 0.789 (95% CI: 0.701–0.877) and 0.735 (95% CI: 0.638–0.835), respectively, and the corresponding combinations of sensitivity and specificity were 0.625 and 0.841, and 0.481 and 0.786, respectively (Fig. 3). Decision curve analysis demonstrated clinical utility across a threshold probability range of 0.05–0.87 (Fig. 4). However, clinicians should interpret model predictions cautiously, as false positives (unnecessary interventions) and false negatives (missed high-risk cases) may occur. Optimal risk cut-offs should be tailored to individual clinical contexts, balancing sensitivity and specificity.

Internal validation via bootstrapping

Bootstrapping-derived optimism-adjusted metrics showed minimal degradation in model performance: the corrected C-index was 0.789 (95% CI: 0.705–0.859) for the training set and 0.698 (95% CI: 0.553–0.843) for the validation set. Calibration slopes after correction were 0.90 and 0.88, respectively. The reduced sample size (n = 84 in validation) may increase overfitting risk, though shrinkage factors were applied to mitigate this. Further validation in larger cohorts is recommended.

Comparison with existing prognostic models

(1) eCura system: The nomogram’s risk classification accuracy was 78.9% (95%CI: 72.3%-85.5%) in the training set, representing a 12.0% absolute improvement over eCura (66.9%, 95%CI: 59.8%-74.0%, P = 0.003); (2) AJCC 8th edition staging (T1a/T1b): The nomogram’s AUC (0.789) was significantly higher than AJCC staging (AUC = 0.652, 95%CI: 0.561–0.743, P = 0.002) in the training set. In the validation set, similar improvements were observed (nomogram AUC = 0.735 vs. eCura AUC = 0.621, P = 0.018; vs. AJCC AUC = 0.638, P = 0.025). These results confirm the nomogram’s superiority in risk stratification over conventional tools.

Competing risk analysis

To account for non-tumor-related deaths (n = 3), the Fine-Gray model confirmed that miR-1246 (sHR = 3.12, 95%CI: 1.21–8.05, P = 0.019), HMGB-1 (sHR = 1.038, 95%CI: 1.000-1.078, P = 0.048), A1AT (sHR = 1.014, 95%CI: 1.003–1.025, P = 0.011), TNF-α (sHR = 1.077, 95%CI:1.026–1.130, P = 0.003), and Helicobacter pylori infection (sHR = 2.68, 95%CI:1.35–5.32, P = 0.005) remained independent predictors of poor prognosis. The adjusted cumulative incidence of poor prognosis at 24 months was 41.2% (training set) and 32.8% (validation set), which were 2.6%-3.1% lower than unadjusted estimates, confirming minimal but meaningful impact of competing events (Supplementary Table 1).

Subgroup analyses

The nomogram demonstrated robust and consistent discriminatory performance across all major patient subgroups. The AUC values remained stable, and there were no statistically significant differences in performance between subgroups based on interaction tests (Supplementary Table 2).

Outcome-Specific analyses

Differential predictors were identified for each distinct clinical outcome: Recurrence (n = 68): Independent predictors included miR-1246 (OR = 3.05) and Helicobacter pylori infection (OR = 2.51). Metastasis (n = 32): HMGB-1 (OR = 1.05) and TNF-α (OR = 1.09) were significantly associated. Tumor-related death (n = 15): A1AT (OR = 1.02) and older age (OR = 0.92) emerged as independent predictors (Supplementary Table 3). These results suggest distinct biomarker patterns underlie different clinical trajectories: miR-1246 and Helicobacter pylori are more closely linked to recurrence, HMGB-1 and TNF-α are associated with metastasis, while A1AT and advanced age relate to tumor-related mortality.

Sensitivity analysis for missing data

To assess whether missing follow-up data (n = 12) biased the model’s results, we compared the performance of the prognostic nomogram between complete case analysis (n = 268) and multiple imputation analysis (n = 280). Model discriminative ability: In the training set, the C-index was 0.789 (95%CI: 0.701–0.877) for complete case analysis and 0.782 (95%CI: 0.693–0.871) for multiple imputation analysis (P = 0.612 for difference). In the validation set, the C-index was 0.735 (95%CI: 0.638–0.835) vs. 0.728 (95%CI: 0.629–0.827) (P = 0.705 for difference). Independent risk factors: The OR of key predictors remained highly consistent between the two approaches. For example, miR-1246 (OR = 3.280 vs. 3.215, P = 0.891), HMGB-1 (OR = 1.041 vs. 1.039, P = 0.924), and Helicobacter pylori infection (OR = 2.762 vs. 2.718, P = 0.887) showed no significant differences in their effect sizes.

Discussion

This study presents the first integrated prognostic model combining miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT and inflammatory markers specifically for early gastric cardia cancer after ESD. Unlike existing models, our approach uniquely addresses: (1) post-ESD surveillance needs, (2) cardia cancer’s distinct biology, and (3) multi-pathway interactions between tumor cells and microenvironment. The standardized synchronous biomarker assessment provides a clinically applicable tool that outperforms conventional staging systems. Logistic regression was selected over survival analysis due to: Interval-censored nature of our recurrence data (scheduled 6-month surveillance); Clinical priority for fixed-time risk stratification (2-year decision-making); Substantial missing exact event times (28% cases); This approach provides conservative estimates, which classifies late events as negative. Users should interpret results as: 24-month absolute risks; Conservative estimates (true risk may be higher); Best suited for fixed-interval decision-making. Our findings on the inflammatory microenvironment align with recent studies highlighting the prognostic roles of HMGB-1 and miR-1246 in gastric cancer. HMGB-1, as a damage-associated molecular pattern molecule, has been shown to promote tumor progression through TLR4/NF-κB signaling. Similarly, miR-1246 overexpression is associated with metastatic potential via suppression of target genes like DENN/MADD.

In this study, the proportion of patients with poor prognosis in the training set was 43.88% (86/196), and 34.52% (29/84) in the validation set. The similar proportions in the two sets indicate that the study grouping has a certain degree of balance and representativeness, which reduces the result bias caused by grouping differences and lays a reliable foundation for subsequent analysis. To address concerns regarding overfitting and optimize variable selection, we incorporated LASSO regression. This analysis objectively identified a parsimonious predictor set: age, miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, TNF-α, and Helicobacter pylori infection. This result enhances the model’s biological plausibility, as these biomarkers represent distinct yet complementary pathways. Conversely, LASSO excluded IL-6 (despite a univariate trend, P = 0.085), confirming no independent prognostic value. This penalized regression approach mitigates overfitting risk, yielding a more robust and clinically translatable model. The results of multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, TNF-α, and Helicobacter pylori infection were independent risk factors for poor prognosis after ESD for Early Gastric Cardia Cancer (All P < 0.05). The C-index of the nomogram model was 0.789 and 0.735 in the training set and the validation set, respectively. This indicates that the model constructed in this study can effectively distinguish patients with good and poor prognosis after ESD for early cardiac cancer. The AUC of the training set and the validation set were 0.789 and 0.735, respectively, suggesting that the model has high predictive value for poor prognosis after ESD for early cardiac cancer. Meanwhile, the sensitivities and specificities of the training set and the validation set were 0.625, 0.841 and 0.481, 0.786, respectively. Calibration curve analysis showed good agreement between the predicted values of the model and the actual observed values. The mean absolute errors of the training set and the validation set were 0.186 and 0.203, respectively, indicating that the prediction results of the model are relatively reliable and can well reflect the actual situation. Decision curve analysis showed that when the threshold probability was between 0.05 and 0.87, using the nomogram model constructed in this study to predict poor prognosis after ESD for Early Gastric Cardia Cancer had more clinical benefits.

Our final biomarker panel was rigorously selected through LASSO and multivariate logistic regression analysis from an initial set of 12 candidates, ensuring each marker provides independent prognostic value. The four retained biomarkers-miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT, and TNF-α-represent distinct but complementary biological pathways involved in post-ESD pathophysiology: miR-1246 mediates tumor-intrinsic regulation of proliferation and apoptosis, HMGB-1 functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern molecule, A1AT maintains systemic protease/antiprotease balance, and TNF-α drives pro-inflammatory signaling. The statistical independence of these biomarkers in multivariate analysis (all variance inflation factors < 2.0) confirms their non-redundant predictive value, with each capturing unique aspects of disease progression. While our expanded cohort (n = 280) addresses initial sample size concerns, three design limitations persist: Single-center recruitment may affect generalizability; Standardized protocols may not reflect real-world variability; Despite meeting EPV criteria, rare subgroups remain underpowered.

A significant synergistic interaction was confirmed between miR-1246 and HMGB-1: Patients with high miR-1246 (≥ 1.51) and high HMGB-1 (≥ 29.34 ng/ml) had a 68.2% risk of poor prognosis, which was 3.2-fold higher than the 21.5% risk in patients with low levels of both. This synergy aligns with their complementary biological roles: miR-1246 drives the proliferation of residual tumor cells left after ESD, while HMGB-1 creates a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that supports tumor survival and invasion. The combined effect of these two processes on the development of tumors is stronger than that of either factor alone-this explains why the simultaneous increase of both factors is associated with a higher risk of adverse prognosis. From a clinical perspective, this finding provides guidance for personalized monitoring: For patients with simultaneous high miR-1246 and HMGB-1, we recommend combining endoscopic monitoring (for local recurrence) with abdominal CT (for distant metastasis) every 3 months, rather than the standard 6-month interval, to improve early detection of poor outcomes.

As a microRNA, miR-1246 can bind to the mRNA of target genes through complementary pairing, inhibit their translation process or promote their degradation, thereby affecting the biological behavior of tumor cells [15]. In early cardiac cancer, high expression of miR-1246 may promote the proliferation of tumor cells, inhibit apoptosis, and enhance their invasion and metastasis ability by regulating certain key genes, leading to poor prognosis after surgery. In gastric cardia cancer, high miR-1246 expression promotes tumor cell proliferation by directly inhibiting CDKN1A, a key cell cycle regulator. This mechanism is supported by our data showing a significant association between miR-1246 levels and poor prognosis (HR = 3.280 P = 0.012) [16].

HMGB-1 is a non-histone chromosomal binding protein with extensive biological activities. In the tumor microenvironment, HMGB-1 can be released by necrotic or activated cells. By binding to various cell-surface receptors, and activates intracellular signaling pathways, promotes inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and the migration and invasion of tumor cells [17]. After ESD for early cardiac cancer, high levels of HMGB-1 may indicate the presence of continuous inflammatory stimulation and active proliferation of tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby increasing the risk of recurrence and metastasis and affecting patient prognosis. In gastric cardia cancer, HMGB-1 overexpression activates NF-κB signaling, leading to increased secretion of TNF-α, which further drive tumor progression, promote the secretion of inflammatory factors, and regulate the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process of tumor cells, enhancing their invasive ability.

A1AT is an important protease inhibitor with anti-inflammatory and immune regulatory functions [18]. Under normal circumstances, A1AT can inhibit the activity of various proteases and maintain the stability of the internal environment of the body. In gastric cardia cancer, elevated A1AT levels are associated with immune suppression through inhibition of natural killer cell activity, facilitating tumor immune escape. In addition, A1AT may also be involved in the process of tumor immune escape, affecting the body’s ability to monitor and clear tumor cells [19]. After ESD for early cardiac cancer, high expression of A1AT may reflect the disorder of the body’s immune regulation function and the malignant biological behavior of tumor cells, which is related to poor prognosis. Studies have found that A1AT can inhibit the activity of natural killer cells, reduce the body’s immune killing effect on tumor cells, and facilitate the survival and proliferation of tumor cells. While these biomarkers (miR-1246, HMGB-1, A1AT) have established roles in other cancers, their specific mechanisms in gastric cardia cancer require further validation through functional studies.

TNF-α, as an important inflammatory factor, has a dual role in the occurrence and development of tumors [20]. In the early stage of tumors, TNF-α can play an anti-tumor role by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting the proliferation of tumor cells. However, in the advanced stage of tumors, continuously high expression of TNF-α can promote the invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis of tumor cells. After ESD for early cardiac cancer, high levels of TNFα may indicate that the tumor microenvironment is in a state of chronic inflammation. This inflammatory microenvironment is conducive to the survival and metastasis of tumor cells and may also inhibit the body’s immune function, leading to poor prognosis. For example, TNF-α can promote tumor cells to secrete matrix metalloproteinases and degrade the extracellular matrix by activating the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby promoting the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells.

Helicobacter pylori infection is one of the important risk factors for the occurrence and development of cardiac cancer [21]. Helicobacter pylori can promote tumorigenesis through various mechanisms, such as inducing inflammatory responses, damaging gastric mucosal epithelial cells, and activating intracellular signaling pathways [22]. After ESD for early cardiac cancer, persistent Helicobacter pylori infection may lead to the continuous existence of local inflammatory responses, promote the proliferation and metastasis of tumor cells, and may also affect the body’s immune function, reducing the ability to clear tumor cells, thereby affecting patient prognosis. Studies have shown that Helicobacter pylori infection can up - regulate the expression of COX-2 in gastric mucosal cells, promote the synthesis of prostaglandin E2, and further promote the proliferation and angiogenesis of tumor cells.

The nomogram provides a practical tool for risk stratification but requires validation in diverse settings before clinical adoption. Key implementation challenges include: (1) Turnaround time for biomarker assays (currently 3–5 days in our lab); (2) Cost-effectiveness analysis showing incremental cost of $120 per patient compared to standard care; and (3) Clear clinical pathways (e.g., intensified surveillance for high-risk patients vs. routine follow-up for low-risk). Future studies should compare this model to existing prognostic scores to establish added value. We wish to explicitly caution against immediate clinical adoption of this model. While internally validated, the absence of external validation means we cannot account for potential biases introduced by our single-center setting, including: (a) standardized ESD technique by a limited number of expert operators, (b) local laboratory protocols for biomarker measurement, and (c) regional epidemiologic characteristics of gastric cardia cancer. These factors may limit generalizability to other practice environments. Prior to clinical implementation, we recommend validation studies must demonstrate:

Consistent performance across at least 3 independent centers; Reproducibility with different biomarker assay methods; Stability across operator experience levels; Utility in diverse healthcare resource settings.

To evaluate economic feasibility, we conducted a preliminary cost-effectiveness analysis using 2023 cost data from our center: (1) Incremental cost of the biomarker panel: $120 per patient (qRT-PCR for miR-1246: $45; ELISA for HMGB-1/A1AT: $55; Helicobacter pylori test: $20). (2) Potential cost savings: (A) High-risk patients (n = 115): Intensified surveillance (q3 months vs. q6 months) reduces missed recurrence (estimated 3.2% absolute risk reduction), avoiding $2,500 per missed recurrence (treatment cost). (B) Low-risk patients (n = 165): Reduced follow-up (q6 months vs. q3 months) saves $300 per patient per year. (3) Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER): Calculated as $1,800 per QALY (assuming 0.2 QALY gain per correctly stratified patient), below the $50,000/QALY threshold accepted in most healthcare systems. This suggests the model is cost-effective for risk stratification.

Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses to address the model’s applicability across key patient strata. The nomogram demonstrated consistent and robust discriminatory performance regardless of age or Helicobacter pylori infection status, with no statistically significant evidence of heterogeneity (all interaction P > 0.05). This consistency is clinically reassuring, which suggests that the biomarker-based prognostic value provided by our model is additive and applicable to a broad spectrum of patients with early gastric cardia cancer who meet the criteria for ESD. However, The predictive utility might be limited to a specific biologic subtype or patient demographic.

The primary limitation of this study is the absence of external validation, which restricts the generalizability of our findings. While internal validation demonstrates robust performance, single-center retrospective studies inherently carry biases related to patient selection, institutional protocols, and population characteristics. These factors may not reflect broader clinical settings. As we acknowledge, external validation fundamentally requires multi-center collaboration and larger sample sizes to ensure model generalizability. This critical gap must be conclusively addressed before clinical implementation. To address this, we explicitly emphasize that our model remains preliminary and requires: (1) prospective validation in diverse healthcare settings, (2) standardized biomarker measurement protocols across centers, and (3) longer-term follow-up data (> 5 years) to evaluate late recurrence patterns. Multi-center studies are still needed for external validation in the future to further evaluate the effectiveness and stability of the model in different patient populations.

We acknowledge that the COVID-19 pandemic may have introduced subtle temporal biases not captured in our data. Several factors warrant consideration: Potential under-ascertainment of asymptomatic cases during peak restrictions; Modified health-seeking behaviors affecting disease stage at presentation; Changes in referral patterns from primary care providers. These factors may limit generalizability to non-pandemic practice environments, and validation in future non-pandemic cohorts would be valuable.

While our study employed rigorous internal quality controls for biomarker measurements, we acknowledge important limitations regarding clinical implementation across different laboratory settings. To address inter-laboratory variability and ensure reproducibility, we propose a detailed 3-phase standardization plan: (1) Protocol standardization: Develop a step-by-step manual covering sample collection (gastric biopsy/serum), storage (-80 °C for ≤ 6 months), and assay procedures (qRT-PCR for miR-1246: TaqMan 478581_mir, U6 normalization; ELISA for HMGB-1/A1AT: Tecan ST51011/R&D DAPTA00 kits). (2) Reference material provision: Distribute certified reference materials (miR-1246 standard RNA: 1 ng/µl; HMGB-1 standard: 50 ng/ml; A1AT standard: 200 mg/dl) to participating laboratories, with inter-assay CV targets < 10%. (3) Proficiency testing: Conduct bi-annual inter-laboratory proficiency testing using blinded samples (n = 20 per test), with laboratories required to meet ≥ 80% agreement with the central laboratory’s results to ensure measurement consistency. Future multicenter studies should prioritize assay harmonization and evaluate the stability of measurements across different testing conditions to ensure reliable implementation.

Our comprehensive comparative analysis confirms that the proposed biomarker-based nomogram provides significant incremental prognostic value over existing tools. The model demonstrated statistically superior discrimination compared to the eCura system, AJCC staging, and a simple clinical model.

This study developed a prediction model based on 24-month follow-up data, with the primary clinical value lying in early risk stratification. The model is most suitable for identifying high-risk patients requiring intensified short-term (0–24 month) surveillance, where negative results only indicate low early recurrence risk but cannot exclude late recurrences, thus serving as a complementary tool to standard 5-year follow-up protocols rather than a replacement. Notably, the predictive performance is time-dependent, with optimal accuracy for outcomes within 24 months, while some low-risk patients may still experience late recurrences.

Our findings on the inflammatory microenvironment are consistent with recent studies underscoring the prognostic importance of HMGB-1 and miR-1246 in gastric cancer. The outcome-specific analyses further explored these associations, suggesting potential differential predictive value across distinct endpoints- which could inform personalized management strategies. Specifically, the strong association between miR-1246 and Helicobacter pylori with recurrence suggests roles in local tumor regrowth and persistent inflammatory stimulation, respectively; these patients may benefit from enhanced endoscopic surveillance. In contrast, HMGB-1 and TNF-α were specifically linked to metastasis, consistent with their known roles in promoting invasion and angiogenesis, indicating that individuals with elevated levels of these markers should be prioritized for systemic imaging. Finally, the association of A1AT and older age with tumor-related mortality highlights the importance of immune dysfunction and reduced physiological reserve in survival outcomes, suggesting that integrated supportive care could improve prognosis in this subgroup. Given the limited number of mortality events, these interpretations are preliminary and require cautious evaluation. Collectively, these observations provide a basis for future research on biomarker-stratified follow-up, ultimately with the aim of enabling more efficient and individualized clinical management.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may introduce unmeasured confounding factors and limit generalizability to broader populations. Second, while standardized protocols were implemented, potential variability in biopsy sampling and biomarker measurements is a consideration for future multi-center applications. Third, the relatively short 24-month follow-up period may not fully capture long-term outcomes. Fourth, the low frequency of competing events limits the precise estimation of their impact on the subdistribution hazards. Furthermore, the performance of the model in surgical candidates or unselected populations remains to be validated. To address these limitations, future work should prioritize multi-center studies with extended follow-up, standardized protocols, and larger sample sizes to robustly validate and refine the model’s clinical utility.

Future research should focus on several key areas. These include conducting multi-center, large-sample studies to enhance the model’s stability and generalizability, performing external validation to assess the effectiveness across diverse patient populations, and extending the follow-up period to enable in-depth investigation of long-term prognosis after ESD for early gastric cardia cancer. These efforts will be crucial for refining the prediction model and improving the ability to guide clinical practice, thereby enhancing treatment outcomes for patients.

Conclusion

This biologically grounded nomogram represents a step toward personalized management of early gastric cardia cancer, though broader validation and protocol standardization remain prerequisites for clinical adoption.
