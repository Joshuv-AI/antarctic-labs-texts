Development and validation of a multivariate model for predicting heart failure hospitalization and mortality in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Wenwu Tang, Ying Zhang, Zhixin Wang, Xinzhu Yuan, Xiaoxia Chen, Xiaohua Yang, Zhirui Qi, Ju Zhang, Jie Li, Xisheng Xie
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) increases their hospitalization rates, mortality, and economic burden significantly. We aimed to develop and validate a predictive model utilizing contemporary deep phenotyping for individual risk assessment of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization in patients on MHD.Materials and MethodsA retrospective review was conducted from January 2017 to October 2022, including 348 patients receiving MHD from four centers. The variables were adjusted by Cox regression analysis, and the clinical prediction model was constructed and verified.ResultsThe median follow-up durations were 14 months (interquartile range [IQR] 9–21) for the modeling set and 14 months (9–20) for the validation set. The composite outcome occurred in 72 (29.63%) of 243 patients in the modeling set and 39 (37.14%) of 105 patients in the validation set. The model predictors included age, albumin, history of cerebral hemorrhage, use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers/“sacubitril/valsartan”, left ventricular ejection fraction, urea reduction ratio, N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide, and right atrial size. The C-index was 0.834 (95% CI 0.784–0.883) for the modeling set and 0.853 (0.798, 0.908) for the validation set. The model exhibited excellent calibration across the complete risk profile, and the decision curve analysis (DCA) suggested its ability to maximize patient benefits.ConclusionThe developed prediction model offered an accurate and personalized assessment of HF hospitalization risk and all-cause mortality in patients with MHD. It can be employed to identify high-risk patients and guide treatment and follow-up.Keywords: Patients on MHD, heart failure, mortality, predictive model, retrospective study

Introduction

Patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) face a 20-fold higher risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) compared to the general population, with heart failure (HF) being the second most common CVD among them (approximately 10.2%) [1–3]. The 2-year mortality rate for patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) without HF is approximately 16.5%, while those with HF experience a much higher rate of 33.3% [4–7].

Compared to patients with HF, patients on MHD encounter more complex uremic cardiomyopathy and HF-related risks due to dialysis, such as rapid hemodynamic and electrolyte changes, and traditional risk factors like hypertension and diabetes [8–10]. However, there is insufficient evidence to effectively evaluate the impact of maintenance dialysis modalities and medication treatments (particularly sacubitril/valsartan) on reducing HF occurrence or the risk of death in these patients [11]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify high-risk hospitalized or critically ill patients on MHD and provide accurate assessment criteria for dialysis modalities and medication treatments.

Predicting the mortality and hospitalization rate of patients with HF using published prediction models is challenging. While only 33% of the 117 published prediction models were validated in independent cohort studies, most of these models presented only moderate predictive performance [10,12]. Joanne’s predictive model had C-statistics of 0.71 and 0.70 for HF hospitalization and death, respectively [13], while those of Joshua’s prediction model were 0.80 and 0.79, respectively [12–14]. However, these prediction models have been developed for the general population and do not account for the specific characteristics of patients on MHD. The recent model by Gotta et al. focused mainly on death prediction in patients on MHD without considering the effect of HF [15].

This study aimed to establish and validate a predictive model for patients undergoing MHD, enabling accurate individual risk assessment for HF hospitalization and all-cause mortality. The goal is to enhance early identification and intervention by clinicians and ultimately improve the prognosis of patients undergoing MHD [16].

Materials and methods

Materials

Subjects

This retrospective study analyzed data from 386 patients undergoing MHD at four centers (Nanchong Central Hospital, Guangyuan Central Hospital, Suining Central Hospital, Peng’an County People’s Hospital) from January 2017 to October 2022 (Figure 1).

Research flow chart: Patient inclusion process based on nadir criteria. MHD, maintenance hemodialysis.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age >18 years; (2) Patients with CKD5 undergoing dialysis, meeting the criteria set by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes developed by the American Society of Nephrology, and having undergone regular hemodialysis for ≥3 months, with a frequency of at least two times per week; (3) Patients who were initially started on MHD.

Exclusion criteria

(i) Patients with malignancy, hepatic, or pulmonary dysfunction; (ii) Patients who had undergone kidney transplantation; (iii) Patients with incomplete clinical data or those who were unwilling to participate or unable to cooperate.

Methods

Diagnostic criteria for HF

Hospital event statistics included diagnostic codes from the 10th International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) revision. This coding system records up to 20 diagnoses per admission or the cause of death. This study identified HF(ICD-10 I50, Table S2) as the primary diagnostic cause of the first hospitalization after the initiation of observation [17–19]. All-cause mortality was defined as any instance of death.

Starting point and endpoint of observation

The observation period for each patient began at the initiation of dialysis treatment. During the first dialysis session, systematic evaluations, including relevant laboratory tests and ultrasound examinations (not based on disease status), were conducted for all patients in each center as part of quality control requirements for subsequent dialysis treatment. The observation endpoint was reached if there was HF hospitalization, all-cause mortality, or when the follow-up period was completed. The period between the start and endpoint was considered the follow-up duration (The follow-up period was at least 3 months). Our study’s primary outcome was a combined measure of admission to HF hospitalization or all-cause mortality. Since patients were required to regularly visit each center for MHD treatment, any interruption in treatment prompted immediate contact with the patient or their family by telephone, and the patient’s current status was recorded in the system. This practice ensured there was no loss of follow-up in the cohort.

Study indicators

Candidate predictor variables were selected based on clinical practice guidelines and a thorough literature review. In cases where potential candidate variables provided similar clinical information and were highly correlated, the most informative variable was chosen based on expert clinical consensus. All variables were assessed for all patients at the onset of the initial event. The study encompassed a total of 36 variables, including (1) general and dialysis-related data, including sex, age, type of dialysis vascular access, reasons for dialysis (such as nephritis), frequency (weekly), and duration (h/time) of dialysis, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula [20], urea reduction ratio (URR), ultrafiltration rate (UFR). (2) Clinical data: pulse pressure, past medical history (coronary heart disease, cerebral hemorrhage), serous cavity effusion, atrial fibrillation, pneumonia, diabetes, oral medications like angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), sacubitril/valsartan (SV), diuretics, and β-blockers. (3) Laboratory examination: N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), high sensitivity troponin T (hs-cTnT), albumin, uric acid, total bilirubin, serum sodium, calcium, and phosphorus. (4) Echocardiographic data: pericardial effusion (mm), right atrial diameter (RA), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left atrial diameter (LA), and left ventricular mass index (LVMI, which was calculated using the formula recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography [21]. The patient information, dialysis treatment details, and clinically related data were extracted from each center’s electronic medical record systems. Venous blood samples were collected from the patients before dialysis therapy, following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. The examinations and cardiac ultrasounds were performed at each hospital’s laboratory and imaging centers.

Construction and evaluation of predictive models

Characteristic factors were chosen from all independent variables. Patients undergoing MHD were divided into modeling and validation groups, and the importance of each index in both groups was compared across different models. The best model was further evaluated and verified following these detailed steps: (1) Screening for characteristic factors: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis using the Glmnet package in R software (version 4.1.2) was conducted, and the results were further used for multifactor Cox regression analysis with SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA). Representative factors with a significance level of p < 0.05 were identified. (2) Analysis of relationship: Pearson’s coefficient (r) was utilized to assess correlations between candidate predictors. Values of r > 0.7 were considered vital, indicating potential collinearity (Python, Sklearn 0.22.1) [22]. (3) Model display: The R software package ‘logreg6.2.0′ was used to draw the nomogram, and the ‘ggplot2’ package was used to draw the forest plot. (4) Data division: Patients were randomly divided into a modeling set (n = 243) and a validation set (n = 105) at a 7:3 ratio, using the random number method in Python with Sklearn (version 0.22.1). (5) The R-software (version 4.2.2) ‘survivalROC’ package was used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the modeling and validation sets, calculating the area under the curve and C-statistics to validate the accuracy of the prediction model [23]. The R-software packages ‘rms’ (version 6.2.0) and ‘timeROC’ (version 0.4) were used to draw calibration curves to measure the model’s predictive ability and reflect the concordance between the predicted and actual risks [23]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using the R-software package ‘ggDCA’ to assess the clinical applicability of the prediction model [24]. Kaplan–Meier (K-M) curves were plotted using the ‘survminer’ package of R software.

Statistical analysis

Variables in the modeling and validation sets were compared. Continuous variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Statistical significance for two-sided tests was set at p < 0.05. SPSS version 26.0 and the R-software package version 4.2.2 were used for statistical analyses. Schoenfeld residuals were used to evaluate the proportional hazards assumption [25].

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of study population

In the study population, 348 patients were included following the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The specific baseline data for the modeling and validation sets are shown in Table 1. The two groups had no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). The median follow-up durations were 14 months (IQR 9–21) for the modeling set and 14 months (9–20) for the validation set. The composite outcome occurred in 72 (29.63%) of 243 patients in the modeling set and 39 (37.14%) of 105 patients in the validation set. Specifically, 63 patients were hospitalized for HF, and nine died in the modeling set, while 36 were hospitalized for HF, and three died in the validation set. Outcome data were available for all patients, with no losses to follow-up or withdrawals from the study.

Model construction

Cox univariate analysis was performed to preliminarily assess the association of candidate factors with the composite outcomes (Table S3). Among the 36 variables subjected to univariate analysis, 19 variables with a significance level of p < 0.05 were identified. LASSO regression analysis was then applied to the composite endpoint of dependent variables, reducing the number of predictors to 13 (Figure 2). Correlation analysis of the screened predictors revealed no evident collinear factors (Figure S1).

LASSO regression. (a) 10-fold cross-validation was utilized, and vertical lines were drawn at the chosen values, where the optimal lambda resulted in fifteen non-zero coefficients. (b) The LASSO model displayed coefficient profiles of 40 variables drawn from the log (λ) sequence. Vertical dotted lines were placed at the minimum mean square error (λ = 0.017) and the standard error of the minimum distance (λ = 0.056). LASSO, least absolute hrinkage and selection operator.

Subsequently, multivariate Cox stepwise regression analysis was performed on these variables to control for confounding factors. The following eight variables were finally screened as significant predictors (p < 0.05): age, albumin levels, history of cerebral hemorrhage, use of “ACEI/ARB/SV” medications, LVEF, NT-proBNP, URR, and RA (Figure 3). The Schoenfeld residual test confirmed that the proportional hazards assumption was met (Figure S2).

A scoring system was developed to comprehensively understand the model’s predictive performance by summing the points for each variable to calculate an aggregate score. The intersection of the line with the probability axis at 6, 12, and 18 months indicated the corresponding probabilities of HF hospitalization or all-cause mortality (Figure 4).

Multi-model comparison

The eight evaluated variables were ranked using Lasso regression analysis, random forest regression, and AdaBoost regression. The results indicated that “ACEI/ARB/SV” and “NT-proBNP” were the two most critical predictive factors (Figure 5). Two models were compared to assess the model’s improvement: model 1, constructed using seven variables, except “ACEI/ARB/SV,” and model 2, including all eight variables.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we developed and validated a clinical model to predict HF hospitalization and all-cause mortality risks in patients undergoing MHD. To our knowledge, it is the first model of its kind. Our results indicated that the model provided accurate and individualized risk estimates for these patients.

Patients undergoing MHD have a significantly higher risk of HF, leading to increased hospitalization rates, mortality, and economic burden. As the number of patients receiving MHD increases rapidly, the risk factors for HF become more complex within the dialysis setting. These factors include rapid hemodynamic and electrolytic changes and inadequate dialysis treatment. Consequently, identifying those at a higher risk of HF-related hospitalization or mortality becomes a priority. Existing prediction models frequently exclude or overlook patients undergoing MHD, making them unsuitable for this population [4–6].

Consistent with previous studies, our model considered age, albumin levels, and the history of cerebral hemorrhage [15,16,27]. Additionally, our model incorporated crucial variables in current HF management, such as NT-proBNP and LVEF [15,16,28,29], cardiac structure (RA), “ACEI/ARB/SV” medication, and URR evaluation. Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed the significance of these prognostic factors in predicting the outcomes; NT-proBNP >12414 pg/mL, LVEF <50%, age >60 years, URR <65%, albumin <35 g/L, RA >40 mm, history of cerebral hemorrhage, and non-administration of oral “AECI/ARB/SV” were identified as independent risk factors that increase the probability of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization. Furthermore, the nomogram provided a user-friendly tool for facilitating clinical implementation.

NT-proBNP and ACEI/ARB/SV were identified as the two most valuable predictors for HF hospitalization or all-cause mortality in patients undergoing MHD, consistent with other studies [30]. Despite the NT-proBNP plasma levels in these patients being influenced by various factors (such as decreased eGFR, volume overload, and MHD treatment), monitoring the changes in their NT-proBNP levels could be valuable in determining prognosis and guiding the maintenance or optimization of pharmacological treatment, consistent with the latest guidelines [31, 32]. In the general population, clinical guidelines and recent studies have shown that ACEI/ARB/SV therapy improves left ventricular function and reverses myocardial remodeling in patients with different subtypes of HF (Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, median ejection fraction and reduced ejection fraction), effectively reducing the risk of HF readmission and mortality [31–36]. However, limited evidence exists regarding using ACEI/ARB/SV in patients undergoing MHD. The findings of this study revealed that oral ACEI/ARB/SV could reduce the risk of HF hospitalization or all-cause mortality in patients undergoing MHD, with SV showing better efficacy compared to ACEI/ARB (ACEI/ARB: 62.9%, SV: 84.1%), consistent with recent research [37]. By comparing multiple models, we discovered that oral administration of “ACEI/ARB/SV” medication could improve patient outcomes beyond the initial baseline.

Furthermore, this study identified other indicators with good predictive value, including age, albumin levels, LVEF, RA, and a history of cerebral hemorrhage. Advanced age, hypoalbuminemia, and lower LVEF are well-known risk factors for HF hospitalization or death in patients undergoing MHD [15, 29, 38]. Despite being frequently overlooked, the structural and functional changes in RA have recently garnered attention for their potential to improve our comprehension of the occurrence, development mechanism, and potential therapeutic targets of HF [39]. A history of cerebral hemorrhage has emerged as a risk factor for HF or death in patients undergoing MHD. According to statistics, approximately one-fifth of the 5.5 million stroke survivors worldwide succumb to subsequent cardiovascular events. The close physiological and pathological interaction between the brain and the heart, known as “brain-heart interactions,” has received widespread attention in recent years [40]. The cerebrovascular disease could trigger an increase in catecholamine release, leading to cardiac toxicity (such as oxidative stress and inflammation), which could explain the significant association between cerebral hemorrhage and HF in the context of patients undergoing MHD [41].

Regarding the dialysis-related predictor, guidelines typically recommend single-pool Kt/V (spKt/V; K, urea dialytic clearance; t, dialysis time; V, urea distribution volume) as the preferred indicator of dialysis adequacy. However, recent studies have shown that spKt/V and URR have similar prognostic values for all-cause mortality [42]. We performed correlation and Cox univariate analysis on spKt/V and URR and found significant collinearity between the two measures. However, the association between spKt/V and outcome events was insignificant (p = 0.202). Therefore, we decided to include URR in the baseline analysis as a dialysis-related predictor. Our study results demonstrated that URR >65% was associated with lower HF hospitalization and mortality rates, consistent with previous research [42]. Although our model did not include many other variables related to dialysis treatment, the Cox univariate analysis revealed that longer dialysis duration (≥4 h, odds ratio [HR]: 0.49, 95% CI [0.320, 0.749]), lower UFR (HR: 1.863, 95% CI [0.801, 4.332]), and higher residual kidney function (eGFR, HR: 0.993, 95% CI [0.944, 1.045]) were associated with lower rates of HF hospitalization and mortality, which aligns with previous studies [43–45].

Despite multiple risk factors for HF and mortality in patients on MHD, an effective predictive model tailored to this population has not been established yet. Our study incorporated essential variables related to HF management and dialysis to assess their predictive value for HF hospitalization or mortality. Our model exhibited excellent predictive performance, maximizing patient benefits by analyzing various metrics such as ROC curves, calibration plots, and DCA curves. However, our research has some limitations. Firstly, this study was a multi-center retrospective cohort study, and the clinical data were retrieved from four hospitals in different geographical regions. These variations in the healthcare system and treatment approaches across the participating centers could impact the model’s management, outcomes, and predictive performance. Secondly, the study outcome was determined by the attending physician alone, and there was no involvement of a ruling committee. However, a systematic meta-analysis has confirmed that differences in diagnostic discretion do not significantly impact the outcomes of cardiovascular outcome trials [46].

Conclusions

This study developed and validated predictive models for all-mortality and HF hospitalization in patients on MHD. The developed model exhibited outstanding predictive performance and proved a valuable tool for risk assessment in this patient population.
