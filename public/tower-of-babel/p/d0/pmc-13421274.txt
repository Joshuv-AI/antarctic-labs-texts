SphB1 export system for antigen presentation to the respiratory tract by a live pertussis vaccine
Authors: Lazara Elena Santiesteban-Lores, Dominique Raze, Violaine Dubois, Morgane Tardy, Anne-Sophie Debrie, Peter Goldstein, Loic Coutte, Camille Locht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractBPZE1 is a live attenuated vaccine currently in advanced clinical development for nasal immunization against pertussis. The ability of this vaccine to induce mucosal and systemic, humoral and cellular immunity make it an attractive mucosal delivery system for heterologous antigens to induce immunity especially in the respiratory tract. Here, we have developed a heterologous antigen expression system based on the pertactin-deficient BPZE1 derivative IB-V002 by using the SphB1 secretion system to present the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (N) as a model antigen to the respiratory mucosa. SphB1 is an autotransporter comprising a subtilisin-like passenger domain and a C-terminal ß-barrel domain. The passenger domain was replaced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein. The recombinant gene was inserted into the urease locus of IB-V002 and placed under the control of the pertactin promoter and signal peptide. Immunoblot analyses indicated that the chimeric protein was produced and secreted by the recombinant strain named BT-V101. Mice intranasally immunized with BT-V101 induced N-specific IgG in serum and IFN-γ production by spleen cells. Importantly, a local N-specific CD8+ T cell response was observed in nose-associated lymphoid tissues by MHC class I tetramer staining. Additionally, immunization with BT-V101 efficiently primed mice to recombinant purified N protein given as a boost either intranasally or subcutaneously. These results demonstrate the suitability of the B. pertussis SphB1 secretion machinery to secret heterologous antigens for the development of a mucosal immunization vaccine platform with the induction of specific humoral and cellular, local, and systemic immune responses.Key points• The SphB1 export system was used to secrete heterologous antigens by B. pertussis.• Recombinant B. pertussis induced systemic and local immunity to foreign antigens.• Recombinant B. pertussis efficiently primed immune responses to foreign antigens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13887-x.Keywords: Pertussis, Live attenuated vaccine, SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, Mucosal immunity

Introduction

BPZE1, a derivative of Bordetella pertussis Tohama I, is a live attenuated pertussis vaccine candidate designed for intranasal administration (Mielcarek et al. 2006). In mouse models, it was shown to protect against virulent B. pertussis infection and to induce strong secretory IgA (SIgA) and IFN-γ- and/or IL-17-producing tissue-resident memory T cell (TRM) responses (Solans et al. 2018), as well as systemic B and T cell responses (Mielcarek et al. 2006). BPZE1 is now in late-stage clinical development, and several clinical trials, collectively including more than 600 participants, have shown that the vaccine is well tolerated in humans and able to induce robust serum and mucosal antibody responses to B. pertussis antigens (Thorstensson et al. 2014; Jahnmatz et al. 2020; Creech et al. 2022; Keech et al. 2023; Gbesemete et al. 2025).

Several live attenuated vaccines have been engineered as recombinant vehicles for heterologous antigens to develop multivalent vaccines (Lin et al. 2015). These include the anti-tuberculosis vaccine Bacille Calmette-Guérin (Stover et al. 1991), live attenuated Salmonella (Clark-Curtiss and Curtiss 2018), measles vaccine (Frantz et al. 2018), and others. While these vaccines are injectable or oral vaccines, nasal vaccine vectors, such as BPZE1, particularly well adapted to induce local immune responses against respiratory pathogens, have been less explored.

We have previously shown that it is possible to use BPZE1 or its precursors for the secretion of heterologous antigens via the B. pertussis filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA) secretion machinery and for their presentation to the respiratory tract. The fusion of the parasite antigen Sm28GST to FHA led to the induction of local and serum antibody responses to Sm28GST (Renauld-Mongénie et al. 1996; Mielcarek et al. 1998) and to protection against Schistosoma mansoni in mice after a single intranasal immunization with the recombinant attenuated B. pertussis strain. Similarly, intranasal administration of recombinant attenuated B. pertussis producing the Neisseria meningitidis transferrin-binding protein B (Coppens et al. 2001) or HtrA from non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae (Alonso et al. 2005) fused to the N-terminal moiety of FHA, named Fha44, also induced strong antibody responses against the corresponding heterologous antigens. Viral antigens, such as the neutralizing peptide SP70 from enterovirus 71 (Ho et al. 2008) or the conserved M2e peptide from influenza A virus (Kammoun et al. 2013; Li et al. 2011), were also secreted by recombinant attenuated B. pertussis via the FHA secretion machinery.

However, not all antigens could be effectively secreted via the FHA secretion machinery. This was the case for antigens such as tetanus toxin fragment C (Reveneau et al. 2002) or the cholera toxin B subunit (Guédin et al. 1998), most likely because of structural constraints. FHA is an elongated protein consisting primarily of a long ß helix (Kajava et al. 2001; Clantin et al. 2004), and mechanistic studies have indicated the crucial importance of the ß strands of the N-terminal moiety of FHA in the interaction with FhaC, the accessory outer membrane protein essential for FHA secretion (Baud et al. 2014). Proteins incompatible with this FhaC interaction, such as those with a globular structure constrained by disulfide bonds, are thus unlikely to be well secreted via the FHA secretion mechanism.

It is therefore important to explore alternative systems. One of them may be the SphB1 secretion system. SphB1 is an autotransporter composed of an N-terminal subtilisin-like protease domain, followed by a 200-residue-long domain of unknown function and a C-terminal ß-barrel (Coutte et al. 2001). Although the secretion mechanism of SphB1 has not been fully elucidated, the fact that it can secrete the globular protease domain suggests that it may be a useful alternative for the secretion of antigens presenting a compact tertiary structure. In this study, we thus explored the ability of the SphB1 system to secrete passenger antigens that are not efficiently secreted by the FHA secretion machinery. As test antigen, we chose the nucleocapsid protein (N) of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, a protein with globular domains and an intrinsically disordered region and capable of oligomerizing (Peng et al. 2020).

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The B. pertussis strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. They were grown at 37 °C for 40 h on Bordet-Gengou agar (Difco, Detroit, Mich.) supplemented with 1% glycerol (Euromedex, Souffelweyersheim, France) and 10% defibrinated sheep blood (Eurobio, Les Ulis, France). For liquid culture, the B. pertussis strains were grown at 37 °C in modified Stainer-Scholte (SS) medium (Stainer and Scholte 1970) containing 1 g/L heptakis (2,6-di-o-methyl) ß-cyclodextrin (MP Biomedicals, Illkirch, France) (Imaizumi et al. 1983). When required, the appropriate antibiotic was added (30 µg/mL gentamycin (Gibco, Illkirch, France), 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Sigma, L’Isle D’Abeau Chesnes, France), or 30 µg/mL nalidixic acid (Sigma)). For growth studies in liquid medium, the optical density at 600 nm (OD600nm) was measured every minute using an EloCheck Quatro device (biotronix GmbH, Hennigsdorf, Germany). Alternatively, 200 µL of bacterial suspensions grown in SS medium at an OD600nm of 0.15 was dispensed into the central 60 wells of 96-well plates and incubated with shaking in a Spark® microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Männedorf, Switzerland), with OD600nm measurements recorded every 30 min for a total of 72 h.

To obtain strains BT-V100, BT-V101, and BT-V102, pRM24, pRM25, and pRM26, respectively, were first introduced into E. coli SM10. The recombinant E. coli strains were then mixed with IB-V002 to allow for conjugative transfer of pRM24, pRM25, and pRM26 to IB-V002. After two consecutive selection steps to allow for double homologous recombination of the chimeric genes into the chromosomal urease locus of IB-V002, using methods as described (Antoine and Locht 1990), the ex-conjugants were screened by PCR to ensure that the chimeric gene was correctly inserted in the B. pertussis chromosome.

Immunodetection of recombinant hybrid proteins in IB-V002 derivatives

To analyze the production of recombinant N in the B. pertussis IB-V002 derivatives, the recombinant strains BT-V100, BT-V101, and BT-V102 and non-recombinant IB-V002 were grown for 20 h in 10 mL Stainer-Scholte medium supplemented with 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation for 15 min at 3000 × g. The supernatants were concentrated using 10-kDa cut-off Amicon® Ultra columns (Millipore, L’Isle D’Abeau Chesnes, France). The cell pellets were resuspended in 0.7 mL PBS containing 100µg/mL DNAse (Sigma) and an EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Boulogne-Billancourt, France). Cells were lysed using a MP FastPrep-24 Tissue homogenizer (MP Biomedicals) for 25 s at 4.0 m/s four times, and the lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The culture supernatants and the whole cell extracts were subjected to SDS/polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using a 12% polyacrylamide gel under reducing conditions and then electro-transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using the Criterion™ cell system (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Mouse monoclonal anti-SARSCoV2 N antibody (3F9, GenScript, Rijswijk, The Netherlands) was used to detect recombinant N. Goat anti-mouse-HRP conjugated antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was then added at a 1:10,000 dilution, and the blot was developed using chemiluminescent substrates (GE Healthcare, Machelen, Belgium). For peptide analysis, BT-V101 culture supernatants were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using a 12% polyacrylamide gel and stained with Coomassie-blue. Stained bands were then excised from the gel, digested by trypsin, and analyzed by mass fingerprinting as described (Coutte et al. 2001).

Animals

Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles Rivers France (Saint Germain Nuelles, France).

Murine respiratory tract colonization

C57BL/6 mice were inoculated intranasally with 20 µL PBS containing 5 × 106 colony-forming units (CFU) of B. pertussis IB-V002 or BT-V101. At various time points (3 h, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, 21 days, 28 days, and 56 days post-infection), 4 mice per group were sacrificed, and lungs and noses were harvested and homogenized in 1 mL PBS as described (Solans et al. 2018). Tenfold serial dilutions were plated onto Bordet-Gengou agar plates supplemented with 10% (v/v) sheep blood, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 30 µg/mL nalidixic acid. CFUs were assessed after incubation at 37 °C for 5 to 7 days.

Immunization

For immunogenicity studies, C57BL/6 mice were intranasally immunized with 20 µL PBS containing either 5 × 106 or 5 × 107 CFU of B. pertussis IB-V002 or BT-V101. Mice inoculated with 20 µL PBS served as controls. Four weeks after the first immunization, mice received a first booster dose of 5 × 106 or 5 × 107 CFU, followed by a second booster dose of 5 × 106 or 5 × 107 CFU or 5 µg of recombinant N protein (rN, Invitrogen, Illkirch, France) in the presence of dimethyldioctadecylammonium (DDA) and monophosphoryl-lipid A (MPLA) (Sigma) 4 weeks later. Two weeks after the second booster dose, mice were sacrificed, and sera, nasal washes, lungs, and spleens were harvested. Nasal washes were harvested by flushing 1 mL PBS containing a proteases inhibitor cocktail (Roche) through the nose and concentrating the washes by using 10-kDa cut-off Amicon® ultra columns (Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antibody detection

To detect serum or nasal wash antibody levels, 96-well plates were coated with rN or B. pertussis whole cell lysate (200 ng/well) in 0.05 M carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) (Invitrogen) overnight at 4 °C. After 1 h saturation with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma) and 3 washes in PBS-Tween 0.05% (PBS/Tw), serially diluted serum or nasal wash samples were added and incubated for 1:30 h at room temperature. After 3 washes in PBS/Tw, goat anti-mouse IgG- or IgA-horseradish peroxidase conjugated antibodies (Abcam) were added, and the plates were incubated for 1:30 h at room temperature. After final washing, 0.1 mL/well of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Interchim, Montluçon, France) was added. Reaction was stopped by the addition of 0.05 mL 1 M H3PO4. The optical density (OD) was measured with a Titertek Multiskan MCC/340 reader at 450 nm (Cytena GmbH, Freiburg, Germany).

In vitro stimulation and production of IFN-γ and IL-17A by lung cells and splenocytes

Lungs and spleens were cut into small pieces, digested with collagenase 60 mg/mL D (Roche) and 20 U/mL DNase I (Sigma) and then passed through a 70-µm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Splenocytes were treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer (Gibco) to lyse red blood cells. Lymphocytes among the lung cells were separated by Percoll (Cytiva, Illkirch, France) density gradient centrifugation. Single-cell suspensions of 1 × 106 splenocytes or 3 × 105 lung lymphocytes per well were seeded into U-bottom 96-well plates and incubated either with Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) (Gibco) or RPMI supplemented with 5 µg/mL of recombinant N protein or B. pertussis whole cell lysate for 40 h at 37 °C. Culture supernatants were collected after removal of cell debris by centrifugation, and IFN-γ and IL-17A productions were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays ELISA using the Mouse IFN-γ (BD Biosciences, Pont-de-Claix, France) and IL-17A (Mabtech, Nacka Strand, Sweden) ELISA Sets, according to the manufacturer instructions.

Flow cytometry assays

Flow cytometry assays were performed as described (Dubois et al. 2021). In order to differentiate tissue-derived (i.v.CD45−) from vasculature-derived (i.v.CD45+) leukocytes, phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-CD45 antibody (Invitrogen) was injected i.v., and 10 min later, mice were euthanized. Nasal associated lymphoid tissue (NALT) was harvested as described (Bittner-Eddy et al. 2017) and passed through a 70-µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were stained with antibodies to CD45 (30-F11), CD3 (17A2), CD4 (RM4-5), CD8 (53–6.7), CD44 (IM7), CD11b (M1/70), CD11c (N418), CD45R (RA3-6B2) (BD Pharmingen, Pont-de-Claix, France; Invitrogen and BioLegend, San Diego, Calif.), H2-Db/N219–227 MHC class I tetramer (Creative Biolabs, Heverlee, Belgium), and Live Dead FixableAqua (Thermo Fischer Scientific). EasySep Positive Selection kit (STEMCELL Technologies, MongKok, Hong Kong) was used to enrich APC-labeled p:MHCI tetramer-bound cells. Stained samples were acquired on Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Cells were gated on singlets, live leukocytes, CD8+ lymphocytes, and tetramer+CD44+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Statistical analyses

The results were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test (GraphPad Software v.11, San Diego, CA). Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Attempts to produce and secrete SARS-CoV-2 N by recombinant IB-V002 via the FHA secretion mechanism

As SARS-CoV-2 N adopts an overall globular structure, we used this protein as model antigen in this study. IB-V002 is a derivative of the B. pertussis live attenuated BPZE1 vaccine in which the gene coding for the surface protein pertactin has been deleted (Solans et al. 2021). This strain showed increased fitness compared to the original BPZE1 strain in the context of acellular pertussis vaccination. SARS-CoV-2 N was genetically fused to Fha44, and the transgene was inserted into the urease locus of the IB-V002 chromosome by homologous recombination (Supplementary Fig. S1a). After growth for 20 h in Stainer-Scholte medium, cell-associated material was separated from the culture supernatants and both were analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-N monoclonal antibody. While purified rN could readily be detected, no protein specifically reacting with the anti-N monoclonal antibody could be detected in the whole-cell extracts or tenfold concentrated supernatants of three independent BT-V100 clones (Supplementary Fig. S1b). There was no difference between any of these three clones and the parental IB-V002, indicating that the Fha44-SARS-CoV-2 N chimera was not efficiently produced and secreted via the FHA secretion mechanism.

Production and secretion of SARS-CoV-2 N by recombinant IBV002 via the SphB1 system

Since SARS-CoV-2 N was not efficiently secreted via the FHA secretion system, we tested the SphB1 secretion system as an alternative and replaced the SphB1 protease passenger domain by N. SphB1 is surface exposed and anchored in the outer leaflet of the bacterial outer membrane by its N-terminal lipidated cysteine. The replacement of the SphB1 lipoprotein signal peptide by the classical signal peptide of pertactin causes the release of SphB1 into the extracellular milieu (Coutte et al. 2003). We therefore constructed two SphB1 variants. One variant was designed to produce the chimeric protein under the control of the pertactin promoter and containing the pertactin signal peptide, while the second variant was designed to produce it under the control of the sphB1 promoter and containing the sphB1 lipoprotein signal peptide. This yielded BT-V101 and BT-V102, respectively (Fig. 1a). The signal peptides were followed by the N-terminal SphB1 domain, from amino acid residue 128 to residue 181, followed by N flanked by the HA (at the N-terminal side) and V5 (at the C-terminal side) epitopes, and finally by the C-terminal domain of SphB1. The transgenes were inserted into the urease locus of IB-V002.

IB-V002, BT-V101, and BT-V102 were grown in modified Stainer-Scholte medium (Stainer and Scholte 1970; Imaizumi et al. 1983) for 20 h, after which the bacterial cells and culture supernatants were harvested and analyzed by immunoblotting as described above for BT-V100. As shown in Fig. 1b, SARS-CoV-2 N could readily be detected in the whole cell lysates of both BT-V101 and BT-V102, while no anti-N antibody-reactive protein was detected in the whole cell extract of IB-V002. The analysis of the culture supernatants also revealed anti-N antibody-reactive protein bands in the culture supernatant of BT-V101, but not in the culture supernatant of IB-V002 or BT-V102.

Interestingly, the major anti-N antibody-reactive proteins in the supernatants and cell-associated material had approximately the same size as the purified rN used as controls in the immunoblots, suggesting that protein maturation occurred during or after protein synthesis. To further characterize the anti-N antibody-reactive proteins secreted by BT-V101, the BT-V101 culture supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE and mass fingerprinting of two bands visible by Coomassie-blue staining and absent in the IB-V002 culture supernatant. The upper band (Supplementary Fig. S3, band 1) contained mass-fingerprint peptides covering the N-terminal domain of SphB1, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein, and domain X of SphB1. The lower band (Supplementary Fig. S3, band 2) contained mass-fingerprint peptides corresponding only to the SARS-CoV-2 N protein. These results indicate that the SphB1 expression and secretion machinery can be used for the production and secretion of heterologous antigens in IB-V002, and that the replacement of the SphB1 signal peptide by that of pertactin strongly enhances secretion of the heterologous chimeric proteins. We therefore continued our studies with BT-V101.

Characterization of BT-V101

To determine whether the expression and secretion of SARS-CoV-2 N may have an impact on bacterial growth, we compared the in vitro growth of BT-V101 with that of IB-V002 in liquid medium using the EloCheck system. The initial velocities of bacterial growth were indistinguishable between the two strains (Fig. 1c). After 24 h of culture the growth of BT-V101 was slightly slower than that of IB-V002, but both reached the same optical density. Similar results were obtained using the Spark® microplate reader, which showed that the overall areas under the curve were very similar between the two strains.

To determine whether the transgene expression might influence colonization of the mouse respiratory tract, C57BL/6 mice were infected with 5 × 106 CFU of IB-V002 or BT-V101, and we followed the bacterial colonization over time up to 56 days after infection. In the lungs, bacterial colonization by both strains increased by roughly two orders of magnitude between day 0 and 3 post-infection, after which colonization gradually declined and fell under the detection limit by day 56 (Fig. 1d). In the noses, bacterial colonization by either strain had increased slightly at day 3 after infection and then decreased by roughly two orders of magnitude by day 56 post-infection. No significant difference could be detected between strains, neither in lung nor in nasal colonization. These results indicate that the production and secretion of the recombinant protein in BT-V101 does not affect the overall bacterial fitness and colonization ability in the murine respiratory tract.

Antibody responses after immunization with BT-V101

To determine whether intranasal vaccination with BT-V101 can induce antibody responses to SARS-CoV-2 N, we administered 5 × 107 CFU BT-V101 or IB-V002 to C57BL/6 mice three times at 4-week intervals (Fig. 2a). Two weeks after the last immunization, sera and nasal wash samples were collected and assayed for the presence of N-specific IgG and IgA, respectively. A modest but significantly higher serum IgG response to N could be detected in BT-V101-vaccinated mice compared to IB-V002-vaccinated mice (Fig. 2b). In contrast, no significant IgA response to N was detected in the nasal washes (Fig. 2c).

T cell responses to SARS-CoV-2 N in BT-V101-vaccinated mice

To analyze the T cell responses to SARS-CoV-2 N in BT-V101-vaccinated mice, we harvested the spleen cells after three intranasal administrations of 5 × 107 CFU BT-V101 or IB-V002 and stimulated them with rN. Stimulation of the splenocytes from BT-V101-vaccinated mice with rN induced significant secretion of IFN-γ compared to splenocytes from mice vaccinated with IB-V002 (Fig. 2d). In addition, splenocytes from BT-V101-vaccinated mice also produced higher levels of IL-17A than splenocytes from IB-V002-vaccinated mice when stimulated with rN, although overall, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2e).

In parallel, we also examined the T cell responses to B. pertussis. The production of IFN-γ (Supplementary Fig. S4a) and IL-17A (Supplementary Fig. S4b) by splenocytes in response to B. pertussis whole cell lysate stimulation could readily be detected after immunization with either BT-V101 or IB-V002, with no statistical difference between the two groups of animals.

Induction of N-specific CD8+ resident memory T cells by BT-V101 vaccination

Considering the importance of CD8+ resident memory T (TRM) cells in protection against viral infections, including SARS-CoV-2 infection (Ferretti et al. 2020), we examined whether BT-V101 could induce N epitope-specific CD8+ TRM cells in the nasal tissue. Two weeks after the last nasal BT-V101 or IB-V002 administration, T cells were isolated from the NALT. To discriminate circulating and resident T cells, the NALT was recovered 10 min after injection of labeled anti-CD45 antibodies and stained with an H2-Db/N219–227 MHC class I tetramer. Tetramer-stained CD44+CD8+CD45iv− cells could be detected in the NALT and spleen from BT-V101-vaccinated mice, but not in the NALT and spleen from IB-V002-vaccinated mice (Fig. 3a and b).

Heterologous BT-V101–rN prime–boosting

One important role of vaccines is to prime the host immune system for rapid and effective responses upon subsequent antigenic exposure. We therefore examined whether BT-V101 could efficiently prime mice to rN given as a boost. In order to enhance the nasal N-specific IgA responses, we intranasally vaccinated C57BL/6 mice twice with either BT-V101 or IB-V002 at a 4-week interval, followed 4 weeks later by an intranasal boost with rN formulated with DDA/MPLA (Fig. 4a). This vaccination schedule resulted in the induction of N-specific IgA levels in the nasal washes of BT-V101-primed mice, which were significantly higher than for IB-V002-primed mice (Fig. 4b).

Moreover, splenocytes from BT-V101-primed mice stimulated in vitro with rN produced substantially higher IFN-γ amounts than the spleen cells from IB-V002-primed mice (Fig. 4c). The T cell response to B. pertussis was not affected by the heterologous boost, as splenocytes were able to secrete IFN-γ in response to B. pertussis whole cell lysate stimulation after the intranasal boost with rN (Supplementary Fig. S5). Significantly higher numbers of CD44+CD8+CD45iv− TRM cells stained with the H2-Db/N219-227 MHC class I tetramer were also detected in the nasal tissues of BT-V101-primed mice than in those of IB-V002-primed mice (Fig. 4d), although the latter also contained tetramer-positive TRM cells in their nasal tissues. Together, these results indicate that intranasal priming with BT-V101 improved the mucosal IgA response, as well as the systemic and local cellular responses, including the levels of CD44+CD8+ TRM cells specific for SARS-CoV-2 N.

We also investigated whether BT-V101 priming is efficient in combination with subcutaneous boosting by purified rN (Fig. 5a). Two priming vaccinations with BT-V101, followed by subcutaneous boosting with rN formulated with DDA/MPLA, resulted in higher N-specific serum IgG levels compared to priming with IB-V002 (Fig. 5b). BT-V101 priming also resulted in enhanced N-specific splenocyte IL-17A (Fig. 5c) responses than with IB-V002 priming. Priming with BT-V101 followed by subcutaneous boosting with rN also resulted in somewhat higher N-specific IL-17A levels in the lungs compared to IB-V002 priming, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5d).

Discussion

In this study, we explored a novel bacterial secretion system for the presentation of heterologous antigens to the respiratory mucosa using a live attenuated B. pertussis vaccine vehicle. Previous work has illustrated the use of the FHA secretion machinery for antigen presentation, but not all antigens, especially antigens with a globular structure, could be successfully secreted using this system (Guédin et al. 1998; Reveneau et al. 2002). As an alternative, we chose the SphB1 expression/secretion system. While SphB1 is a member of the autotransporter family (Coutte et al. 2001), including pertactin (Emsley et al. 1996), Vag8 (Finn and Amsbaugh 1998), Phg (Middendorf et al. 2005), tracheal colonization factor (Finn and Stevens 1995), BrkA (Fernandez and Weiss 1994), and others, all of which are ß helix proteins like FHA, SphB1 may constitute an exception within the family, as its passenger domain is a subtilisin-like protease (Coutte et al. 2001). Although the structure of SphB1 has not been determined yet, subtilisin-like proteins have been shown to adopt a globular structure composed of α helices and ß strands (Wright et al. 1969), and alpha-fold prediction of SphB1 strongly suggests that the subtilisin-like passenger domain adopts a globular fold and is composed of alpha helices and beta stands (Fig. 7a, b). Therefore, the SphB1 system may be suitable for the secretion of globular proteins. As a model antigen to test the hypothesis, we used SARS-CoV-2 N, since this protein has a globular structure composed of α helices and ß strands (Peng et al. 2020) and may therefore be incompatible with secretion via the FHA system but compatible with the SphB1 secretion system (Fig. 7c, d). The alpha-fold prediction also suggests that the antigen fused to SphB1 is surface exposed (Fig. 7c) and therefore accessible to the immune system after administration of the recombinant bacterium.

The subtilisin domain is represented in green, the domain X in light blue, the site of SphB1 proteolytic maturation in dark blue, and the SphB1 C-terminal ß-barrel domain in orange. c Side view and d top view of the SphB1-N chimera modeled using alphafold. In agreement with this hypothesis, we found that SARS-CoV-2 N could indeed be produced and secreted by the recombinant pertussis vaccine candidates BT-V101 via the SphB1 secretion machinery, provided the pertactin signal peptide was used, while the use of the FHA secretion machinery did not result in N secretion. This system can therefore be used for the secretion of proteins that are difficult to secrete via the FHA system. Strikingly, when cell-associated material from BT-V100 was analyzed, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein could not be detected in any of the clones. This is likely due to the rapid degradation of the periplasmic misfolded or FhaC-stalled secretion intermediates of the recombinant protein. Previous work has shown that Fha44 chimeric proteins are rapidly degraded when FhaC-dependent secretion through the outer membrane is impaired (Guédin et al. 1998; Reveneau et al. 2002). Expression of N via the SphB1 system did not hamper bacterial growth nor did it significantly interfere with bacterial colonization of the upper and lower respiratory tract in mice.

Intranasal vaccination with BT-V101 resulted in modest but significant induction of N-specific serum IgG responses. Significant antigen-specific SIgA in the nasal washes could not be detected after BT-V101 vaccination when compared to vaccination with the non-recombinant IB-V002 strain. While serum IgG responses to N remained modest after BT-V101 vaccination and are unlikely to neutralize the virus, T cell responses, including local T cell responses, were robust. BT-V101 vaccination induced Th1-type T cell responses, as evidenced by IFN-γ secretion by splenocytes from BT-V101-vaccinated mice. Some Th17 responses were also induced by BT-V101 vaccination. The induction of T cell responses may be of particular interest for protection against viral diseases, including COVID-19 (De Bert et al. 2020), and has been associated with protection against influenza (McKinstry et al. 2012).

In addition to systemic Th1-type T cell responses, intranasal BT-V101 vaccination also induced N-specific CD8+ TRM cells in the nasal mucosa. CD8+ T cells, especially CD8+ TRM cells, play a central role in protection against viral infections, as well as in the control of tumors. Yet their induction by vaccination has proven to be difficult, and tools for CD8+ TRM cell induction are limited. The use of recombinant live attenuated pertussis vaccines presenting viral antigens via the SphB1 secretion machinery may therefore be an attractive vaccination strategy to generate CD8+ T cell responses in the respiratory tract.

In addition, virus-specific CD8+ TRM cells are long-lived (Lu et al. 2021), in contrast to serum IgG levels, which wane within a period of months. However, most currently used COVID-19 vaccines are based on the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein with the aim to induce serum IgG that neutralize the virus after intramuscular vaccination. These vaccines have been remarkably effective in preventing disease, especially severe disease, most likely due to their ability to induce strong virus-neutralizing antibodies. However, they do not prevent infection and transmission of the virus, and their effectiveness may be affected by the rapid evolution in the spike protein to escape vaccine-induced immunity. In contrast, SARS-CoV-2-specific CD8+ T cells target a broad range of antigens, including structural and non-structural proteins, such as N, particularly well conserved among the various viral variants (Karl et al. 2015).

Virus-specific CD8+ T cells have been associated with reduced COVID-19 disease severity and viral load. However, early induction of CD8+ T cell responses appears critical for protection, as severe COVID-19 has been associated with delayed CD8+ T cell responses (Tan et al. 2021). Hence, a vaccine that is able to effectively prime specific CD8+ T cell responses, especially in the respiratory tract, as shown here for BT-V101, may be useful to rapidly clear viral infections. We found that BT-V101 is also very effective in priming both N-specific serum IgG, nasal SIgA, as well as T cell responses, including specific CD8+ TRM cell responses in the nasal tissues. These responses could be strongly boosted by heterologous exposure to the same antigen in the form of purified rN, either provided nasally or subcutaneously. We noticed that N epitope-specific CD8+ TRM cell responses were also detected in mice primed with IB-V002 and intranasally boosted with rN in the presence of DDA/MPLA. This may be due to some level of cross-reactivity of the vector and rN. Alternatively, it may also be possible that the combination of the antigen with the adjuvant given nasally resulted in the induction of these CD8+ TRM cell, as has been shown for a similar adjuvant in combination with mycobacterial antigens (Christensen et al. 2017). In any event, vaccines such as BT-V101 may constitute useful tools as effective local and systemic T and B-cell priming vaccines.

In conclusion, we have shown here that the SphB1 system can be used for the secretion of heterologous antigens by recombinant derivatives of the live attenuated pertussis vaccine candidate and that intranasal administration of such strains induces local and systemic immune responses, especially T cell responses and in particular CD8+ TRM cells in the respiratory tract. This may therefore constitute a promising alternative to the previously explored FHA secretion system and particularly well suited for the secretion of antigens with a folded globular structure, which have been proven difficult to secrete via the FHA system. Future studies will be geared towards optimizing both expression/secretion systems and determining the limits inherent for each secretion system.
