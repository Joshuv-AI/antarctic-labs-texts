Brain entropy as a biomarker of major depression in adolescents and young adults: insights from multimodal resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Ruoxi Lu, Jianyu Li, Yiran Li, Xinglin Zeng, Yan Guo, Danian Li, Ying Cui, Xinyu Liang, Hanyue Zhang, Jing Wang, Baohua Cheng, Yujie Liu, Ze Wang, Shijun Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) in adolescents and young adults is increasingly prevalent, yet accurate diagnosis remains challenging due to the limitations of conventional neuroimaging metrics. Traditional resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) measures such as amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), regional homogeneity (ReHo), and functional connectivity density (FCD) primarily capture static aspects of brain activity and may overlook critical neural dynamics. Brain entropy (BEN), which quantifies temporal irregularity in rs-fMRI signals, may offer a complementary approach to better characterize neural alterations in MDD.MethodsWe analyzed multimodal rs-fMRI data from 204 individuals aged 12–24 years (119 with MDD and 85 healthy controls). BEN was computed alongside ALFF, ReHo, and FCD to extract region-wise features across the brain. A support vector machine with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) was used to classify MDD and healthy controls based on various feature combinations. Classification performance was evaluated using repeated cross-validation and permutation testing. Additionally, partial Spearman correlations were performed between selected brain features and clinical measures including depression severity, childhood trauma, sleep quality, and cognitive control.ResultsModels incorporating BEN consistently outperformed those using traditional rs-fMRI features alone. The combination of BEN, ALFF, and FCD achieved the highest classification accuracy (AUC = 0.877, permutation test P < 0.001). The most frequently selected brain regions contributing to MDD classification included the putamen, paracentral lobule, cuneus, middle frontal gyrus, and rectus. BEN features also showed preliminary correlations with clinical variables such as childhood trauma and sleep quality, suggesting functional relevance.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that BEN provides complementary diagnostic information to traditional rs-fMRI features in classifying adolescent and young adult MDD. BEN-related alterations in brain activity may reflect underlying neurobiological disruptions and show potential as a functional neuroimaging biomarker for depression during a critical stage of brain development.

Introduction

Depression is a leading contributor to global disease burden during adolescence and early adulthood, with recent epidemiological data indicating a marked increase in prevalence over the past decade, particularly among females (Thapar et al., 2022). Depression onset during this critical developmental window is associated with long-term consequences, including an increased risk of recurrent depressive episodes, comorbid psychiatric conditions, and disruptions in daily functioning (Thapar et al., 2022). Despite its significant burden, depression in adolescents and young adults remains frequently underdiagnosed. Over 40% of cases go unrecognized and clinicians often report limited diagnostic confidence (Korczak et al., 2023). While molecular and neuroimaging biomarkers have actively been investigated, their clinical utility and reliability in this age group remain inconclusive (Zwolińska et al., 2023).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a non-invasive and objective means of investigating structural and functional brain alterations associated with mental disorders (Lui et al., 2016). Among the various MRI modalities, resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) has been widely adopted to characterize functional abnormalities in major depressive disorder (MDD), revealing consistent alterations in large-scale brain networks in both adolescents and adults (Javaheripour et al., 2021). While most previous neuroimaging studies on adolescent depression have focused on individuals under 19 years of age, emerging evidence suggests that continued brain maturation and vulnerability to psychopathology continue into early adulthood (Kerestes et al., 2014). In line with the extended definition of adolescence spanning ages (10–24 years) (Sawyer et al., 2018) the present study focused on individuals aged 12–24 years.

Several traditional rs-fMRI metrics have been used to characterize brain activity in depression, including the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) (Yu-Feng et al., 2007), regional homogeneity (ReHo) (Yu-Feng et al., 2007), and functional connectivity density (FCD) (Tomasi and Volkow, 2010). Reduced ALFF and ReHo in prefrontal–limbic regions in MDD have been reported in several studies, suggesting impaired local neural activity and synchronization (L. Wang et al., ). Ke Zou et al. (2016) found decreased FCD in the orbital frontal cortex, precuneus, and temporal–occipital regions, suggesting disrupted functional integration in MDD. While encouraging, these metrics primarily focus on the low-frequency fluctuations of the underlying spontaneous brain activity, overlooking other aspects of neural dynamics. An emerging technique to address this limitation is the rsfMRI-based brain entropy (BEN) mapping (Z. Wang et al., 2014).

Rather than focusing on a specific empirical subband, BEN quantifies signal irregularity or randomness of a voxel’s rs-fMRI time series in the entire sampled spectrum. In the context of fMRI, this irregularity reflects how diverse and unpredictable the BOLD signal fluctuations are over time, providing an index of the complexity of spontaneous neural activity rather than random noise. High BEN means high irregular resting state brain activity and low BEN indicates a more temporally coherent brain activity. Over the past decade, BEN has been increasingly studied in healthy brain and brain disorders. Evidence from healthy populations has first established its methodological stability and behavioral relevance. In the healthy brain, BEN shows a high test–retest stability (Z. Wang et al., 2014; Del Mauro and Wang, 2024a); it increases with age but decreases with education years, and lower regional BEN correlates with better cognitive functions (Z. Wang, 2021; Del Mauro and Wang, 2024a). Beyond its reliability, BEN also demonstrates distinct informational value compared with other functional MRI metrics. Compared to more widely adopted measures such as ALFF and cerebral blood flow, BEN presents unique information that cannot be explained by the other measures (Song et al., 2019a). Interestingly, resting BEN predicts task activation and deactivation (Lin et al., 2022), suggesting that it captures intrinsic dynamics relevant to brain responsiveness. Extending from healthy samples to clinical populations, BEN alterations have been demonstrated in various neurological (Zhou et al., 2016; Z. Wang and for the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, 2020) and psychiatric disorders (Liu et al., 2013, 2014; Li et al., 2016; Z. Wang et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2019; X. Liu et al., 2020a; Jiang et al., 2023). Importantly, regional BEN in healthy subjects has been demonstrated to be modifiable through repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) (Song et al., 2019a, 2025; P. Liu et al., 2025a), caffeine (Chang et al., 2018), and task performance (Camargo et al., n.d.; Z. Wang et al., 2014). Similarly, its plasticity has also been observed in clinical contexts. In patient populations, regional BEN has been shown to be modifiable by rTMS (Chang et al., 2018a; Jordan et al., 2023, 2024) and medicine (X. Liu et al., 2020). Among psychiatric disorders, depression has received particular attention. BEN has been assessed in two depression studies (X. Liu et al., 2020; Song et al., 2024). X. Liu et al. (2020) found that higher baseline BEN (before treatment) in the medial orbitofrontal cortex was associated with higher depression severity, which was reversed after 8 weeks of successful antidepressant treatment. The reversion was correlated with improved depression symptoms (X. Liu et al., 2020). Song et al. (2024) identified regional BEN increase in mild to moderate depression, which was suppressed by nonpharmacological treatment.

The above substantial literature clearly demonstrates the validity of BEN as a potential marker for assessing regional brain function and for assessing mental disorder-related functional brain alterations. The purpose of this study was to assess the efficacy of resting BEN for MDD classification in adolescents and young adults. We hypothesize that combining BEN with other widely used rs-fMRI metrics, including ALFF, ReHo, and FCD, will improve MDD classification accuracy in adolescents and young adults.

Materials and methods

Participants

rs-fMRI data were collected from 204 participants aged 12–24 years, including 119 individuals diagnosed with MDD (37 males, 82 females; mean age 18.4 ± 3.5 years) and 85 demographically matched healthy controls (HC; 34 males, 51 females; mean age = 19.2 ± 3.8 years). MDD was diagnosed in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, and was confirmed by psychiatrists at the psychiatric outpatient clinic of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. All MDD participants were first-episode and drug-naïve at the time of enrollment, with no history of antidepressant treatment or psychotherapy. HCs were recruited from the community and screened through structured clinical interviews to confirm the absence of any current or past psychiatric or neurological disorders. Exclusion criteria for all participants included any history of neurological disorders, substance abuse, severe head trauma, comorbid psychiatric disorders, and contraindications for MRI scanning. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (No. K-2023–048).

Clinical measures

Clinical assessments included Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) to quantify depressive symptom severity (Hamilton, 1960), Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) for childhood trauma exposure (Bernstein et al., 1994), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for assessing sleep quality (Buysse et al., 1989), and Stroop Color-Word Test for evaluating cognitive control (Stroop, 1935).

MRI data acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a 64-channel radiofrequency head coil. rs-fMRI data were collected using the Siemens simultaneous multislice (SMS) blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) sequence, specifically a two-dimensional (2D) multiband gradient echo echo-planar imaging (SMS-EPI) sequence. The acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 500 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 60°, matrix size = 64 × 64, field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, 35 contiguous slices (no slice gap), and 960 volumes.

To assist with image registration and normalization, high-resolution anatomical images were acquired using a three-dimensional T1-weighted (3D-T1WI) magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE = 2530/2.98 ms, inversion time = 1100 ms, flip angle = 7°, matrix size = 256 × 256, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm, no slice gap, and 192 sagittal slices.

Thick-slice axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) images were acquired for clinical screening. Two experienced radiologists assessed the images and confirmed that all included participants exhibited no visible brain abnormalities or lesions.

Data preprocessing

rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (v.24.1.1), a robust and standardized pipeline based on Nipype. Anatomical T1-weighted images were corrected for intensity non-uniformity, skull-stripped, segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and spatially normalized to MNI152Lin and MNI152NLin2009cAsym templates via nonlinear registration (ANTs).

Functional images underwent slice-timing correction, motion correction (MCFLIRT), and were co-registered to the T1w image using boundary-based registration (BBR) with six degrees of freedom. Confound regressors included framewise displacement (FD), DVARS, global signals, and both anatomical and temporal CompCor components. Volumes with FD > 0.5 mm or DVARS > 1.5 were marked as motion outliers. All data were resampled to 2 mm isotropic MNI space (MNI152Lin: res-2) using a single interpolation step.

Denoising and spatial smoothing

After basic preprocessing steps, additional nuisance regression was applied to further reduce physiological and motion-related artifacts. Confound regressors were generated by fMRIPrep, including six motion parameters (three translations and three rotations) and the first five anatomical CompCor components (aCompCor). Confound regression was performed in voxel space by Nilearn, which applied voxelwise linear regression to remove the selected nuisance signals from the BOLD time series. No global signal regression was applied in this pipeline.

Spatial smoothing was performed by FSL (v.6.0.4), applying a Gaussian kernel with a sigma of 1 mm (approximately FWHM ≈ 2.35 mm). This kernel was applied to ALFF, ReHo, FCD, and BEN, while ReHo was calculated from unsmoothed data.

Calculation of traditional rs-fMRI metrics and BEN

Regional features, including ALFF, ReHo, FCD, and BEN, were extracted from preprocessed rs-fMRI data using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas (90 regions; Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002). For each metric, individual whole-brain maps were first computed in standard space. Subsequently, mean values within each AAL region were extracted to obtain region of interest (ROI)-level features.

ALFF

ALFF was computed to quantify the intensity of spontaneous BOLD signal oscillations within the low-frequency range (0.01–0.1 Hz). For each subject, preprocessed rs-fMRI data were analyzed using FSL tools to derive voxel-wise power spectrum of the time series. The amplitude spectrum was extracted, restricted to the 0.01–0.1 Hz band, and averaged over time to yield individual ALFF maps. These maps represent the average amplitude of low-frequency BOLD fluctuations at each voxel.

ReHo

ReHo computation was performed using the y_reho function from the DPABI toolbox (Yan et al., 2016), which implements the Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (KCC) among the time series of a given voxel and its neighbors. A cluster size of 27 voxels was used for the ReHo computation, corresponding to the voxel and its 26 immediate neighbors in a 3 × 3 × 3 cube. A standard brain mask in MNI space was applied to constrain ReHo calculation to gray matter regions.

FCD

FCD mapping (Del Mauro et al., 2024) was conducted to quantify the voxel-wise distribution of functional connections in the brain. For each voxel, the number of functional connections with all other voxels within the brain mask was computed based on the temporal correlation of their BOLD signals. A minimum of 30 volumes (-c 30) was used to ensure reliable correlation estimation. The resulting FCD maps reflect the degree centrality of each voxel and were saved in standard space for further analysis.

BEN

BEN maps were calculated from preprocessed fMRI data using the BEN mapping toolbox (BENtbx), which is based on the sample entropy (SampEn) algorithm. The BENtbx software is freely available at https://www.cfn.upenn.edu/zewang/BENtbx.php and https://github.com/zewangnew/BENtbx. In this study, the window length (m) was set to 3 and the similarity threshold (r) was set to 0.6, based on parameters optimized in previous studies. Consistent with the standard parameter configuration validated in previous BEN studies, the embedding dimension was set to m = 3 and the similarity threshold to r = 0.6 × SD, a combination shown to yield stable and reproducible entropy estimates for fMRI time series (Chen et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2014).

Multimodal classification and ROI ranking

Linear regression was used to remove sex, age, and education effects from all ROI-level features (ALFF, ReHo, FCD, BEN). The residuals were then z-score standardized across subjects to ensure comparability between different metrics.

Classification analyses were conducted using a support vector machine with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) employing a linear kernel. Class imbalance was handled by applying a weighting strategy during training. Hyperparameter optimization of the regularization parameter (C, range: 0.01–1.0) was performed via GridSearchCV using 5-fold inner cross-validation. The classification model’s performance was rigorously validated using a 5 × 5 repeated stratified cross-validation approach, with ROC-AUC as the primary evaluation metric. Statistical significance was evaluated using permutation testing (n = 1000 permutations).

Following SVM-RFE classification, the top 15 ROIs were selected based on the highest absolute SVM feature weights for each multimodal combination. ROIs appearing at least twice across different multimodal combinations were further identified and ranked by frequency of appearance and mean absolute feature coefficients, highlighting consistently discriminative brain regions across different model configurations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and machine learning procedures were conducted with Python (v.3.9.6), using standard scientific and neuroimaging libraries. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Within the MDD group (n = 119), partial Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between selected ROI-level features and clinical variables (HAMD, CTQ, PSQI, Stroop scores), while controlling for sex, age, and education to minimize potential demographic confounds and focus on disease-specific effects. To correct for multiple comparisons in the correlation analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, with a corrected significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, we used rs-fMRI-derived BEN to classify MDD in adolescents and young adults aged 12–24 years. To assess the complementary value of BEN, we incrementally incorporated three conventional rs-fMRI metrics, ALFF, ReHo, and FCD, into classification models. Three major findings emerged. First, inclusion of BEN improved classification performance across nearly all feature combinations, with entropy-augmented models achieving higher AUCs, accuracies, and F1-scores compared to entropy-free models. AUC values generally ranged from 0.75 to 0.88, with results robust across repeated cross-validation and confirmed by permutation testing. Second, several regions, including the putamen, paracentral lobule, and cuneus, consistently ranked among the most discriminative features for distinguishing MDD from controls. Finally, these regions also showed preliminary associations with individual differences in childhood trauma, sleep quality, and cognitive control.

BEN enhances classification performance

Our results demonstrate that incorporating BEN systematically improves MDD classification accuracy. Two combinations that included BEN achieved the highest classification performance: BEN + ALFF + FCD (AUC = 0.877) and BEN + ALFF + REHO + FCD (AUC = 0.844). In contrast, the best-performing BEN-independent combination (ALFF + REHO + FCD) reached only AUC = 0.761. This comparison highlights the unique and complementary contribution of BEN to MDD classification, beyond what can be achieved with established rs-fMRI metrics alone.

Previous machine-learning studies on MDD have reported variable classification performance depending on sample characteristics and imaging features. A recent meta-analysis across rs-fMRI studies covering broad age ranges of MDD patients reported a pooled AUC of approximately 0.86 (95% CI 0.83–0.89) (Chen et al., 2023), reflecting moderate yet heterogeneous diagnostic performance across modalities and algorithms. Within adolescent cohorts, studies based on ReHo (Mao et al., 2020) or static and dynamic connectivity (Sen et al., 2021) achieved AUC or accuracy values around 0.75–0.82 in relatively small single-site samples.

Building upon these earlier findings, our study extended this line of work to a larger developmental cohort (n = 204) and further incorporated BEN as a complementary feature, achieving an AUC of 0.877, within the upper range of previously reported values.

The superior classification performance of BEN-augmented models probably reflects BEN’s ability to capture signal irregularity or complexity that are particularly relevant to MDD (Z. Wang et al., 2014; X. Liu et al., 2020; D.-H. Song et al., 2024). Unlike conventional measures that describe amplitude (ALFF), local synchronization (ReHo), or connectivity density (FCD), BEN quantifies the uncertainty and high-order signal variability (Shannon, 1948; Richman and Moorman, 2000). These properties may relate to the flexible allocation of cognitive and emotional resources, which are often compromised in depression (Kaiser et al., 2015; Z. Wang, 2021; Del Mauro and Wang, 2024a). Although our primary focus was on classification rather than group-level differences, BEN’s sensitivity to temporal irregularities may provide a distinct advantage in detecting subtle disruptions in neural function that conventional rsfMRI metrics are less equipped to capture (D.-H. Song et al., 2024).

Prior work has demonstrated BEN’s functional distinctiveness. It exhibits high test–retest reliability (Del Mauro and Wang, 2024; Del Mauro et al., 2024; Mauro and Wang, 2025; Z. Wang et al., 2014), accounts for less than 5% variance with ALFF and cerebral blood flow (Song et al., 2024), and has been shown to be predictive of task-evoked activation and deactivation patterns (Lin et al., 2022). Additionally, BEN has demonstrated sensitivity to both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, including rTMS (Chang et al., 2018b; Jordan et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; P. Liu et al., 2025), antidepressants (X. Liu et al., 2020), and caffeine (Chang, Song, et al., 2018), further supporting its neurobiological interpretability.

Functional roles of the top-ranked brain regions

To better understand the functional significance of the most informative features, we examined the top 15 ROIs identified based on their frequency and contribution across models. These regions included the putamen, orbitofrontal cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, thalamus, insula, precuneus, gyrus rectus, supramarginal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, and several sensory-related areas including the Rolandic operculum, Heschl’s gyrus, and paracentral lobule. These ROIs were derived from multiple feature types (BEN, ReHo, ALFF, and FCD) and consistently emerged across different model combinations, underscoring their robustness.

Collectively, these regions map onto functional domains repeatedly implicated in MDD, including affect regulation, cognitive control, reward processing, self-referential thinking, and sensory integration (Disner et al., 2011). For instance, the putamen and orbitofrontal cortex are central to reward learning and motivational drive, both of which are disrupted in depression and manifest clinically as anhedonia (W.-N. Zhang et al., 2013). The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and thalamus are critical to executive function and attentional control, and dysfunction within these regions in MDD has been associated with impaired information integration and reduced cognitive control capacity (Koenigs and Grafman, 2009). Meanwhile, the insula serves as a hub for interoceptive awareness and emotional salience detection (Menon and Uddin, 2010), and accumulating evidence indicates that MDD is associated with altered insular function, particularly involving aberrant integration and weighting of interoceptive information within salience-related networks (Paulus and Stein, 2010; Drysdale et al., 2017). Other high-order association areas, including the precuneus, gyrus rectus, supramarginal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus, contribute to self-referential processing, social-affective cognition, and internally directed thought, all processes that are often dysregulated in depression (Cavanna and Trimble, 2006; Northoff et al., 2006).

In addition to these canonical MDD-related regions, several top-ranked ROIs were located in primary and secondary sensorimotor cortices. The Rolandic operculum and Heschl’s gyrus form part of the peri-insular auditory network, while the paracentral lobule lies within the medial sensorimotor cortex. Recent evidence suggests that these sensorimotor regions are closely associated with abnormal integration of exteroceptive and interoceptive signals in patients with depression, suggesting that perceptual and somatosensory processing may be broadly impaired in adolescent and young adult MDD (Ries et al., 2019; R. Zhang et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2024).

Top brain regions and their relevance to MDD

To assess clinical relevance beyond classification, we correlated the values of top-ranked ROIs with clinical indices including HAMD, CTQ, PSQI, and Stroop scores within the MDD group (n = 119). Several ROI–symptom associations showed trends at uncorrected significance levels, reflecting exploratory findings that may inform future hypothesis generation. For instance, ReHo in the right cuneus was positively correlated with total childhood trauma scores, and BEN in the right paracentral lobule was negatively associated with emotional neglect. These associations may tentatively reflect links between regional coherence or signal complexity in sensory-motor and visual areas and early-life adversity, consistent with prior observations (Tomoda et al., 2012). Sleep quality also showed preliminary associations: ALFF in the left cuneus and ReHo in the right cuneus were both negatively correlated with PSQI scores, suggesting potential involvement of visual regions in sleep-related alterations.

Futhermore, ReHo in the right putamen was negatively associated with interference accuracy of Stroop task, which may point to a relationship between striatal coherence and executive control performance. The putamen is part of the fronto-striatal loop known to support response inhibition, particularly in tasks requiring suppression of prepotent responses such as the Stroop task (Aron et al., 2004). Finally, FCD in the left Heschl’s gyrus and Rolandic operculum showed positive correlations with sexual abuse scores, suggesting a possible involvement of sensory–perceptual regions in trauma-related alterations. This direction is consistent with prior reports linking childhood maltreatment to functional and structural alterations in these regions (Tozzi et al., 2016). Similarly, Dadomo et al. (2022) identified the Heschl’s area and putamen as predictors of sexual trauma in individuals with borderline personality disorder. These trends suggest that somatosensory regions may be relevant to the neural effects of early traumatic experiences.

Although these associations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, their spatial distributions and functional characteristics reflect trends that have been observed in prior research. These findings offer preliminary support for the interpretability of the model-selected features and suggest that they may reflect individual variability in symptom dimensions beyond categorical diagnosis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the wide developmental age range may introduce residual neurodevelopmental heterogeneity despite statistical control for age. Second, although all patients were first-episode and drug-naive, detailed information on illness onset and duration was not quantified and may still contribute to inter-individual variability. In addition, sex-stratified analyses were not performed due to the limited number of male participants, which may affect generalizability. Third, imaging and clinical correlations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Fourth, ROI-level features were extracted using the AAL90 atlas, which facilitates interpretability and dimensionality reduction but may obscure finer grained spatial heterogeneity. Lastly, classification performance was evaluated using internal cross-validation without an independent external validation dataset; thus, the reported results should be considered internal validation estimates. Future studies using longitudinal designs and independent multi-site cohorts will be important to further establish the developmental specificity, robustness, and clinical utility of BEN-based biomarkers.

Conclusion

This study investigated the classification of adolescent and young adult MDD using multimodal rs-fMRI features. Across analyses, BEN demonstrated consistent complementary value, enhancing classification performance when combined with other commonly used rs-fMRI indices. The most frequently selected regions were primarily located in circuits involved in reward processing, salience detection, and self-referential cognition—networks repeatedly implicated in MDD. Exploratory correlation analyses further suggested that these features may capture clinically meaningful variability related to symptoms such as childhood trauma, sleep quality, and executive control. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of BEN and other functional indices as informative biomarkers of MDD in youth, while also underscoring the need for longitudinal and multimodal studies to establish their mechanistic and clinical significance.
