Linkages among foraging movements, prey provisioning, and reproduction in a central place foraging seabird
Authors: Aliya Caldwell, Elizabeth Craig, Nathan T. Hermann, Nathan B. Furey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA seabird’s ability to efficiently find prey directly affects reproduction, and seabird population declines are frequently linked to forage fish depletion. Reduced forage fish availability often limits seabird egg production and chick provisioning, but impacts to foraging movements remain largely unresolved, as does the effect of movement flexibility on provisioning and reproduction in conditions of reduced forage fish availability.MethodsThis study assesses drivers of foraging movement and the role of foraging movement in mediating provisioning and reproduction in a central place foraging seabird, the common tern (Sterna hirundo), by integrating movement (GPS telemetry), chick provisioning, and reproductive productivity data. Data were collected across four breeding seasons (2021–2024) on the largest common tern colony in the Gulf of Maine, where ocean warming is precipitating shifts in forage fish populations.ResultsIn poor prey and productivity years, when clutch size (eggs per nest), fledging success, and provisioning of preferred prey (herrings [Clupea harengus, Alosa spp.], hakes/rockling [Merluccius bilinearis, Urophycis spp., Enchelyopus cimbrius], sand lances [Ammodytes spp.]) were reduced, common terns increased foraging trip durations by 23%–87% and expanded foraging ranges by 17%–51%. Range expansions occurred regardless of sex or breeding status and were driven by increased use of distant waters (up to ~150 km from the colony) rich in forage fishes preferred by common terns. In years of increased reproductive success and high preferred prey provisioning, foraging movements were shorter, and foraging locations focused on waters immediately surrounding the colony (within ~10 km), as well as two nearby river mouths (Piscataqua River and Merrimack River).ConclusionsOur results suggest that common tern foraging movements are flexible in response to forage fish availability, but that increased movement effort in years of reduced prey availability may be insufficient to maintain chick diet and survival. In regions like the Gulf of Maine, where ocean-warming continues to induce shifts in forage fish populations, additional protection may be critical to central place foraging seabirds. Without proactive management, breeding seasons marked by increased foraging movement effort with little resulting reproductive success may become increasingly frequent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-026-00675-5.

Background

Foraging movements are critical to mobile predators, as survival and reproduction are driven by the ability to find sufficient and suitable food [1, 2]. Thus, foraging movements impact species persistence and population dynamics, in turn contributing to predator-prey dynamics, ecosystem diversity, nutrient transport, and energy transfer [3, 4]. Describing and quantifying foraging movements and their drivers can improve understanding of foraging strategy and habitat and lend insight into predator responses to changes in prey populations [3–5].

Seabirds are long-lived, highly mobile top predators, often foraging across broad spatial scales to meet their energetic requirements, and during the breeding season, those of their chicks [6]. Therefore, most movements of breeding seabirds are dedicated to foraging. A seabird’s ability to efficiently find prey directly affects survival and reproduction, and declines in seabird populations are often linked to prey depletion [7–9]. During the breeding season, when prey availability is critical to egg production (particularly for income breeders using exogenous rather than stored nutrients and energy to produce eggs [10]); and chick survival [6], many seabirds employ a central place foraging strategy in which their movements are restricted to some area around their nesting location, from which they come and go to balance self-feeding, nest tending, and chick provisioning [11, 12]. Many seabirds also display site fidelity to their previous nesting colonies [13], restricting their ability to adjust the location of their central place among years. Single-prey-loading seabirds (i.e., species that capture and provision one prey item at a time) are additionally constrained, as they must attempt to maximize search and foraging effort in relation to the energetic value of the single prey item carried back to their chicks [12].

While breeding seabirds experience such constraints to their foraging movements, their prey (often small, schooling, lipid-rich fishes, henceforth “forage fish” [14]); tend to be distributed in patches which vary in distribution and abundance within and among years [15–18]. To maintain energy intake and maximize reproductive success in the face of such variability, seabirds must exhibit some behavioral flexibility, which might include prey-switching or altering foraging movements [19–22]. Despite the role of movement in the foraging process, understanding of interannual variation in – and links among – foraging movement, provisioning, and reproductive success in seabirds are sparse (but see [20, 23], and [24]), limiting understanding of both the ability of central place foraging seabirds to adjust movements in response to changes in prey resources over time, and the potential provisioning and reproductive consequences of adjustments to foraging movement. As climate change continues to precipitate shifts in prey landscapes, describing potential impacts to foraging movements will become increasingly critical for predicting distributional, energetic, and reproductive consequences for predators [25–27].

The common tern (Sterna hirundo) is a small seabird with a high degree of breeding colony site fidelity, migrating from the southern to the northern hemisphere each year [28]. Common terns breed on coasts and coastal islands including many in the Northwestern Atlantic Ocean’s Gulf of Maine, which supports a large common tern breeding population [29]. Common terns are income breeders, single-prey loaders, and central place foragers that specialize on forage fishes, which they capture by plunge-diving into the top meter of the water column [12, 29–31]. In the Gulf of Maine, waters warming more rapidly than most of the world’s oceans are leading to significant shifts in the abundance and distribution of forage fishes comprising the common tern prey base [32–34]. Most concerning are changes to populations of herrings (Clupea harengus, Alosa aestivalis, and A. pseudoharengus), hakes (Merluccius bilinearis, Urophycis chuss, and U. tenuis), and sand lances (Ammodytes americanus and A. dubius [17, 35–37]), which are preferred prey of common terns in the region [30, 31] given their historic abundance, suitable morphology, and high energy density (particularly herrings and sand lances [38, 39]). Also concerning is the movement of warmer-water prey into the Gulf of Maine, particularly the Atlantic Butterfish (Peprilus triacanthus), which may appear suitable to common terns upon capture but are too deep-bodied for most chicks to swallow [37, 40]. Extensive long-term monitoring throughout the Gulf of Maine provides insight into how such shifts in forage fish populations can impact common tern provisioning and reproduction including egg production and chick survival [10, 31, 40–44]. However, impacts to foraging movements remain unclear in the Gulf of Maine and across systems (but see [45–47]), as does the role of foraging movement flexibility in mediating common tern prey provisioning and reproduction in the face of shifting forage fish populations.

This study aims to assess both drivers of central place foraging common tern movements among years and the role of foraging movement in mediating provisioning and reproductive success in a system with shifting forage fish communities. We leveraged Global Positioning System (GPS) data collected on the Gulf of Maine’s largest common tern breeding colony to describe foraging distribution and movement dynamics across four consecutive breeding seasons of variable reproductive productivity and prey provisioning on the colony reflective of local forage fish conditions. We expected foraging movement effort to vary among years, with birds increasing their foraging effort in years of reduced prey availability and reproductive productivity regardless of sex and breeding status, the latter of which we predicted would also impact movements. We specifically expected movements to increase in birds after nest failure in response to relaxation of central place restrictions upon the change in breeding status. Lastly, we expected that movements would increase over the course of each breeding season as chicks grew to increasingly require less tending or in response to progressive prey depletion surrounding the colony. Understanding such drivers of common tern forging movements in the rapidly warming Gulf of Maine, and in particular, the degree to which movements are flexible among years of variable prey availability, could lend insight into how climate-driven changes to forage fish communities might impact central place foraging seabirds.

Methods

GPS tagging

This study was conducted on White and Seavey Islands, located approximately 10 km off the coast of New Hampshire in the Gulf of Maine, USA. The two islands are ~3.25 hectares in size, are connected by a land bridge during half of the tide cycle, and supported between 3,069 and 3,413 common tern nesting pairs during each breeding season (May–August) of this study.

Solar-powered GPS tags were deployed on adult common terns incubating eggs or brooding young chicks (<3 days old) in June and July of 2021–2024 (Table 1). Pathtrack LTD NanoFix GEO+FR units were deployed in 2021 and 2022, and NanoFix GEO+RF Mini units were deployed in subsequent years. Birds were captured using walk-in traps and banded with a U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) metal band and a plastic field readable (PFR) band on the opposite leg; all chicks of each tagged bird were also banded as described. Wing cord and weight were measured prior to tag deployment and contour feather samples (3–5 feathers) were collected for genetic-based sex determination (conducted by Avian Biotech [48]). Tags were affixed via leg-loop harnesses (constructed following methods described in [49, 50]) designed to detach after the breeding season due to material degradation or loosening when adults lost weight during fall migration [49, 50]. The combined mass of the tags and harnesses were between 3 and 4% of each bird’s body mass [51]; Table 1), and handling times (capture to release) ranged from 4 to 12 minutes (mean = 7.6 min). For each tagged bird, the time between release and return to the nest was noted as a measure of immediate tagging effects, and although a previous study testing the effects of the leg-loop harness tag attachment method on common terns found no evidence of detrimental impacts to behavior, reproduction, or survival [49], tagged birds were monitored closely throughout the breeding season. While control nests were not established, the chicks belonging to GPS tagged birds were also monitored throughout the breeding season to confirm that the fledge rates of tagged bird’s nests were consistent with colony-level fledge rates. In years after tagging, opportunistic sightings of tagged birds (via PFR bands) were collected to generate re-sight rates for previously tagged birds. The GPS tags attempted fixes according to a programmed schedule, calculating positions when in line-of-sight of four or more GPS satellites and if battery voltage was sufficient. The tags were programmed differently in each year due to annual technological improvements to battery and data collection capacities (Table 1). Tags were typically programmed to collect fixes every 10 or 20 minutes and to turn off for a portion of the night (most often 22:00 EST– 03:00 EST) to save battery life for consistent daytime fixes (see Table 1 for programming details). Nighttime off periods were chosen to prioritize data collection during daylight because terns are visual predators and therefore forage less frequently at night, especially during chick-rearing [52, 53]. Positional data were stored internally and offloaded via UHF link to base stations located centrally on the colony when birds were within range of the base station antennas (~200 meters); most tags were programmed to attempt data offloads every 60 minutes during the breeding season (see Table 1 for programming details). GPS data were processed locally using PathTrack Host Software (V2.38). Data from June and July were retained for the present study focused on breeding season movements; positions from August were removed as a precaution to exclude potential pre-migratory movements that could be inconsistent with typical breeding season movement behavior [28].

Prey provisioning and reproduction

Provisioning and reproductive data were collected during colony monitoring conducted annually since 1999 by the Isles of Shoals Tern Restoration Project [54]. In the four years of this study (2021–2024), provisioning data were collected from 40 to 62 nests per year (most of which were not nests of GPS-tagged birds) to generate annual colony-level proportional abundance estimates of prey provisioned to chicks, which served as an indicator of prey availability conditions on the colony in each year. Data were collected during feeding observation periods conducted from three observation blinds on the colony and from stationary video recorders. During each observation period, ~1–10 nests were monitored for ~1–3 hours depending on the number of nests with surviving chicks at the time of observation, crew size, and observation method (blind vs stationary video). Data recorded for each provisioning event included prey identity and total length (measured relative to the adult tern’s bill in cm). To characterize prey provisioning on the colony, data were extracted for five prey types (grouped via methods adapted from [30]) that in combination constituted > 90% of identified prey items in the colony’s diet in each study year. Of these five, prey types preferred by common terns based on energetic quality and morphological suitability were herrings, hakes/rockling (Enchelyopus cimbrius indistinguishable via visual observation from hakes as juveniles), and sand lances [30, 31, 38, 39]. The two remaining prey types were Atlantic butterfish, which most tern chicks are unable to swallow [40], and invertebrates (dominated by Amphipoda spp. and Euphausiidae spp.), which tend to be small with low energy density compared to fish [38]. Annual estimates of colony-level reproductive productivity, a secondary indicator of prey availability, were generated using data collected from 72 to 116 nests per year (most of which were not nests of GPS-tagged birds) bounded within plots designed to prevent chicks from escaping. The plots were distributed throughout the colony, and their locations and sizes were consistent across years to enable interannual comparisons. Nests within each plot were visited every one to two days until all eggs hatched. Once hatched, chicks were banded (with USGS metal bands and PFR bands on opposite legs) and nests were checked every three to four days until all chicks died or fledged (i.e., survived to 15 days post-hatch, the standard measure of fledging success for Gulf of Maine common terns [55]), unlike nests of GPS-tagged birds which were monitored for the duration of the breeding season. Metrics calculated for each nest included clutch size (number of eggs laid) and fledge rate (number of chicks surviving to fledge). Proportional abundance of nests with 1-, 2-, 3-, and 4-egg clutch sizes and 0-, 1-, 2-, and 3-chick fledge rates were summarized annually to describe colony-level reproductive productivity, with clutch size also serving as an indicator of early-season prey availability on the colony [10, 42].

Provisioning and reproductive outcomes in study years (2021–2024) were compared to a 10-year reference period (2011–2020) for which prey provisioning and productivity metrics were generated for each year using the same methods described above. Metrics were averaged across reference period years for comparison with study years via one-sample proportions tests (with Bonferroni corrected p-values to account for 4 years ×5 prey type groups = 20 comparisons in provisioning models and 4 years ×4 clutch size/fledge rate groups = 16 comparisons in reproduction models). For each study year, changes in proportions of each prey and productivity metric from the reference period average were considered biologically significant if both statistically significant (p < 0.05) and of a magnitude ≥ 0.10 (Δ proportion ≥ 0.10). Study years with provisioning of preferred prey types (i.e., herrings, hakes/rockling, and sand lances), fledge rates, and clutch sizes similar to or improved above the reference period were characterized as “good prey and productivity years” on the colony (see Results). Years with decreased preferred prey provisioning and increased unpreferred prey provisioning (i.e., butterfish and invertebrates), decreased fledge rates, and reduced clutch sizes were characterized as “poor prey and productivity years” on the colony (see Results).

Foraging movement

To differentiate movements associated with periods of incubation, chick rearing, and nest failure, breeding statuses were assigned to GPS positions daily for each tagged bird based on the makeup of their nest throughout the breeding season. “Incubating” breeding status was assigned to data collected prior to the first chick’s hatch date. Following the hatch of the first chick, positions were assigned to “chick-rearing” status for the duration of time in which nest monitoring confirmed that the tagged bird was still tending at least one chick. If a tagged bird was confirmed to have no surviving chicks, all subsequent positions were assigned a breeding status of “failed”. If an incubating bird failed to hatch eggs after one month, all subsequent data were also assigned a status of “failed”. “Unknown” breeding status was assigned to any positions for which chick condition could not be confirmed on the date of GPS data collection.

To identify movements associated with foraging behavior, GPS data were fit to Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), which are a robust method for characterizing animal movement data into behavioral states based on step lengths and turning angles among positions [56–58]. HMMs are frequently applied to movement data from common terns and other seabirds [59, 60]. For each year, three-state HMMs were fit in R package ‘moveHMM’ [61] to differentiate nesting, transiting, and foraging positions. Positions characterized as foraging (defined by short step lengths and sharp turning angles among positions) by best-fit HMMs for each year were extracted for further analysis. For more information on HMM data preparation and modeling, see Supplement 1 Table S1.1.

Foraging range

To describe foraging range, GPS positions identified as foraging by the HMMs were fit to kernel utilization distributions (KUDs) in R package ‘adehabitatHR’ using bivariate normal kernel density estimation (Gaussian kernel function [62]). For each study year, KUDs were generated for chick-rearing and failed breeding status positions as well as for GPS positions of all breeding statuses combined (including unknown and incubating). KUDs were not generated for incubating breeding status alone because most birds were tagged after chicks hatched, therefore incubation data were sparse or absent in some study years. From each KUD, areas were calculated to describe core (50% KUD contour) and full (95% KUD contour) foraging range in each year. Prior to KUD fitting, foraging position sample sizes were equalized among years by randomly resampling positions from each year to match the sample size of the year in which the fewest positions were collected (2021, n = 6,079 positions). Annual KUDs were also generated for each individual bird, with foraging position sample sizes equalized among individuals prior to fitting by randomly resampling positions from each bird (with ≥200 positions) to match the sample size of the individual with the fewest positions. Core and total foraging range areas were calculated for each individual-level KUD to further summarize interannual variations in foraging distribution and for use in rarefaction analyses, which were run to confirm that observed among-year differences in KUD size represented real changes in distribution rather than variations in the number of birds tagged among years. Rarefaction analyses determined the sample size of tagged birds at which colony-level estimates of space use via KUD areas stabilized (i.e. minimum tagged bird sample size) to be ~5–10 individuals (see Supplement 3 for rarefaction methods and results).

Foraging trips

Movement metrics were calculated for each foraging trip to further examine common tern foraging movement behavior. GPS data from 2023 and 2024 (5- or 10-min fix rates) were resampled to match the 20-minute fixes collected in 2021 and 2022, and positions were characterized as being “at” or “away from” the colony, with positions > 0.4 km from the colony (haversine distance calculated in R package ‘geosphere,’ [63]) considered “away.” Foraging trips were defined as tracks departing from and arriving back to the colony that included at least one HMM-characterized foraging position. Each trip was assigned a breeding status (incubating, chick-rearing, failed, or unknown) based on the status of the tagged bird’s nest on the trip’s start date. Duration (cumulative travel time), distance (cumulative distance traveled), and extent (maximum distance from colony reached) were calculated for each trip taken by birds with known sex and breeding status (73% of trips retained).

To assess sources of variation in foraging trip metrics, Generalized Linear Mixed-Effects Models (GLMMs) were fit in a Bayesian framework with gamma distributions (log link functions) in R package ‘brms’ [64]. The GLMMs modeled each foraging trip metric (duration, distance, extent) separately as a function of the additive effects of year (2021–2024), Julian day (ordinal date), sex (male or female), and breeding status (incubating, chick-rearing, or failed), and an interaction term between breeding status and year (Supplement 2 Table S2.1). Individual was included as a random effect in each model to account for repeated measures (Supplement 2 Table S2.1). Model checks included Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) convergence assessment via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat R$$\end{document} and examination of effective sample sizes (ESS; number of independent samples effectively produced during the MCMC process) of the central tendency and extremes for each parameter [64]. Model fit was assessed via Bayesian R2, Leave-One-Out Cross Validation (LOO-CV), and posterior predictive checks (visual comparison between observed and simulated data [64]). Parameter estimates were considered statistically significant when the 95% credible interval (CI) around the posterior mean did not overlap zero.

Results

Prey provisioning and reproduction

During the 10-year reference period (2011–2020), common terns on the colony provisioned chicks with preferred prey of herrings (mean ± SD proportion of prey = 0.29 ± 0.14 and of lengths = 0.32 ± 0.13), hakes/rockling (mean ± SD proportion of prey = 0.45 ± 0.13 and of lengths = 0.41 ± 0.15), and sand lances (mean ± SD proportion of prey = 0.08 ± 0.06 and of lengths = 0.09 ± 0.07), and with unpreferred prey of invertebrates (mean ± SD proportion of prey = 0.07 ± 0.11 and of lengths = 0.02 ± 0.02) and butterfish (mean ± SD proportion of prey = 0.13 ± 0.11 and of lengths = 0.15 ± 0.13). In 2022 and 2024, preferred prey provisioning was similar to (Δ proportion < 0.10, p > 0.05) or increased above (Δ proportion ≥ 0.10, p < 0.05) the reference (aside from a significantly reduced proportion of hakes/rockling lengths in 2022), and provisioning of unpreferred prey was similar to the reference (aside from a significantly increased proportion invertebrates in 2024; Fig. 1, Table S4.1). In 2021 and 2023, provisioning of herrings and hakes/rockling (preferred prey) was significantly reduced compared to the reference period (aside from a non-significant reduction in proportion of herring lengths in 2023), and that of sand lances (preferred prey) was similar to the reference (aside from significantly increased proportion of lengths in 2023; Fig. 1, Table S4.1). During the 10-year reference period, 2- and 3-egg clutch nests were laid more frequently (mean ± SD proportion of nests = 0.58 ± 0.10 and 0.26 ± 0.12 respectively) than 1- and 4-egg clutch nests (mean ± SD proportion of nests = 0.15 ± 0.05 and 0.01 ± 0.01 respectively). During the same period, more than half of monitored nests fledged one or two chicks (mean ± SD proportion of nests = 0.45 ± 0.21 and 0.15 ± 0.15 respectively) while fewer failed to fledge a chick (mean ± SD proportion of nests = 0.36 ± 0.18). In 2022 and 2024, clutch sizes and fledge rates were similar to the reference (Δ < 0.10, p > 0.05), with most nests containing two or three egg clutches and fledging at least one chick (Fig. 1, Table S4.1). In 2021 and 2023, when provisioning of most preferred prey was reduced and that of unpreferred prey increased, proportions of 1-egg clutch and 0-fledgling nests increased significantly above the reference (Δ > 0.10, p < 0.05; Fig. 1, Table S4.1). From the provisioning and reproduction results, 2022 and 2024 were characterized as good prey and productivity years and 2021 and 2023 were characterized as poor prey and productivity years for the colony.

Foraging movement

GPS tagging effects

Re-sight rates for GPS-tagged birds ranged from 20% for birds tagged in 2021 to 80% for those tagged in 2022 (2023 = 58% and 2024 = 25%; Table 1). On average, tagged birds returned to their nests within 19 minutes of release (SD = 29 min, median = 8 min, n = 41 birds). The proportion of successfully fledged nests was generally higher for tagged birds than for those monitored for colony productivity in each year (2021 proportion of GPS nests = 0.53 vs productivity plot nests = 0.32; 2022 proportion of GPS nests = 0.80 vs productivity plot nests = 0.66; 2023 proportion of GPS nests = 0.21 vs productivity plot nests = 0.35; and 2024 proportion of GPS nests = 0.80 vs productivity plot nests = 0.71), but note that GPS nests and those monitored for colony productivity might not be directly comparable given the fates of chicks in GPS nests were tracked more intensively and via additional methods (i.e. stationary video).

Foraging range

Foraging range KUDs were generated from HMM-characterized foraging positions which comprised an average of 46% (SD = 4%) of GPS positions collected in each year (vs transiting mean ± SD = 23% ± 3% and nesting mean ± SD = 30% ± 7%; see Supplement 1 Tables S1.2–S1.3 and Figure S1.1 for detailed HMM results). In good prey and productivity years (2022, 2024), the full foraging range of the colony (95% KUD of all positions in each year) spanned coastal and offshore waters from southern Maine (ME) to northern Massachusetts (MA), as well as coastal waters of eastern Cape Cod, MA. In good years, the colony’s core foraging range (50% KUD) and both the core and full foraging ranges of chick-rearing birds (50% and 95% KUDs of all positions of chick-rearing breeding status in each year) were restricted New Hampshire (NH) and northern MA waters directly surrounding the colony (Fig. 2). In poor prey and productivity years (2021, 2023), when preferred prey provisioning, clutch sizes, and fledge rates were reduced, the colony’s full foraging range and that of chick-rearing birds alone grew to include coastal and offshore waters of central and southern MA and northeastern Cape Cod. The colony’s core foraging range also expanded in poor prey and productivity years, notably to include waters off the coast of northern Cape Cod that were not represented in the colony’s core foraging range during good years (Fig. 2). In all years, core and full foraging ranges were larger for failed breeders than for chick-rearing breeders; regardless, in poor prey and productivity years both failed and chick-rearing birds expanded their foraging ranges (Fig. 2). Discussion

Our results suggest that forage fish availability can drive common tern foraging movement. Common terns foraged locally (within ~40 km of the colony) in 2022 and 2024, undergoing foraging trips of shorter duration over smaller distances and extents. In these years marked by foraging in local waters, common tern reproductive success was high, likely due to increased provisioning of energy-dense herrings and hakes, which are known to support chick growth and survival [40, 65, 66]. Larger clutch sizes in the same years indicate that adults had access to similarly high-quality prey early in the 2022 and 2024 breeding seasons, as common terns are income breeders that rely on energy from real-time prey capture to produce eggs [10, 42]. In contrast, common tern foraging movement effort increased in 2021 and 2023, with the core and full foraging ranges of chick-rearing birds expanding by up to ~25 km (~3-fold increase) and ~100 km (>3-fold increase), respectively. Chick survival was significantly reduced in these years, likely due to increased provisioning of unsuitable and energy-poor prey [40, 65, 66]. Small clutch sizes in the same years suggest that forage fish availability was reduced during early-season egg laying periods of 2021 and 2023 [10, 42], with subsequent decreases in preferred prey provisioning and chick survival indicating that prey scarcity was sustained throughout chick-rearing [30, 31, 43, 44, 67]. Although local forage fish availability was not measured directly, small proportions of high-quality fish in common tern diets can be linked directly to local forage fish depletion [68] and forage fish distribution and abundance have been found to correlate with common tern diet and reproductive data throughout the Gulf of Maine, suggesting that observations of variable chick provisioning and survival on our colony were similarly indicative of local prey availability [10, 30, 31, 42–44, 67]. Therefore, the increased foraging movement effort observed in poor prey and productivity years is likely explained by reduced forage fish availability forcing terns to spend more time foraging over greater distances, a behavior observed in other seabird species [20, 69–71] and well-supported by optimal foraging theory [2, 12]. Significant increases in foraging trip metrics over the course of the breeding season in all years provide further evidence that foraging movements were driven by prey availability, as such within-season increases in movement are often indicative of an “Ashmole’s halo” effect [72] in which central place foragers deplete local prey throughout the breeding season, leading birds to forage progressively further from the colony [73, 74].

In good prey and productivity years, common terns foraged locally in coastal waters and river mouths (Piscataqua River, NH and Merrimack River, MA), a behavior typical of common terns [45, 60, 75] and expected given freshwater-ocean interfaces are often associated with dense forage fish aggregations which attract predators including seabirds [76–79]. In contrast, the longer foraging trips taken by birds in poor prey and productivity years expanded the foraging range of the colony (and of chick-rearing breeders alone) southward to include waters of central and southern MA > 150 km from the colony. While average foraging trip distances and extents in all years were on the high end of those measured in terns previously, the expanded ranges and long trips south observed in poor years were increased well above previous estimates for small terns including common terns [19, 60, 80–84]. Notably, the region of increased use to the south of the colony includes the Stellwagen Bank, a diverse and productive ocean ridge with particularly high sand lance abundance [30, 31, 85] encompassed within the Gulf of Maine’s only national marine sanctuary (Stellwagen Bank National Marine Sanctuary). Therefore, in years of poor prey availability it may be energetically preferable, or even necessary, for common terns to make longer trips to exploit the bank’s predictably abundant prey patches [86]. Although capture locations of each prey type could not be assessed directly, we found that while most preferred prey were consistently reduced in the colony’s diet in poor years, sand lance provisioning remained near to or increased above the 10-year reference period. Maintaining sand lance provisioning in poor prey and productivity years suggests that chick-rearing birds may have increased their use of waters in and around Stellwagen Bank and Cape Cod specifically to exploit sand lance-rich foraging grounds, a behavior for which evidence exists in Netherlands-breeding sandwich terns [81]. As ocean warming continues to destabilize forage fish populations, identifying and protecting areas of forage fish collocation with top predators like Stellwagen Bank [87, 88], will become increasingly important for seabird conservation [8, 89, 90].

Although common terns can apparently adjust their foraging movements in response to local prey conditions, our results also suggest that such flexibility may be insufficient to mediate common tern provisioning and reproductive outcomes in years of reduced prey availability. In poor prey and productivity years on the colony, chick survival and provisioning of most preferred prey types were reduced despite increased foraging movement effort. In contrast, provisioning of sand lance was maintained or increased in such years, suggesting that movement flexibility might mediate chick outcomes to some extent, though not enough to achieve survival and provisioning rates comparable to reference levels. Such years of poor chick survival and diet despite increased foraging effort might occur when additional effort fails to increase prey capture sufficiently, impacting chick provisioning and survival directly or indirectly if the additional energy expended forces parents to increase their own prey intake over provisioning their chicks [23, 91]. Because common terns are long-lived and may breed up to 20 times throughout their life [92], adults are expected to prioritize their own survival over that of their chicks to ensure their ability to breed in the future [93–95]; therefore, some degree of fluctuation in annual reproductive output may not impact long-term population stability. However, breeding seasons marked by increased foraging movement effort with little resulting reproductive success may become increasingly frequent for common terns and other central place foraging seabirds in regions like the Gulf of Maine, where climate-change induced shifts in forage fish communities are projected to accelerate [32–34]. If such pressure is sustained and seabirds are unable to sufficiently modify their foraging behavior, including movements, the persistence of central place foraging seabird populations may be threatened.

Our findings are limited by untested factors that likely contribute to the complex links among common tern provisioning, reproductive productivity, and movement. First, reproductive success may have been impacted by factors other than prey availability, including interannual variations in predation pressure or climatic events. Precipitation was increased in June and July of 2021 and 2023 (compared to 2022 and 2024), likely contributing to mortalities in young chicks unable to thermoregulate independently, especially when their body condition is undermined by poor diet [96–98]. Weather (primarily wind) might also contribute to variability in foraging movement dynamics [99], as could constraints to physiology, cognition, and behavior outside the scope of consideration for this study [1, 100]. Aside from untested factors, the assumption that chick provisioning data reflect forage fish availability might be complicated by prey selection dynamics [25, 101] and limitations of visual prey identification methods. Visual identification can be biased (e.g. increased likelihood of identifying larger and/or common prey items) and does not capture adult prey consumption, but recent fecal DNA metabarcoding analyses on the colony indicated that visual identification methods are robust and that adult and chick diets contain similar proportions of prey types [102, Clucas, Unpublished]. Still, inclusion of spatially explicit measures of forage fish distribution and abundance might improve future analyses of correlations between forage fish populations and seabird movement, especially if combined with exploration of within-season movement trends and deeper analyses of HMM outputs including time-activity budgets and transition probabilities among movement states [103–106].

Conclusions

Central place foraging seabirds with restricted diets and foraging ranges may face significant threats if forage fish communities experience rapid or extreme shifts in distribution and abundance that force seabirds to alter their foraging behavior, including movement, beyond energetically sustainable thresholds [21, 26, 107]. The interannual variations in foraging movements observed suggest that central place foraging common terns are able to modify their movements in response to forage fish availability; however, observations of reduced prey quality and high chick mortality in some years despite adjustments to foraging movement suggest that intensifying movement effort may not be sufficient to meet the energetic needs of both parents and chicks when forage fish availability is reduced. These results lay the conceptual groundwork for future integration of movement, diet, and reproductive data into bioenergetic and individual-based models [108] to develop a mechanistic understanding of how prey quality, forage fish availability, and foraging movements interact to affect energetic and reproductive outcomes for common terns and other central place foraging seabirds. Such mechanistic models could be integrated with climate and fish population modeling to predict movement and reproductive responses to future scenarios [109–111]), representing a critical step in risk assessment and evaluation of management options for the many threatened and declining populations of marine predators under pressure from climate-induced shifts to forage fish populations.
