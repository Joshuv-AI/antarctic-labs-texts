Comparative analysis of ERK and CREB activity in normal and cryptorchid testes of bulls
Authors: Changjin Yun, Sohee Jeong, Yongbin Cho, Sang-Ik Park, In Sik Shin, Jun-Gyu Park, Changjong Moon, Sohi Kang, Joong-Sun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceCryptorchidism, defined as the failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum, is a pathological condition that adversely impacts testicular development and spermatogenic function. In livestock, particularly bulls, such impairment can lead to substantial economic losses due to compromised fertility. A comprehensive understanding of the morphological and molecular alterations associated with cryptorchidism is essential for efforts to develop effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine morphological changes and changes in the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CREB) activity in the testes of bulls with cryptorchidism.MethodsTestes from the 18-month-old unilateral cryptorchid bulls, including the descended (normal) and undescended (cryptorchid) testes from each animal, were analyzed through gross examination, histology, immunohistochemistry (Ki-67, phosphorylated p-ERK, p-CREB), and Western blotting.ResultsCryptorchid testes were smaller and exhibited reduced epithelial thickness, although seminiferous tubule area remained unchanged. Expression of Ki-67, p-ERK, and p-CREB was significantly decreased, indicating impaired cell proliferation and signaling. Total ERK and CREB protein levels were unchanged, but phosphorylation levels were notably reduced in cryptorchid samples.Conclusions and RelevanceOur findings demonstrate that cryptorchidism alters testicular morphology and disrupts key signaling pathways, particularly those involved in cell growth and function. Such alterations may contribute to infertility and highlight the importance of early diagnosis and intervention in the context of reproductive management in livestock.Keywords: Cryptorchidism, cyclic AMP response element-binding protein, extracellular signal-regulated MAP kinases, spermatogenesis

INTRODUCTION

Cryptorchidism, a developmental disorder characterized by the failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum, is a serious condition affecting male fertility across various mammalian species [1]. This condition has several clinical implications: it can lead to infertility due to abnormal sperm production in non-descended testes, cause hormonal imbalances affecting sexual development, and increase the risk of testicular damage due to elevated temperatures in the abdomen [2].

Due to the warm temperature of the abdominal cavity, cryptorchidism may impair spermatogenesis and disrupt hormone balance, ultimately decreasing fertility [3,4]. In bulls, cryptorchidism occurs relatively rarely, with an incidence rate of less than 1% [5], but it has substantial economic and genetic implications for the cattle industry. This condition influences the fecundity of affected animals; if not correctly identified and managed, it can introduce undesirable genetic characteristics into breeding programs.

Additionally, cryptorchidism may be hereditary, potentially impacting future breeding programs. Surgical intervention to correct the condition is often necessary to prevent testicular atrophy or loss of function; affected animals may require exclusion from breeding to avoid passing on the trait [6].

Despite its importance, the molecular mechanisms of cryptorchidism-induced testicular dysfunction, particularly concerning key signaling pathways regulating spermatogenesis and function, are still not fully understood.

Spermatogenesis, the process of sperm cell development, is critically dependent on precise cellular signaling mechanisms. The extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CREB) pathways play pivotal roles in this process [7,8]. ERK signaling is involved in the proliferation and differentiation of spermatogonia [9], whereas CREB is essential for the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in spermatocyte maturation and survival [10]. Disruptions in these pathways can lead to impaired spermatogenesis, as observed in cryptorchid testes. This study aimed to elucidate the impact of cryptorchidism on testicular morphology and molecular signaling in mature bulls, focusing on the roles of the ERK and CREB pathways. An understanding of these molecular alterations will provide essential insights into the pathophysiology of cryptorchidism and clarify potential therapeutic targets that can mitigate its effects on fertility.

METHODS

Animal and testes sampling

Three unilateral cryptorchid bulls (Hanwoo, Korean native cattle, 18 months of age) with undescended testes, which were considered the cryptorchidism group, and descended testes, which were considered the normal group, were obtained from local farms. Bulls were castrated, and the testes were harvested. The procedures and protocols followed in the present study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chonnam National University (approval number: CNU IACUC-YB-2022-16). For histological analysis, one hemisphere of each testis was fixed for 1 week in 10% buffered formalin. The contralateral hemispheres were frozen and stored at −70°C until Western blotting.

Histological examination

The preserved tissues were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices. These sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin using an established procedure [11]. The stained slides were analyzed using an Easyscan Digital Slide Scanner (Motic, China).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed in accordance with a previously published procedure [12]. Briefly, 4-μm-thick sections were prepared by deparaffinization and hydration, followed by blocking in 5% normal goat serum (Vector ABC Elite Kit, PK-6101; Vector Laboratories, USA). These sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following rabbit polyclonal primary antibodies: anti-Ki-67 (1:500 dilution; DRM004; Acris Antibodies GmbH, Germany), anti-phosphorylated (p-)ERK (1:100 dilution; 4370s; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), or anti-p-CREB (1:200 dilution; 9198S; Cell Signaling Technology). After primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Vector ABC Elite Kit, PK-6101; Vector Laboratories), then incubated with an avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex Vector ABC Elite Kit, PK-6101; Vector Laboratories). The peroxidase reaction was developed using a diaminobenzidine substrate (DAB kit, SK-4100; Vector Laboratories), and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin before mounting. Stained slides were analyzed using an Easyscan Digital Slide Scanner (Motic).

Immunofluorescence

Tissue sections were prepared following the same protocol for deparaffinization and rehydration as described in the immunohistochemistry procedure. After antigen retrieval, sections were treated with 0.3% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide in distilled water for 20 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by blocking with 1% (v/v) normal goat serum (NGS) (S-1000; Vector Laboratories) and 0.5% (v/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A9647; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Phospho-p44/42 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) (20G11) Rabbit mAb (1:100 dilution, 4370S; Cell Signaling Technology), Phospho-CREB (Ser133) (87G3) Rabbit mAb (1:200 dilution, 9198S; Cell Signaling Technology), or mouse anti-vimentin (1:200 dilution, Clone V9; Neomarkers, USA) was added to antibody diluent (003218; Life Technologies, USA) and incubated overnight at 4°C. After three washes in PBS with Tween 20, the sections were incubated in anti-Mouse Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500 dilution, A28175; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody Alexa Fluor Plus 594 (1:500 dilution, A32754; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in blocking solution for 2 h at room temperature. The nuclei on the immunofluorescence slides were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1:200 dilution, D1306; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After three washes in PBS, coverslips were mounted onto the glass slides using VECTORSHIELD HardSet Antifade Mounting Medium (H-1400-10; Vector Laboratories). The cells were visualized 72 h after initial staining. Images were captured with a 10× lens using an LSM 900 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed in accordance with an established procedure [13]. Protein samples (40 μg) from normal and cryptorchid testes were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with a blocking buffer composed of 1% (v/v) NGS (#S-1000; Vector Laboratories) and 0.5% (v/v) BSA (#A9647; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (pH 7.4). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C in the same blocking buffer containing one of the following rabbit polyclonal primary antibodies: anti-p-ERK (1:2,000 dilution, 4370S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-ERK (1:1,000, dilution 4695S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-CREB (1:2,000 dilution, 9198S; Cell Signaling Technology), or anti-CREB (1:1,000 dilution, 9197S; Cell Signaling Technology). After primary antibody incubation, membranes were treated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5,000 dilution, 31460; Invitrogen, USA). Specific protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (SuperSignal West Pico or Femto; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Signals were normalized to β-actin, which was detected using a mouse anti-β-actin primary antibody (1:5,000 dilution, A5441; Sigma-Aldrich).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1; GraphPad Software, USA). The results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between normal and cryptorchid testes groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. The threshold for statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, indicating a meaningful difference between the groups.

RESULTS

Cryptorchid testes exhibited reduced testicular size

Gross examination revealed a significant reduction in testicular size in the cryptorchid group compared to the normal group (Fig. 1A). The weight of cryptorchid testes was markedly lower than that of normal testes (23.9 ± 1.9 g for normal vs. 16.8 ± 1.8 g for cryptorchid, p < 0.001; Fig. 1B). Similarly, the length was significantly reduced in the cryptorchid group (13.1 ± 0.6 cm for normal vs. 8.7 ± 2.0 cm for cryptorchid, p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). In contrast, the width decreased but did not reach statistical significance (6.3 ± 1.0 for normal vs. 4.8 ± 0.3 cm for cryptorchid, p = 0.06; Fig. 1D). These findings indicate that cryptorchidism is associated with testicular underdevelopment and reduced testicular mass, likely reflecting impaired spermatogenesis and compromised endocrine function.

Representative images and quantitative comparison of testicular size and weight in normal and cryptorchid bulls. (A) Gross morphology and dimensions of testes from normal and cryptorchid animals. (B) Testis weight, (C) testis length, and (D) testis width were measured and compared between groups. Values are presented as mean ± standard error. Scale bar = 1 cm.

*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs. normal group.

Cryptorchid testes exhibited reduced epithelial thickness

Histological analyses were performed to examine differences between normal and cryptorchid testes. Normal bull testes exhibited typical histology with multiple types of germ cells, including spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids, and Sertoli cells in the spermatogenic epithelium. In contrast, cryptorchid bull testes showed spermatogenic epithelium solely composed of Sertoli cells and some spermatogonia, without post-meiotic germ cells (Fig. 1B). To quantitatively evaluate morphological changes, seminiferous tubule area and epithelial thickness were measured. Tubule areas were similar between groups (44,403 ± 3,859 μm2 for normal vs. 33,019 ± 6,086 μm2 for cryptorchid, p = 0.19; Fig. 2B). However, epithelial height was significantly reduced in the cryptorchid group (65.91 ± 6.71 μm for normal vs. 30.25 ± 2.09 μm for cryptorchid, p < 0.01 Fig. 2C).

Quantitative analysis of (B) seminiferous tubule area and (C) seminiferous epithelium height in each group (n = 3 per group). Values are presented as mean ± standard error. Scale bar = 50 μm.

H&E, hematoxylin and eosin.

**p < 0.01 vs. normal group.

Cryptorchid testes showed reduced phosphorylation of ERK and CREB proteins

Western blot analysis was performed to assess the phosphorylation status of relevant proteins in bull testes (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis revealed that phosphorylated ERK levels were substantially lower in cryptorchid testes than in normal controls (1.00 ± 0.05 for normal vs. 0.18 ± 0.03 for cryptorchid, p < 0.001; Fig. 3B). p-CREB levels were substantially decreased in cryptorchid testes relative to controls (1.00 ± 0.07 for normal vs. 0.11 ± 0.01 for cryptorchid, p < 0.001; Fig. 3C).

Western blot analysis of ERK and CREB in bull testes. (A) Representative Western blots showing detection of total and p-ERK and p-CREB. (B) ERK activity levels were normalized to total ERK expression. (C) CREB activity levels were normalized to total CREB expression. Values are presented as mean ± standard error.

ERK, extracellular signal-regulated kinase; CREB, cyclic AMP response element-binding protein; p-, phosphorylated.

***p < 0.001 vs. normal group.

Cryptorchid testes showed decreased expression of Ki-67, p-ERK, and p-CREB in the seminiferous tubule

We conducted immunohistochemical analysis to assess cellular morphology in control and cryptorchid bull testes. In the control group, numerous Ki-67-positive cells were detected in the seminiferous tubules, specifically within spermatogonia and early spermatocytes (Fig. 4A). However, in the cryptorchid testes, only a few Ki-67-positive cells were observed in the basal layer of the seminiferous epithelium. Similarly, p-ERK expression was abundant in the control group, with strong signals in Sertoli cells located within the seminiferous tubules (Fig. 4B). In contrast, no p-ERK-positive cells were detected in the seminiferous epithelium of cryptorchid testes. Additionally, p-CREB expression was clearly visible in spermatocytes within the seminiferous tubules of normal testes (Fig. 4C). This signal was entirely absent in the cryptorchid group; no p-CREB-positive cells were evident in the seminiferous epithelium.

Immunohistochemistry results of Ki-67, p-ERK, and p-CREB staining in bull testes. Representative images show the expression of (A) Ki-67, (B) p-ERK, and (C) p-CREB immunoreactivity in the testes from each group. Scale bar = 100 μm.

p-ERK, phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase; p-CREB, phosphorylated cyclic AMP response element-binding protein.

Cryptorchid testes showed reduced ERK and CREB phosphorylation in Sertoli cells

Double immunofluorescence staining for vimentin, a Sertoli cell marker, along with p-ERK and p-CREB, was performed on testicular sections from normal and cryptorchid bulls (Fig. 5). In the normal group, vimentin and p-ERK were co-localized, indicating active ERK signaling within vimentin-positive Sertoli cells. In contrast, the cryptorchid group exhibited a marked reduction in p-ERK expression, accompanied by decreased vimentin expression, which was mainly confined to the basal region of the seminiferous tubules. The typical triangular morphology of Sertoli cells was disrupted, suggesting cytoskeletal disorganization and loss of cell polarity (Fig. 5A).

Double immunofluorescence staining of vimentin with p-ERK and p-CREB in testicular sections from normal and cryptorchid bulls. (A) Representative images showing immunofluorescence staining for vimentin (green) and p-ERK (red) with DAPI nuclear counterstaining (blue). (B) Immunostaining of vimentin (green) and p-CREB (red) with DAPI (blue). Merged images display co-localization patterns. Insets indicate magnified regions of interest. Scale bars = 20 µm.

p-ERK, phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase; p-CREB, phosphorylated cyclic AMP response element-binding protein; DAPI, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole.

Regarding the p-CREB expression, the normal group showed broad distribution across the seminiferous epithelium with rare overlap with vimentin-positive cells, suggesting that p-CREB is primarily active in germ cells or other non-Sertoli populations. However, p-CREB expression was significantly diminished in the cryptorchid group, consistent with impaired signaling activity (Fig. 5B). DAPI staining confirmed no apparent difference in basal cell density between the groups, indicating maintained structural integrity at the basal level (Fig. 5A and B).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we observed notable histological and molecular alterations in cryptorchid testes compared to normal testes in mature bulls. The reduction in testicular size and the thinning of seminiferous epithelium indicate a disruption in the structural and functional integrity of the testis, which may underlie impaired spermatogenesis. This finding are consistent with previous reports indicating the detrimental impact of elevated intra-abdominal temperatures on testicular development and function.

The decrease in Ki-67 expression in spermatogonia of cryptorchid testes suggests a significant decline in cellular proliferation capacity. This observation implies compromised spermatogenic activity because Ki-67 is a widely recognized cell proliferation marker. Similar trends have been reported in various species, including humans [14], dogs [15], and boars [16], reinforcing that thermal stress interferes with the early stages of germ cell development [17,18].

Our results also indicate that key regulatory pathways involved in spermatogenesis, such as ERK and CREB signaling, are functionally disrupted in cryptorchid testes. Although total protein levels remained comparable between groups, the p-ERK and p-CREB were significantly reduced. Since phosphorylation is required for their activity, this reduction likely reflects impaired signaling essential for spermatocyte development and survival. ERK plays a critical role in the meiotic division of spermatogonia [19], whereas CREB regulates genes essential for spermatocyte maturation. When CREB phosphorylation is impaired, spermatocyte apoptosis may be induced [20]. The p-ERK and p-CREB is a central regulatory mechanism in the proliferation and differentiation phases of spermatogenesis [21]. Therefore, the decreased phosphorylation observed in this study offers a plausible molecular mechanism by which cryptorchidism disrupts spermatogenesis and ultimately reduces fertility. While ERK and CREB are critical for testicular function, it is well understood that a broader network of signaling pathways governs spermatogenesis. Molecules such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/AKT, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, p38 MAPK, and apoptotic regulators such as Bcl-2 and Bax also play pivotal roles [7,22,23]. Further studies exploring these additional pathways will be valuable for providing a more comprehensive understanding of testicular degeneration mechanisms in cryptorchidism.

Although we did not directly measure intra-abdominal or testicular temperatures, our molecular findings support the hypothesis that thermal stress contributes to altered signaling in the undescended testis. To further validate this association, additional studies using temperature-controlled in vitro models or heat-induced animal models would be beneficial in delineating the causal relationship between hyperthermia and molecular dysregulation. Future studies should incorporate environmental and physiological heat indices to better evaluate the role of thermal stress [24,25]. Testicular development is known to be highly sensitive to temperature alterations, and increased scrotal or testicular temperature can impair spermatogenesis and germ cell survival [26,27]. In particular, thermal stress during key developmental windows may have lasting impacts on testicular architecture and function.

Furthermore, while this study primarily focused on testicular architecture and function, the epididymis also plays a critical role in sperm maturation and fertility. Investigating epididymal changes in the context of cryptorchidism would offer additional insights into how testicular dysfunction impacts the entire male reproductive tract.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Due to the low incidence and sporadic occurrence of cryptorchidism in bulls, sample collection was constrained. Nevertheless, consistent patterns observed in ERK/CREB expression and histopathological features support the biological relevance of the findings. Future studies with larger cohorts will be necessary to confirm and extend these observations.

In summary, our findings highlight key structural and molecular alterations in cryptorchid testes, particularly involving the ERK and CREB signaling pathways. Continued investigation into broader molecular mechanisms and adjacent reproductive structures will be essential for advancing our understanding and potentially informing therapeutic strategies to mitigate reproductive deficits associated with cryptorchidism.
