Ginger supplementation and metabolic health: an umbrella review of meta-analyses of randomized trials
Authors: Vali Musazadeh, Mehrnaz Morvaridi, Seyed Mohammad Hosseini-Roknabadi, Mahsa Mahmoudinezhad, Ali Jafari, Farzad Shidfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGinger is recognized as a valuable therapeutic and non-pharmacological intervention. Given its widespread use, clarifying its efficacy in modulating metabolic profiles is essential. This umbrella review evaluated the available evidence on the effectiveness of ginger in improving metabolic parameters.MethodsA comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Google Scholar up to January 2025 to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses assessing ginger‐related metabolic health outcomes. Extracted data included the number of studies, sample size, participants’ mean age, dosage and duration of ginger administration, and effect sizes of reported outcomes. The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using the AMSTAR‐2 tool.ResultsA total of 36 publications, encompassing 296 trials and 43,570 participants, were included. Ginger supplementation was consistently associated with significant reductions in C-reactive protein (range of reported WMD: − 0.42 to − 1.00 mg/L) and interleukin‐6 (range of reported WMD: − 0.45 to − 2.26 pg/mL) across all included meta‐analyses. Likewise, measures of oxidative stress and antioxidant status; including malondialdehyde, glutathione peroxidase, and total antioxidant capacity showed significant improvement. Most meta‐analyses also reported favorable effects on lipid profiles triglycerides (range of reported WMD: − 0.61 to − 24.80 mg/dL), total cholesterol (range of reported WMD: − 0.33 to − 13.31 mg/dL), and LDL-C (range of reported WMD: − 0.30 to − 11.22 mg/dL), though results for high‐density lipoprotein were limited. In addition, fasting blood sugar (range of reported WMD: − 0.68 to − 18.81 mg/dL) and HbA1c (range of reported WMD: − 0.27 to − 1.66%), HOMA-IR (range of reported WMD: − 0.54 to − 1.67) and insulin (range of reported WMD: − 0.54 to − 1.69) improved significantly in most meta‐analyses.ConclusionOverall, this umbrella review demonstrates that ginger supplementation yields clinically relevant benefits across a range of health outcomes, particularly in reducing inflammation, improving glycemic and lipid profiles, and supporting weight management.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02150-y.Keywords: Ginger, Glycemic control, Insulin resistance, C-reactive protein, Triglycerides, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) has been used throughout human history to manage a wide range of conditions, including arthritis, rheumatism, nausea, and elevated blood pressure. Its primary biological effects include modulation of the immune system, inhibition of tumor formation, reduction of inflammation, prevention of cell death, lowering of blood glucose, improvement of lipid profiles, and alleviation of nausea [1]. The anti-inflammatory properties of ginger have been extensively investigated, and it is considered a safe adjunctive treatment for reducing inflammation, a key risk factor for numerous diseases, in both healthy individuals and those with chronic medical conditions [2]. The pungent phenolic compounds known as gingerols, present in the rhizome, are believed to be largely responsible for its pharmacological activity [3]. Gingerols and shogaols exert their effects through multiple molecular pathways, including inhibition of NF-κB signaling, activation of Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, modulation of AMPK activity to support metabolic regulation, antagonism of 5-HT3 receptors involved in nausea, and suppression of COX and LOX enzymes that reduce prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to decreases in inflammatory markers (e.g., tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), improvements in glycemic control, and favorable alterations in lipid profiles, thereby linking their molecular actions to the clinical outcomes observed [4–6].

The health benefits of ginger have been linked to both its nutritional and medicinal properties, with evidence drawn from pharmacological and epidemiological studies [5]. Outcomes examined in relation to ginger supplementation include fasting blood glucose (FBS), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), total cholesterol, and inflammatory markers such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, total antioxidant capacity (TAC), malondialdehyde (MDA), and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2).

Many meta-analyses have evaluated the effects of ginger therapy administered either orally or through topical application. While the medicinal properties of ginger have attracted considerable research interest, findings have been mixed: some meta-analyses have reported significant effects [2, 7, 8], whereas others have not [9–11]. These inconsistencies may be attributed to considerable heterogeneity among studies in terms of dosage, duration of intervention, routes of administration, study populations, and outcome measures. Despite the availability of numerous meta-analyses examining individual outcomes, a comprehensive synthesis that integrates findings across metabolic and inflammatory domains, while also evaluating methodological quality and grading the strength of the evidence is still lacking. This gap highlights the need for an umbrella review to consolidate existing evidence and provide a clear, evidence-based perspective. Accordingly, this umbrella review aimed to summarize and grade evidence from meta-analyses of randomized trials of ginger supplementation across metabolic and clinical outcomes.

Methods

This umbrella review of meta-analyses was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines [12]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD420251122514) and is available at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251122514.

Search strategy

International scientific databases PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were systematically searched for studies meeting the inclusion criteria. The search covered all records from inception of each database up to January 2025 and was limited to English-language publications. Both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant title/abstract keywords were used (Supplementary Table 1). Two independent reviewers screened all retrieved records against predefined eligibility criteria. Screening was performed in two stages: [1] titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance, and [2] full-text articles were reviewed in detail. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible studies were meta-analyses of randomized clinical trials investigating the effects of ginger supplementation on metabolic health outcomes, including inflammation, pain, diabetes, blood pressure, and lipid profiles. Studies were excluded if they were network meta-analysis, in vitro, in vivo, or ex vivo experiments; case reports; observational studies; or quasi‐experimental designs. Only articles published in English were included.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of eligible meta-analyses was independently assessed by two reviewers using AMSTAR 2 [13]. The assessment focused on key and critical domains, including protocol registration or a priori design, comprehensiveness of the literature search, duplicate study selection and data extraction, assessment and integration of risk of bias, and evaluation of publication bias. Overall confidence in the results of each meta-analysis was rated as high, moderate, low, or critically low according to AMSTAR 2 guidance.

Data extraction

From each included meta-analysis, the following data were systematically extracted: year of publication, total sample size, geographic location of included studies, characteristics of the study population (e.g., type 2 diabetes, obesity, pregnancy), number of primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) included, dosage and duration of ginger supplementation, and type of administration (e.g., powder, extract, capsule). Detailed descriptions of intervention and comparator groups were recorded, along with reported effect measures (e.g., mean difference, standardized mean difference) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The types of outcomes assessed were also documented, including primary metabolic outcomes (e.g., glycemic control, lipid profile, and blood pressure), secondary outcomes (e.g., inflammatory biomarkers and pain scores), and patient-reported outcomes. For pooled effect estimates, we extracted the values as reported by the original meta-analyses. Because this was an umbrella review, we did not perform any new statistical analyses. All pooled effect estimates, including those from fixed- or random-effects models, as well as measures of heterogeneity and small-study effects, were extracted exactly as reported in the original meta-analyses, without reanalysis or recalculation. When both fixed-effect and random-effects results were presented, the random-effects estimates were prioritized, in line with the approach used in the original studies. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy, consistency, and reproducibility. Additionally, when the same primary RCT was included in more than one meta-analysis, overlapping data were identified and managed to prevent double counting. In such cases, preference was given to the most recent and/or methodologically robust meta-analysis.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 2,327 studies were initially identified from the following databases: Web of Science (n = 324), PubMed (n = 439), Embase (n = 287), Cochrane Library (n = 509), and Scopus (n = 768). After removing 486 duplicates, the remaining 1,841 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 1,790 were excluded (1,264 due to unrelated title and abstract; 526 animal studies). The full texts of the remaining 51 articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 15 were excluded. Ultimately, 36 meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 43,570 patients were included in the final analysis. The study selection process and results are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 36 systematic reviews and meta-analyses investigating the effects of ginger (Zingiber officinale) supplementation were included in this umbrella review (Table 1 and Table 2). These studies assessed a wide range of clinical, metabolic, inflammatory, and patient-reported outcomes across various health conditions, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, dysmenorrhea, migraine, and nausea and vomiting associated with chemotherapy, surgery, and pregnancy.

The included meta-analyses encompassed between 3 and 45 primary studies, with sample sizes ranging from 58 to 1873 participants and a total of 43,570 participants across all studies [2, 4, 6, 14–44]. The duration of interventions ranged from 4 to 12 weeks, with ginger dosages typically between 400 mg and 2500 mg per day, administered either orally or topically. The mean age of participants varied between 34 and 61 years across studies. Across the included meta-analyses, the most frequently reported dosage of ginger supplementation was 0.5–2 g per day, administered predominantly in capsule form. Other forms included powder, tablets, topical gel (4%), and standardized extracts. The duration of interventions varied by health condition, with metabolic outcomes typically studied over 6 to 12 weeks, while analgesic or antiemetic applications often involved shorter courses of ≤ 10 days.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the 36 included systematic reviews and meta-analyses was evaluated using AMSTAR 2 (Supplementary Table 2). Key and critical domains assessed included protocol registration or a priori design (Q2), comprehensiveness of the literature search (Q4), duplicate study selection and data extraction (Q5–Q6), assessment and integration of risk of bias (Q9, Q12, Q13), and evaluation of publication bias (Q15). While most reviews clearly defined their research questions (Q1) and used appropriate meta-analytical methods (Q11), common methodological limitations included the absence of registered protocols, inconsistent duplicate processes, and insufficient consideration of risk of bias and publication bias in result interpretation. Based on performance across these domains and in accordance with the AMSTAR 2 confidence criteria, 18 meta-analyses were rated as moderate quality [6, 17, 18, 20, 21, 23–29, 31, 33, 35, 42, 43], 15 were rated as low quality [2, 11, 16, 19, 22, 30, 32, 34, 36–41, 44], and three meta-analyses were rated as critically low quality [4, 14, 15].

No review fulfilled all AMSTAR 2 critical domains. The downgrading of confidence was primarily driven by lack of protocol registration, insufficient integration of risk-of-bias assessments, and limited evaluation of publication bias. Most of the included meta-analyses did not apply the GRADE approach. However, four of the meta-analyses conducted a GRADE assessment, reporting the quality of evidence as moderate [45–47].

Effects on inflammatory biomarkers

Across 5 meta-analyses, ginger supplementation significantly reduced CRP (range of reported WMD: − 0.42 to − 1.00 mg/L) and TNF-α (range of reported WMD: − 0.85 to − 1.33 pg/mL) with moderate-to-low certainty. IL-6 was reduced in 3 meta-analyses (range of reported WMD: − 0.45 to − 2.26 pg/mL), with moderate certainty, but certainty was not formally assessed in most studies. Ginger supplementation was consistently associated with reductions in key inflammatory biomarkers, particularly CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6. Among the included meta-analyses, five reviews reported the effects of ginger on CRP levels, all showing statistically significant reductions (Table 3) [6, 14, 21, 24, 25]. For example, Abolfathi et al. [6] reported a decrease of − 0.42 mg/L (95% CI: − 0.78, − 0.05), while Mazidi et al. [24] and Morvaridzadeh et al. [25] reported larger reductions of − 0.69 mg/L (95% CI: − 1.00, − 0.39) and − 0.75 mg/L (95% CI: − 1.34, − 0.15), respectively. The most pronounced CRP reduction was observed in Jalali et al. [21] (–1.00 mg/L, 95% CI: − 1.53, − 0.53), followed closely by Askari et al. [14] (–0.80 mg/L, 95% CI: − 1.17, − 0.43), indicating a potentially robust anti-inflammatory effect across various populations and health conditions.

TNF-α, a key upstream cytokine in the inflammatory cascade, was assessed in three meta-analyses, all showing significant decreases following ginger supplementation. Askari et al. IL-6 levels were examined in three reviews as well. The most pronounced effect was reported by Askari et al. [14], showing a reduction of − 2.26 pg/mL (95% CI: − 4.00, − 0.52), followed by Jalali et al. [21] (–1.02 pg/mL, 95% CI: − 1.71, − 0.34). Although Morvaridzadeh et al. [21] assessed PGE2 and found a marginal decrease (–0.32, 95% CI: − 0.63, 0), bordering statistical significance. Morvaridzadeh et al. [25] examined ICAM and reported a minimal effect (–0.05, 95% CI: − 0.36, 0.26), which was not statistically significant.

Effects on oxidative stress and antioxidant status

Across 4 meta-analyses, ginger supplementation significantly increased total antioxidant capacity (TAC; range of reported SMD: 0.40 to 1.26) and reduced MDA; range of reported SMD: − 0.29 to − 1.45) with moderate-to-low certainty. Effects on GPx were large and significant in 2 studies (range of reported SMD: 1.64 to 1.93), whereas catalase showed a non-significant trend in only 1 study. Ginger supplementation demonstrated a consistent beneficial effect on oxidative stress and antioxidant defense mechanisms. As shown in Table 4, TAC significantly increased in all four included meta-analyses [10, 14, 21]. The largest improvement in TAC was reported by Askari et al. [14], with a mean difference of 1.26 (95% CI: 0.17 to 2.35), followed by Jalali et al. [21], who observed an increase of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.65 to 1.33). Morvaridzadeh et al. [10] and Sheikhhossein et al. [11] also reported favorable changes, although in the latter, the confidence interval slightly overlapped with zero (0.42, 95% CI: − 0.03 to 0.88), suggesting a borderline significance.

MDA, a widely recognized marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage, was reported in all four studies. [11], who observed a non-significant but positive trend (1.09, 95% CI: − 0.07 to 2.25).

Effects on lipid profile

Across 8 meta-analyses, ginger supplementation significantly reduced triglycerides (range of reported WMD: − 0.61 to − 24.80 mg/dL), total cholesterol (range of reported WMD: − 0.33 to − 13.31 mg/dL), and LDL-C (range of reported WMD: − 0.30 to − 11.22 mg/dL) with moderate-to-critically low certainty. HDL-C showed a small, inconsistent change (range of reported WMD: − 0.45 to + 2.87 mg/dL). Ginger supplementation was associated with favorable changes in lipid profile parameters, including reductions in TG, TC, and LDL-C, with more variable effects observed on HDL-C. As presented in Table 5, triglyceride levels were significantly reduced in all but one study. For example, Zhu et al. [31] reported the most pronounced effect (–24.80 mg/dL, 95% CI: − 36.06 to − 13.54), followed by Pourmasoumi et al. [27] (–17.60, 95% CI: − 29.32 to − 5.87) and Fakhri et al. [16] (–14.96, 95% CI: − 22.13 to − 7.79). Even studies with smaller effect sizes, such as Salih et al. [29], still found statistically significant reductions (–0.61, 95% CI: − 1.14 to − 0.08). Only one study, Ebrahimzadeh et al. [2], reported a non-significant effect on TG (–13.32, 95% CI: − 29.61 to 2.97), likely due to wider confidence intervals and fewer included trials.

Effects on glycemic control

Across 10 meta-analyses, ginger supplementation significantly reduced FBS (range of reported WMD: − 0.68 to − 18.81 mg/dL) and HbA1c (range of reported WMD: − 0.27 to − 1.66%) with moderate-to-low certainty. HOMA-IR improved in 6 meta-analyses (range of reported WMD: − 0.54 to − 1.67) and fasting serum insulin in 4 meta-analyses (range of reported WMD: − 0.54 to − 1.69), with no formal GRADE assessment specifically for insulin outcomes in any study. Ginger supplementation was associated with favorable effects on glycemic indices, including FBS, A1C, insulin, and insulin resistance measured by HOMA-IR. As illustrated in Table 6, significant reductions in FBS were reported across multiple meta-analyses. The greatest effect was observed by Ebrahimzadeh et al. [2], who reported a mean difference of − 18.81 mg/dL (95% CI: − 28.70 to − 8.92). Similar findings were reported by Garza et al. (2024) (–17.12, 95% CI: − 29.60 to − 4.64) [17], Zhu et al. (2018) (–15.28, 95% CI: − 26.26 to − 4.30) [31], and Jafarnejad et al. (2017) (–14.93, 95% CI: − 19.83 to − 10.04) [20], indicating a consistent glucose-lowering effect. Although Schumacher et al. [30] also reported a reduction (–13.68, 95% CI: − 32.85 to 5.49), the wide confidence interval crossing zero suggests non-significance. Only one study (Huang et al., 2019) [19] reported a non-significant increase in FBS (+ 1.38, 95% CI: − 0.53 to 3.30).

Effects on blood pressure and heart rate

Across 3 meta-analyses, ginger supplementation significantly reduced systolic blood pressure (range of reported WMD: − 4.2 to − 6.36 mmHg) and diastolic blood pressure (range of reported WMD: − 1.61 to − 2.12 mmHg) with moderate certainty. Heart rate was reduced in only 1 meta-analysis (WMD − 0.42 beats/min, 95% CI − 0.77 to − 0.08), with no formal GRADE assessment provided for this outcome. Ginger supplementation demonstrated modest yet statistically significant effects on blood pressure in adults, particularly in systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) measures. As shown in Table 7, Ebrahimzadeh et al. [2] reported a reduction in SBP of − 4.2 mmHg (95% CI: − 7.64 to − 0.77) and a DBP reduction of − 1.61 mmHg (95% CI: − 3.04 to − 0.18), both of which were statistically significant. Hasani et al. [18] observed a slightly larger effect, with SBP decreasing by − 6.36 mmHg (95% CI: − 11.27 to − 1.46) and DBP by − 2.12 mmHg (95% CI: − 3.92 to − 0.31).

[4] found a smaller, non-significant effect on weight (–0.43 kg, 95% CI: − 1.39 to 0.53), suggesting variability in outcomes may be due to population differences or intervention length.

[32] found virtually no change in BMI (0.00, 95% CI: − 0.11 to 0.10), which may reflect shorter follow-up periods or low baseline adiposity in their population.

Waist circumference and body fat percentage were less frequently assessed, but the available evidence suggests favorable trends. Rafieipour et al. [28] reported a significant reduction in WC (–1.04 cm, 95% CI: − 1.93 to − 0.15) and BFP (–0.873%, 95% CI: − 1.71 to − 0.03), indicating that ginger may contribute to reductions in central and total adiposity. Regarding WHR, Maharlouei et al. [23] found a statistically significant decline of − 0.49 (95% CI: − 0.82 to − 0.17), suggesting possible improvements in fat distribution, though this was not widely measured in other reviews.

Effects on liver enzymes

In the single available meta-analysis, ginger supplementation significantly reduced ALT (WMD − 0.43 U/L, 95% CI − 0.85 to − 0.07) with low certainty, whereas AST remained unchanged (WMD + 0.66 U/L, 95% CI − 0.81 to 2.12). Zhou et al. [32] assessed alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) as surrogate markers of liver function (Table 9). The results indicated a small but statistically significant ALT reduction, suggesting a potential hepatoprotective effect. In contrast, AST showed a non-significant change with a wide confidence interval crossing zero, indicating insufficient evidence for a consistent effect on this marker.

Effects on clinical outcomes

Across 8 meta-analyses for pain, ginger supplementation significantly reduced pain intensity (range of reported SMD: − 0.30 to − 7.88) with moderate-to-low certainty in dysmenorrhea, migraine, postoperative pain, osteoarthritis, and rheumatoid arthritis. Across 5 meta-analyses for nausea/vomiting, ginger significantly alleviated symptoms (range of reported SMD: − 0.25 to − 1.20) with moderate certainty in chemotherapy-induced (CINV), postoperative (PONV), and pregnancy-related nausea/vomiting (NVP). Functional/disability outcomes showed small improvements only in osteoarthritis (range of reported SMD: − 0.22 to − 1.61), mostly non-significant.

Pain Outcomes: Ginger supplementation has shown consistent effectiveness in alleviating various forms of pain across multiple clinical conditions. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a chronic inflammatory disease, DAS28 scores were significantly reduced (Amirah et al.,) [33], suggesting improved joint function and reduced systemic inflammation with 1500 mg/day ginger over 12 weeks. In osteoarthritis (OA), a degenerative condition with pain and stiffness, topical ginger (4%) showed large effects on pain (SMD = − 7.88), though Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) results were more modest or variable (Araya-Quintanilla et al., [34]; Bartels et al., [35]). In dysmenorrhea, which involves prostaglandin-mediated uterine cramping, multiple studies demonstrated consistent pain reduction. Daily et al. [38] and Chen et al. [36] showed substantial improvements in VAS scores. Negi et al. [41] further confirmed these effects on both pain severity and duration, although the latter did not reach statistical significance. In migraine, Chen et al. [37] showed significant pain relief, supporting ginger’s possible neuroinflammatory modulation. In postoperative patients, Novitasari et al. [44] found significant pain relief linked to ginger, possibly due to its anti-inflammatory and antiemetic actions. Heterogeneity in pain outcomes mainly reflects route of administration (topical vs. oral), duration (acute 3–4 days vs. chronic 12 weeks), and dose (400–1500 mg/day) (Table 10).

Discussion

This umbrella review demonstrates that ginger supplementation provides consistent benefits across multiple health outcomes, including reduction of nausea, pain, inflammation, and improvements in metabolic and anthropometric parameters. The most pronounced effects were observed in managing pregnancy-related nausea and dysmenorrhea, with additional evidence supporting its role in cardiometabolic health and oxidative stress modulation. Overall, ginger appears to be a safe and effective complementary therapy supported by findings from high-quality meta-analyses.

Cardiometabolic and anthropometric effects

Ginger supplementation (1000–2000 mg/day, 8–12 weeks) consistently improves cardiometabolic and anthropometric profiles in individuals with metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, or overweight/obesity, primarily through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and insulin-sensitizing mechanisms [2, 4, 16, 20, 22, 23, 27–29, 31]. Glycemic control is enhanced via AMPK activation and GLUT4 upregulation, reducing fasting blood glucose [48–55], HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR. Lipid parameters improve through HMG-CoA reductase inhibition and lipoprotein lipase activation, lowering TG, TC, and LDL-C [2, 4, 16, 20], though HDL-C effects remain inconsistent. Blood pressure declines via nitric oxide stimulation, calcium channel antagonism, and ACE inhibition [56, 57]; one study noted a small heart rate decrease, possibly reflecting improved autonomic balance [58, 59].

Anthropometric benefits include modest weight loss, BMI reduction, and decreases in waist circumference and body fat percentage, driven by thermogenesis (catecholamine-mediated), appetite modulation (leptin/ghrelin), and enhanced energy expenditure [60–65]. Inconsistencies (e.g., non-significant HDL-C, insulin, or anthropometric changes) likely arise from heterogeneity in baseline status, intervention duration, ginger formulation, sample size, or uncontrolled lifestyle factors. Stratified, standardized trials are needed to clarify dose-, population-, and duration-specific effects.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects

Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that ginger supplementation exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects across diverse clinical populations, including those with obesity, metabolic syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, and diabetes. Doses of 1000–2000 mg/day for 6–12 weeks, mostly in capsule form, were associated with significant reductions in CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6, particularly in chronic inflammatory conditions [6, 14, 21, 24, 25]. Concurrently, improvements in oxidative stress markers were reported, with increased TAC and GPx activity and reduced MDA levels, suggesting attenuation of lipid peroxidation and enhanced redox balance [11, 14, 21, 25].

Mechanistically, ginger bioactives such as [6]-gingerol, [8]-gingerol, and [6]-shogaol inhibit NF-κB activation and suppress COX and LOX pathways, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and eicosanoid synthesis [66–68]. Antioxidant effects are mediated by upregulation of endogenous defense systems, including SOD, CAT, and glutathione-related enzymes, alongside direct ROS scavenging [69, 70].

Despite these favorable findings, some inconsistencies were noted. For instance, not all reductions in IL-6 and MDA reached statistical significance across meta-analyses. In some cases, such as Morvaridzadeh et al. (2020), confidence intervals crossed the null line [25]. These discrepancies may reflect variability in study design, inconsistency in baseline levels of inflammation, limited sample sizes, or inadequate intervention durations in certain subgroups. Furthermore, heterogeneity in biomarker selection and measurement techniques could partially explain the variation in findings.

Hepatoprotective activity

Ginger supplementation shows preliminary evidence of hepatoprotective activity in NAFLD, based on a single meta-analysis reporting a small but statistically significant reduction in ALT, AST remained unchanged. The selective reduction in ALT may reflect ginger’s targeted effects on early hepatocellular stress and lipid peroxidation—common drivers of ALT elevation in NAFLD, through enhanced antioxidant defenses (e.g., upregulation of SOD and GPx) and improved hepatic fatty acid oxidation via PPAR-α activation [71, 72]. In contrast, AST, which is released during more severe or mitochondrial injury, may require longer intervention or greater disease progression to show detectable changes. The lack of effect on AST, combined with wide confidence intervals, underscores the preliminary nature of this evidence [73, 74]. Given that only one meta-analysis addressed liver enzymes, and with limited sample sizes, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger, well-designed trials in liver disease populations are needed to confirm hepatoprotective potential and clarify enzyme-specific mechanisms.

Antiemetic effects

Among the diverse therapeutic applications of ginger, its antiemetic properties are the most robustly and consistently supported by clinical evidence. Meta-analyses included in this review demonstrate that ginger significantly reduces both the severity and frequency of nausea and vomiting across multiple clinical contexts, including pregnancy-associated nausea and vomiting (NVP), postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV). These benefits are typically observed with daily doses ranging from 1000 to 1750 mg, most commonly administered in capsule form over relatively short intervention periods of 4 to 10 days [39, 40, 42–44].

Mechanistically, gingerols and shogaols antagonize 5-HT₃ receptors in both the gastrointestinal tract and central nervous system, thereby blocking serotonin-mediated vagal afferents to the vomiting center, a pathway central to CINV, PONV, and NVP [75, 76]. This directly accounts for the observed reductions in the nausea domain in our review. Furthermore, gingerols activate AMPK while inhibiting NF-κB signaling, attenuating gastroduodenal inflammation and indirectly contributing to lowered serum CRP levels consistent with the anti-inflammatory signals detected in meta-analyses [24, 77]. Ginger also enhances gastric emptying and motility independent of serotonergic pathways [78].

Analgesic effects

Ginger supplementation has shown consistent analgesic effects in both acute and chronic pain conditions, including RA, OA, dysmenorrhea, migraine, and postoperative pain. Effective doses are generally 1000–2000 mg/day, with durations of 4–12 weeks for chronic conditions and 3–7 days for acute pain [34–38].

In OA and RA, ginger reduced pain intensity (VAS, WOMAC, DAS28) and modestly improved physical function [33, 35]. Topical ginger (e.g., 4% extract) also showed benefits in OA [34]. For dysmenorrhea, ginger significantly reduced pain severity and duration, likely via prostaglandin suppression [36, 38, 41, 79]. In migraine, it may alleviate pain through modulation of neurogenic inflammation and inhibition of CGRP [37].

Mechanistically, ginger’s analgesic properties are primarily attributed to inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX-1/COX-2) and lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways, reducing proinflammatory prostaglandins and leukotrienes [80]. This dual COX/LOX inhibition directly links to both the pain domain and the observed reductions in plasma CRP in our review, given the pivotal role of these eicosanoids in systemic inflammation [81, 82]. Additionally, gingerols and shogaols exert central antinociceptive effects via TRPV1 modulation, while [6]-shogaol upregulates Nrf2, mitigating oxidative stress and potentially improving glycemia and endothelial function—mechanisms that may indirectly contribute to blood pressure regulation [83, 84]. Ginger may also modulate the endogenous opioid system, although evidence in humans remains limited [85, 86].

Despite these positive findings, substantial heterogeneity was noted. For instance, several trials reported confidence intervals crossing the null for WOMAC or functional outcomes in OA [34]. These likely arise from: (a) formulation variability (oral capsule vs. 4% topical gel), (b) dosing differences (400–1750 mg/day), (c) intervention duration (3 days to 12 weeks), (d) baseline risk (age > 15 years dysmenorrhea vs. mean 61.4 years severe OA), and (e) co-interventions (concurrent NSAIDs or rescue medication).

Supporting evidence from the included meta-analyses further clarifies these sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses in several reviews indicated that higher doses (≥ 1500–2000 mg/day) and longer durations (> 8–12 weeks) were associated with greater analgesic effects, particularly in chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis [33, 34]. Topical formulations showed larger effect sizes compared with oral administration in osteoarthritis pain [34], consistent with direct local anti-inflammatory action via COX/LOX inhibition. In dysmenorrhea, acute high-dose regimens (≥ 1000 mg/day for ≤ 7–9 days) yielded stronger pain reduction than lower chronic doses [36, 38, 41]. Although formal meta-regression for pain outcomes was limited in the included reviews, dose- and duration-dependent trends observed in broader inflammatory and metabolic outcomes [21, 23] likely extend to analgesic efficacy, given the shared underlying pathways involving prostaglandins and oxidative stress.

Strengths and limitations

This umbrella review represents a major strength through its hierarchical synthesis of 36 high-level meta-analyses, providing the highest level of evidence below individual patient data meta-analyses. By integrating evidence across > 15 diverse health domains (metabolic, inflammatory, cardiovascular, pain, nausea) and > 10,000 participants, it offers unparalleled breadth and generalizability, identifying consistent signals amid clinical heterogeneity. The inclusion of varying doses (400–2500 mg/day), durations (4 days–12 weeks), and formulations (oral, topical) enables practical insights into real-world applicability.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a review of secondary syntheses, findings depend on the quality and completeness of included meta-analyses, with potential propagation of primary study biases or incomplete outcome reporting. Primary trial overlap across meta-analyses could inflate precision estimates, though not quantified here. Publication bias was assessed in only a minority of reviews and may skew positive effects. English-only restriction risks language bias, potentially excluding relevant non-English meta-analyses. Methodological quality of primary studies varied, with inconsistent risk-of-bias assessment. Many meta-analyses pooled heterogeneous populations, durations, and baseline risks, contributing to high I². Bioactive content of ginger was poorly standardized, limiting formulation-specific conclusions. Some effects were non-significant in smaller/shorter trials, with limited subgroup analyses (age, sex, severity). Long-term safety remains unclear, especially in patients on anticoagulants or with bleeding disorders.

Clinical applications

Ginger may be considered a conservative adjunct in integrative care, with oral doses of 1000–2000 mg/day (capsules) for 8–12 weeks in metabolic or inflammatory conditions, or ≤ 7 days in acute nausea or pain; 4% topical gel is suitable for localized osteoarthritis. Patients with type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, dysmenorrhea, or chemotherapy-induced, postoperative, or pregnancy-associated nausea and vomiting are most likely to benefit. Avoid use near surgery or in bleeding disorders; exercise caution with anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs), antidiabetics (risk of hypoglycemia), or gallstones (choleretic effect); monitor in pregnancy if exceeding 1000 mg/day for > 4 days. Its low cost, accessibility, and favorable short-term safety support use where pharmacologic options are limited, but long-term data are lacking.

Recommendations for future research

Despite encouraging evidence for the therapeutic potential of ginger, several areas require further investigation to strengthen the current knowledge base. Future randomized controlled trials should prioritize methodological rigor, including adequate sample sizes, placebo control, and double-blinding, particularly in understudied populations and health conditions. Standardized ginger preparations with quantified gingerol/shogaol content are essential to reduce variability and enable replication. Dose–response trials and interventions longer than 12 weeks are needed for metabolic endpoints (glycemia, lipids, anthropometrics) to establish optimal regimens and sustained efficacy. Head-to-head comparisons with other nutraceuticals (e.g., curcumin, berberine, cinnamon) would clarify relative potency and synergistic potential. Mechanistic studies in humans are also warranted to confirm the molecular pathways through which ginger exerts its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, metabolic, and analgesic effects. Finally, future systematic reviews and meta-analyses should apply robust quality assessment tools and transparently report study overlap to minimize bias in pooled estimates and allow for more accurate clinical recommendations.

Conclusion

This umbrella review demonstrates that ginger supplementation provides clinically relevant benefits across a spectrum of health outcomes, with the most consistent evidence for reductions in CRP, FSI, HOMA-IR, SBP/DBP, TG, and TC. Strong and uniform benefits were also observed in antiemetic and analgesic effects, as well as glycemic control. These effects were generally achieved with doses of 1000–2000 mg/day administered over 6 to 12 weeks. However, evidence remains limited for liver enzyme modulation (based on a single meta-analysis) and HDL-C improvements (mixed findings across studies). Despite heterogeneity in outcomes and methodological variation, ginger appears to be a safe, accessible, and effective complementary intervention. Clinicians should exercise prudence in recommending ginger, particularly in patients on anticoagulants or with bleeding disorders, until long-term safety data are available. Future rigorously designed trials with standardized formulations and long-term follow-up are essential to further validate its therapeutic potential and inform clinical guidelines.
