Prenatal and Early Life Exposure to Ambient Air Pollutants Is Associated with the Fecal Metabolome in the First Two Years of Life
Authors: Elizabeth
A Holzhausen, Bridget N Chalifour, Youran Tan, Nathan Young, Fred Lurmann, Dean P Jones, Jeremy A Sarnat, Howard H Chang, Michael I Goran, Donghai Liang, Tanya L Alderete
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal and early life air pollution exposure has been linked with several adverse health outcomes. However, the mechanisms underlying these relationships are not yet fully understood. Therefore, this study utilizes fecal metabolomics to determine if pre- and postnatal exposure to ambient air pollutants (i.e., PM10, PM2.5, and NO2) is associated with the fecal metabolome in the first 2 years of life in a Latino cohort from Southern California. The aims of this analysis were to estimate associations between (1) prenatal air pollution exposure with fecal metabolic features at 1-month of age, (2) prior month postnatal air pollution exposure with fecal metabolites from 1-month to 2 years of age, and (3) how postnatal air pollution exposure impacts the change over time of fecal metabolites in the first 2 years of life. Prenatal exposure to air pollutants was associated with several Level-1 metabolites, including those involved in vitamin B6 and tyrosine metabolism. Prior month air pollution exposure in the postnatal period was associated with Level-1 metabolites involved in histidine metabolism. Lastly, we found that pre- and postnatal ambient air pollution exposure was associated with changes in metabolic features involved in metabolic pathways including amino acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism.Keywords: PM10, PM2.5, NO2, fecal metabolomics, early life

Introduction

Ninety-nine percent of the global population is exposed to levels of air pollution exceeding the recommendations of the World Health Organization.1 In the United States, there are important disparities in exposure to environmental toxicants, where communities of color and lower socioeconomic regions are disproportionately exposed to higher levels of air pollution.2−4 For example, during pregnancy, Hispanic and Latino mothers are 4.7 times more likely to live in areas of high air pollution compared with white mothers.4 Prenatal air pollution exposure has previously been associated with adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, asthma, and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.5−11 Additionally, air pollution exposure during childhood and early life has been linked with reduced cognition and increased risk of asthma, cardiovascular disease, obesity, hypertension, and pediatric irritable bowel disorder.12−16 Despite this, the mechanisms driving these relationships are not yet fully understood.

Particulate matter (PM) is an important component of air pollution, whose sources vary from natural to anthropogenic activities, including dust, agriculture, construction, and mining.17 PM is a mixture of solid and liquid particles, which vary in size and chemical composition, and can include nitrates, sulfates, elemental and organic carbon, organic compounds, biological compounds, and metals.18 The health implications of exposure to PM are directly related to particle size, where small particles with a diameter <10 μm (PM10) and fine particles with a diameter <2.5 μm (PM2.5) are of particular concern due to their ability to be inhaled and penetrate into the lung.17 Though larger particles (e.g., 5–10 μm) are likely to settle in the tracheobronchial tree, smaller particles (e.g., 1–5 μm) can penetrate more deeply into the lung and may eventually enter the bloodstream.19 Similarly, nitrogen dioxide (NO2) is a ubiquitous air pollutant that can originate from natural (e.g., forest fires, lightning) and anthropogenic sources, where the primary source of anthropogenic NO2 is fossil fuel combustion, including heating, power, and motor vehicles.

Exposure to ambient and traffic-related air pollutants can cause inflammation in the lungs and adipose tissue,20−22 and components of PM like transition metals and lipopolysaccharides can activate inflammatory pathways.23 This increased inflammatory response can lead to changes to the gut microbiome24 and to metabolic dysfunction, by inducing gut barrier dysfunction and a pro-inflammatory cascade.25 In addition to systemic inflammation, increased air pollution exposure may contribute to poor health by disrupting endocrine function, damaging DNA, and increasing oxidative stress.26,27 Metabolomic studies, the global identification and characterization of thousands of metabolic features associated with exogenous exposure and endogenous processes, can offer important insight into these mechanisms by providing information on the intermediate- and end-products of cellular processes and gene–environment interactions.28,29 Air pollution exposure during pregnancy and early life may be particularly impactful for health because the fetus or child is rapidly developing during these periods and is especially vulnerable to environmental toxicants.30 In particular, exposure to air pollution has been shown to alter the morphology of the placenta,31 and fine particulate matter (PM) may cross the placental barrier and enter the fetal bloodstream.32 Though the exposure routes differ after birth, the first years of life remain a sensitive period because children have increased ventilation rates compared to adults, contributing to increased levels of inhaled air pollution exposure.33 In addition to inhalation of air pollutants, the solid components of air pollution may directly reach the gut via ingestion, following deposition in the airways and mucociliary clearance.34,35 Additionally, young children may be especially vulnerable to air pollutants since this period is characterized by substantial growth and development, including biological systems related to metabolism, the motor cortex, and the immune system.36−38

While pre- and early postnatal studies are limited, short-term (9-days) PM2.5 exposure has been linked with urinary metabolites related to lipids, purines, neurotransmitters, and coenzymes in a crossover study of 45 healthy college students in Shanghai.39 Similarly, in a study of 54 college students, near-roadway air pollution has been linked with signaling pathways related to oxidative stress, inflammation, and nucleic acid damage repair.40 There are limited to no previous studies that have explored the relationship between air pollution exposure and fecal metabolomics. However, there is evidence that there is some consistency in metabolic perturbations across sample types.29 Therefore, this study adds to the current literature by assessing the feasibility of fecal metabolomics as a biomarker of human exposure to air pollutants. Furthermore, to date, only one study has examined the relationships between prenatal air pollution exposure and the infant metabolome. This study assessed neonatal blood spots from 241 children born in California and found that prenatal exposure to traffic-related PM2.5 was associated with changes in the neonatal metabolome that was related to lipid and amino acid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways.41 Oxidative stress and inflammation pathways contribute to outcomes including miscarriage, preeclampsia, low birth weight, and preterm birth.42−44 The aims of this study build upon the previous literature in examining whether pre- and/or postnatal air pollution exposure (i.e., PM10, PM2.5, and NO2) is associated with changes in the infant fecal metabolome during the first 2 years of life in Latinos, a population disproportionately exposed to air pollution. To accomplish this, we examined (1) Level-1 confirmed metabolites (i.e., features whose m/z and retention time matched authentic standards analyzed under identical conditions) and unidentified metabolic features to quantify the associations between prenatal air pollution exposure with fecal metabolite intensity at 1-month of age, (2) prior month postnatal air pollution exposure with Level-1 confirmed metabolites and unidentified fecal metabolic features from 1-month to 2 years of age, and (3) how postnatal air pollution exposure impacts the change over time of Level-1 confirmed fecal metabolites in the first 2 years of life.

Methods

Study Population

The Southern California Mother’s Milk Study is an ongoing, longitudinal study of 219 Latino mother–infant pairs.45 Between 2016 and 2019, participants were recruited from Los Angeles County maternity clinics. Participants were determined to be eligible for this study if they were at least 18 years old at the time of delivery and had a healthy, singleton birth. They were also eligible if they could read at a fifth-grade level in Spanish or English; did not have any diagnoses known to impact mental or physical health, nutritional status, or metabolism; and did not currently use tobacco or recreational drugs. The Institutional Review Boards of the University of Southern California, Children’s Hospital Los Angeles, and the University of Colorado Boulder approved the study procedures. All research was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from mothers/parents at the time of enrollment.

Study Design

Participants were enrolled approximately 1-month postpartum and attended follow-up visits at 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-months postpartum. Overall, 219 mother–infant dyads were initially enrolled in the Mother’s Milk Study. Socioeconomic status was estimated using a modified version of the Hollingshead index, as previously described.45,46 Questionnaires were used to assess infant feeding practices, which include the frequency of human milk and formula feeding as well as the age that solid foods were introduced. Infants were characterized as exclusively breastfed if a parent reported that the infant received no formula. Otherwise, infants were characterized as not exclusively breastfed. The healthy eating index (HEI) was calculated for infants following the introduction of solid food. HEI is a composite dietary measure that assesses whether dietary intake aligns with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans.47 Additional funding supported the analysis of approximately 600 fecal metabolomics samples in this cohort. Specifically, a subset of 127 participants were selected to undergo fecal metabolomics analysis to maximize the number of repeated fecal metabolome samples in the first 2 years of life. Overall, 101 infants completed fecal metabolomics sampling at all five time points, and the remaining 27 infants completed fecal metabolomics at four of the five time points (Supporting Figure 1).

High-Resolution Metabolomics

As previously described,48,49 OmniGene GUT kits were used to collect infant stool samples at 1-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24- months of infant age. Untargeted high-resolution analysis was carried out by the Emory Clinical Biomarkers Laboratory, as previously described.40,50 Briefly, stool samples were first added to ice-cold acetonitrile to precipitate proteins. Samples were stored on ice for 30 min, then centrifuged at 14 000g for 10 min, and stored at 4 °C until analysis. Extractants were analyzed in triplicate using high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Fusion).

Instrumentation and Analytical Conditions

As previously described,48,49 hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) (Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP HILIC column; 2.1 × 50 mm2, 2.6 μm particle size) with positive electrospray ionization (ESI) and reverse phase (C18) chromatography (Higgins Targa C18 2.1 × 50 mm2, 3 μm particle size) with negative ESI was used to enhance the coverage of the metabolic feature detection. HILIC analyte separation was conducted using water, acetonitrile, and 2% formic acid mobile phases following the subsequent gradient elution. The initial 1.5 min period consisted of 22.5% water, 75% acetonitrile, and 2.5% formic acid, followed by a linear increase to 75% water, 22.5% acetonitrile, and 2.5% formic acid at 4 min, followed by a final hold for 1 min. Analyte separation for the C18 chromatography column was conducted using water, acetonitrile, and 10 mM ammonium acetate mobile phases under the following gradient elution. The initial 1 min period consisted of 60% water, 35% acetonitrile, and 5% ammonium acetate, followed by a linear increase to 0% water, 95% acetonitrile, and 5% ammonium acetate at 3 min with a final hold for the last 2 min. Mobile phase flow rate was 0.35 mL/min for the first minute and was increased to 0.4 mL/min for the last 4 min for both the HILIC and C18 chromatography columns. LC-HRMS was run in full scan mode, with 120 k resolution, and had a range of mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) from 85 to 1275. Tuning parameters for sheath gas were 45 (arbitrary units) for positive ESI and 30 for negative ESI. For positive ESI, auxiliary gas was set to 25 (arbitrary units), and spray voltage was set at 3.5 kV, and for negative ESI, auxiliary gas was set to 5, and spray voltage was set to −3.0 kV. Internal standards included pooled stool and standard reference materials for human metabolites in the stool. These internal standards were added at the beginning and end of each 20-sample batch for quality control and standardization.

Metabolite Confidence and Identification

Data from HILIC positive ESI and C18 negative ESI were analyzed separately. Raw files were converted into .mzXML format, and metabolomic signals (i.e., metabolic features) were extracted and aligned using apLCMS with modification of xMSanalyzer for quality control and reduction of batch effects following instrument analysis.51,52 Metabolites with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 30 were removed, and intensities of metabolic features were averaged across triplicates. Metabolic features detected in <10% of samples were excluded. A total of 2037 features were removed from the HILIC column, with 1325 features removed due to a CV > 30 and 712 features removed with <10% detection. In the C18 column, a total of 1416 features were removed, with 835 removed due to a CV > 30 and 581 removed with <10% detection. Metabolic features were annotated and confirmed using the Metabolomics Standards initiative criteria.53 Level-1 confidence was assigned to confirmed features whose m/z and retention time matched the authentic standards analyzed with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) under identical conditions (within 10 ppm and 50 s).

Ambient Air Pollution

Ambient air pollution (i.e., PM10, PM2.5, NO2, O3) was modeled using spatial interpolation of monitoring station observations with an inverse distance-squared weighting algorithm (IDW2), which was based on residential address histories and up to four monitoring stations within 50 km of participants’ homes. Residential address histories were assessed via questionnaire at each visit and were geocoded using the Texas A&M Geocoder (http://geoservices.tamu.edu/Services/Geocode/). Monthly pollutant exposures were estimated using the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s Air Quality System (AQS, www.epa.gov/ttn/airs/airsaqs). Although monthly estimates of O3 were obtained, this cohort was not originally designed to assess the health effects of O3 exposure, and as such, there are limited exposure contrasts for O3 and NO2. Thus, O3 results are presented in the Supporting information.

Information regarding the date of conception was unavailable; therefore, prenatal air pollution exposure was estimated based on the 9 months of exposure prior to birth. We also calculated both long-term mean exposure to air pollutants and short-term prior month exposures. Mean postnatal exposure was the mean of all prior month air pollution estimates for an individual (i.e., the mean from birth to 24-months postpartum). Short-term exposures were the estimated air pollution exposure 1-month prior to each visit.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.0).54 Base R and RStudio were used to conduct descriptive analyses. Pearson correlations were used to estimate the associations between air pollution measures, using the corrplot package.55 We took three approaches to analyze the impact of air pollution exposure on Level-1 infant fecal metabolites. First, we assessed whether prenatal air pollution exposure was associated with log-transformed metabolite intensity at 1-month. Next, we assessed whether prior month air pollution exposure was longitudinally associated with log-transformed fecal metabolite intensity. Finally, we explored how postnatal air pollution exposures impacted the change over time of log-transformed fecal metabolite intensity and tested for evidence of interaction between air pollution exposure and sex. We analyzed the untargeted metabolic features by exploring the associations between prenatal air pollution exposure and log-transformed intensity of metabolic features at 1-month and postnatal prior month air pollution exposure and intensity of metabolic features from 1-month to 2 years of age.

To inform the adjustment set in our statistical models, we assessed whether pre- and postnatal air pollution exposures were associated with mother’s characteristics, including mother’s age, prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status, antibiotic exposure since delivery, and season of visit. We additionally examined whether pre- and postnatal air pollution exposures were associated with infant characteristics, including age, sex, delivery mode, breastfeedings and formula feedings per day, age of solid food introduction, healthy eating index, and antibiotic exposure since birth (Supporting Table 1). Overall, we found that age of solid food introduction and season of visit were significantly associated with prenatal air pollution exposure, and infant age and season of visit were significantly associated with postnatal air pollution exposure. These analyses informed our final adjustment set, which included infant age, infant’s sex, socioeconomic status, season of visit, mother’s age, and frequency of formula and breastmilk feedings per day. Because of the known relationship between O3 and NO2, all models that considered O3 exposure additionally adjusted for NO2 exposures. We are most interested in understanding how short-term fluctuations in air pollution exposure impact the fecal metabolome, since fecal metabolomics change on a relatively short time scale. Therefore, we adjusted for long-term air pollution (i.e., mean across all visits [1-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-months]) and reported findings related to short-term deviations (i.e., the difference in the prior month air pollution from the mean across all visits), which is consistent with previous studies that have examined the health effects of air pollution exposure in early life.15,56 Thus, to assess the association between short-term air pollution and fecal metabolite intensity, we additionally adjusted postnatal air pollution models for long-term mean air pollution exposure.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to analyze the associations between prenatal PM10, PM2.5, NO2, and O3 exposure with the logged intensity of both Level-1 confirmed metabolites and untargeted metabolic features assessed at the 1-month study visit while adjusting for confounders (i.e., infant age, infant’s sex, socioeconomic status, season of visit, mother’s age, and frequency of formula and breastmilk feedings per day) (eq 1). Next, we used linear mixed-effects models to estimate the relationship between prior month postnatal exposure to each individual air pollutant (i.e., PM10, PM2.5, NO2, O3) with the logged intensity of Level-1 confirmed metabolites and untargeted features longitudinally from the 1-month to 2-year study visit (eq 2). These models included a random intercept for participants to account for within-participant repeated measures and adjusted for mean air pollution exposure, infant age, infant’s sex, socioeconomic status, season of visit, mother’s age, formula and breastmilk feedings per day, and age of solid food introduction. As a sensitivity analysis, we next stratified prenatal models on infant feeding status (exclusively vs nonexclusively breastfed) to ensure results were not due to residual confounding by the feeding group.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Population characteristics are described in Table 1. Briefly, visits occurred at approximately 1-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-months of infant age. At the initial visit, 46% of infants were male, and 27% were born by cesarean section. Changes to non-time-varying characteristics in this population are due to participants with missing follow-up visits. For instance, at the 1-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month visits, there were 3, 11, 7, 4, and 1 participant(s) with missing fecal metabolomics, respectively (Supporting Figure 1). At the 1-month visit, parents reported that infants received an average of 6.6 human milk feedings per day (range: 0–8) and 2.4 formula feedings per day (range 0–8). On average, infants were introduced to solid food at 5.9 months (range: 2–12 months). At the baseline visit, mothers were 29 years of age on average; 27% had normal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), 35% had overweight, and 38% had obesity.

Overall, we extracted 11 345 unique features in the HILIC chromatography column and 8619 in the C18 chromatography column after quality control. Among these features, we were able to confirm the chemical identities of 143 metabolites from the HILIC chromatography column and 104 from the C18 chromatography column with Level-1 evidence (i.e., features could be matched to authentic standards with MS/MS under identical conditions using m/z and retention time).53

Pre- and Postnatal Exposure to PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 was Associated with Level-1 Fecal Metabolites in the First 2 Years of Life

Prenatal PM10 was associated with 17 Level-1 confirmed metabolites that were measured at the 1-month study visit (PBH < 0.2). For example, higher prenatal exposure to PM10 was associated with increased intensity of pyridoxamine, 4-hydroxy-phenylglycine/pyridoxal, thymidine, and β-alanine/sarcosine (Figure 2; a complete list of nonsignificant results is presented in Supporting Table 2). Pyridoxamine and pyridoxal are both metabolites involved in vitamin B6 metabolism, while thymidine and β-alanine/sarcosine are involved in pyrimidine metabolism.61 Prenatal PM2.5 was associated with the intensity of 27 Level-1 confirmed metabolites, including positive associations with the intensity of thymidine and β-alanine/sarcosine. In addition, prenatal PM2.5 was associated with lower tyrosine and 3-methoxy-4 hydroxymandeleate/vanillymandelate, which are involved in tyrosine metabolism.61 Prenatal exposure to NO2 was associated with the intensity of 37 Level-1 confirmed metabolites. For example, higher prenatal exposure to NO2 was associated with a lower intensity of tyrosine and 3-methoxy-4 hydroxymandeleate/vanillymandelate and with a higher intensity of thymidine, β-alanine/sarcosine, and 4-hydroxy-phenylglycine/pyridoxal. We also assessed whether prenatal O3 was associated with metabolite intensity at 1-month but did not find any statistically significant associations. As shown in Supporting Table 3, prenatal exposures were largely unrelated to fecal metabolites at subsequent follow-up visits (i.e., 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-months).

We next looked longitudinally across study visits from 1- to 24-months of infant age and found that prior month postnatal PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 was associated with 31, 1, and 5 metabolites confirmed with Level-1 evidence, respectively (Figure 3; a complete list of nonsignificant results is presented in Supporting Table 4). For example, higher prior month PM10 exposure was associated with a lower intensity of urocanate, β-alanine/sarcosine/alanine, histamine, and histidinol, metabolites that are involved in histidine metabolism.61 Higher prior month PM2.5 exposure was also associated with a lower intensity of urocanate. Additionally, higher prior month exposure to both NO2 and PM10 was associated with a lower intensity of 3-methoxytyramine and 4-pyridoxate, metabolites that are involved in tyrosine metabolism and vitamin B6 metabolism, respectively.61 Next, we examined whether the association between postnatal air pollution exposure and metabolite intensity changed as infants aged (Table 2). Overall, when examining Level-1 confirmed metabolites in the first 2 years of life, we found that the relationship between prior month NO2 and PM2.5 exposure and intensity of aminophenol (2, 3, or 4) and methylvanillate differentially changed as infants aged (as defined by study visit). Additionally, we found that hypoxanthine intensity was inversely associated with prior month O3 exposure (Supporting Table 4).

Associations between Air Pollution Exposure and Fecal Metabolite Intensity Differed among Male and Female Infants

Previous studies have shown differential effects of air pollution exposure by infant’s sex.57,58 Therefore, we examined whether the associations between pre- or postnatal air pollution exposure and the fecal metabolome differed among males and females (the Methods section eq 3). We did not find any statistically significant interactions between infant’s sex and prenatal air pollution exposure. However, postnatally, there were 6 metabolites whose association with PM10 differed by infant’s sex (Table 3). For four of the six metabolites (i.e., phenehylamine, 5-oxo-proline, hypoxanthine, and 3-methoxytyramine), there was evidence of qualitative interaction by sex. For example, among females, higher prior month PM10 exposure was associated with a decrease in hypoxanthine intensity. Conversely, in males, higher prior month PM10 exposure was associated with an increase in hypoxanthine intensity. β-alanine/sarcosine/alanine and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate diethyl acetal did not have a qualitative interaction by sex.

Fecal Metabolic Pathways Were Associated with Pre- and Postnatal Air Pollution Exposure

Incorporating all of the metabolic features in our data set (i.e., including those that could not be identified with Level-1 evidence), we analyzed putative pathways that were associated with air pollution exposure using Metapone.59 Prenatal (Figure 4) and postnatal (Figure 5) exposure to PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 was associated with metabolites enriched in major biological pathways, including amino acid metabolism, bile acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and xenobiotics metabolism. More specifically, prenatal PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 exposure were associated with several pathways related to amino acid metabolism, including cysteine and methionine metabolism, histidine metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. Prenatal exposure to air pollution was also associated with several lipid metabolism pathways that included fatty acid activation, fatty acid biosynthesis, and glycerophospholipid metabolism. Pathways associated with prenatal exposures largely overlapped with those linked with postnatal exposures; however, postnatal NO2 exposure was also associated with arachidonic acid metabolism, a pathway related to lipid metabolism.

Discussion

This current study extends our understanding of the fecal metabolome by uncovering significant associations between early life exposure to air pollution and the development of the fecal metabolome within this critical time frame. Our data reveals that exposure to ambient air pollutants, both before and after birth, is associated with changes in fecal metabolites and metabolic pathways that are linked to inflammation and oxidative stress. Notably, our analysis showed that higher prenatal exposure to air pollution was associated with higher intensity of several Level-1 confirmed metabolites at just 1-month of age, particularly those involved in amino acid and lipid metabolism. Furthermore, we observed that exposure to air pollution in the month prior to postnatal assessment was associated with changes in specific Level-1 confirmed metabolites, such as urocanate, histidine, and 4-pyridoxate. Lastly, our study suggests that exposure to air pollutants can alter the developmental changes over time of the fecal metabolome, affecting compounds like aminophenol and methylvanillate. These findings enhance our understanding of the potential mechanisms by which early life exposure to air pollution may exert long-term adverse health effects.

To our knowledge, only one previous study explored whether prenatal exposure to PM2.5 was associated with the early life metabolome.41 In this study, third trimester PM2.5 exposure was associated with Level-1 metabolites in newborn dried blood spots, including positive associations with hypoxanthine, sphingosine, arachidonic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid as well as negative associations with methylcobalamin and 3-hydroxybenzyl alcohol. In untargeted analyses, this study also found that PM2.5 exposure during the third trimester was associated with metabolic pathways related to fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid activation, glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, methionine and cysteine metabolism, and carnitine shuttle. In the present study, prenatal air pollution was not associated with any of the Level-1 confirmed metabolites identified in this previous work. However, we found that prenatal PM10 exposure was associated with metabolic pathways, including fatty acid activation, glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and methionine metabolism. We also found that prenatal PM2.5 exposure was associated with metabolic pathways, including fatty acid activation and methionine and cysteine metabolism, and NO2 exposure was associated with metabolic pathways, including fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid activation, glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, methionine and cysteine metabolism, and carnitine shuttle. Although the untargeted analyses are largely consistent, there are several differences between the studies, which may explain discrepancies in the Level-1 confirmed metabolite findings. For example, the current study analyzed fecal metabolites during infancy, while the previous study examined metabolites in newborn dried blood spots.

In this study, we found that prenatal exposure to air pollution was associated with increased abundance of metabolites related to pyrimidine metabolism. This finding is consistent with prior metabolomics studies conducted in adult populations, which have reported that pyrimidine metabolism is associated with exposure to traffic-related air pollution.29,62,63 Pyrimidine metabolism plays an important role in biochemical processes in the body, including nucleic acid damage and repair. Our findings, in concordance with previous studies,64−66 indicate that DNA damage may be one mechanism driving the adverse effects of air pollution exposure. We also found that increased prenatal air pollution exposure was associated with decreased intensities of metabolites related to tyrosine metabolism. In previous work, tyrosine metabolism during pregnancy has been linked with fetal growth restriction and preeclampsia.67,68 In adults, tyrosine metabolism is perturbed in association with air pollution exposure,66,69 and alterations to tyrosine metabolism are implicated in acute inflammatory responses.

Our study also identified several metabolites that were longitudinally associated with postnatal air pollution exposure. For example, prior month PM10 exposure was associated with decreased abundance of β-alanine/sarcosine/alanine, histamine, histidinol, and urocanate. Each of these metabolites is part of the histidine metabolism pathway.61 Histidine is the precursor to histamine, a critical inflammatory agent in immune responses.70,71 In a previous study of obese and nonobese women, histidine was inversely associated with oxidative stress and inflammation.72 Further, our finding that histidine is inversely associated with air pollution exposure is highly consistent with previous metabolomics studies.40,73,74

In this study, we observed differences in the association between fecal metabolomic intensity and PM2.5 and PM10. For example, postnatal PM10 exposure was associated with the intensity of 31 unique fecal metabolites, while postnatal PM2.5 exposure was associated with the intensity of one fecal metabolite. This may be due to differences in sources of particulate matter, since PM10 is more likely to be the result of suspended dust, including industrial dusts from construction or coal and oil combustion.75 Although PM2.5 can also result from coal and oil combustion, it can also be produced by transformation of NOx and SO2, high-temperature processes, smelting, and steel mills.75 Therefore, the differences observed between PM10 and PM2.5 in this study may be due to differences in source or chemical composition, impacting the biological effects of these pollutants. Future studies of this cohort will additionally examine the composition of each pollutant to provide further insight into the observed differences.

This study also observed differences in the metabolites and metabolic pathways identified by the Level-1 and untargeted analyses. This may be because Level-1 metabolites are more likely to be endogenous, while untargeted analyses provide a more holistic assessment of both host- and microbial-associated metabolic changes. Using fecal metabolomics, we are unable to separate host-associated from microbially associated metabolic changes. To address this limitation, our future work in this cohort will build on the present study by incorporating measures of the gut microbiome, which will provide further insight into microbial changes that are associated with metabolic perturbations. Additionally, though untargeted metabolomics analyses are beneficial in their ability to provide pathway information for metabolic features whose identity is unknown, they are limited in their ability to address the directionality of these relationships. Within a pathway, these tools do not distinguish between up- and down-regulated metabolites and instead report on perturbation within a pathway. However, untargeted metabolomics analyses remain a vital piece of hypothesis generation for future metabolomics studies, where untargeted metabolomics can aid in the design of studies that will use targeted metabolomics to comprehensively measure metabolites belonging to these pathways.

In this study, air pollution exposure was estimated using residential-based estimates. This method has several strengths, including low participant burden. However, because of the focus on residential addresses, there may be some misclassification due to time spent away from residences or changes to behavioral patterns. However, previous work has shown that the bias in these cases is likely toward the null hypothesis.76 Use of spatial interpolation also prevents us from assessing other sources of air pollutant exposure such as nonambient, localized sources, including environmental tobacco smoke (ETS). While mothers who smoked were excluded from the study, we cannot rule out exposure to ETS due to smoking by other household members. Additionally, spatial interpolation may have lower accuracy in estimating pollutants like NO2, which can vary significantly over small spatial scales.77 Future studies interested in measuring localized sources of air pollutants should consider alternate methods for assessing air pollution exposures. Additionally, the period prior to pregnancy may be an important exposure window with repercussions for infant development and growth.78 However, we were unable to assess prepregnancy air pollution exposure in this cohort because residential data prior to pregnancy was not collected. This study assessed exposure to PM10, PM2.5, NO2, and O3. We were unable to assess other air pollutants (e.g., NOx, elemental carbon). Therefore, an observed association in this study may not necessarily be causal and could be representative of multiple, correlated pollutants within a complex mixture. Future work in this cohort will include multipollutant models to account for the cumulative impact of exposure to multiple pollutants and will utilize speciation data on PM2.5 to further understand how components of air pollution impact the fecal metabolome.

In the present study, all analyses were adjusted for multiple testing using a 20% false discovery rate. Therefore, some of our results may be false positives. However, because previous work has not explored the associations between pre- and postnatal air pollution exposure with the developing fecal metabolome, this study’s goal was hypothesis generation. Further, we note that the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure may result in overpenalization of our adjusted p-values, due to the correlated nature of metabolomics data, and the selection of less-stringent p-value thresholds is common in metabolomics analyses.29,79,80

This study has several strengths. For example, this is the first study to explore the fecal metabolome to assess the impact of air pollution on infant health during pregnancy and the first 2 years of life. Fecal samples are particularly beneficial in young children, as they are noninvasive and easy to collect, and this study demonstrates the feasibility of using fecal metabolomics in biomonitoring studies involving infants. Our findings also demonstrate the feasibility of using fecal samples in air pollution health research, given the consistency of the pathways compared to those reported by previous serum, plasma, and blood-based metabolomics investigations. However, there are some limitations worth noting. For example, stool samples were collected using OMNIGene GUT kits. The use of these kits may lead to a reduction in the number of identified metabolites, compared to immediate freezing.81 However, other work has shown that biological effects such as individual variation outweigh technical effects like collection methods.82 Finally, this study was conducted on a fully Latino cohort, which may limit generalizability. However, focusing on Latino populations is of critical importance due to their disproportionately high burden of exposure to air pollution. Since participants were excluded based on preterm birth, low birth weight, and use of cigarettes or recreational drugs, there are additional limitations related to the generalizability of these findings to these subgroups of maternal–child pairs. For these reasons, future work is needed to replicate these findings in other populations.

The study of fecal metabolomics is in its infancy, and the current study, in conjunction with our previous work, shows that factors related to individual, age, and diet may all impact the developing fecal metabolome.48,49 This study adds to the growing literature by revealing that prenatal and early life exposure to air pollutants can also significantly influence the fecal metabolome. Notably, we found that both pre- and postnatal exposure to ambient air pollutants is linked to metabolites and fecal metabolomic pathways commonly associated with inflammation and oxidative stress. These insights suggest that the detrimental effects of early life exposure to air pollution may be partly attributable to its impacts on the developing fecal metabolome.
