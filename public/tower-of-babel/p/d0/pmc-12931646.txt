Virtual Trauma Sensitive Yoga Intervention Reduces Posttraumatic Stress Disorder, Depression, and Anxiety Symptoms Among Women Survivors of Sexual Trauma: A Pilot Study
Authors: Brooke McKenna, Ilana Ander, Ashanti J. Brown, Jasmine Irish, Abigail Powers, Ursula Kelly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Studies increasingly suggest Trauma Center Trauma-Sensitive Yoga (TCTSY) can reduce psychopathology among women who experienced sexual trauma. However, barriers inhibit in-person engagement in TCTSY interventions, particularly among underserved women.Methods:This single-arm pilot study examined the efficacy of a virtual 10-week TCTSY intervention for 27 sexual trauma survivors seeking care at a public safety-net hospital. Women self-reported posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety symptoms preintervention and 2-weeks and 3-months postintervention.Results:Women who completed seven or more sessions experienced a reduction in PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms following the intervention (tavg = −2.36, pavg = 0.035) with effects largely sustained at the 3-month follow-up (tavg = −2.16, pavg = 0.054). These effects were weaker when including women who completed six sessions (tavg = −2.09, pavg = 0.065), suggesting greater symptom reduction with increased session attendance (i.e. “dose effects”) for a virtual TCTSY treatment.Conclusion:Overall, results from this study provide preliminary but promising evidence that a brief telehealth intervention can contribute to measurable improvements in trauma-related outcomes for underserved women. Given the affordability and accessibility of virtual TCTSY relative to other trauma interventions, these findings may hold important implications for public health and healthcare policy, including support for bolstered virtual healthcare infrastructure and the integration of trauma-informed yoga interventions into outpatient mental healthcare practices.

Introduction

Sexual assault is a major public health issue, with over half of women (53%) experiencing sexual violence and one in four women experiencing completed or attempted rape in their lifetime.[12] Survivors face risks of trauma-related psychological symptoms, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety.[3] Historically, marginalized women experience disproportionately higher rates of sexual assault and mental health challenges than their more privileged counterparts.[4] This underscores the need for accessible, trauma-informed interventions for these populations.

Traditional PTSD treatments, such as cognitive processing therapy, eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, and prolonged exposure, have proven effective in clinical settings.[5] However, these therapies often show low acceptability and high dropout rates, especially in marginalized groups.[6] These treatments tend to focus on cognitive aspects of trauma, neglecting the embodied symptoms experienced by sexual assault survivors. Thus, new approaches are needed to address both mental and physical aspects of trauma.

Trauma Center Trauma Sensitive Yoga (TCTSY), a Hatha-yoga-based intervention, emphasizes interoceptive awareness, empowerment, and safety for individuals with PTSD. Clinical trials have shown TCTSY to be effective in reducing PTSD, anxiety, and depression among sexual assault survivors.[78] Despite TCTSY’s growing empirical support, there is limited research on the effectiveness of virtual TCTSY, particularly for underserved women who often face barriers to in-person care. With the rise of telehealth, virtual interventions present an opportunity to address barriers such as cost, accessibility, and logistical challenges.[910]

The current single-arm pilot study examines the preliminary efficacy of a virtual TCTSY intervention for PTSD, anxiety, and depression in underserved women with a history of sexual trauma. By exploring the feasibility and effectiveness of a trauma-informed telehealth intervention tailored to address the needs of a historically marginalized group, this study fills a critical gap in the literature while informing the utility of expanding holistic mental healthcare for sexual trauma survivors.

Methods

Participants

Participants were sampled from a database of hospital patients who indicated a history of sexual trauma. Initial recruitment of participants occurred in the emergency department, primary care, and gynecology and obstetrics waiting rooms at an urban, safety-net hospital in the Southeastern United States. Individuals were randomly approached, screened for PTSD using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), and, if eligible, consented to be added to the research database and contacted for future research studies. Inclusion criteria for recruitment into the current study included self-reported gender identity of “woman,” history of sexual trauma, clinically significant PTSD symptoms (per the PCL-5), and availability for scheduled yoga sessions. Exclusion criteria included active suicidality and medical conditions that would be exacerbated by participating in yoga. The final sample included 27 women between 18 and 64 years old (M = 38.7). Participants were largely from racially and/or socioeconomically marginalized backgrounds; 20 participants self-identified as Black, 18 had an annual household income under $50,000, and only six participants held a college degree or higher.

Procedure

Study procedures were approved by Emory University’s Institutional Review Board (STUDY00004835) and informed consent was obtained for each participant prior to enrollment. The 10-week intervention consisted of weekly 60-min virtual yoga sessions delivered on a HIPAA-compliant video platform. The intervention was administered in two cohorts to accommodate differences in participant schedules, with one held in the morning and the other in the late afternoon. The same two TCTSY-certified yoga facilitators led sessions for both cohorts using the TCTSY protocol developed by David Emerson and Jennifer Turner.[11] Sessions were not formally rated for fidelity; however, facilitators consulted with David Emerson weekly to address questions, discuss experiences, and ensure fidelity to the standardized protocol. Participants’ PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms were assessed at three time points: preintervention/baseline (T1), 2 weeks postintervention (T2), and 3 months postintervention (T3).

Outcomes measures

Symptoms of PTSD were assessed using the PCL-5,[12] a well-validated (Cronbach’s α =0.83–0.97) 20-item self-report questionnaire with a 5-point Likert scale (0 – not at all to 4 – extremely). Anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7),[13] a well-validated (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) seven-item self-report questionnaire with a 4-point Likert scale (0 – not at all to 4 – nearly every day). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-15),[14] a well-validated (Cronbach’s α = 0.80) 15-item self-report questionnaire with a 3-point Likert scale (0 – not at all bothered to 2 – bothered a lot); the PHQ-15 assesses both primary depressive symptoms and secondary somatic symptoms, which were of interest in the current study given the somatic focus of the intervention. Each of the three outcome variables was analyzed as continuous summed scores.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the jamovi project (2025). jamovi (Version 2.6) [Computer Software, Retrieved from https://www.jamovi.org]. Linear mixed-model regression analyses were used to examine the change in PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms from T1 to T2 and T3. Three sets of analyses were conducted for each outcome, with: (1) participants who completed at least seven TCTSY sessions, (2) participants who completed at least six TCTSY sessions, and (3) all enrolled participants, comparing “completers” (i. e., participants who completed at least six/seven sessions) to “noncompleters” (i.e. participants who completed fewer than six/seven sessions, respectively). The seven-session cutoff follows the TCTSY protocol for treatment completion, while the six-session cutoff was based on significant dropout after six sessions in the current study. Although the sample size was too small to statistically analyze dose effects, comparing these groups offered a preliminary look at whether six versus seven sessions are sufficient for symptom reduction. Unstandardized effect estimates are reported alongside P values to enable clinical interpretation of observed changes.

Results

Complete data were available for all variables. At T1, all but one participant (n = 26; 92.6%) met the threshold for clinically significant PTSD (PCL-5 >30); one participant with a PCL-5 >30 at screening reported subclinical symptoms at T1. Most participants also met the threshold for a moderate-to-severe depressive disorder (n = 23; 85.2%) and moderate-to-severe generalized anxiety disorder (n = 20; 74.1%). Of the 27 women who participated in T1, 12 completed six or more sessions and 9 completed seven or more sessions. Of the 27 women who participated in T1, 10 completed seven or more sessions, 3 completed six sessions, and 14 completed five or fewer sessions.

Did symptoms decrease among women who participated in seven or more trauma sensitive yoga sessions?

In women who completed seven or more sessions, PTSD symptoms decreased significantly from T1 to T2 (b = −12.50, standard error (SE) =5.10, t = −2.45, P = 0.028) and then moved back toward T1 levels at T3 (b = −10.10, SE = 5.10, t = −1.98, P = 0.067). Depression symptoms showed a similar but nonsignificant trend (P < 0.10), such that symptoms decreased from T1 to T2 (b = −8.38, SE = 4.00, t = −2.09, P = 0.055) and then increased at T3 (b = -7.75, SE = 4.00, t = −1.94, P = 0.073). Generalized anxiety symptoms demonstrated a sustained decrease, with a significant decline in symptoms from T1 to T2 (b = −5.25, SE = 2.06, t = −2.55, P = 0.023) that remained stable at T3 (b = −5.25, SE = 2.06, t = −2.55, P = 0.023). Results are summarized in Supplemental Table 1. We illustrate in Figure 1 that these symptom changes resulted in clinically-meaningful decreases in both depressive and anxiety symptoms, with depressive symptoms moving from above to below the clinical threshold and anxiety symptoms moving from moderate to mild. Although PTSD symptoms remained above the clinical threshold, participants experienced a clinically significant change from T1 to T2 (12-point reduction) and a reliable change from T1 to T3 (9-point reduction) according to prior classifications of minimally clinical important difference for the PCL-5.[15]

Did symptoms decrease among women who participated in six or more trauma sensitive yoga sessions?

With participants who attended only six sessions added to the analytic sample, PTSD symptoms did not significantly decline from baseline to T2 (b = −7.83, SE = 4.40, t = −1.78, P = 0.089) or T3 (b = −7.19, SE = 4.53, t = −1.59, P = 0.13). Depression symptoms did significantly decline from T1 to T2 (b = −8.58, SE = 2.94, t = −2.92, P = 0.008) and increased at T3 but remained significantly lower than at T1 (b = −6.71, SE = 3.13, t = −2.14, P = 0.044). Generalized anxiety symptoms demonstrated a continuous decline, with a significant reduction in symptoms from T1 to T2 (b = −3.33, SE = 1.56, t = −2.14, P = 0.045) and a further reduction at T3 (b = −4.19, SE = 1.66, t = −2.53, P = 0.02). Results are summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

Did “completers” show a greater reduction in symptoms relative to “noncompleters?”

We then examined whether the symptom reductions observed for “completers” were statistically greater than symptom improvements for “noncompleters.” We ran two sets of analyses; the first with a completion threshold of seven sessions and the second with a threshold of six sessions. Interaction results are provided in Table 1. With completers defined as those participating in seven or more sessions, PTSD symptoms trended lower at T2 in completers versus noncompleters (P < 0.10). Otherwise, neither PTSD nor depression symptoms declined more significantly in completers than noncompleters. However, GAD symptoms were significantly lower at T3 in completers than in noncompleters (P < 0.05). These results remained similar when defining completers as those participating in six or more sessions, although the reduction of GAD symptoms at T3 for completers was no longer significantly different than for noncompleters.

Discussion

This single-arm pilot study explored the efficacy of virtual TCTSY in reducing PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms among underserved, predominantly minoritized women with a history of sexual trauma. Women who completed seven or more sessions showed significant reductions in PTSD, depression, and anxiety from pre- to 2 weeks postintervention. PTSD symptoms decreased while remaining clinically significant, depressive symptoms decreased below the threshold of clinical significance, and anxiety symptoms decreased from moderate to mild. These findings are consistent with a recent in-person TCTSY intervention study with women veterans who experienced military sexual trauma, in which women experienced a significant reduction in PTSD symptoms but remained in the clinically significant range.[7]

We found anxiety symptoms remained lower 3 months postintervention, while PTSD and depression symptoms approached preintervention baselines. Exploratory interaction analyses also indicated that anxiety, but not PTSD or depression, symptom improvement was significantly greater in completers than noncompleters. Unlike findings from in-person TCTSY interventions, which show a sustained reduction in PTSD symptoms,[16] our findings suggest PTSD and depressive symptoms could be receptive to a virtual TCTSY intervention but may require longer or more intensive treatment to enact improvements that sustain after completion. Several factors that differentiate telehealth from in-person interventions may explain these outcome differences. Participants engaging in virtual TCTSY were often in their homes, which varied in noise level, availability of space, and absence of distractions. While participants were asked to find a quiet and private space for the sessions, facilitators observed that participants were occasionally confronted with distractions, both in the home and due to technological issues, that may have interfered with the mechanisms through which TCTSY reduces symptoms (e.g., mindfulness[17]). It is also possible that participants felt less connected to their counterparts via telehealth, which may reduce intervention efficacy given prior evidence that TCTSY may exert benefits through feelings of normalization, solidarity, and relational connectedness with other participants.[18] Nevertheless, as more traditional CBT-based treatments for PTSD and depression show patterns of relapse and recurrence can be prevented with continuation of treatment,[19] it is likely that the benefits of virtual TCTSY would also show greater sustainment with longer or more intensive treatment.

Additionally, our findings suggest dose effects of virtual TCTSY. Women completing seven or more sessions showed greater reductions in PTSD and anxiety symptoms compared to those who completed even one fewer session. The reduction in depressive symptoms was significant only for those completing six sessions, though this may reflect statistical power limitations rather than a true dose effect. Overall, the minimum number of sessions for symptom reduction appears consistent across in-person and virtual TCTSY interventions.

Our results are preliminary and must be interpreted in light of limitations. The study lacked a randomized controlled trial design, and small sample size limited the ability to adjust for covariates. Moreover, participants varied in PTSD severity, age, physical mobility, prior yoga experience, access to a calm and quiet space, and other factors that could moderate the impact of the virtual TCTSY intervention. Future research would benefit from investigating intervention efficacy in larger samples that could discern individual differences that may influence TCTSY treatment response.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, this is the first evidence suggesting that at least seven sessions of virtual TCTSY can reduce PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms in underserved women with a history of sexual trauma. Given the accessibility challenges of traditional trauma treatment, our findings provide promising evidence that a short, affordable, and accessible telehealth intervention can contribute to measurable improvements in trauma-related outcomes for underserved women. These findings may hold important implications for public health and healthcare policy, such as support for bolstering virtual healthcare infrastructure and integrating trauma-informed yoga interventions into outpatient mental healthcare practices.
