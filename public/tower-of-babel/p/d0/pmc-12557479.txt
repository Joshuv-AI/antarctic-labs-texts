A protein dynamics–based deep learning model enhances predictions of fitness and epistasis
Authors: Ngoc Huynh, I. Can Kazan, Jin Lu, Bethany Kolbaba-Kartchner, Jeremy H. Mills, S. Banu Ozkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificancePredicting the effects of multiple mutations on protein function is challenging due to the intricate interplay between residues. Machine learning has advanced these efforts, but traditional neural networks still struggle to capture complex epistatic interactions, especially when trained on deep sequencing data covering limited mutational subsets. Here, we introduce a dynamics-based graph convolutional neural network that integrates protein dynamics as prior knowledge. Without training on experimental epistasis, our model outperforms existing methods in predicting epistasis and the effects of multiple mutations on protein function, including those distal to the active site. This approach can accurately predict protein function and has applications in protein design, namely, the ability to modulate protein activities by targeting mutations to previously hidden, allosterically important residues.

Proteins are highly versatile molecules whose functions in biology are correlated with their three-dimensional structures. Over the last few decades, experimental techniques including NMR, X-ray crystallography, and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) have led to the determination of structures of over 200,000 proteins. Simultaneously, advancements in the fields of DNA sequencing and high-throughput screens of protein function have generated a significant amount of biological data that have facilitated efforts to better understand the relationship between point mutations in proteins and their impacts on protein function. These data have led to a more thorough understanding of natural evolutionary processes, improved our ability to diagnose genetic diseases, and enhanced our ability to rapidly engineer protein variants with desirable properties (1, 2). As the amount of protein data has grown, machine learning [particularly deep learning approaches that use language models (3–5) and other neural networks (6, 7)] has emerged as a powerful tool for identifying key determinants within the complex relationship between a protein’s sequence and its function. By inferring the intricate patterns and abstract features of proteins directly from the raw sequence information, deep learning approaches have proven their ability to predict functional consequences of point mutations including the identification of disease-causing variants (8, 9). Because such approaches seamlessly handle massive quantities of data, they enable the training of sophisticated models that appear to be extensible to diverse protein families (10, 11).

Despite the successes of deep learning models in predicting the impact of point mutations on activity or function, one major challenge lies in modeling mutational epistasis, which is the nonadditive effects resulting from simultaneous mutation of pairs of residues. Fully characterizing all single point mutations in a given protein is both time consuming and expensive, and only becomes more daunting when multiple mutations are considered simultaneously. Thus, even high-throughput experimental approaches such as deep mutational scanning typically focus on exploring mutations in groups of fewer than three residues at a time (12). As a result, machine learning models trained on these datasets often struggle to effectively capture effects arising from epistasis (13).

To address the scarcity of curated data in protein function prediction, several recent studies (1, 14) have incorporated physics-based features including protein dynamics into machine learning models. The influence of dynamics on a protein’s function has been proposed as a critical factor in explaining cases where function cannot be explained by static structure alone (15–17). For example, point mutations rarely result in dramatic alterations to the overall three-dimensional structure of a protein yet can have profound effects on its activity. That these functionally altering point mutations can also be found very far from crucial functional residues (e.g., enzyme active sites or substrate binding sites) is equally confounding. One way these “distal” mutations might impact protein function is by altering local networks of residue–residue interactions, which then propagate throughout the protein, ultimately altering long-distance conformational dynamics (18, 19). While multiple computational methods have sought to use the dynamic attributes of proteins to gain functional inferences (16, 20), systematically integrating the conformational dynamics inherent in proteins into deep learning frameworks as input features is challenging for multiple reasons: First, accurately sampling the conformational space of a target protein is difficult. Second, sampling the dynamic signatures of all mutational variants is computationally expensive when traditional approaches (e.g., molecular dynamics simulations) are used.

In this work, we addressed these challenges by leveraging an asymmetric version of the Dynamic Coupling Index metric (DCIasym) that was previously developed in our laboratory (21) to construct a connected graph based on protein dynamics. In contrast to the established dynamics-based deep learning approaches (1, 22), our method utilizes this dynamic metric not merely as an input feature, but as a foundational element in the architecture of our deep learning model; this enables a more efficient and comprehensive analysis of the relationship between protein dynamics and protein function. The DCIasym metric can rapidly assess the lopsided influence that each member of a residue pair has on the other’s dynamic properties and, in turn, provide information regarding asymmetrical dynamic allosteric networks that might exist within a protein. A better understanding of these networks would allow us to build a protein dynamics–based graph neural network.

Here, the DCIasym metric was used to identify dynamic allosteric networks within well-characterized proteins that were in turn used to build a “dynamics aware” graph convolutional network (GCN). We then evaluated the performance of our GCN model on deep mutational scanning datasets from four proteins with diverse functions, ranging from protein–protein interaction to various enzymatic activities. Our results suggest that our approach outperforms both Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architectures by capturing epistatic effects more effectively. We also conducted a blind prediction study to validate and compare our GCN model’s performance against the two other architectures using TEM-1 β-lactamase as our model protein. To assess our GCN’s predictive power, we generated a series of TEM-1 mutants using the Rosetta computational protein design software (23, 24). Our design strategy targeted residues that were both distal to and dynamically coupled to the TEM-1 active site. The sequences of our designed proteins served as inputs to our GCN, CNN, and MLP models, which were used to predict the mutants’ activities against the β-lactam antibiotic ampicillin. Experimental characterization of multiple mutants demonstrated that our GCN model outperformed both MLP and CNN in terms of prediction accuracy and overall performance when high-order epistatic effects were considered. These results suggest that our dynamics-based GCN model could have far-reaching implications in better modeling epistatic relationships in proteins as well as providing a pathway for the rational design of proteins with enhanced functions.

Results and Discussion

Capturing Allosteric Networks Using DCI Asymmetry.

Multiple studies (25, 26) have suggested that the behavior of proteins is often influenced by regions far from, but coupled to functionally important residues through dynamic interaction networks. Thus, understanding the communication pathways between functional sites (e.g., catalytic or ligand binding residues) and distal regions of a protein is crucial for unraveling the complex dynamics that underlie protein activity (1, 27, 28). In this study, we used the Dynamics Coupling Index [DCI, a computational method developed in our laboratory based on perturbation response analysis (29, 30)] to rapidly identify networks of communication within proteins and explore long-distance dynamic coupling of catalytic residues within TEM-1 β-lactamase (Methods).

It is important to note that dynamic coupling between residues is not always symmetric, which can allow for directional communication to occur in certain cases. To account for this, we previously developed an asymmetric version of the DCI metric (DCIasym) (21), which is calculated by finding the difference between DCI values of the two positions being compared. Observing asymmetry in dynamic coupling suggests that one residue exerts dynamic control over the other. The significance of asymmetric dynamic control in establishing allosteric networks that collectively modulate the dynamics of the protein as a whole has been previously demonstrated for the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) (21). In this study, we identified two classes of residues that appeared to have functional importance in DHFR: residues that influenced the dynamics of catalytic residues and residues whose dynamic signatures were influenced by the catalytic residues; we termed these sets of residues “controller” and “controlled” residues, respectively. Interestingly, in DHFR, residues classified as “controllers” exhibited peaks of positive relative fitness when mutated; we interpret this to indicate that mutations to “controller” residues might enable subtle modulation of enzymatic activity. In contrast, mutations to “controlled” residues tended to have predominantly detrimental effects on DHFR activity.

To explore whether similar dynamic control networks were present in TEM-1, we applied DCIasym to the enzyme and classified positions that either influence the dynamics of the catalytic residues or were controlled by the catalytic residues as controller or controlled residues, respectively. Fig. 1A illustrates that each of the active site residues interacts with a distinct set of controller and controlled residues, resulting in varied dynamic impacts. Notably, we observed negative DCIasym values for the catalytic nucleophile S70 in the Ω-loop of TEM-1 (Fig. 1A). The Ω-loop comprises a region of TEM-1 from residue 164 to 179 and has previously been suggested to have profound impacts on the catalytic ability of this enzyme (31, 32). This DCIasym profile suggests that specific mutations within the Ω-loop could positively influence the catalytic cycle of TEM-1. This aligns with previous studies showing that mutations in the Ω-loop reorganize the hydrogen-bond networks involving water molecules and residue S70, leading to an expansion of the active site region and broader substrate specificity (31, 32). Our findings highlight the role of Ω-loop as a dynamic controller that modulates enzyme activity through its influence on catalytic residues.

Consistent with our previous analysis of DHFR (21), controller residues exhibit peaks of positive relative fitness when mutated, while mutations to controlled residues frequently result in diminished function. Thus, our analyses of both DHFR (21) and TEM-1 suggest that an intricate dynamic network exists in these enzymes, and that controller residues play a critical role in orchestrating the fundamental dynamic processes. This points to the potential for applying a dynamics-based framework within a neural network architecture using controller residue connections to enhance the accuracy of predictions related to the functional outcomes of mutations in TEM-1 and other enzymes.

Encoding Dynamic Neighbors Defined by DCIasym for a Graph Neural Net Architecture.

Our analyses of allosteric networks in TEM-1 and DHFR using DCIasym suggest that integrating protein dynamics into a graph neural network could improve the modeling of mutations in enzymes that alter enzymatic function through allostery even in the absence of exhaustive mutational data. Traditional GNN models (specifically graph convolutional networks, GCNs), focus on gathering information from nearest neighbors within a 3-D structure using a predefined distance cutoff (6) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, we propose a network construction where each residue is connected to a specified set of other residues whose dynamics influence it, rather than connecting each residue to all others based solely on spatial interactions (Fig. 2B). This approach allows us to incorporate functionally crucial protein dynamics through long-distance allosteric networks, thereby facilitating training solely on protein sequence information to better estimate the effects of mutations on protein function.

A schematic representation of the GCN architecture. (A) Traditional graph construction links residues to spatial neighbors within a cutoff distance (≤7 Å), capturing solely structural relationships. (B) Our dynamics-based graph construction uses the DCIasym to connect residues to their primary allosteric controllers, as indicated by arrows whose sizes indicate the asymmetric nature of the coupling between the highlighted residues. This approach reflects functional regulatory relationships. (C) Our workflow for the dynamics-based GCN is shown. Input features including amino acid embeddings (one-hot encoding and physicochemical properties) and local interactions (covalent neighbors), are processed through a CNN layer. A GCN layer then incorporates dynamic controller networks derived from DCIasym updating residue embeddings based on allosteric regulation. The output from the GCN is passed through a fully connected layer to predict the function score.

To construct this network, we employ a two-step process: First, our DCI metric is systematically applied to each residue in the protein to identify all other residues to which our target residue is dynamically coupled. Second, we use DCIasym to enumerate which of the aforementioned residues exert lopsided dynamic control over the target residues (Fig. 1A). As mentioned above, such controller residues appear to play a crucial role in facilitating message propagation between residues within the GNN framework (Fig. 2B).

The architectural details of our model (which is composed of two distinct neural networks) are illustrated in Fig. 2C. First, we processed the residue embeddings in the CNN module to extract features from the covalent neighbors. The CNN module is followed by a GCN module, in which we use DCIasym values to construct directed graphs in which edges are formed between controlled nodes (residues) and their controllers. In contrast to most applications of GNNs in protein science, where the 3-D structure and the amino acid positions in contact are used to define the graph, our approach uses DCIasym to guide our model to extract information regarding allosterically connected neighbors. The GCN module enables our model to capture relational information (including long-distance allosteric coupling networks) and learn representations that integrate both local (via CNN) and global (via GCN) information to map sequences to fitness values. Finally, the fully connected layer at the end of the model allows for the integration of the learned features across all positions in the protein, thereby providing a comprehensive representation of the protein for prediction tasks.

Evaluating Neural Net Models Using Deep Mutational Scanning Datasets.

We evaluated four deep learning architectures to explore how different internal representations influence the ability to learn the mapping from protein sequence to function. These architectures included an MLP model, which is a fully connected neural network; a CNN model that applies convolutional operations to detect local patterns, such as covalent neighbors; GCNcontact, a GCN that uses a contact map derived from the protein’s three-dimensional structure to define connections between residues; and GCNasym, which constructs a graph based on DCIasym, connecting each residue to its key dynamic “controllers,” thereby creating an allosteric network representation. We then examined the predictive performance of each model on double mutants under constrained data conditions, where training relied solely on limited experimental measurements. In this setting, models were trained exclusively on single mutant data and tested on double mutants, a task that is particularly difficult due to potential nonadditive (epistatic) interactions between mutations. To examine how the amount of training data influences predictive accuracy, we systematically varied the fraction of single mutants used for training: 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of the available data. For each training fraction, we performed fivefold cross-validation, and to ensure robust estimates, we repeated the data splitting and training process five times with different random seeds. As expected, increasing the fraction of trained single mutants led to higher Pearson correlations between predicted and experimental double mutant scores. All models eventually achieved correlations above 0.8 when trained on 100% single mutants except MLP (Fig. 3C).

Among the models, GCNasym, GCNcontact, and CNN achieved similar predictive performance when trained on the full single mutant datasets, while MLP consistently underperformed the other models (Fig. 3C). In β-lactamase, this similarity persisted across all training fractions (Fig. 3C, first panel), likely because most double mutants involve mutations at adjacent residues (i and i+1). In such cases, local sequential context captured by CNNs is sufficient, resulting in comparable performance to graph-based models. However, notable differences emerged in more complex systems such as KRAS, a protein characterized by long-range allosteric regulation (39). Here, GCNasym consistently outperformed both GCNcontact and CNN, particularly under limited training conditions (20% and 40% of single mutants; Fig. 3C, second panel). These results highlight the unique advantage of GCNasym in capturing asymmetric, long-range interactions that are not fully accessible to models relying solely on local patterns or static symmetric contact maps.

To further assess the models’ capabilities beyond correlation-based accuracy, we evaluated their ability to reproduce the full distribution of double mutant effects by calculating the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between predicted and experimental distributions (Fig. 3D). Across all proteins, GCNasym consistently achieved the lowest KL divergence, demonstrating its superior capacity to capture both the magnitude and the distributional features of mutational effects, which outperformed both CNN and GCNcontact. Taken together, these results emphasize the strength of GCNasym in modeling mutational effects driven by long-range and asymmetric interactions, providing a clear advantage over models that primarily rely on local sequence features or static contact information.

We next explored whether adding attention mechanisms through Graph Attention Networks (GAT) could improve performance. While GATasym models achieved comparable predictive accuracy to GCNasym in several proteins, they did not provide consistent benefits and incurred additional computational costs due to learning attention weights. Furthermore, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1, GCNasym consistently achieved the lowest KL divergence, indicating its superior ability to capture the distribution of mutational effects. Given its strong performance, sparser graph structure compared to 3-D contact map (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), and greater computational efficiency, GCNasym was selected as the preferred model for subsequent analyses.

Assessment of Our Neural Networks’ Ability to Capture Epistatic Relationships.

We then evaluated the predictive abilities of various models in capturing epistasis. Instead of training on experimental epistasis values, we trained on mutational data (see Table 1 for details) to predict fitness scores for double mutants and then calculated the epistasis scores using the predicted double mutant fitness scores for each residue pair. Fig. 4 shows scatter plots of predicted epistasis versus the experimentally measured epistasis values. For the GB1 dataset, models trained without exposure to double mutation data gave relatively poor predictions of epistasis and Pearson’s correlation coefficients (R) ranging from 0.095 to 0.126 (Fig. 4A). However, when 30% of the double mutant data were included in the training dataset, the performance of the models improved significantly, eventually reaching a correlation coefficient of 0.838 (Fig. 4B). Exposure to double mutant data enables the model to learn context-dependent effects and interaction patterns, thereby enhancing its ability to capture epistatic behavior. Notably, these results were achieved without training our data on any experimental epistasis scores.

A poor prediction of epistasis was observed for all models, as evidenced by PCC ranging from 0.095 to 0.126. (B) The performance of various deep learning models on GB1, when 30% of the double mutants are included in the training set, is shown. In contrast, the β-lactamase dataset’s baseline performance without being trained on any double mutants was higher compared to the GB1 dataset and gave correlation coefficients of 0.263, 0.323, and 0.404 for MLP, CNN, and GCNasym, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). We believe this is because the double mutants in the β-lactamase dataset consist of adjacent residue pairs (i.e., i and i+1), which implies that it is easier for all models to capture the epistatic interactions between double mutants that occur at adjacent positions. When 30% of the double mutants in the β-lactamase dataset were introduced into the training set, the correlation coefficient of epistasis predictions increased from 0.314 to 0.652 among the different architectures (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). The relatively smaller improvement observed for β-lactamase compared to GB1 could be attributed to its limited experimentally measured epistatic interactions, which primarily cover nearest neighbors (i, i+1) mutations. Despite this limitation, GCNasym consistently outperformed MLP and CNN in predicting the epistasis for double mutants. A consistent pattern was also observed in KRAS and GFP, where GCNasym achieved the highest correlation between predicted and experimental epistasis when training on 0% and 30% double mutant data (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5), suggesting that, among the three architectures, GCNasym might best capture epistatic interactions.

Blind Prediction of Designed TEM-1 Variants.

To further assess the quality of our GCNasym model, we used it to blindly predict the functions of a series of novel TEM-1 variants generated using the Rosetta computational protein design software (40) (Methods). In previous studies of ancestral and modern β-lactamase variants (16, 17), we found that nature often subtly alters enzyme function by targeting mutations to residues that are distal from, but highly dynamically coupled to the active site rather than mutating the active site residues themselves. We refer to such residues (i.e., those distant from but highly coupled to active site residues) as “dynamic allosteric residue coupling” (DARC) sites. Using the DCI metric as a filter for high coupling to the active site, we identified residues C77, S82, R83, I84, Q99, G116, T188, and A213 as DARC sites within TEM-1. We reasoned that targeting mutations to residues proximal to DARC sites might significantly alter TEM-1’s overall dynamic profile, which could in turn lead to substantial functional changes. Thus, we developed a RosettaDesign protocol that generated “design shells” (virtual spheres with radii of 8 Å, 10 Å, and 12 Å) that were centered on each DARC residue listed above and then subjected residues falling within these shells (including the DARC site itself) to random mutagenesis in silico; the identities of residues falling outside of the design shells were maintained. After mutagenesis, the total energy of the protein was optimized using the Rosetta FastRelax protocol (41–43). Multiple independent design trajectories were carried out, which targeted each of the eight DARC sites listed above.

We ultimately selected 37 protein variants, containing between 2 and 16 total mutations, for experimental characterization. The only criterion used in choosing a design for experimental analysis was that the energy of the designed protein [as calculated by the Rosetta full-atom score function (44)] was within ~10 Rosetta energy units (either above or below) of the initial score of the input protein. Importantly, this selection criterion is agnostic to the nature of the mutations that were generated by Rosetta but should eliminate sets of mutations that are predicted to substantially disrupt protein function. Genes encoding each designed protein were ordered from GenScript (Piscataway, NJ) in standard pET29b expression vectors. Designed TEM-1 variants were experimentally characterized by assessing the minimum inhibitory concentration of ampicillin (MICamp). The MICamp value (in μg/mL) for each designed variant is shown in SI Appendix, Table S1 and directly reports on the relationship between a given set of mutations and enzyme function.

Prior to the MICamp assays, we predicted the activities of our TEM-1 variants using pretrained MLP, CNN, and GCNasym architectures. It is worth noting that these neural networks have not been trained on variants that have a mutation count higher than two, which makes it challenging to infer the epistatic relationships among the variants and subsequently to relate these relationships to functional outcomes. The blind prediction performance of the MLP model was found to be subpar when dealing with variants with multiple mutations, conversely CNN and GCNasym both achieved high accuracies (Fig. 5A). With MLP, many deleterious variants (MICamp lower than 1,500 μg/mL) are predicted as natural-like (fitness scores of 0.8 to 1.0), which reveals a significant limitation of MLP in accurately classifying deleterious mutations. Moreover, the prediction accuracy of MLP is limited and shows high variance, while both CNN and GCNasym are more precise in their prediction ranges.

The mutations in our designed TEM-1 variants span a wide range of distances from the active site residues. We therefore assessed whether a correlation exists between the distances of designed mutations from active site residues and the log-transformed MICamp values (SI Appendix, Fig. S6); our analysis found no significant correlation. Historically, prediction of the functional consequences of mutations that are distal from the active site has been notoriously difficult (45). Notably, GCNasym demonstrates a remarkable ability to decode complex interactions within mutational data, regardless of their proximity to the active site. Importantly, GCNasym identifies variant D6 as having the highest activity variant, a significant finding given that all mutations in D6 are located far from the active site (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Moreover, GCNasym uniquely identifies D34 as deleterious (Fig. 5), underscoring its superior performance in distinguishing between deleterious and functional mutations. These findings emphasize the model’s strength in handling spatially distributed, long-range effects.

It is worth noting that GCNasym was trained on only single and double mutants, yet it generalizes effectively to predict the function of designed variants containing up to 16 mutations. This highlights the robustness of its learned dynamics-based representation. Among all models tested, GCNasym exhibits the highest correlation between predicted fitness and log(MICamp) and uniquely succeeded in distinguishing between functional and nonfunctional multimutant variants. For example, GCNasym is the only model capable of accurately predicting the activities of not only variant D6 but also the four native-like variants D5, D8, D9, and D10 (Fig. 5B). In comparison, a Potts model-based evolutionary method (46) predicted our designed functional variants D5, D6, D8, D9, and D10 as nonfunctional with negative fitness changes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Meanwhile, ESM-1v (47), a transformer-based protein language model, correctly identified only D6 as functional and incorrectly predicted two nonfunctional variants as functional (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Collectively, these results suggest that our dynamics-based GCNasym model is unique in that it captures both epistatic relationships between mutations in enzymes as well as the functional implications of mutations that occur far from active site residues.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed deep learning models that utilize long-distance directional dynamics information, captured by our DCIasym metric, to construct graph neural networks. By integrating dynamics into our deep learning framework, we effectively captured the role of allosteric neighbors in facilitating communication between residues. Our model, which combines CNN and GCN architectures, significantly improved the representation of dynamically coupled nodes and the prediction of functional outcome of mutations. Evaluation of our model using four deep mutational scanning datasets (β-lactamases, KRAS, GB1, and GFP) showcased its enhanced performance. Namely, our GCNasym model outperformed other architectures and achieved the highest Pearson’s correlation coefficients and lowest KL divergence in all datasets. Moreover, when exploring the models’ capability to capture epistasis, our GCNasym model exhibited superior performance relative to other models in four datasets, highlighting its effectiveness in predicting complex interactions between residues. This dynamics-based GNN architecture enables us to train models using limited mutational data while accurately predicting the effects of new mutations on protein function. Finally, we evaluated our model’s performance through blind predictions of engineered enzyme variants containing multiple mutations. This assessment demonstrated the model’s capability to achieve high correlation and effectively generalize to complex variants.

We acknowledge that our method, as currently implemented, depends on supervised learning and thus required labeled data; this poses a challenge for generalizing to novel proteins without curated datasets. Nevertheless, our deep learning framework, enhanced with dynamic coupling information, offers a powerful approach for understanding protein dynamics, predicting protein function, and investigating the intricate effects of mutations within protein sequences. This dynamics-based graph neural network also holds significant potential for applications in protein design, enabling the development of proteins with tailored properties.

Methods

DCI.

The DCI is a metric that quantifies the degree of dynamic coupling between two residues in a protein (21, 48). It uses a Perturbation Response Scanning (PRS) technique that combines Elastic Network Model (ENM) and Linear Response Theory (LRT). In ENM, the protein structure is represented as a network of Cα atoms connected by elastic springs that are governed by harmonic potentials. Such harmonic representations have been widely used across many protein systems to capture intrinsic flexibility and correlated motions (28–30). The second derivatives of the harmonic potentials are used to construct the Hessian matrix (H) in Eq. 1. During PRS, Brownian-like unit forces F are applied sequentially to each residue as perturbations. where ΔRki=ΔR2 denotes the average response at position i due to perturbations on k. This procedure is repeated several times in different directions for each position to ensure that forces are isotropically sampled. Then, the averaged Perturbation Response Matrix A is used to calculate the DCI per residue pair.[3]DCIij=ΔRji∑k=1NΔRki/N.

This index is often more useful when represented as a percentile since the DCI range varies for different proteins. Therefore, the DCI percentile is calculated as[4]%DCIij=n≤iN,

where N is the total number of residues, and n≤i is the number of residues with DCI value ≤DCIij. The DCI value represents the magnitude of displacement of position i upon perturbation to a single position (or a subset of positions) j, relative to the average fluctuation response of position i when all of the positions within a structure are perturbed (1). Due to the monodirectional nature of DCI, an inherent asymmetry exists within its values. Therefore, DCIasym can be defined as the quantitative measure of the disparity or imbalance between the DCI values of two residues, indicating the strength and directionality of their coupling relationship. One important tool we use is DCIasym (21), which measures preferential information transfer through asymmetric dynamic coupling. DCIasym is calculated as[5]DCIasym=DCIij-DCIji, [6]%DCIasym=%DCIij-%DCIji.

In a previous study (49), we demonstrated that DCIasym can effectively capture important biophysical properties in dual mutant substitution outcomes. This metric can provide insights into the mechanisms underlying nonadditive amino acid substitution outcomes (i.e., epistasis). In our study, we incorporated DCIasym in the construction of a directed graph, which was then utilized in a GCN for our analysis. While the DCIasym used to define the graph structure in our model is agnostic to amino acid identity, information about each residue is introduced through the node features that incorporate physicochemical properties of each residue. This design allows us to integrate dynamic allosteric communication with mutational context to capture complex, functionally relevant behavior.

Neutral Network Architectures.

Protein descriptors of input features.

A general method in machine learning approaches involves representing the sequence of amino acids as a vector of amino acid properties (50). In this study, we applied convolutional filters that require each residue to have its own input vector. We used two amino acid level encodings: one-hot encoding (a 21-dimensional vector indicating amino acid identity) and a reduced AA-index representation. The AA-index, which captures physicochemical properties, was adopted in its PCA-compressed form from Gelman et al. (6), yielding 19 features that preserve 100% of the variance while reducing redundancy.

MLP.

MLP is a type of neural network where every node in one layer is fully connected to all nodes in the next layer (51). In our implementation, we used up to three layers, each containing either 100 or 1,000 nodes, with the Rectified Linear Unit activation function. The network ends with a final linear output with no activation function, suitable for regression tasks to obtain the final function score.

CNN.

CNN is a type of neural network that uses convolutional filters to capture local patterns in structured data such as images or protein sequences (52, 53). In our study, we utilized 2D convolutional filters in our CNN model that have been successfully applied to study protein stability and function (6, 7). Each filter was operated along both the amino acid sequence dimension and the protein embedding dimension, enabling the extraction of high-level information from neighboring positions. We explored the effects of varying filter sizes and strides in the convolutional layers. The filter width was fixed at 40 while the height was altered using values of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15. We applied padding on top and bottom of the feature map such that the dimension of the feature map has the same length as the protein sequence. Additionally, we varied the stride, which controls the step size during the convolution process, using values of 1 and 3. The number of hidden channels varies between 64 and 128. The resulting feature maps were then flattened and fed into a fully connected layer before the output layer for final predictions.

Graph convolutional neural network (GCN).

GCN is a class of neural networks designed to operate on graph-structured data by aggregating feature information from a node’s neighbors. We adopted the GCN layer proposed by Thomas N. Kipf (54). The number of hidden channels in the graph convolutional layer varies between 64 and 128, while the number of GCN layers varies between 1, 2, and 3. The graph is constructed using the DCI metric such that the target node (residue) is connected to all other nodes (residues) that are highly coupled (%DCIij > 0.5) to the target node. Additionally, the connection requires these residues to exert asymmetric dynamic control over the target residue, with the condition %DCIasym falls below one of the following thresholds: −0.3, −0.25, −0.2, −0.15, or −0.1.

Graph attention neural network (GAT).

GAT is a type of graph neural network that uses attention mechanism to assign different weights to edges, allowing the model to learn how much information each node should receive from its neighbors during message passing (55). In our implementation, we constructed graphs where each amino acid residue is represented as a node, and edges were defined based on the dynamics-based approach as in GCNasym, which captures directional coupling between residues. An attention coefficient was computed for each edge based on the features of the connected nodes, and node features were updated through multihead attention. The resulting node embeddings were pooled and passed through a fully connected layer, followed by a final linear output layer with no activation function to obtain the final function score. The hyperparameter sweep used for GAT was the same as that for GCN, with addition of one extra parameter: the number of attention heads, which we varied across values of 1, 4, 8, and 16.

Training pipeline.

To evaluate model accuracy, we tested our approach on four deep mutational scanning datasets covering diverse protein functions (Table 1): β-lactamase (antibiotic resistance), GB1 and KRAS (protein–protein interactions), and GFP (fluorescence). β-lactamase fitness scores reflect resistance to ampicillin relative to wild-type TEM-1. GB1 fitness measures binding to IgG FC, KRAS scores reflect SOS1 binding, and GFP fitness corresponds to brightness. These varied functional readouts allow us to assess the generalizability of our GCN across different biochemical contexts.

We split each dataset into 90% training/validation and 10% held-out test data. The 90% was further divided for fivefold cross-validation: In each iteration, four folds were used for training and one for validation, rotating so each fold served once as the validation set. This setup enabled full coverage of training data for validation while keeping the test set untouched for final evaluation. Early stopping (patience = 50) was used to prevent overfitting, halting training if validation loss did not improve for 50 epochs. To ensure robustness, we repeated the entire data splitting and training process five times with different random seeds, allowing us to assess performance stability across varying partitions. For each round of training, we employed Adam Optimized (56) and MSE loss as the training approach for our models. We set the maximum number of epochs for each training to 500.

To assess performance under data scarcity, we trained each model on single mutants and tested on double mutants, using 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of the single mutant data. For each fraction, we performed fivefold cross-validation and repeated the process five times with different random seeds. This setup evaluated the model’s ability to generalize from limited single mutant data to double mutant predictions.

Experimental epistasis analysis.

Nonadditivity in fitness effects from two or more mutations, termed epistasis, can result in compensation of deleterious mutations or negation of beneficial mutations. The pairwise epistasis of mutations A and B are defined as[7]εAB=logwABwowAwB,

where wo is the fitness of wild-type protein, wAB is the fitness of double mutants, wA is the fitness of mutation A, and wB is the fitness of mutation B (34).

Experimental Characterization of Rosetta-Designed Enzymes by Minimal Inhibitory Assays.

Minimal inhibitory concentration assays (MIC assays) were performed as described previously (57). Briefly, genes encoding each designed protein were ordered from GenScript (Piscataway, NJ) and were cloned into pET29b expression vectors containing a kanamycin resistance marker. After sequence confirmation, vectors were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 DE3* cells and plated on LB agar plates containing kanamycin at a concentration of 50 μg/mL. After incubation overnight at 37 °C, five colonies were used to inoculate separate 5 mL cultures in LB medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin. These cultures were grown in a shaking incubator at 37 °C until confluence was reached (~12 h). The overnight cultures were diluted in LB medium (supplemented with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, IPTG) to concentrations of 106 cfu/mL as judged by OD600 measurements. Serial dilutions of ampicillin were then made in LB medium containing 1 mM IPTG to give a range of ampicillin concentrations from 0 to 10,000 μg/mL. To begin the MIC assays, 105 cfu of each variant in LB medium were transferred to a sterile 96-well plate containing LB medium with ampicillin at one of the concentrations in the aforementioned range. Inoculated plates were then covered and incubated at 37 °C for 17 h at which time the optical density of each well at 600 nm was assessed using a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices, LLC, San Jose, CA); fresh LB medium was used as a blank. The reported MICamp values represent the lowest concentration of ampicillin where an OD600 value less than 0.1 was observed. All MIC assays were carried out a minimum of 3 times.

Rosetta Methods.

All Rosetta Design calculations were carried out using the XML-based RosettaScripts interface to Rosetta (58). We identified a series of eight DARC residues (C77, S82, R83, I84, Q99, G116, T188, and A213) that were targeted for design. We used the “DesignAround” task operator within RosettaScripts to construct “design shells” of size 8 Å, 10 Å, or 12 Å around each DARC residue. Residues falling within each design shell (including the DARC residues themselves) were subject to mutagenesis to any of the 20 standard amino acids. All residues within 16 Å of the targeted DARC residue were subject to repacking (i.e., rotamer sampling) calculations after in silico mutagenesis. After the initial design step, all proteins were “relaxed” using the “FastRelax” protocol in Rosetta, which is an iterative series of repacking and minimization steps that serves to reduce the total energy of the protein without additional mutagenesis. Following the design and relax algorithms, each protein was scored using the Rosetta all-atom energy function (44). A detailed description of our design protocol (including all files required to run Rosetta) can be found in SI Appendix.
