Raising systems and diet type in Japanese quails production: Effects on growth performance, carcass characteristics, fatty acid profiles and meat quality
Authors: Seyed Davood Sharifi, Abbas Rahimi, Hasan Rouhanipour, Hossein Pasha Zanussi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of raising systems and diet types on growth performance, carcass characteristics, pH content, fatty acid profiles and meat quality in Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica). A total of 608 seven-day-old quail chicks were arranged in a 2 × 2 factorial design, with two raising systems (cage and free-range) and two diet types (conventional and organic). The experiment employed a completely randomized design with four treatments, four replicates per treatment, and 38 birds per replicate. Quail chicks were raised for 42 d in cages and 51 d in free-range systems, depending on the treatment. Feed intake (FI), live body weight (LBW), and mortality were recorded weekly, and daily feed intake (DFI), body weight gain (BWG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated. At the end of the experiment, birds were slaughtered to analyze fatty acid profiles, malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations, and pH in meat. Results showed FCR from 8 to 42 d of age improved in birds fed a conventional diet in cage system compared to those fed an organic diet raised in either free-range or cage systems (P < 0.05). During the periods of 8 to 42 d and 8 to 51d, BWG was higher in birds raised in the cage system fed a conventional diet compared to quails fed an organic diet raised in either free-range or cage systems (P < 0.05). Intestine length was higher in male quails raised in cages and fed a conventional diet compared to male quails raised in the free-range system fed an organic diet (P < 0.05). The gut relative weight decreased in female quails raised in cage system compared to both male and female quails raised in free-range and cage systems, respectively (P < 0.05). Meat pH decreased in quails fed an organic diet compared to those fed a conventional diet (P < 0.05). Free-range quails had higher levels of beneficial long-chain fatty acids and a more favorable n-6: n-3 ratio in their meat compared to cage-reared birds (P < 0.05). Organic diets increased alpha-linolenic acid but also elevated MDA levels, indicating greater lipid oxidation. MDA was lower in free-range birds, especially those fed organic diets, than in caged birds (P < 0.05). In conclusion, Japanese quails fed conventional diets in cage systems showed better growth and feed efficiency, while free-range birds exhibited improved meat fatty acid profiles and oxidative stability. Organic diets increased lipid oxidation, especially in caged birds, highlighting the trade-off between production performance and meat quality across rearing systems. Further studies are recommended to explore these aspects in greater detail.Keywords: Performance, pH, MDA, Fatty acid profile, Japanese quail

Introduction

The increasing consumer demand for organic foods has expanded market opportunities for producers and significantly influenced the organic food industry. While early organic farming practices primarily emphasized crop production, organic animal farming has emerged as a vital sector over the past decade (Gamage et al., 2023). A key component of organic animal farming is animal welfare, with regulatory frameworks mandating that livestock be raised under conditions that facilitate a broad range of natural social and behavioral activities (Bist et al., 2024).

In organic poultry production systems, the use of synthetic feeds and chemicals is prohibited, ensuring that the nutritional and environmental requirements of the birds are met while preserving their natural behaviors and physiological functions (De Azevedo et al., 2023; Tabashsum et al., 2023). These production methods often require greater space and environmental enrichment to provide animals with greater freedom of movement and opportunities to express a broader range of behaviors. In contrast, large-scale conventional poultry operations dominate commercial production due to their efficiency in maximizing meat yield while minimizing costs. However, this system's inherent space limitations can potentially affect animal welfare. As a result, increasing consumer demand for ethically and sustainably produced poultry has prompted producers to adopt alternative systems, such as free-range farming, which allows birds access to outdoor environments (Shi et al., 2019; Ricke and Rothrock, 2020).

Free-range poultry production, as defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), refers to systems in which birds have regulated access to outdoor areas (Rothrock et al., 2019). This production method has gained popularity among consumers due to its perceived benefits in promoting animal welfare and more natural husbandry practices (Fanatico et al., 2006). Free-range systems provide birds with outdoor environments that encourage natural behaviors such as foraging, food selection, and physical activity, which are theorized to enhance overall welfare (Husak et al., 2008; Inci et al., 2016).

The farming of Japanese quail presents a promising alternative for meat and egg production worldwide. Quail meat is highly regarded for its superior nutritional profile, characterized by high-quality protein, low-fat content, high biological value, and a lower calorie content compared to other poultry products (Jeke et al., 2018; Ribarski and Genchev, 2013). Additionally, quails exhibit rapid growth, require less feed and space than chickens, and produce meat rich in indispensable amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, and antioxidants essential for human health (Genchev et al., 2008; Cavani et al., 2009). These nutrients play a vital role in normal growth and brain development (Bourre, 2004). As a result, there is increasing research interest in optimizing quail meat composition by reducing fat content and pH while improving fatty acid profiles. Quail breeders focus on producing high-quality meat at minimal cost and within the shortest production cycle. Compared to other meats, quail meat is lower in calories and higher in protein, making it a nutritionally favorable option (Ribarski and Genchev, 2013).

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the effects of outdoor access on poultry growth performance. While some research has found no significant differences in growth performance between free-range and indoor production systems (Mikulski et al., 2011; Jiang et al., 2011), other studies have observed lower weight gain and reduced feed conversion efficiency in free-range birds compared to those raised indoors (Castellini et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2009). Meat yield differences between conventional and free-range poultry systems are generally negligible (Fanatico et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2011). However, meat from slow-growing broilers raised in organic systems is often considered more flavorful than that from fast-growing broilers in conventional systems, likely due to increased physical activity, which enhances myogenesis (Overbeke et al., 2006; Castellini et al., 2002). Additionally, organic chickens are raised with unrestricted access to pastures, further contributing to their perception as "natural" products (Skřivan et al., 2015; Ferreira et al., 2022). Furthermore, consumer preference for free-range poultry meat has been shaped by perceived improvements in bird welfare, as well as differences in fat and fatty acid composition. The growth and performance of specialty poultry are influenced by a range of factors, including genotype, age, sex, diet, stocking density, environmental conditions, exercise, and pasture intake (Gordon and Charles, 2002).

Understanding the factors that influence poultry growth, and their interactions is essential for optimizing performance in free-range systems, which often exhibit greater variability in carcass and meat quality outcomes. While research on poultry production in organic systems has primarily focused on chickens, there is limited literature on these interactions in other species, such as Japanese quails. Therefore, this study aims to assess the effects of different rearing systems and diet types on growth performance, carcass characteristics, pH levels, fatty acid profiles, and overall meat quality in Japanese quails.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the Department of Animal Science, University of Tehran, under approval number 9222213/5 (15/02/2021).

Bird management and treatments

The experiment was conducted in a single, dedicated research facility at the University of Tehran's poultry farm to maintain consistent environmental conditions across all treatments. A total of 608 seven-day-old Japanese quail chicks were used in a 2 × 2 factorial design with two raising systems (cage and free-range) and two diet types (conventional and organic). The experiment followed a completely randomized design with four treatments, four replicates per treatment, and 38 birds per replicate. The experimental durations were 42 d for cage systems and 51 d for free-range systems, reflecting the longer growth period required to achieve marketing weight in organic systems. Quail chicks were selected based on similar body weight and production characteristics, with equal proportions of males and females randomly assigned to the experimental units. Sexing was performed at day 7 by a trained professional using the vent sexing method, a reliable technique for distinguishing males from females based on cloacal examination. Each replicate in the cage system was housed in a cage with dimensions of 90 cm (length) × 40 cm (width) × 40 cm (height). The stocking densities for each housing system were established in accordance with standard industry and regulatory guidelines. Specifically, the conventional cage system maintained a density of approximately 105.5 birds/m², consistent with common commercial practices for intensive quail production. In the free-range system, indoor pens measuring 200 cm × 200 cm were stocked at 9.5 birds/m², while outdoor pens measuring 3 m × 15 m provided a density of 0.8 birds/m². These free-range densities were designed to comply with, or exceed, established organic and free-range poultry welfare standards, including USDA Organic regulations and EU Council Regulation 889/2008. Indoor and outdoor ambient temperatures and relative humidity were recorded daily at noon. The indoor temperature was gradually reduced to 24°C by the fourth week and maintained at this level thereafter. Specifically, birds were given access to the outdoor pens for a daily duration of approximately 8 hours (from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM) when outdoor temperatures were within the acceptable range of 18°C to 32°C, with relative humidity varying between 48 % and 68 %. The indoor and outdoor spaces were connected by walls with small doors, enabling the quails to move freely between the two areas. The outdoor area consisted of a natural environment, comprising soil, sand, and vegetation, with a particular emphasis on clover and wild grasses. Quails with outdoor access were returned to the indoor pens at night. All chicks were raised under standardized management and sanitary conditions. For the first three days, all groups were exposed to light intensities of 30 lx, which were then reduced to 20 lx starting on the third day, as measured using a lux meter (TES 1335, Digital Light Meter). The commercial groups followed a lighting schedule of 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness, while the organic groups were exposed solely to natural daylight, in accordance with regulations governing organic production.

The conventional and organic diets were formulated to have distinct nutrient profiles, reflecting the typical differences between these two production systems. The conventional diet (Table 1) was formulated according to NRC (1994) guidelines to meet the crude protein and metabolizable energy requirements of Japanese quail. In contrast, organic diet was formulated based on established guidelines specific to organic quail production, ensuring compliance with organic feed standards and nutrient requirements appropriate for this system. Birds in the conventional groups were fed a commercial diet and organic groups received certified organic feed throughout the trial. The organic diet consisted of grains and oilseeds cultivated locally using traditional farming practices and processed through chemical-free methods. Non-organic premixes allowed for use in organic poultry production were included in the diet. Feed and water were provided ad libitum to all experimental groups. In the conventional cage system, linear trough feeders were attached to the front of each cage, allowing all 38 birds per replicate to access feed simultaneously. The feeders were equipped with raised lips to prevent spillage and minimize waste. In the free-range pens, multiple circular hanging feeders were strategically placed throughout the indoor and outdoor areas. This arrangement provided ample feeding space and ensured that all quails, regardless of social hierarchy, had continuous and uniform access to feed.

Growth performance

Mortality was recorded daily, while FI and LBW were measured weekly. Feed intake was recorded for each experimental unit by subtracting the weight of the residual feed from the amount provided at the beginning of the week. The resulting value was divided by the number of chick-day for that period to determine the DFI per bird. Feeder space in all experimental units exceeded the standard requirements for the number of birds housed, ensuring ad libitum access to feed for all individuals. The BWG, DFI, and FCR were calculated and adjusted for mortality. Birds in the conventional system were slaughtered at 42 d of age after weighing, while the rearing period for the organic group continued until day 51. Live body weight at 42 and 51 d of the experiment were considered as the market weight for the conventional and organic systems, respectively. Daily mortalities were weighed and subsequently discarded. Mortality percentages were calculated separately for the two groups (conventional and organic systems) over their respective study periods of 42 and 51 d.

Carcass characteristics

On day 42 (conventional system) and day 51 (free-range system), five quails with average body weights were randomly selected from each pen and euthanized by cervical dislocation for carcass evaluation, in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) guidelines for animal euthanasia. The abdominal cavity was surgically opened, and the entire alimentary canal was carefully excised. The gastrointestinal tract, liver, and heart were then collected and weighed individually. The carcass weight was measured after removing feathers, blood, head, feet, and internal organs. Then, the carcass was partitioned, and the breast and legs (thighs and drumsticks) were carefully separated and weighed. Meanwhile, the length of the small intestines such as duodenum (pancreatic loop), jejunum (from the pancreatic loop to Meckel’s diverticulum), and ileum (from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction) was tape-measured (in cm). Carcass yield and the relative weights of internal organs were calculated as percentages of the LBW and the relative weights of breast and thigh were calculated as percentages of the carcass weight (Celik et al., 2014).

Meat pH assay

The pH of breast meat was measured by homogenizing meat samples with distilled water and filtering the homogenates. PH values were recorded immediately after slaughtering (pH0) and four hours post-slaughter (pH4) using a pH meter (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland) equipped with a glass electrode and temperature compensation. Breast muscle samples were stored at 4°C for four hours before pH2 determination (Genchev et al., 2008).

Fatty acid profile and malondialdehyde (MDA) of meat

Fatty acid profiles in raw, skinless breast and thigh muscles were analyzed using gas chromatography (GC; Agilent 6890 N, Agilent Technologies, Paris, France) interfaced with mass spectroscopy (MS; Agilent 5973 N). Samples (0.5 g of homogenized breast and thigh meat) were mixed with 1 mL of a 2 % methanol solution, and the total lipid extract was obtained following the method of Du et al. (2000). The mixture was heated in a water bath at 90°C for 50 minutes, then cooled to room temperature and combined with 2 mL of n-hexane and 5 mL of water. After thorough mixing and overnight phase separation at room temperature, the upper hexane layer, containing methylated fatty acids, was separated and transferred to a 1.5 mL Falcon tube. Fatty acid analysis was performed using a GC equipped with an auto-sample injector and flame ionization detector. A capillary column (SUPERCOWAX-10, 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film thickness; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) was used with ramped oven temperature conditions: 190°C to 200°C at 5°C/min (held for six minutes), 200°C to 220°C at 10°C/min, and 220°C to 230°C at 5°C/min (held for eight minutes). Inlet and detector temperatures were 280°C and 320°C, respectively. Helium was used as the carrier gas, with a column flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Fatty acids were identified using a mass-selective detector (Agilent model 5973), with spectra compared to the Wiley library. Results were expressed as percentages of total lipids.

Lipid peroxidation was measured in breast meat of birds after slaughter at 42 d (for quails in the conventional system) or 51 d (for quails in the organic system) of the experiment. Lipid oxidation was assessed by determining MDA levels using a modified version of the TBA method on meat samples stored at −20°C for three days (Botsoglou et al., 1994; Galobart et al., 2001). Briefly, 1 g of each sample was transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube, and 8 mL of 5 % aqueous trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and 5 mL of 0.8 % butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) in hexane were added. The mixture was homogenized using an Ultra-Turrax for 30 seconds at high speed and then centrifuged at 3,000 g for three minutes. The top hexane layer was discarded, and the aqueous layer was filtered through Whatman filter paper and adjusted to 5 mL with TCA. A 2.5 mL aliquot of the aqueous layer was mixed with 3 mL of 0.8 % aqueous TBA and heated in a water bath at 70°C for 30 minutes. After cooling, the samples were analyzed using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Model UV-160A, Tokyo, Japan) to measure absorbance between 400 and 650 nm. MDA concentrations were determined from third-order derivative peaks at 521.5 nm using a standard calibration curve prepared with 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene´s tests. Growth performance data, which involved repeated measurements on the same animals over time, were analyzed using a mixed-effects model with the PROC MIXED procedure of SAS (SAS version 9.1, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The photoperiod was a fixed, defining characteristic of the rearing system treatments and was not included as an independent variable in the statistical model. The statistical model included rearing system, diet type, and their interaction as fixed effects. The random effect of pen (cage ID) nested within the rearing system was included in the model to account for the lack of independence among birds housed in the same replicate. The repeated nature of the measurements was handled by specifying a repeated statement with an appropriate covariance structure (e.g., autoregressive, auto regressive of order 1). For carcass characteristics, meat quality, and fatty acid profiles, which were measured at a single time point, data were analyzed using a General Linear Model with a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement. The model included the main effects of rearing systems, diet types, and sex, as well as their interactions. Differences among treatment means were determined using Tukey’s test, and statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Growth performance

The growth performance results of Japanese quails during the experimental period are presented in Table 2, Table 3, Table 4. The interaction effects between raising systems and diet types significantly influenced DFI, BWG, FCR from 8 to 42 d (P < 0.05). Higher DFI was observed in birds raised in a cage system fed a conventional diet compared to those raised in the cage system and with an organic diet (P < 0.05). BWG was higher in birds raised in the cage system fed a conventional diet compared to quails in a free-range system that fed either conventional or organic diet (P < 0.05). The FCR was significantly lower in birds in the cage system which fed a conventional diet compared to quails fed an organic diet in both the free-range or cage systems (P < 0.05). According to Table 3, body weight and marketing weight were higher in birds raised in the cage system fed a conventional diet (at 42 d) compared to quails fed either conventional or organic diets in the free-range system (at 51 d; P < 0.05). The DFI was higher in birds in the free-range system fed an organic diet compared to those in the free-range system fed a conventional diet and those in the cage system fed an organic diet (P < 0.05). BWG was higher in birds in the cage system fed a conventional diet compared to quails fed an organic diet in both the free-range and cage systems (P < 0.05). Quail chicks in the cage system fed a conventional diet showed a lower FCR compared to quails in the free-range system fed either conventional or organic diets (P < 0.05). Additionally, mortality rates were reduced in birds raised in the cage system compared to those raised in a free-range system (P < 0.05). The DFI, FCR, and mortality at 51d of age were decreased in birds raised in the cage system compared to quails raised in the free-range system (P < 0.05). On the other hand, quail chicks raised in the cage system showed higher levels of BWG and LBW compared to quails raised in the free-range system (P < 0.05).

Animals with increased activity levels expend more energy and consume more feed (Wilson et al., 2022). In this experiment, birds raised in the cage system fed a conventional diet demonstrated improved overall performance. A key factor differentiating the conventional and free-range systems was the photoperiod. The conventional system employed an extended lighting regimen (23 hours light, 1 hour dark), a common practice in intensive production aimed at maximizing feeding time and promoting growth (Arowolo et al., 2019). In contrast, free-range birds were exposed exclusively to natural daylight, which fluctuates seasonally. This disparity in photoperiod likely influenced feed intake, energy expenditure, and overall growth performance, beyond the effects attributable to diet or housing conditions alone. The prolonged photoperiod in the conventional system probably stimulated increased feed consumption and accelerated growth rates, thereby contributing to higher body weights and improved feed conversion ratios observed in this group (Li et al., 2025). Conversely, the shorter and variable natural photoperiod experienced by free-range birds may have restricted feeding opportunities, potentially explaining their lower DFI and slower growth (Sánchez-Casanova et al., 2020). Although free-range birds exhibited higher DFI, they had a higher FCR and significantly BWG, LBW, and market weight compared to birds housed indoors. While the cage birds showed higher activity levels, the energy expenditure may have reached a point where additional FI compensated for the extra energy consumed. Additionally, the high ambient temperature during the experiment may have suppressed the birds' appetite, further contributing to reduced FI. Previous studies have reported that birds raised for 42 din conventional systems achieved greater weight than those raised in organic systems (Ward et al., 2001). Similarly, birds housed in cages exhibited heavier body weights compared to organically raised birds (Küçükyılmaz et al., 2012; Milošević et al., 2005). Growth performance in poultry is influenced by various factors, including genotype, production system, diet, age, sex, stocking density, photoperiod, temperature, and activity levels (Gordon and Charles, 2002). Stocking density, lighting, and temperature variations may also affect results (Fanatico et al., 2008). In this study, the reduced FI observed in birds fed organic diets, irrespective of the production system, may be attributed to differences in diet composition. Nutritional analysis of experimental diets revealed significant disparities in key nutrient levels, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Specifically, the organic diet contained substantially lower crude protein (18.4 % vs. 25.1 %) and crude fat (1.52 % vs. 4.10 %) compared to the conventional diet. For example, the high proportion of fish meal (10 %) and the absence of fats in the organic diet may have negatively affected palatability (Tabeidian et al., 2006). Additionally, the lack of industrial amino acids such as methionine in organic diets could have contributed to reduced feed consumption.

Although the stocking density in this study was within acceptable limits for conventional production, the cage system inherently restricts quails’ movement and natural behaviors compared to free-range housing. This limitation may partly explain the superior FCR and weight gain observed, likely due to reduced activity and energy expenditure rather than diet alone. These findings emphasize the production efficiency of cage systems while underscoring the inherent trade-off between productivity and animal welfare, an issue of growing significance in the poultry industry.

Carcass yield and weight internal of organs

The results of raising systems, diet types, and sex on carcass yield and the relative weight of internal organs in Japanese quail are shown in Table 5, Table 6. Birds raised in the cage system exhibited a higher thigh percentage and intestine length compared to those in the free-range system (P < 0.05). In terms of diet, the relative weight of the liver and heart decreased in birds fed a conventional diet compared to those fed an organic diet (P < 0.05). Conversely, intestine length was greater in birds fed a conventional diet (P < 0.05). Additionally, a significant sex effect was observed, with female birds displaying a lower liver percentage compared to males (P < 0.05).

No significant interaction effects were observed between raising systems, diet types, and sex on carcass yield, breast, thigh, heart, or digestive system percentages (Table 6). However, intestine length was higher in males raised in cages fed a conventional diet compared to male birds raised in the free-range system fed an organic diet (P < 0.05). The relative weight of the liver increased in male quails raised in cages with an organic diet compared to female quails fed organic diets in cages (P < 0.05). Significant interactions were observed between raising systems and sex, as well as diet types and sex, for intestine length, liver, and gut relative weight (P < 0.05). A higher liver relative weight was noted in male birds raised in cages compared to female birds raised in cages (P < 0.05). The gut relative weight decreased in female quails raised in cages compared to both male and female quails raised in free-range and cage systems, respectively (P < 0.05). Additionally, gut relative weight and intestine length increased in male quails fed an organic diet compared to females fed organic diets (P < 0.05).

Despite the lower stocking density in the free-range treatment, the production system had no significant effect on eviscerated carcass, breast, thigh, or wing yields, consistent with the findings of Fanatico et al. (2005). However, Castellini et al. (2002) observed increased breast and thigh meat yield in birds raised in free-range systems with lower stocking densities, likely due to enhanced motor activity. In contrast, the absence of differences in carcass yield between indoor and outdoor birds, as reported by Fanatico et al. (2005), suggests that factors other than rearing system may influence carcass efficiency. Free-range birds may develop a more extensive gastrointestinal tract due to increased fiber and feed intake, which could decrease carcass efficiency. Also, the lower body weight in these birds may offset this effect, as observed in Table 4. Male chicks exhibited heavier carcasses compared to females, and the sex-based differences in live weight variation were statistically significant (Horn et al., 1999).

Meat pH

The effects of raising systems, diet types, and sex on the pH of breast meat are presented in Table 7, Table 8. No significant interaction effects of raising system, diet type, and sex were observed on the pH of breast meat, measured both immediately after slaughter and four hours post-slaughter. However, the pH values were recorded immediately after slaughtering (pH0) of meat decreased in quails fed an organic diet compared to those fed a conventional diet (P < 0.05). Meat pH is a critical parameter influencing storage and quality. According to Riegel et al. (2003), pH values in poultry meat typically range between 6.02 and 6.41. Glycolysis in the pectoral muscle generally ceases 45 minutes post-slaughter, after which pH values stabilize (Drbohlav and Drbohlavova, 1987). Lower pH values observed in outdoor-reared animals may result from reduced pre-slaughter stress and glycogen depletion (Castellini et al., 2002). Enfält et al. (1997) suggested that lower ultimate pH in outdoor-reared animals could reflect better substrate utilization during transport. These findings align with reports indicating improved welfare conditions in organic systems, which reduce stress pre-slaughter (Scott and Moran, 1993). However, the lack of significant differences in meat pH between cage-reared and free-range birds in this study is in contrast with earlier findings by Castellini et al. (2002) and Alvarado et al. (2005). This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including the specific genetic strain of quail used and the relatively limited duration of outdoor access in our free-range system, which may not have been sufficient to induce physiological changes. Additionally, Lengkey et al. (2013) showed that acute stress factors such as handling and transport can significantly influence meat pH, which may have masked any subtle effects of the rearing system in our study. Therefore, our results suggest that the effects of the rearing system on meat pH are not generalizable and may be highly dependent on specific management and environmental conditions. The lower pH observed in quails fed the organic diet may be related to differences in diet composition. Some studies, such as the one by Choi (2023), have shown that specific dietary components or supplements can directly influence meat quality and pH. Given the distinct nutrient profiles of our organic diet, it is plausible that these ingredients or their metabolic effects contributed to the observed pH differences, independent of the rearing system.

The saturated fatty acids: unsaturated fatty acids ratio (UFA: SFA) was significantly lower in birds raised in the free-range system compared to those raised in cages (P < 0.05). Additionally, the ratio of Σ n-6 to Σ n-3 fatty acids was reduced in the meat of birds in the free-range system fed an organic diet compared to those in the cage system receiving the same diet (P < 0.05). Lower levels of MDA were detected in the meat of birds raised in the free-range system fed an organic diet compared to those raised in cages fed an organic diet (P < 0.05). MDA levels in the meat of quails raised under the free-range system were significantly lower than those raised in the cage system (P < 0.05). Furthermore, birds receiving an organic diet exhibited significantly higher MDA concentrations in meat compared to those fed a conventional diet (P < 0.05). Consumer preferences in Europe and the United States increasingly favor broiler meat from free-range production systems. These systems typically use slower-growing genotypes slaughtered at 81 d, fed cereal-based diets, and allowed limited pasture access. By contrast, conventional systems rely on fast-growing genotypes slaughtered at 35-42 d. Organic broilers exhibit higher levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), n-3, and n-6 fatty acids but lower saturated and monounsaturated fatty acid levels compared to conventional and free-range birds (Castellini et al., 2002). Pasture intake, though limited, may contribute to differences in fatty acid profiles. Polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as EPA and DHA, are considered healthier for human diets (Nicolosi et al., 2001). However, this study found lower n-3 PUFA levels in free-range broilers compared to birds supplemented with fish oil, highlighting the limitations of pasture-based systems in enhancing meat fatty acid profiles. The ratio of Σ n-6: Σ n-3 fatty acids is an important factor for assessing meat quality. Lower ratios are associated with improved cardiovascular health (Milinsk et al., 2003). Organic diets rich in linolenic acid (C18:3n-3) can suppress arachidonic acid synthesis (C20:4n-6) due to competition for the Δ−6-desaturase enzyme (Mazalli et al., 2004). Similarly, increased omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids in the diet may reduce monounsaturated fatty acid levels by inhibiting Δ−9-desaturase activity. These mechanisms explain the observed differences in fatty acid composition in this study. Optimizing the dietary omega-6 to omega-3 ratio within a range of 3.5 to 15 is essential for enhancing meat nutritional quality (Pacetti et al., 2005). Direct supplementation with long-chain PUFA may offer a more effective strategy for improving meat fatty acid profiles compared to pasture-based systems alone. MDA concentration is a common indicator of lipid peroxidation (Botsoglou et al., 1994). Under oxidative stress, free radicals, particularly oxygen, can damage tissues through enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways, leading to the formation of lipid peroxides such as MDA (Valko et al., 2007). Antioxidants, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), play crucial roles in maintaining oxidative homeostasis and neutralizing free radicals (Ighodaro et al., 2018). Stress-induced lipid oxidation is a major factor in reduced meat quality, affecting flavor, color, and other characteristics (Bejaoui et al., 2023). In this study, birds raised in cages exhibited higher MDA levels in their meat, potentially reflecting higher oxidative stress. Organic diet in the cage system also increased MDA levels due to their higher proportions of fish meal and unsaturated fats, which are prone to rapid oxidative degradation.

Conclusions

This study suggests that Japanese quails raised in conventional cage systems and fed a conventional diet exhibited superior growth performance, characterized by increased body weight gain, improved feed conversion ratio, and reduced mortality, compared to those raised in free-range systems or fed organic diets. The extended photoperiod and restricted physical activity inherent to cage systems likely contributed to these enhanced production parameters. Although free-range birds consumed more feed due to lower stocking densities, their production efficiency was reduced, highlighting the inherent trade-off between animal welfare and productivity. Carcass traits were generally unaffected by the production system, but significant differences in relative internal organ weights and intestine length were influenced by diet and sex. Fatty acid profiling revealed that free-range birds, especially those on organic diets, had higher levels of beneficial long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids and a more favorable omega-6 to omega-3 ratio, which are important markers of meat nutritional quality. However, organic diets were associated with elevated malondialdehyde levels, indicating increased lipid oxidation, particularly in cage-reared birds. These findings emphasize the complexity of balancing production efficiency, meat quality, and animal welfare in quail production systems and underscore the necessity of optimizing diet formulation and management practices to meet diverse industry and consumer demands.
