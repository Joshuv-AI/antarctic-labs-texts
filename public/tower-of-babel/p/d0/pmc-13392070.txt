Multicenter real world validation of on device single lead ECG parameters for remote cardiac assessment
Authors: Sumei Fan, Deyun Zhang, Yue Wang, Shijia Geng, Kun Lu, Meng Sang, Weilun Xu, Haixue Wang, Qinghao Zhao, Chuandong Cheng, Peng Wang, Shenda Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) parameter measurement outside hospital environments is essential for real-time cardiac health monitoring and telemedicine, yet comprehensive validation of on-device computational methods across heterogeneous real-world populations remains limited. We conducted a real-world validation using two datasets: HeartVoice-ECG-lite (369 participants with single-lead ECG recordings annotated by two cardiologists) and PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ (21,354 patients with 12-lead ECG recordings and diagnostic annotations). FeatureDB (https://github.com/PKUDigitalHealth/FeatureDB) was applied to compute PR, QT, and QTc intervals from single-lead signals. Accuracy was assessed using mean absolute error (MAE), correlation, and Bland-Altman analysis. Diagnostic performance for first-degree atrioventricular block (AVBI, based on PR) and long QT syndrome (LQT, based on QTc) was benchmarked against commercial 12-lead systems (12SL, Uni-G) and an open-source algorithm (Deli) using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. FeatureDB-derived parameters showed high concordance with annotations, with MAEs comparable to inter-observer variability and Pearson correlations ranging from 0.836 to 0.960. For AVBI detection, FeatureDB achieved an AUC of 0.787; for LQT, an AUC of 0.684. These results indicate that FeatureDB enables accurate real-time on-device ECG parameter computation suitable for scalable telemedicine, decentralized screening, and continuous community monitoring.Subject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) continue to pose a significant global health challenge, accounting for over 17.9 million deaths annually and representing nearly one-third of all global deaths1. Despite advances in diagnostics and therapy, the rising prevalence of CVDs—especially in aging populations and low- and middle-income countries—underscores the urgent need for accessible strategies enabling early detection and continuous monitoring2. As such, early identification and monitoring of cardiac abnormalities are critical to mitigating the societal and economic impact of these diseases. In recent years, single-lead devices have revolutionized cardiac monitoring by offering non‑invasive, continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters that bridges traditional intermittent assessments and patient care3–5. Their portability and real‑time data transmission enable the detection of transient arrhythmias and enhance patient engagement4,6. These devices democratize cardiac care by expanding access to key ECG parameters — heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval — that are vital for diagnosing arrhythmias, ischemia, and other cardiovascular conditions, guiding risk stratification and therapy7. Precise parameters support large‑scale population health and personalized medicine initiatives8. To realize their clinical potential, however, the computational methods embedded in these devices must achieve accuracy comparable to doctor.

Recent studies have explored algorithmic approaches for calculating ECG parameters from wearable devices, leveraging signal processing and machine learning to improve precision under conditions of noise, motion, or low signal quality9–11. Ho et al.9 demonstrated that Apple Watch and Garmin Forerunner provide heart rate estimates closely aligned with ECG parameters during exercise. Hwang et al.10 confirmed the overall accuracy of consumer wearables in measuring heart rates during supraventricular tachycardia, though performance varied by device. Lu et al.11 reported strong correlation (r = 0.84, p < 0.01) between single-lead and conventional ECG in heart rate parameters, suggesting potential reliability for arrhythmia detection. While these findings highlight progress in wearable cardiac monitoring12,13, most prior studies have focused on limited parameters or small, controlled cohorts. Comprehensive validation of single-lead devices in real-world, heterogeneous populations remains lacking. In particular, there is a need to assess their performance in calculating multiple ECG parameters simultaneously, beyond simple heart rate estimation, and to benchmark these outputs against both ECG machines and expert evaluations.

To address this gap, this study aims to conduct a comprehensive real-world validation of ECG parameter calculation methods (Feature Database, FeatureDB) tailored for single-lead monitoring devices in real-world14–16 (Fig. 1). The primary aim is to assess the concordance and correlation between ECG parameters obtained from single-lead devices and observed by the doctor, leveraging a collected dataset from the real-world. Additionally, the research systematically compares the performance of single-lead device-based parameter calculations against doctor assessments across multiple datasets, including a publicly available benchmark dataset and proprietary data from single-lead devices. We emphasize that this work demonstrates technical feasibility for screening rather than diagnostic equivalence to clinical-grade systems. By validating FeatureDB across wearable and clinical ECG datasets, we aim to establish its potential as a preliminary screening tool for identifying individuals who may benefit from further clinical evaluation, providing evidence for its deployment in decentralized cardiac screening and long-term health management.

Results

A total of 21,723 participants were included across the two datasets analyzed in this study (Table 2). The HeartVoice-ECG-lite cohort comprised 369 individuals (mean age 42 ± 17 years, 49.3% female), representing real-world users of single-lead wearable devices in community and ambulatory monitoring settings. In contrast, the PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ dataset included 21,354 patients (mean age 60 ± 17 years, 47.9% female) collected from clinical 12-lead ECG systems, serving as a reference for benchmarking FeatureDB against established diagnostic platforms. Anthropometric characteristics, including height and weight, were comparable between the two cohorts. Collectively, these datasets provided complementary perspectives—HeartVoice-ECG-lite emphasizing practical deployment in low-resource, real-world contexts, and PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ reflecting clinical diagnostic standards for robust external validation.

FeatureDB demonstrated high concordance with expert annotations from two cardiologists in the HeartVoice-ECG-lite dataset. The MAE between FeatureDB and Doctor A was 8.46 ms (95% CI: 7.64–9.36) for PR, 9.03 ms (95% CI: 8.24–9.83) for QT, and 10.09 ms (95% CI: 9.19–11.08) for QTc. The MAE between FeatureDB and Doctor B was 6.53 ms (95% CI: 5.76–7.32) for PR, 10.14 ms (95% CI: 9.21–11.14) for QT, and 11.09 ms (95% CI: 10.16–12.19) for QTc. These values were comparable to the inter-observer MAE between the two cardiologists: 6.40 ms (95% CI: 5.67–7.14) for PR, 15.01 ms (95% CI: 13.95–16.12) for QT, and 16.54 ms (95% CI: 15.33–17.66) for QTc. Conversely, Fig. 2D highlights a challenging case where subtle changes in QRS-wave morphology resulted in minor discrepancies between the algorithm’s calculation and the human annotations, though the difference remained clinically marginal. These combined findings support the robustness and high parameter fidelity of FeatureDB across diverse ECG signal qualities and morphologies, making it highly suitable for real-time monitoring applications in resource-constrained environments.

The correlation analysis further validated the precision of the FeatureDB algorithm, demonstrating a strong linear relationship between its output and the doctors’ annotations across all tested ECG parameters (Fig. 3). For the PR interval, FeatureDB showed a strong correlation with both Doctor A (r = 0.836, p < 0.001) and Doctor B (r = 0.908, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, FeatureDB’s correlation with Doctor B was nearly as high as the inter-observer correlation between the two doctors (r = 0.911, p < 0.001, Fig. 3C). Similar high correlations were observed for the QT interval (FeatureDB vs. Doctor A: r = 0.948, p < 0.001; FeatureDB vs. Doctor B: r = 0.960, p < 0.001; Fig. 3D, E), which compared favorably to the inter-observer correlation (r = 0.951, p < 0.001, Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the correlation for the QTc remained high (FeatureDB vs. Doctor A: r = 0.892, p < 0.001; FeatureDB vs. Doctor B: r = 0.916, p < 0.001; Fig. 3G, H), again closely matching the inter-observer correlation (r = 0.889, p < 0.001, Fig. 3I). The consistency across all parameters underscores the algorithm’s capability to replicate expert-level parameters, a critical requirement for autonomous, on-device computation. Bland-Altman analysis was conducted to assess the agreement and potential systematic bias between FeatureDB and the expert annotations (Fig. 4). For the PR interval (FeatureDB vs. Doctor A and Doctor B, Fig. 4A, B), the mean differences (bias) were very close to zero, -0.715 ms and -5.076 ms, respectively, indicating minimal systematic error. The limits of agreement (LoA) for PR were tight, comparable to the LoA observed between Doctor A and Doctor B (Mean: -5.076 ms, Fig. 4C). Analysis of the QT interval also showed strong agreement, though the bias for FeatureDB vs. Doctor A was slightly positive (5.098 ms, Fig. 4D), while FeatureDB vs. Doctor B was negative (-9.767 ms, Fig. 4E). Importantly, the spread of the data within the LoA for FeatureDB was generally tighter than the inter-observer LoA for the QT interval (Mean: -14.885 ms, Fig. 4F), particularly when compared to Doctor B. Similar strong agreement with small bias was demonstrated for the QTc (FeatureDB vs. Doctor A: 5.730 ms; FeatureDB vs. Doctor B: -10.638 ms; Fig. 4G, H). The minimal bias and narrow LoA across all parameters confirm that FeatureDB provides parameters that are interchangeable with expert annotations in clinical practice, further supporting its use in decentralized cardiac monitoring.

We benchmarked the diagnostic performance of FeatureDB in identifying cardiac abnormalities against two commercial clinical ECG analysis systems (12SL and UniG) and an open-source algorithm (Deli) using the PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ dataset, focusing on AVBI (based on PR) and LQT (based on QTc) (Table 1). For AVBI detection, FeatureDB achieved a high AUC of 0.787 and an accuracy of 0.809, demonstrating strong capability. This performance was slightly lower than that of the two established 12-lead commercial systems (12SL AUC: 0.859, UniG AUC: 0.812) but significantly outperformed the Deli algorithm (AUC: 0.501). FeatureDB also maintained a clinically relevant sensitivity of 0.762 and specificity of 0.811 for AVBI. For LQT detection, FeatureDB yielded an AUC of 0.684 and an Accuracy of 0.75. While its AUC was lower than that of 12SL (0.716), it was superior to both UniG (0.605) and Deli (0.569). It is noteworthy that FeatureDB achieved a sensitivity of 0.617 for LQT detection, which was comparable to 12SL (0.600) and higher than Uni-G (0.278). However, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as FeatureDB operates on single-lead input while 12SL and Uni-G utilize 12-lead recordings. Furthermore, the LQT positive class was small (n = 115, 0.54%), these sensitivity estimates carry wide 95% confidence intervals. All LQT performance estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution given the limited number of positive cases. The results indicate that single-lead FeatureDB can achieve a meaningful level of screening performance in resource-limited settings, though not equivalent to the clinical reference standard provided by 12-lead systems. This positions FeatureDB as a preliminary screening tool rather than a diagnostic replacement for clinical-grade ECG systems.

Discussion

This study addressed a critical gap in decentralized cardiac monitoring by providing a comprehensive, real-world validation of a computational method, FeatureDB, for accurately deriving key ECG parameters from single-lead devices. Our primary objective was to rigorously benchmark the performance of FeatureDB against both expert human annotations and established 12-lead clinical systems across diverse datasets, including the proprietary HeartVoice-ECG-lite collected in China and the public PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ dataset. The deployment of single-lead devices offers a vital, accessible pathway for early detection and continuous monitoring in resource-limited and community settings. Our results confirm that the ECG parameters (PR, QT, and QTc) calculated by FeatureDB demonstrate a high level of concordance and correlation with cardiologist assessments, with parameter accuracy comparable to inter-observer variability (Figs. 2–4). Furthermore, when applied to cardiac abnormality detection, FeatureDB achieved clinically meaningful diagnostic performance for AVBI and LQT, rivaling or even surpassing some commercial and open-source algorithms (Table 1). These findings strongly validate the feasibility and reliability of on-device single-lead ECG parameter computation, enhancing the potential for these wearable technologies to revolutionize routine cardiovascular screening and management outside of traditional healthcare settings. We acknowledge that comparing FeatureDB (single-lead) with 12SL and Uni-G (12-lead) involves different input modalities, which preclude a straightforward head-to-head accuracy comparison. The purpose of our benchmark was to contextualize single-lead device performance against established clinical standards. Single-lead wearable devices are not intended to replace 12-lead ECG for definitive diagnosis, but rather to enable accessible screening and continuous monitoring in settings where conventional ECG is unavailable. Our results demonstrate that single-lead devices can achieve a clinically meaningful level of performance—particularly for parameter estimation (PR, QT, QTc)—though the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity remain below those of 12-lead systems.

Accurate computation of ECG parameters enables early identification of conduction abnormalities (e.g., AVBI via PR) and repolarization abnormalities (e.g., LQT) in settings where standard 12-lead ECGs are unavailable or impractical. Previous work has shown that single-lead devices can reliably monitor heart rate and detect arrhythmias4, but fewer studies have addressed multi-parameter computation (PR/QT/QTc) in real-world settings17,18. To position our work within the current landscape, we compared FeatureDB with recent approaches for single-lead ECG parameter computation. Alam et al.19 developed QTNet, a deep learning model for estimating QT intervals from Lead-I ECG, achieving mean absolute errors of 12.63 ms and 12.30 ms on internal and external validation sets, respectively, with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.91–0.92. Hoek et al.20 conducted a systematic review of smart devices for QT interval measurement, reporting that QT and QTc parameters are feasible but generally less reliable than simple heart rate estimation. In our study, FeatureDB achieved approximately 10 ms MAE for QT interval estimation on the HeartVoice-ECG-lite dataset, which is comparable to deep learning approaches while offering complementary advantages: full interpretability as a rule-based algorithm and on-device computational efficiency without requiring large-scale training datasets. From a clinical perspective, continuous monitoring of QT or PR intervals could facilitate outpatient surveillance of drug-induced conduction changes or QT prolongation (e.g., in anti-arrhythmic, psychiatric, or oncology medications)19, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to cardiologists or 12-lead ECG devices is constrained. The translation from ECG parameter accuracy to improved clinical outcomes remains to be established, and future work should quantify how algorithm-based monitoring impacts clinical decision-making and cardiovascular event reduction21,22.

Our results demonstrated high correlation coefficients between FeatureDB-computed parameters and physician annotations (r > 0.90 for PR, QT, and QTc intervals), yet moderate sensitivity for detecting AVB1 and QTc prolongation. This apparent discrepancy warrants careful interpretation, as correlation and sensitivity measure fundamentally different aspects of algorithm performance. Correlation coefficients quantify overall agreement across all samples, whereas sensitivity measures the ability to correctly identify cases exceeding specific diagnostic thresholds. Several interconnected factors contribute to this observation. First, diagnostic thresholds represent sharp boundaries in a continuous parameter space; patients with values near these thresholds are particularly susceptible to misclassification due to minor measurement errors, even with high overall correlation23. Second, class imbalance in our validation datasets (AVBI 3.59%, LQT 0.54% in PTB-XL) means that false negatives have a disproportionate impact on sensitivity, as conventional classifiers tend to be biased toward the majority class in imbalanced datasets24. Third, inter-observer variability in ECG parameter measurement introduces noise at classification boundaries; Völker et al.25 reported that the Q2/Q98 span for QT interval measurement among 18 expert investigators ranged from 39 to 99 ms, indicating that 96% of measurements fell within a 68 ms range for the same ECG beat. Fourth, FeatureDB’s sequential pipeline means that errors in earlier stages (e.g., P-wave onset detection) propagate to downstream calculations, potentially shifting borderline cases across thresholds. These factors collectively explain why high parameter correlation does not guarantee high classification sensitivity, particularly for conditions with low prevalence and narrow diagnostic margins.

One of the key strengths of our study is the validation of FeatureDB across both a real-world, community single-lead dataset (HeartVoice-ECG-lite) and a large benchmark clinical 12-lead dataset (PTB-XL/PTB-XL+). This dual-dataset approach enhances generalizability–we show that the algorithm remains accurate under variable signal quality, device conditions, and real-world noise. Prior reviews have emphasized that many wearable ECG studies are conducted under controlled conditions or small cohorts26, and that generalizability across populations, devices, and settings remains a major hurdle. Lim et al.27 posits that real-world, long-term performance and diverse demographic inclusion are needed to move toward clinical adoption. Our usage of a Chinese single-lead ECG dataset and a large international dataset addresses this gap in part. Importantly, the low mean absolute errors and high agreement metrics in our single-lead cohort under ambulatory conditions suggest that device-algorithm performance is robust beyond laboratory settings. Moreover, the use of wearables in heterogeneous populations (e.g., variation in age, body habitus, signal noise) is crucial to ensure equitable utility in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The literature on wearable ECG in LMICs remains sparse. Thus, our work contributes to filling this evidence gap. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate the feasibility of on-device parameter computation for parameter-based screening, though further validation in accordance with regulatory requirements is needed before clinical application.

The advent of single-lead ECG monitoring paired with on-device or edge-embedded algorithms opens new possibilities for decentralized cardiovascular screening and continuous monitoring—especially relevant in low-resource settings. Traditional 12-lead ECG machines require infrastructure, trained personnel, and often hospital-based workflows; in contrast, wearable or patch-based single-lead systems enable ambulatory or home-based acquisition with minimal supervision28. Our findings indicating that doctor-level parameters accuracy support a paradigm in which primary care, community health workers, or even patients themselves can deploy monitoring, triggering referral or intervention when ECG parameter abnormalities (e.g., QTc or PR) are detected. Wearable device reviews have highlighted that single-lead ECG devices can bridge intermittent screening gaps and capture transient events29. The scalability is especially compelling: in aging populations or in LMICs with rising cardiovascular disease burden, such decentralized monitoring can reduce the burden on tertiary centers and enable earlier detection, thereby potentially reducing downstream morbidity and cost. From the public health viewpoint, large-scale data collection from wearables also offers population-level risk stratification and monitoring of intervention uptake. However, successful implementation hinges on algorithmic trustworthiness, user adoption, device affordability, connectivity, data privacy, and health system integration. While our algorithmic results provide a strong technical basis, the next steps include implementation studies assessing workflow integration, user adherence, referral pathways, and cost-effectiveness in low-resource settings. Nevertheless, our work advances the vision of continuous “always-on” cardiac monitoring using single-lead devices and embedded analytics.

Despite the promising findings, our study has limitations that merit discussion and highlight future research directions. First, although our cohorts included a real-world single-lead dataset and a large benchmark dataset, certain populations were under-represented (e.g., pediatric subjects, extreme body habitus, severe arrhythmia burden), and limited baseline clinical characterization precluded subgroup analyses by demographic or clinical factors. Other studies3 show that in children, the accuracy of single-lead ECG parameters may be lower (Intra-class Correlation Coefficient for QTc 0.53). Second, although we validated the accuracy of ECG parameters and the detection of specific diagnoses (AVBI, LQT), the translation to long-term outcomes (e.g., arrhythmia incidence, adverse events) remains untested30. In particular, because the HeartVoice-ECG-lite dataset did not collect comorbidity, medication, or clinical indication data, no subgroup analysis of drug-induced QT prolongation could be performed. Third, the diagnostic classification performance should be interpreted with caution due to class imbalance in the datasets, with positive cases representing only 3.59% for AVB1 and 0.54% for LQT detection, which may bias performance estimates despite appropriate metric selection.

This study demonstrates the feasibility and accuracy of on-device ECG parameter computation using FeatureDB in real-world, low-resource settings. The system achieved performance comparable to cardiologists in PR, QT, and QTc parameter estimation and maintained robustness across varying signal qualities. When benchmarked against 12-lead clinical systems, FeatureDB provides a meaningful reference point for understanding the capabilities and limitations of single-lead devices in cardiac abnormality detection. These findings support the potential of single-lead devices for scalable, real-time cardiac health screening and continuous monitoring outside conventional hospital environments, while acknowledging that they serve a complementary role rather than a replacement for 12-lead ECG diagnosis. Moreover, further optimization and validation are warranted to advance toward clinical applicability.

Methods

Study design

This study conducted a comprehensive real-world validation of ECG parameter calculation methods for single-lead monitoring devices, comparing their performance with both doctor and standard 12-lead ECG machines. Two distinct datasets were used (Table 2, Fig. 5). The first single-lead dataset, HeartVoice-ECG-lite, comprised ECG recordings collected via WenXinWuYang14–16 from 369 participants during routine cardiac health monitoring in real-world settings. Unlike conventional clinical trials that enroll subjects under controlled conditions, HeartVoice-ECG-lite uses a real-world data collection paradigm in which participants are ordinary device users. Participants were randomly sampled from the broader user base of the WenXinWuYang single-lead ECG device in China rather than self-selected, and all recordings were obtained in community- and home-based (non-hospital) real-world settings. Each participant contributed a single Lead-I recording of approximately 30 s. To maximize user adherence and sustainability of daily monitoring, the data collection protocol did not mandate detailed clinical background information (e.g., medical history, medications); basic demographic characteristics were recorded. This dataset included real-world ECG signals annotated with waveform parameters by two doctors. The second dataset (PTB-XL and PTB-XL + 31,32) contained ECG parameters derived from two commercial ECG analysis software systems (12SL and Uni-G) and an open-source algorithm (Deli), along with doctor-reported cardiac abnormality labels. These datasets provided complementary perspectives: HeartVoice-ECG-lite highlighted the practical applications of emerging wearable technologies and enabled evaluation of discrepancies between FeatureDB-derived ECG parameters and expert annotations, whereas PTB-XL31 and PTB-XL + 32 reflected real-world clinical practice and served as standardized benchmarks for assessing FeatureDB’s capability in cardiac abnormality detection. By integrating these diverse data sources, this study robustly evaluated the performance of single-lead ECG parameter calculation methods under both controlled and real-world conditions, underscoring the potential of single-lead devices to enable scalable and accessible cardiac health monitoring. The study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-23189). Due to the retrospective study and the use of fully de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center.

Feature Database

FeatureDB (https://github.com/PKUDigitalHealth/FeatureDB) is a dedicated method developed for the computation of ECG parameters from single-lead ECG signals acquired by wearable devices14–16. The algorithm implements a complete pipeline for ECG parameter extraction from single-lead signals and is composed of five sequential stages: signal preprocessing, R-peak detection, average waveform template construction, waveform boundary delineation, and interval calculation with QTc correction (Fig. 1). The full algorithmic specification is provided below.

Signal preprocessing. The raw ECG signal is first resampled to 500 Hz using linear interpolation. Baseline correction is performed by subtracting a local mean calculated from a moving average filter with a window radius of 3 samples. Amplitude normalization is then applied using histogram percentile-based scaling with a 1% truncation threshold to suppress extreme outliers.

Signal preprocessing. The raw ECG signal is first resampled to 500 Hz using linear interpolation. Baseline correction is performed by subtracting a local mean calculated from a moving average filter with a window radius of 3 samples. Amplitude normalization is then applied using histogram percentile-based scaling with a 1% truncation threshold to suppress extreme outliers.

R-peak detection. A 50 Hz IIR notch filter with a quality factor Q = 50 is applied to remove powerline interference. The signal then passes through two stages of Butterworth bandpass filtering: a first stage (0.5–45 Hz, 4th order, zero-phase filtfilt implementation) to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise, and a second stage (3–40 Hz, 4th order, zero-phase) to optimize the signal for peak detection. R-peak positions are identified using the ecg_peaks function from the NeuroKit2 Python library.

R-peak detection. A 50 Hz IIR notch filter with a quality factor Q = 50 is applied to remove powerline interference. The signal then passes through two stages of Butterworth bandpass filtering: a first stage (0.5–45 Hz, 4th order, zero-phase filtfilt implementation) to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise, and a second stage (3–40 Hz, 4th order, zero-phase) to optimize the signal for peak detection. R-peak positions are identified using the ecg_peaks function from the NeuroKit2 Python library.

Average waveform template construction. The signal is filtered again using a Butterworth bandpass filter (0.75–35 Hz, 4th order) optimized for waveform morphology. Individual heartbeats are extracted by segmenting the signal around each R-peak, from 35% of the median RR interval before the R-peak to 80% after. A representative average waveform template is constructed by computing the point-wise mean across all segmented beats.

Average waveform template construction. The signal is filtered again using a Butterworth bandpass filter (0.75–35 Hz, 4th order) optimized for waveform morphology. Individual heartbeats are extracted by segmenting the signal around each R-peak, from 35% of the median RR interval before the R-peak to 80% after. A representative average waveform template is constructed by computing the point-wise mean across all segmented beats.

Waveform boundary delineation. The average waveform is upsampled to 2000 Hz for improved temporal precision. An a-trous (undecimated) wavelet transform is applied with decomposition across 9 scales (2⁰ to 2⁸). The wavelet filter bank uses a quadratic spline wavelet with low-pass H filter coefficients [1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8] and high-pass G filter coefficients [2, −2]. Between scales, the filter length is extended by inserting 2ˠ − 1 zeros between coefficients (dyadic zero-insertion). P-wave and T-wave peaks are identified by detecting zero-crossings between adjacent extrema of opposite polarity in the wavelet coefficients. The search window for the T-wave is defined from half the QRS width to twice the RT interval after the R-peak, analyzed at scale 2³ (adaptively adjusted based on heart rate); the P-wave is searched in a window before the R-peak at scale 2². Waveform boundaries are determined by threshold crossing on the wavelet coefficients: QRS complex onset and offset, 50% threshold at scale 2²; P-wave onset and offset, 40% threshold at scale 2²; T-wave onset, 60% threshold at scale 2³; T-wave offset, 40% threshold at scale 2³. All threshold percentages are relative to the maximum absolute wavelet-coefficient amplitude within the corresponding search window.

FeatureDB is a rule-based algorithm parameterized according to established signal-processing principles and domain expertise. Unlike machine-learning approaches, it does not involve data-driven optimization or model training and therefore does not require data partitioning for training/validation splits. Neither the HeartVoice-ECG-lite nor the PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ datasets were used during algorithm development; both were accessed exclusively for independent validation.

Single-lead device and doctor annotation

The HeartVoice-ECG-lite dataset comprises single-lead ECG recordings obtained via the WenXinWuYang device within real-world cardiac monitoring contexts. The device utilizes a Lead I configuration—defined as the potential difference between the left and right arms—aligning with the standards of most consumer-grade wearable ECG systems. Signals were acquired at a native sampling frequency of 125 Hz and were uniformly resampled to 500 Hz prior to parameter computation. Ground truth labels were established independently by two board-certified cardiologists. These experts manually measured the PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval, with the QTc interval subsequently derived using Bazett’s formula. To ensure unbiased assessment, both annotators remained blinded to the algorithmic outputs and to each other’s measurements throughout the independent annotation phase. This rigorous approach aimed to provide a gold standard reference for evaluating the performance of ECG parameter calculation methods derived from single-lead devices. By incorporating expert-level annotations, the HeartVoice-ECG-lite dataset offers a valuable resource for benchmarking algorithm accuracy in diverse and practical settings, highlighting its importance in advancing the field of wearable technology for scalable and reliable cardiac health monitoring.

PTB-XL and PTB-XL+ dataset

The PTB-XL31 and PTB-XL + 32 dataset are a large collection of clinical 12-lead ECG recordings with diagnostic annotations, serving as a benchmark for evaluating ECG analysis techniques. We utilized two commercial ECG analysis systems—12SL (GE Healthcare)33 and Uni-G (The University of Glasgow ECG Analysis Program)34—and one open-source algorithm, Deli35, for comparative analysis. Lead I was extracted from the 12-lead recordings for FeatureDB analysis. A detailed description of the dataset, including patient characteristics and diagnostic label distributions, is provided in Supplementary Note 1. For the PTB-XL/PTB-XL+ dataset, Lead I was extracted from the 12-lead ECG recordings for FeatureDB analysis to ensure consistency with the single-lead input modality.

Outcomes

This study evaluated the accuracy of FeatureDB in calculating ECG parameters in real-world settings from two core perspectives. First, from the ECG parameters perspective, FeatureDB-derived results were compared with cardiologist annotations, and the distribution, mean absolute error (MAE), agreement, and correlation between the two were analyzed to assess parameters performance. Second, from the cardiac abnormality detection perspective, FeatureDB was compared with commercial 12-lead ECG machines, reporting the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in detecting cardiac abnormalities, using first degree atrioventricular block (AVBI) and long QT syndrome (LQT) as representative examples. For AVBI classification, FeatureDB PR intervals were offset-calibrated, whereas QTc was left uncalibrated. It is important to note that this study does not constitute a head-to-head accuracy comparison between single-lead and 12-lead ECG analysis systems, as they operate on fundamentally different input modalities. Rather, we establish a cross-modality reference benchmark: the performance of commercial 12-lead systems (12SL, Uni-G) serves as a clinical reference standard, against which the parameter measurement capability of single-lead FeatureDB can be contextualized. This comparison addresses a practical question—what level of parameter measurement and screening performance can be achieved by single-lead devices in settings where 12-lead ECG is unavailable or impractical? The distinct input modalities should be considered when interpreting these results.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Pearson correlation was used for continuous variables conforming to normal distribution, with p-values calculated using t-test. Bland-Altman plots were used to assess the level of consistency between two methods or devices, with mean and 1.96 standard deviation (SD). The diagnostic performance of FeatureDB were assessed by using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, F1 score, Positive prediction value (PPV), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). To derive binary positive/negative predictions for sensitivity and specificity, AVBI was defined as PR > 200 ms and LQT using a sex-specific QTc threshold of > 450 ms in males and > 460 ms in females. The same thresholds were applied uniformly to the PR and QTc values output by all evaluated algorithms (FeatureDB, 12SL, Uni-G, and Deli); the decision threshold was thus held constant across methods and does not confound the sensitivity comparison. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) for MAE, AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, PPV, and MCC were calculated using the bootstrap method with 1000 resamples. All data were analyzed by Origin 2018.
