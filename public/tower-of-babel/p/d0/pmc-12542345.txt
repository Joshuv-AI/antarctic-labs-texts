Accessibility and difficulties of COVID-19-related healthcare services for Vietnamese migrants in Japan: a cross-sectional online survey
Authors: Kyoko Sudo, Mihoko Fujii, Azusa Iwamoto, Moe Moe Thandar, Miwa Kanda, Haru Hoshino, Saki Ikeda, Masami Fujita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic revealed significant gaps in healthcare access for migrants, including Vietnamese migrants in Japan who are young and growing. This study aimed to clarify the accessibility and difficulties related to COVID-19 healthcare services (vaccination, testing, and medical care) and identify factors associated with these difficulties.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted from March to April 2023 through a Vietnamese social media platform with 1.5 million followers. Data from 418 respondents were analysed descriptively, and multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with experienced difficulties. Independent variables included age, education, residential status, length of stay in Japan, Japanese language proficiency, underlying diseases, and health insurance.ResultsAmong 418 participants, 47.1% were Technical Intern Trainees, and 93.3% were under 30. Over 95% of the participants perceived that COVID-19 vaccination, testing, and medical care were accessible if needed; however, 26.6% had unmet healthcare needs. The self-reported uptake rate was 90.4% for vaccination and 84.4% for testing among all 418 participants, and 84.0% of 97 participants who tested positive accessed medical care. Among those who received services, 18.8% experienced difficulties with vaccination, 24.1% with testing, and 69.1% with medical care. Major difficulties included cost, administrative requirements, and long waiting times. Factors such as age, education, length of time living in Japan, and underlying diseases were associated with greater difficulties. Japanese language proficiency specifically reduced the odds of difficulties in accessing medical care (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.008, confidence interval [CI] = 0.001-0.117, p <.001) but not for vaccination (AOR = 0.481, CI = 0.186–1.247, p =.132) or testing (AOR = 0.559, CI = 0.230–1.356, p =.198).ConclusionsDespite high self-reported accessibility to COVID-19-related healthcare services, unmet needs for general healthcare persisted among one-fourth of the participants. These challenges were influenced by individual and structural factors, particularly language, education, and system navigation. Tailored interventions such as multilingual support, simplified administrative processes, and enhanced community outreach are essential to address disparities for Vietnamese migrants. These findings underscore the importance of integrating migrants into health systems and preparedness frameworks to ensure equitable healthcare access during future health emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13174-x.

Background

Migrant health, which includes the vulnerability of chronic and infectious diseases due to a variety of different physical and mental health needs, their country of origin, their host country’s integration policies, and living and working conditions, is a global issue considering the increasing numbers of migrant [1]. It has been recognized that migrant health is an integral part of achieving universal health coverage and sustainable development goals due to its inextricable link to an entire nation’s health [2]. Therefore, global efforts beyond nation and region are needed based on the evidence [3]. The gap in health impacts between migrants and host country residents has been highlighted during the pandemic of Corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [4–7]. In Japan, COVID-19 vaccination started in February 2021 free of charge for all residents including migrants [8]. The Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare notified municipalities that migrants with various types of residency status should be included in the vaccinations [9, 10], but there were news reports of vaccination rates being lower among certain municipalities [11–13]. Practically, there is little research on responses to health conditions related to COVID-19 infection in Japan [14, 15]. However, studies in other countries examined a wide range of barriers to healthcare access for migrants including financial constraints [16], communication encompassing linguistic and cultural aspects [17], poor health literacy, and a lack of understanding of the healthcare system and low awareness of their right to healthcare [18]. Organizational and administrative issues [19] and bureaucratic obstacles including paperwork and registration systems especially for refugees and asylum seekers [20] were also found in a previous study. From COVID-19 pandemic responses, innovative interventions such as commencing online medical consultation and prescriptions and renewing the database for infectious diseases have emerged. In Japan, remote medical interpretation is recognized as one of the effective interventions to improve healthcare access for migrants in Japan to mitigate exposure to infectious diseases [21]. However, countermeasures to facilitate healthcare access against the above-mentioned barriers especially for migrants still require innovation after experiencing health security responses.

Around 520,000 Vietnamese live in Japan and account for 16% of the total number of migrants in 2022, the second-highest nationality in number among migrants, and this has been increasing [22]. About half of the technical intern trainees in Japan are Vietnamese [23]. The Technical Intern Trainees (TIT) scheme was established in Japan in 1993 to develop human resources that will contribute to the economic development of their own country by transferring skills, techniques, and knowledge obtained in Japan [24]. The number of Vietnamese TIT began to increase in 2017 when the Act on Proper Technical Intern Training and Protection of Technical Intern Trainees was enacted [25]. Moreover, Vietnamese in Japan are characterized as a young-generation community as 65% of them are 20 to 30 years old [22]. This proportion might be caused by the residential status of the Vietnamese, of whom 62% are classified as TIT (Ginou-jisshuu), Specified Skilled (Tokutei-ginou), and Students (Ryugaku) and are members of the young generation and without family. Vietnamese migrants in Japan comprise a young generation and have just begun to increase in number around the past 5 years from 2018 [26].

Four levels of migrant vulnerability have been identified, including individual, family, community, and structure [27]. Individuals and their families live within a community context in which a community with strong networks and access to resources can support individual and family protection [27]. From the viewpoint of vulnerability, the Vietnamese community could be an immature or unformed community without networks and resources to mitigate individual and family risk. They could face difficulties in responding easily to risks, including those related to health issues, due to Vietnamese migrants’ characteristics in Japan that they are young and without family if compared with other communities in which several generations have lived in Japan. The vulnerability in the Vietnamese community was also evidenced in our preliminary survey during the COVID-19 pandemic, which showed them not taking tests for a COVID-19 diagnosis because of the costs, the huge gap in vaccination uptake rate between those with and without residential status, and the difficulty in paying living costs due to quitting a job or a change in working conditions [28].

This study aimed to clarify the accessibility and difficulties of COVID-19-related healthcare services including vaccination, testing, and medical care for Vietnamese in Japan and identify factors associated with these difficulties when they receive these services. The findings could serve as evidence to integrate migrants into the processes of pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response of the host country in the potential next pandemic.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

A cross-sectional online survey was performed through the Facebook page “TAIHEN” platform, which has approximately 1.5 million Vietnamese followers of whom 100,000 live in Japan. Data were collected from 9 March to 30 April 2023. The followers who found the article about survey recruitment responded voluntarily to the questionnaire through the Microsoft Forms link. The recruitment article was posted three times during the data collection period. This survey was conducted anonymously, and a unique ID with an 8-digit birthday and 2 or 3 initial alphabets of their name was used to identify one response and avoid duplication of responses. The inclusion criteria of the participants were Vietnamese nationals living in Japan who agreed to and provided their consent to participate in the survey, were aged 18 years and over, who could access an internet connection, and could read and write Vietnamese unaided. The exclusion criterion was living in Japan for 6 months or less, which we assumed to mean that the migrants were still unfamiliar with daily life in Japan. Moreover, many Vietnamese who arrived within six months of the study may have been reluctant to seek COVID-19 healthcare in Japan, as vaccination schemes were already accessible in both Japan and Vietnam by March 2023.

Questionnaire and variables

The questionnaire was developed originally for our survey by the authors referring to relevant research [29, 30]. Initially, it was developed in both English (Supplement 1) and Japanese, and then the Japanese version was translated into Vietnamese, followed by verification by Vietnamese native speakers and a pilot test conducted to confirm the accuracy of the wording. The questionnaire consisted of three parts: (1) demographic characteristics, (2) perception of healthcare accessibility and unmet healthcare needs in Japan, and (3) accessibility of COVID-19-related healthcare services and difficulties when they received them. The demographic characteristics included age, sex, education attainment, residential status, length of stay in Japan and so on. Perception of healthcare accessibility was indicated with the answer “Yes”, “No”, or “Don’t know” to the question “Do you think you would be able to get health care (e.g. Vaccination, Examination, seeing a doctor) in Japan if you think you need it?” Unmet healthcare needs were indicated with the answer “Yes” or “No” to the question “In the past 12 months, were you ever unable to see a doctor in Japan for an illness/condition despite wanting to?” Accessibility of COVID-19-related healthcare services was composed of perception and uptake. The perception was indicated with “Yes”, “No”, or “Don’t know” responses to the questions of thoughts being able to obtain vaccine, testing (polymerase chain reaction test or antigen test), and medical care for COVID-19 when needed. The uptake rates were based on participant responses. Difficulties experienced in receiving COVID-19 vaccination, testing, and medical care were responded to with “Yes” or “No” answers, which were collected by those who received COVID-19-related healthcare services.

Statistical analysis

The participants’ demographic characteristics, perception of healthcare accessibility and the rate of unmet healthcare needs in Japan, and the rates of perception of accessibility, uptake, and of experiencing difficulties in COVID-19 vaccination, testing, and medical care were analysed descriptively. Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using demographic data dichotomized except for a place of residence. Place of residence was excluded because it was indicated only at the prefectural level in this study and did not reflect detailed regional characteristics such as urban or rural settings, which are critical for understanding transportation barriers or healthcare accessibility differences. Additionally, the survey sample showed a geographical bias, with a concentration of participants in certain prefectures, limiting its representativeness for regional analysis. Including place of residence in the model without more granular data (e.g., rural/urban classification or access to healthcare facilities) could introduce misinterpretation of the results. Then multiple logistic regression was conducted to identify the factors associated with difficulties in vaccination, testing, and medical care during the entire process of COVID-19. The same independent variables, which were related to at least one of those factors of vaccination, testing, and medical care by univariate analysis (p <.05), were used for each factor with the forced entry method. Missing values were not imputed. Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0), and the p-value to indicate statistical significance was set at < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Center for Global Health and Medicine, Japan (the institution’s name at the time of ethical approval; currently the Japan Institute for Health Security). All participants joined the survey after providing written informed consent online.

In total, 423 participants joined the survey after three postings of the article including an invitation link to the survey on the Facebook page. The number of viewers at the first post as of 9 March was 61,319, the second post as of 16 March was 72,259, and the last post as of 30 March was 75,358. Five of the 423 respondents were excluded based on the criteria and responses with missing data and the remaining 418 respondents were analysed. As shown in Tables 1, 51.4% (n = 215) of the participants were male, 93.3% (n = 386) were in their 20s (Mean (SD) = 26.8(2.50)), and 69.6% (n = 291) had graduated from high school or lower. Almost half of them, 49.3% (n = 206), lived in the Tohoku region including Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Akita, Yamagata, and Fukushima followed by Ibaraki. Their residential status was classified as Technical Intern Training (TIT) in 47.1% (n = 197), Skilled Labor in 14.4% (n = 60), and Student in 13.9% (n = 58). Length of stay in Japan at the time of the survey was less than three years in 84.4% (n = 353). Self-rated Japanese language proficiency was either Not at all or Limited (Not very well) for 82.7% (n = 346) of all participants. The majority lived in Japan alone (41.4%) or with family (44.3%). Of the participants, only 6.7% (n = 28) had underlying medical conditions, and 5.5% (n = 23) did not have a health insurance card.

Of all 418 participants, 93.5% (n = 391) of those perceived that they would be able to get healthcare in Japan when they needed it. In contrast, 26.6% (n = 111) had unmet healthcare needs. Reasons for unmet healthcare needs were collected using a multiple-choice format, with a total of 112 responses. The most commonly reported reason was “Did not know how to receive care” (n = 49, 43.8%), followed by “Language barriers” (n = 20, 17.9%), “Did not know where to receive care” (n = 18, 16.1%), “Transportation issues” (n = 14, 12.5%), “No time / cannot be absent from school or work” (n = 8, 7.1%), and “Concern about the cost” (n = 3, 2.7%).

Accessibility was measured by perception and uptake. Positive perceptions of participants in getting vaccine, testing, and medical care in Japan were high at 95.7%, 94.7%, and 95.2% among 418 participants, respectively. The self-reported uptake rate was 90.4% for vaccination and 84.4% for testing among all 418 participants, and 84.0% for medical care among 97 participants who tested positive. The most common reason for not receiving a COVID-19 vaccination in Japan was “Did not receive vaccine voucher” (47.5%), followed by “Lost vaccine voucher” (17.5%) and “Illness or vaccination contraindication” (15.0%). For those who did not get tested despite having symptoms, the primary reason was “Confusion and uncertainty regarding testing guidelines and where to go for testing” (50.8%), followed by “Transportation issues” (20.0%) and “Did not have time to get tested” (9.2%). Among participants who did not receive medical care after testing positive, the most frequently cited reasons were “Transportation issues” (31.3%), “Confusion and uncertainty regarding guidelines for receiving care and where to go” (25.0%), and “Did not have time to receive medical care” (18.8%) (Supplement 2).

Difficulties experienced with COVID-19-related healthcare services

The rate of experiencing difficulties in receiving COVID-19 vaccination, testing, and medical care was 18.8% (n = 71) among 378 participants who were vaccinated, 24.1% (n = 85) among 353 participants who were tested, and 69.1% (n = 56) among 81 participants who received medical care (Table 3). On the other hand, the rate of experiencing difficulties in each healthcare service out of 64 participants who received three healthcare services was 71.9% (n = 46) for vaccination, 87.5% (n = 56) for testing and 75.0% (n = 48) for medical care.

In addition, experiences of difficulties in hospitalization, quarantine at a designated hotel or home care, and daily life after infection were also collected from 97 participants who tested positive. In Japan at that time, a person diagnosed as having COVID-19 by any of the test types had to follow regulations that determined where the person was quarantined, including at a hospital, designated hotel, or home. Among those 97 participants who tested positive, 63.9% experienced difficulties in quarantine periods at the hospital, designated hotel, or home, and 71.1% experienced difficulties in daily living after infection. The specific reasons for difficulties in accessing COVID-19 related healthcare services were “Long wait times for vaccination, receiving test results and medical care”, “Administrative requirements to check documents related to visa”, “Booking process”, “Inability to communicate in Japanese”, and “Cost of testing or medical care”. (Supplement 3).

Factors associated with difficulties in receiving COVID-19-related healthcare services

Table 4 shows the results of univariate logistic regression analysis with variables associated with difficulties in obtaining COVID-19-related services. Age (odds ratio (OR) = 0.549, 95% confidence interval (CI): [0.320–0.942], p =.028), length of stay in Japan (OR = 0.340, 95% CI [0.131–0.882], p =.021), and health insurance (OR = 0.327, 95% CI [0.113–0.951], p =.043) were associated with difficulties in COVID-19 vaccination. Age (OR = 0.463, 95% CI [0.277–0.772], p =.003), education (OR = 2.267, 95% CI [1.362–3.773], p =.001), residential status (OR = 1.839, 95% CI [1.115–3.032], p =.016), length of stay in Japan (OR = 0.367, 95% CI [0.151–0.891], p =.022), underlying diseases (OR = 3.835, 95% CI [1.568–9.381], p =.002), and health insurance (OR = 0.296, 95% CI [0.108–0.815], p =.030) were associated with difficulties in obtaining COVID-19 testing. Education (OR = 0.261, 95% CI [0.096–0.712], p =.007), Japanese language proficiency (OR = 0.060, 95% CI [0.017–0.219], p <.001), and underlying diseases (OR = 0.120, 95% CI [0.029–0.505], p =.003) were associated with difficulties in obtaining medical care. Variables associated with difficulties in obtaining each COVID-19-related service were different between vaccination, testing, and medical care. No common variables associated with difficulties in obtaining COVID-19-related services were identified.

Discussion

Interpretation of COVID-19 healthcare service uptake data among Vietnamese in Japan

In this survey, 90% of the participants received COVID-19 vaccine at least once in Japan. Disaggregated data collection by nationality was a challenge in Japan’s recording and reporting of COVID-19 vaccination as such unique data has not been collected before. However, this data differed from those in previous studies [5–7], which had highlighted differences in access to COVID-19 vaccine between migrants and non-migrants. These studies were conducted when a high level of vaccine uptake was required to increase immunogenicity in society. In the present study, the uptake rate was defined as at least one uptake focusing on the participants’ vaccination experience to realize whether they were experiencing a difficulty. Moreover, self-reported uptake rates of receiving testing and medical care were also high, similar to that for vaccination. These results should be interpreted with consideration that the study period was from March to April 2023, when vaccination, testing, and medical care were more systematically available in Japan.

Accessibility to COVID-19-related healthcare services: implications for improving unmet needs for general healthcare

Accessibility was measured by the perception of being able to receive COVID-19-related healthcare services and the self-reported uptake rate, and both were high in this survey. However, 27% of all survey participants had unmet needs in regard to general healthcare in the past 12 months before the survey response date. In a previous study, 32% of Nepalese in Japan reported unmet healthcare needs [31]. While it is difficult to compare our results with this previous study of migrants, nevertheless, similar rates of 28% in European countries [32] and 30% in the Republic of Korea were reported [33]. The unmet healthcare needs identified in the present survey indicate a rate similar to that of previous studies, despite the fact that this survey period and its past 12 months fell within COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, 49 of 111 participants with unmet healthcare needs cited a lack of knowledge about how to receive care as their reason. During the pandemic, effective COVID-19-related healthcare services included providing exact information, utilizing reliable information sources, employing multiple information channels, engaging the community, and offering multilingual support. For example, the Osaka municipality posted COVID-19 vaccine information through company websites advertising recruitment of migrant workers. It distributed updated information on COVID-19 vaccination by continuously collaborating with more than 120 migrant support groups in the community [34]. Preparation of special mass events such as COVID-19-related services, which were expected as global contributions and might lead migrants to promote uptake, could contribute to resolving unmet healthcare needs by applying and adopting this experience to general healthcare services.

Difficulties in COVID-19-related healthcare services and associated factors for Vietnamese migrants in Japan

Differences in experienced difficulties concerning COVID-19 vaccination, receiving testing, and medical care

There were notable differences in difficulties among those receiving COVID-19 vaccination (18.8%, 71/378), testing (24.1%, 85/353), or medical care (69.1%, 56/81). Although perceived accessibility to these services was generally high (see Table 3), a notable proportion of participants who successfully accessed services still reported experiencing difficulties. This suggests that access alone does not eliminate the barriers migrants may face during the process of receiving care. These groups are not entirely overlapping, since vaccination covers a broader population, whereas testing and medical care typically involve those with symptoms or positive tests. Among individuals who underwent all three services, difficulty rates were consistently high, significantly exceeding the overall sample. More interactions with the healthcare system naturally raise the risk of encountering challenges, and those needing all three services may have more severe illnesses or higher health risks, intensifying barriers like cost and access. Finally, difficulties in one step can affect subsequent steps. Medical care tends to be more challenging than vaccination or testing because it specifically targets individuals with suspected symptoms.

The difficulties experienced in obtaining the three COVID-19-related healthcare services were similar, such as long waiting time, administrative requirements, inability to communicate in Japanese, and cost, although the administrative procedures of medical care are not simplified, for instance, as when people use the system for reimbursement of medical care costs. Vaccination and testing schemes for COVID-19 had already been prepared during this study period, thus suggesting that fewer survey participants experienced difficulties in receiving vaccination and testing than medical care.

Factors associated with difficulties in COVID-19 vaccination

From the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis, age, education, and length of stay in Japan were associated with experiencing difficulties in COVID-19 vaccination. Length of stay in the host country, as shown in previous studies [35, 36], is a key social determinant of health. It reflects the process of acculturation, which significantly influences health outcomes and access to healthcare among migrant populations.

Regarding education, we found that participants with higher educational background were more likely to report difficulties in COVID-19 vaccination. This association was particularly evident among TIT, who are generally considered to have lower educational backgrounds [37, 38]. Within this subgroup, participants with higher education were significantly more likely to experience difficulties (reference: high school graduate and lower, OR = 5.351, 95% CI [1.991–14.384], p <.001). In contrast, this pattern was not observed in other residency status subgroups, even Specified Skilled or Students, despite all three groups typically receiving vaccinations through structured, institutionally supported schemes (e.g., workplaces or schools). Based on these results, the observed association between higher education and vaccination difficulty appears to be specific to the TIT group and may not be explained solely by residency status or educational backgrounds. Further investigation is needed to explore contextual, psychosocial, or role-based factors that may contribute to this unexpected trend. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that individuals with higher education may still require targeted support, even though higher education is generally associated with better health literacy and health-seeking behavior.

Factors associated with difficulties in receiving COVID-19 testing

Age, length of stay in Japan, education, and underlying diseases were associated with experiencing difficulties in receiving testing, with the first three factors also associated with vaccination. Underlying diseases were associated with receiving testing and medical care. The present survey dealt with three services: vaccination, testing, and medical care for COVID-19. These findings might indicate that vaccination involved a public health approach, in this context, which refers to strategies aimed at the broader population level, such as mass vaccination campaigns conducted in centralized locations with standardized protocols and minimal customization for individuals. Whereas testing and medical care were provided mainly through an individualized approach, which conversely refers to healthcare services tailored to individual needs, such as diagnostic testing and medical care, which require specific assessments, personalized treatment plans, and more direct interaction with healthcare providers.

Factors associated with difficulties in receiving medical care for COVID-19

Underlying diseases and Japanese language proficiency were associated with difficulties experienced in receiving medical care. A person with underlying diseases always has difficulties concerning the provision of healthcare, contraindications, and side effects of medicine, and transportation to health facilities, among others. Being a migrant is a factor associated with difficulties in accessing healthcare, and having underlying diseases may make access to healthcare even harder. The inequities between migrants and non-migrants in access to healthcare services including overuse of emergency services and underuse of primary healthcare services has been shown [39]. However, the results of the present survey indicated that underlying diseases were negatively associated with difficulties in accessing COVID-19 medical care. People with underlying diseases usually have regular access to health facilities and, therefore, might have found it easier to receive medical care during the pandemic due to increased attention and reliable instructions from physicians.

A unique factor associated with difficulties in medical care was Japanese language proficiency. Those participants with intermediate or native Japanese language proficiency had fewer difficulties in obtaining medical care. As Vietnamese TIT experienced huge difficulties due to limited Japanese language skills when they went to see a doctor by themselves, they delayed seeing a doctor and going to a hospital without support, were unable to confirm queries and complaints, and refrained from going to hospital due to work priorities [40]. Even highly skilled foreign professionals felt that the capacity to communicate in Japanese was a prerequisite for receiving appropriate healthcare [41]. The present study findings on language proficiency confirmed existing evidence reported in these previous studies. Japanese language proficiency was not associated with difficulties in obtaining vaccination and testing, which may indicate that accessing medical care requires more complicated and sensitive language processes than accessing vaccination and testing.

Data collection for migrant health in Japan

The demographic characteristics of the sampled population in the present study are largely similar to the overall Vietnamese migrant populations in Japan in terms of factors such as age, sex, residency status, educational attainment, and length of stay in Japan. However, the places of residency in Japan of those surveyed in this study were concentrated in a specific geographical region. Among the study participants, 47% were living in the north-east region, whereas national statistics show the number of Vietnamese is highest in Aichi and Osaka prefectures, which are located in the middle and western parts of Japan, followed by Osaka, Tokyo, and prefectures surrounding Tokyo [22]. This difference in characteristics between the survey participants and the general Vietnamese migrant population occurred due to unstructured sampling and revealed the challenges of obtaining representative samples from hard-to-reach populations when research targeting vulnerable populations including migrants is conducted and its results are used as evidence data. However, a concentration of survey participants in a specific geographical area might be due to active dissemination of the survey information by the influencer, such as trusted community leaders or key individuals who play a significant role in information sharing within their networks. If so, systematic mobilization of such influencers living in different geographical areas could help in obtaining samples from different geographical areas in future studies. Through the activities COVIC (COVID-19 Vaccination Information Center for International Citizens) [42, 43], a huge gap in migrant support and inclusion policy between prefectures in Japan has been exposed. It is explicit especially for undocumented migrants, as only 23% of municipalities issued vaccine vouchers to them, and the foreign resident ratio was positively associated with vaccination access [44]. This research finding suggested that a smaller number of migrants could be one reason for less focus on or prioritization of migrant health. Well-conducted research that includes a representative target population could contribute more to improving migrant health in Japan. Furthermore, finding key persons or directly contacting certain groups in the target population is also crucial to collecting adequate amounts of data, as previous research has shown [45], but this still might lead to biased samples. Data collection via a combination of social network sites and influencers could be one solution.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the survey was conducted online through Facebook, which introduced selection bias. Individuals who lacked internet access, literacy, or the ability to understand the survey contents were excluded. Consequently, the most vulnerable populations—such as the elderly, individuals with lower educational levels, and those in precarious living conditions, who often face heightened risks during health emergencies like COVID-19—may be underrepresented. Furthermore, undocumented migrants, who face unique challenges such as unstable residential status and limited access to healthcare services, were not included in this study. Including these populations in future research could provide more comprehensive insights to inform Japanese health policy and address healthcare inequities among migrants. To mitigate selection bias, future studies should adopt mixed methods, combining online and in-person data collection, to ensure greater inclusivity. Second, the study relied on self-reported perceptions and experiences, which may have been influenced by the context of COVID-19 infection control measures and the evolving pandemic situation during the survey period. Additionally, important social determinants of health, such as financial constraints, health literacy, and awareness of healthcare rights, were not directly analyzed but are recognized as critical factors affecting healthcare access. Incorporating these variables in future research would provide a more holistic understanding of the barriers faced by migrant populations. Finally, wide confidence intervals observed in some results, likely due to small sample sizes or rare events, suggest imprecision in the estimates. Larger and more diverse samples are needed to validate the findings and improve the reliability of the conclusions.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the experiences of Vietnamese migrants in Japan regarding access to COVID-19-related healthcare services. High self-reported uptake rates for vaccination, testing, and medical care reflect the improved availability of these services during the pandemic. However, systemic barriers persisted, with 27% of participants reporting unmet healthcare needs, primarily due to insufficient knowledge of how to access care and language challenges.

Key factors such as age, length of stay in Japan, education, and underlying diseases significantly influenced the difficulties migrants faced in accessing healthcare services. Vaccination efforts benefited from public health strategies targeting broad populations, while testing and medical care relied on more individualized approaches. These personalized services presented greater challenges for migrants, especially those with limited Japanese language proficiency, underscoring the critical need for multilingual support in healthcare delivery.

Preparing for future pandemics requires addressing the barriers highlighted in this study. Multilingual information and clear guidance are essential to improve healthcare access for migrants during health emergencies. Simplifying administrative procedures and providing tailored support for those unfamiliar with the healthcare system could further enhance accessibility. Additionally, collaboration with community networks and trusted individuals can help disseminate accurate health information and reach underserved populations.

Finally, collecting more comprehensive and representative data on migrant populations is vital to designing interventions that ensure equitable healthcare access during future public health crises. By tackling these challenges, healthcare systems can better support migrant populations and promote health equity in times of emergency.
