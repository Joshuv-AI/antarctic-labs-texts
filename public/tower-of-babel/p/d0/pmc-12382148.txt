A multifaceted early clinical experience course in internal medicine fosters motivation and professional growth from the perspective of first-year medical students
Authors: Eliska Potlukova, Lynn Dubs, Sören Huwendiek, Elianne Kassi, Matthias von Rotz, Josephine Nehring, Samuel Etienne, Olivia Müller, Katharina Thies, Oliver Boss, Max Schuenemann, Martin Rohacek, Stefano Bassetti, Marten Trendelenburg, Michael Wilde, Gabriele Voigt, Sonja Seelmann, Andrea Meienberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly clinical experience within a vertically integrated curriculum might contribute to the development of the desired competencies from the onset of studying medicine. However, most qualitative studies focused on the effects of early clinical experience on students were performed within a primary care setting in the second and third year of studies. Our aim was to explore, from the perspective of first-year medical students, the effects of an early clinical experience course in internal medicine within a tertiary hospital setting on their professional and personal development.MethodsWe used an inductive approach to conduct a conventional content analysis of 27 reflective writing reports written by first-year medical students after having completed a 60-hour early clinical experience course in the inpatient setting of a university hospital, comprising 48 h in the healthcare setting (primarily internal medicine and its subspecialties) and 12 h in team-building social events. Writing reports aimed to make students openly reflect on their course experience, elaborating on any aspect of perceived relevance.ResultsAll 27 students invited to participate wrote a reflective report. Background

New educational strategies are needed to ensure that physicians are trained to meet the challenges of medicine in the 21 st century. The most striking change is the progressive move to a new model of medical education: competency-based medical education (CBME) [1–4].

To train competencies from the beginning of medical studies, an incorporation of early clinical experience into the curricula has been proposed [5]. Early experience describes students engaging in authentic workplace contexts from the start of their medical studies [6]. The rationale for early clinical experience has been set in a consensus survey and proven in systematic reviews: to strengthen and deepen cognitively, broaden affectively, contextualize and integrate medical education [6–8]. Vertical integration of basic sciences and clinical medicine stimulates profound learning and understanding of biomedical principles in a problem-based setting. Integration leads to better retention of knowledge and the ability to apply basic science principles in the appropriate clinical context [9, 10]. Weijnen-Meier et al. consider vertical integration a philosophy of education that impacts students’ maturation and engagement with the profession, applying not only to undergraduate education but also to the lifelong learning of professionals [11]. Early clinical experience within a vertically integrated curriculum might contribute to developing the desired competencies from the beginning of medical studies and facilitate professional identity formation [12–15].

Based on these presumptions, an increasing number of medical schools around the world have been implementing programs using early clinical experience, mainly in the USA [16–19] but also in Australia [20], the UK [13], India and other countries [21, 22]. In Europe, the Netherlands and other countries have implemented early clinical experience in their medical curricula [10, 11, 23–28].

The Faculty of Medicine of the University of Basel introduced a concept of early clinical experience for medical students in small groups several years ago, whereby various clinical departments invited first-year medical students to visit the wards and/or outpatient offices regularly [29].

Within this framework, we developed and implemented a multifaceted early clinical experience course in internal medicine based on principles of CBME in alignment with PROFILEs, the Swiss national outcomes reference framework for the undergraduate medical curriculum [29–32].

Early clinical experience has been shown to positively affect medical students’ learning. However, the available studies used methods such as surveys or questionnaires in first-year medical students [33], or were performed in the outpatient setting and in more advanced students [13, 20].

Therefore, our aim was to explore, from the perspective of first-year medical students, the effects of an early clinical experience course in internal medicine within a tertiary hospital setting on their professional and personal development using qualitative methodology.

Methods

We performed a conventional content analysis according to Hsieh and Shannon [34] of 27 reflective writing reports of first-year medical students after having finished the course in the setting of a university hospital in Switzerland.

Description of the course

Participation at an early clinical experience course (Learning on a Project) within the first two semesters is mandatory for all first-year medical students in the bachelor-level undergraduate program at the University of Basel’s Faculty of Medicine. These courses are designed and provided by various clinical departments affiliated with the University of Basel, while keeping the scheduled number of learning hours (60 h; Fig. 1) [29]. The leaders of each course are senior physicians who closely cooperate with two student peer tutors in their 3rd or 4th year of studies. Allocation of students to the courses is based on their preferences, formed by information provided during a joint session in which student-tutors present the available options.

Curriculum design of the early clinical experience course “Learning on a project in internal medicine – where Dr. House seeks advice” (LaP Dr. House course); a total of 60 h during the first two semesters of the bachelor’s program

Our course on early clinical experience in internal medicine is a part of the above-described program and takes place each year at the Division of Internal Medicine at the University Hospital Basel in Basel. We named the course ‘Learning on a Project in Internal Medicine – where Dr. House seeks advice’, in further text referred to as ‘LaP Dr. House course.’ During the first two semesters, our students spend 48 h in the healthcare setting and 12 h participating in team-building social events. The clinical experience is primarily devoted to internal medicine and its affiliated subspecialties. Time spent in the clinical setting is divided between theoretical seminars—focusing on basic clinical skills, clinical reasoning, and special topics—and practical clinical components, including shadowing physicians in inpatient and outpatient settings, bedside teaching, and patient case discussions. The structure of the course is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Over the years, we performed slight variations in the course. During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, the number of students per senior physician was reduced from four/five to two, whereas the number of senior physicians involved increased. Minor changes were made to the number of hours allocated to the different components of the course.

Setting and team

The Faculty of Medicine at the University of Basel is publicly funded and provides bachelor and master degrees in medicine to approximately 2800 students across five study programs, supported by roughly 500 habilitated researchers, medical staff, and postdoctoral fellows. Clinical education occurs in close collaboration with the University Hospital Basel, the University Children’s Hospital Basel (UKBB), the University Psychiatric Clinics (UPK), the Cantonal Hospital Baselland (KSBL), and other affiliated hospitals.

The University Hospital Basel is one of the five university hospitals in Switzerland. Of the 700 hospital beds, 204 are at the disposal of the Division of Internal Medicine, with more than 7000 inpatients treated per year. The Outpatient Medical Department accounts for approximately 25,000 visits per year.

The core study team consisted of three attending physicians in internal medicine (EP, SS, AM) and one fourth-year medical student from the Faculty of Medicine, University of Basel (LD), who had previously participated in the course as a first-year medical student. EP and SS participated in delivering the course, whereas AM was not involved. The course was initially designed by EP, MvR, MT, MW, and GV. Senior physicians serving as clinical teachers in the course included EP, SS, EK, MvR, JN, SE, OM, KT, OB, MS, MR, and SB. The study was advised externally by one expert from the University of Bern (SH).

Data collection

We collected reflective writing reports from participating students in three academic years. The students were given the following instructions: ‘Please reflect on your experience in the Dr. House LaP course. Describe anything that comes to mind, including both positive and negative aspects, any effects the course had on you, and any suggestions you may have for its improvement.’ Students were asked to write approximately 1.5–2 A4 pages in Microsoft Word in a single-line spacing manner.

Sampling strategy and participants

We invited all first-year medical students enrolled in the LaP Dr. House course during three academic years (2018–2019, 2020–2021, and 2021–2022) to participate in the study. Each year included nine students, resulting in a total of twenty-seven participants. Our initial plan was to encompass three consecutive academic years; however, the SARS-CoV-2 nationwide lockdown in spring 2020 necessitated the omission of the 2019–2020 academic year from our study due to its non-representative nature, owing to substantial modifications. There were no exclusion criteria.

In the 2018–2019 academic year, students were required to write a reflection on the course as part of their coursework. In subsequent years, we made writing the reflections voluntary to respect the autonomy and willingness of the students to share their experiences. However, even on a voluntary basis, all invited students opted to write the reflective reports.

Participation in the study and content of their reflective reports did not have any effect on allocation of credits for attendance of the course, as these credits were only allocated based on their presence at various course activities.

Data processing and data analysis strategy

In acknowledging general ethical concerns, we ensured the anonymity of participants and allocated an artificial number to each student (‘R1 - R27’) in the data analysis. Their statements were exclusively stored on electronic devices, identified only by number codes to safeguard their identities. Access to the key code table, which could link these codes to specific individuals, was limited to the first and last authors of the study (EP, SS, AM), thereby ensuring a high level of confidentiality and data protection.

We used an inductive approach to conventional content analysis [34]. After the three core team members (EP, AM, SS) read all reflective reports, we initiated the coding phase by carefully rereading each transcript. We highligted passages describing any effect of the course on students and annotated them with keywords or short phrases capturing the students’ perceptions, applying color coding to thematically related statements. Throughout this process, we strove to keep the set of emerging codes as concise as possible. The development of codes was based on memos and observations that were identified when reading the reports.

After the initial open coding of the first six reflective reports, we met and discussed the preliminary codes until we reached a consensus. We then coded the remaining transcripts and recoded the original ones, and added new codes if the data did not fit into the existing ones. We met several times and invited the student researcher (LD) to discuss the codes from her point of view.

Once all reports had been coded, we re-examined all data with a particular code. Some codes were combined into defined categories, whereas others were split and redefined. Through this, we could find the categories of the codes. In a final step, we found a consensus on final organization of the codes into a hierarchical structure and defined the themes.

The repeated discussion and inclusion of a student researcher served as a tool to increase the trustworthiness of the results in the sense of triangulation. Moreover, we discussed the results with all study authors. Finally, we synthesized the data and set the results in the context of other studies.

The study was assessed by the local ethical committee (EKNZ, Ethical Committee Northwest and Central Switzerland), which, according to the Swiss Law on Research on Humans, waived the requirement of formal approval by the local ethical committee.

Results

All twenty-seven students invited wrote the reflective report (response rate 100%, 9 in each academic year), with 16 women (59%) and 11 men (41%).

Early clinical exposure in the LaP Dr. House course allowed students to gain insights into the daily work of physicians while building respect for patients and other members of the healthcare team. They started the process of developing their professional identity and learned how to deal with their emotions facing difficult situations, including death. Most effects were transmitted by senior physicians as role models, building the basis for students’ professional growth and development of professionalism. Some of the reflective writing reports surprised us with an unexpected depth of understanding of what it means to be a physician, thoughts that we would rather expect after years of practical experience in medicine.

Overall, we identified three themes with their respective categories of codes: (1) Professional growth, including formation of professional identity, dealing with emotions and experience with death; (2) Reinforcing motivation for further studies and work as a physician, including integration into medical studies, shaping a supporting environment and course as a highlight of the studies; and (3) Immersion into the medical field based on real-world exposure, including benefitting from early patient contact and exploration of the field of (internal) medicine. Throughout the reflective reports, role modelling appeared repeatedly as a driving element for the observed effects (Fig. 2). Finally, we discussed feasibility and the students’ suggestions for improvement.

Themes and categories of codes summarizing the effects of the LaP Dr. House course on first-year medical students

Theme ‘professional growth’

‘Professional growth’ was the strongest and mainly represented theme, closely linked to and partially overlapping with the other two, and including the following categories of codes:

Category ‘Development of professional identity’

Almost three-quarters of the students discussed topics related to the formation of their professional identity. They ‘gained a perspective on the job and can imagine now how it is to work in the health system, how the various components of it cooperate and which roles a physician can perform’ (R21). The students could ‘imagine themselves as a physician’ (R27) and could recognize the professional symbolism of the white coat: ‘it was a very special but also a good feeling to go to patients in a white coat for the first time and ask them questions’ (R27).

The students also noticed an improvement in their self-confidence: ‘…the contact with patients took the fear away from me’ (R22); ‘At the beginning, I felt like thrown into cold water….in the end, contact with patients felt much more natural. This confidence was the most precious I took from this course’ (R26). ‘In the end, I approached the patients with more self-confidence, and when I meet a patient now, I no longer feel like an impostor because, thanks to the LaP Dr. House course, I can draw on a considerable wealth of experience, despite the short duration of my previous studies’ (R19).

Another critical issue was the building of a relationship with the patients and social competence: ‘I can imagine that developing a relationship with patients and accompanying them on the whole way could be something nice’ (R1); ‘At that moment I knew that there was a long way in front of me and that I must focus on developing my social competencies at least as much as on the theoretical aspects of the studies’ (R6).

Another student commented on the difficulty of maintaining distance from the patients’ moving fates: ‘Some of the life stories I faced were extremely touching - they showed me how difficult daily work of a physician can be: to find a distance to work after coming home’ (R3).

Repeatedly, the students praised the patient encounters under the guidance of a senior physician, where they could ‘learn how to take a medical history and how to examine abdomen, lungs and heart, and had to deal personally with the patients (R15). Some of the students ’did not have the skills to communicate naturally’; nevertheless, ‘this can be learned and built upon during the career’ (R15). Learning basics of medical history taking and physical examinations already in the first year was very motivating (R17). It was ‘strange to ask unfamiliar people intimate questions and to touch them’ but as it was natural for experienced physicians, R17 assumed ‘everyone can get used to it’. He also understood that he needed to ‘gain enormous knowledgem while motivating him to participate in the theoretical lectures”.

Three statements merit particular interest due to their complex insight into the medical profession dealing with meaning of medicine, meaning of life, basis of trust and non-negotiable respect (Fig. 3):

Shaping future physicians—insights from three first-year medical students after completing the Dr. House LaP course

Category ‘Dealing with emotions and experience with death’

Approximately one-third of the students spoke about distinctly positive emotional topics—compassion, trust, hope, courage, and respect. Some also described strong moments that felt negative or left them shaken – specifically, concerning their first experience with death or threat of it.

The students discussed learning to distance themselves from the fates of their patients: ‘there were sometimes very moving fates that touched me and have shown me how difficult it must be in daily life as a doctor to take care of yourself and to distance yourself from them after work’ (R19). They spoke of trust between physicians and the patients: how ‘amazing it was how quickly a relationship of trust developed within the protected framework of confidentiality and how openly the patients spoke about their illness and life history without directly benefiting from it’ (R16), as well as trust between the physicians and the students: I lost my inhibitions more and more because I noticed that both the patients and the senior physician placed much trust in me, even though I am ‘only’ a student’ (R19). The students also discussed the topic of giving hope to the patients: ‘…how good it feels to talk to patients who are on the mend and to know that the treating doctors can give people hope”(R19), giving courage to the students: ‘…these patient seminars demanded that I take courage early on…’ (R23) and that ‘the contact with the patients took away our fears/inhibitions and taught us how to treat the patient with respect’ (R24).

Some students might have been overwhelmed with their first experience with death. One respondent stated being surprised to come in contact with the body of a deceased patient so early in the studies: ‘It was special at first, but over time you got used to it’ (R17). Several students were strongly touched during an unexpected situation while witnessing a complication of an elective coronary angiography – a cardiac tamponade with a successful resuscitation: ‘It was very upsetting for me just to stand by while they were trying to save the patient’s life and not being able to do anything myself. I was very overwhelmed with the situation’(R3).

Theme ‘motivation for further studies and work’

Motivation for further studies and becoming a physician was a strong, repeatedly stated topic in approximately half of the reflective reports. Students gained a clearer understanding of what and why they should learn and felt more integrated into the medical student community.

Category ‘Integration into medical studies’

Through the course, the students were able to understand why they were studying and what is awaiting them: ‘The course gave me much motivation because it kept reminding me what I was studying for and in which disciplines you can deepen your knowledge as a doctor in internal medicine’ (R19); “The course was the most interesting part of this academic year for me. It gives an outlook on what we could expect professionally and thus an additional motivation to complete this long training’ (R16).

The cohort of students whose participation in the course coincided with the second year of the coronavirus pandemic benefitted the most from in-person contact, as at that time most of their lectures were online: ‘The course gave me the motivation for my studies in addition to all the online lectures and showed me once again that I am in the right place’ (R19).

Repeatedly, the motive of gaining skills early in the studies increased the motivation for further studies: ‘We learned our first manual skills, which was a real benefit in addition to the online lectures. … I often left the course events feeling confident; they always reminded me of where my studies could lead me’ (R19).

Often, the students praised the course as strongly motivating for further studying as opposed to the theoretical parts of the curriculum: “The LaP seminars were the highlight of my first bachelor’s degree. It was a way out of the serene theoretical lectures and a chance to experience the real world of medicine’ (R19).

The students acknowledged that they had very little knowledge at this stage. Nevertheless, they praised the timing of the course: ‘I am convinced that the first year is also the perfect time for LaP Dr. House lessons. Of course, I was technically overwhelmed, but the insights into the profession made me confident that I was in good hands studying medicine (R19).

Category ‘Shaping a supporting environment’

Several students praised their student peer tutors for helping them during the challenging first year of studies: ‘I found it very helpful to have senior students with me. They created a safe environment for us in which we could ask questions about our studies on a friendly level’ (R15); ‘We always had competent experts whom we could consult in case of difficulties and who were happy to help us. This gave security for the sometimes insecure everyday life in medical studies’ (R25). Many students appreciated the social aspects of the course, allowing them to gain friends and integrate better into their studies: ‘Social events that are fun and not only bring you joy but also a nice form of motivation for your studies based on shared experiences’ (R21).

Category ‘Course as a highlight of the studies’

For some students, the course in general and its components were the ‘highlight of their first year’ (R10 and R23). The students also specifically praised the online competition in clinical reasoning (R14), a night shift in the emergency room (R1), the seminars about medicine in developing countries (R19), or a visit to the operating room during cardiac surgery: ‘It was one of the most magical moments in my life thus far when I saw a beating heart for the first time. I still have this picture today clearly in my mind’s eye’ (R15).

Theme ‘immersion into the medical field’

Students appreciated the early exposure to real-life clinical medicine. Many codes were linked to meaningful experiences and insights gained during their initial interactions with patients and healthcare professionals in the unfamiliar setting of everyday clinical practice.

Category ‘Benefits from early patient contact’

The first contact with patients left a strong impression on the students. More than three-quarters of the students discussed this in their reports. They were impressed by the possibility of learning first medical skills: ‘I find it helpful to practice communication with patients in the first semester. It is so essential that you should learn it for a long time, and the best way to do that is to start early’ (R12); or ‘I think it is great that we were often able to apply what we had learned directly: medical history, listening to heart, breathing and intestinal noises with a stethoscope, lung percussion, ultrasound and taking blood. I would have liked to have practised these practical skills even more’ (R18). This category partially overlaps with development of professional identity as well as dealing with emotions (see above).

Category ‘Exploration of the field of internal medicine’

Approximately one-third of the students praised the possibility of exploring several areas of medicine and were thus able to start the selection process for their future careers: ‘The visits to the hospital with the various subject areas gave us a good insight into the opportunities we have after graduation and gave us a chance to find possible interests that we want to pursue in the future’ (R25). They acknowledged the broad spectrum of internal medicine: “Through the course, I was able to gather many different impressions of internal medicine. I particularly like the versatility and size of this area: you have to look at the patient from head to toe in order to make the right diagnosis’ (R17), as well as the exciting aspects of the field: “Internal medicine has action! So much that I can see myself in this field’ (R17), making it possible to imagine working in internal medicine in the future.

‘Role models’ as a connecting element

The category of ‘role models’ appeared throughout the reflective writing reports, acting as a connecting element between the three themes. The students mentioned senior physicians – as well as the student peer tutors - as role models, helping them understand what it means to be a physician, or seeing what one can learn along their way. They praised the possibility of discussing everyday issues like the compatibility of career and family: ‘I have always worried how will I be able – as a woman – to combine work with family in the future, imaging the shift work and overtime that awaits us. My concerns have eased somewhat over the year as I have seen many examples of how it can still work’ (R1); ‘It was encouraging to hear that our LaP doctors both have children and still work in the hospital as senior doctors’ (R4). The students were impressed to witness senior physicians in action, for example performing a resuscitation during a coronary angiography: ‘The precise and calm reaction of the medical personnel involved, in particular of the doctor, deeply impressed me’ (R4), or during typical general practitioner’s work: ‘I was extremely impressed with the vast knowledge of the family doctor and her ability to be on the same wavelength during communication with each patient’ (R4).

Also the student peer tutors (4th year of medical students) were seen as role models: ‘It was impressive to see how our student tutor was able to perform neurological examination’ (R7).

Feasibility and suggestions for improvement

We did not specifically elicit the students’ perspectives on the course’s feasibility and students did not address it by themselves; hence, we cannot present direct quotations on this issue. Nonetheless, their active participation, minimal absences, and overall satisfaction with the course suggest that it was feasible in this setting. The students also maintained close communication with their supervising physicians and coordinated session schedules in a mutually convenient manner, further supporting the viability of the course. Additionally, the course has consistently received high ratings in the faculty’s evaluation reports since its inception in 2016.

Concerning suggestions for improvement, some students raised wishes for even more teaching of clinical skills (R#18), conducting more contests in clinical reasoning (R#18) or using less professional language (R#11).

Discussion

The present study provides insights into how medical students experienced a multifaceted early clinical experience course in internal medicine during the first year of their medical studies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to examine this topic from the perspective of first-year medical students in a tertiary hospital setting. Our findings suggest that implementing such a course has distinct positive perceived effects on students’ learning and professional development. Participants reported benefits in three key areas: (1) professional growth, (2) motivation for further studies and work as a physician, and (3) immersion into the medical field. Role modeling by both senior physicians and peer tutors emerged as a central influence across the three identified themes and served as the foundation for many of the course’s perceived effects.

Professional growth

The students reported on various aspects connected with professional growth. They started to understand what it means being a physician and learned how build relationships with patients and other healthcare professionals. Some of the students developed surprisingly complex insights into the meaning of life and the role of medicine, speaking of non-negotiable respect, trust, and the contrast of transiency giving meaning to life.

Our findings are in line with previous literature. Learning integrity, altruism and composure, as well as forming a professional identity, have been described as effects of role models [35]. Further, early clinical exposure has been suggested to contribute to students’ understanding of the healthcare team’s roles, improving their ability to communicate with healthcare professionals, and fostering an understanding of their future role through observing positive and negative role models [16, 17]. We add to this knowledge by showing that these desirable effects are possible even very early in the studies – within the first weeks and months.

We show that first-year medical students are capable of thinking like physicians, thereby igniting the development of their professional identity. This aligns with the perspective of Cruess et al., who describe the principles of professional identity development, emphasizing the process of ‘thinking, acting, and feeling like a physician’ [36]. Consequently, curriculum developers should think about how to ensure that medical graduates receive maximum support in this personal transformation.

In alignment with the CanMED framework, which is anchored in national training regulations and national undergraduate medical education in Switzerland, professionalism is one of the core competencies of a medical expert [37, 38]. Therefore, our course contributes significantly to the development of at least one of the core competencies within medical studies.

Motivation for further studies and work

Most students regarded the course as highly motivating in two aspects. The first included the upcoming choice of a job profile: they achieved profound insight into the practical aspects of their professional future as physicians and internists. This aspect could be of particular interest, considering the lack of internists in the healthcare setting in Europe [39–42]. To our knowledge, there are few or no data on the influence of early clinical experience courses on the further selection of medical specialization.

The second aspect viewed the practical course as a welcome change to the regular study routine and thus increased the motivation for further studies. This is in line with previous studies highlighting the positive learning experiences induced by early clinical experience [15, 22, 27]. Several students regarded the course as a kind of “counterweight” to the theoretical learning content.

Immersion into the medical field

The students benefited from the early patient contact mostly in the sense of achieving the first basic skills of a physician: learning how to communicate with a patient and learning how to perform the first clinical examination techniques. Surprisingly, the students reported these positive effects at this very early stage of medical studies – even before basic knowledge in anatomy, physiology and pathophysiology could have been gained.

Our results are in line with findings of other studies performed in different settings. For example, Henderson et al. showed that the participation of medical students in preclinical years in an early clinical experience program in a primary care setting creates positive learning experiences in students, including student skills, fostering confidence and competences [20]. Moreover, the general practitioners involved in the course profited by the ignition of enthusiasm.

Furthermore, the course gave the students insights into several areas of medicine, helping them to start the selection process for their future careers, and reinforce them in their decision to become physicians.

Our results indicate that certain conditions are especially relevant to achieve these positive effects. The course should take place under the guidance of senior physicians acting as role models and creating a safe environment. The friendly atmosphere with a solid social aspect may be a prerequisite, either through role modelling and/or social events. In this way, we were able to avoid negative experiences, e.g., the induction of cynicism, as other authors described [33]. The students repeatedly reported that spending time with the same group of people helped them develop friendships and integrate into the new university environment. We interpret this as one of the factors that leads to increased motivation for further studies, as well as the development of professional identity.

Finally, we also acknowledge that early exposure to clinical medicine may carry the risk of potentially negative or stressful experiences for first-year medical students. While such situations can be minimized through close supervision by experienced senior physicians and appropriate debriefing, they cannot be entirely avoided and should be considered in the curricular planning of similar early clinical experience courses.

Our study has several strengths. We have a sufficient sample size of students, as the finding of codes in the inductive approach to the content analysis was saturated after having analysed the reports of the students of the first two cohorts. Our response rate reached 100%. We explain this by the reported high student satisfaction with the course and possibly their knowing that the course leaders would read the reports. In addition, we did not frame the students’ thinking. We asked them to write the reflective report very broadly while avoiding any specific questions. Therefore, all the identified codes reflect students’ genuine perceptions of the effects of the course.

Our study also has several limitations. First, there is a potential selection bias, as only students who voluntarily chose our course were included. Second, our methodology relied on reflective writing reports without further insight from interviews, which may have limited the depth of our data. Third, the single-center context of a tertiary hospital restricts the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, there may be a risk of confirmation bias from the senior physicians involved in the study, as their enthusiasm and expectations could have influenced the analysis. However, one core member of the study team (AM) did not participate in delivering the course, which helped mitigate this potential bias. Finally, the feasibility of implementing similar courses may vary across different settings. We noted that for such a course to succeed, a high level of personal engagement from senior physicians, irrespective of external incentives, is essential. Consequently, we propose that senior physicians’ motivation is a key factor in determining whether this approach can be effectively replicated.

Nevertheless, we believe that our results showing that early clinical experience fosters professional growth, motivation for further studies and work, and integration into the medical field are significant for the broad medical education community, including curriculum developers.

Moreover, early clinical experience in internal medicine may benefit not only future physicians but also society at large [6]. In Switzerland, a study by Reinhard et al. projects that 44% of today’s full-time-equivalent (FTE) workforce in general internal medicine (primary care providers and internists at hospitals)—approximately 2 321 FTEs—will be lost by 2033, mainly through retirement and workload reduction [43]. Addressing this impending shortfall in healthcare delivery will require multifaceted strategies. Enhancing the attractiveness of internal medicine during undergraduate training might represent one of them.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we show that the implementation of an early clinical experience course in internal medicine in the hospital setting has distinct positive perceived effects on the initiation of professional growth, motivation for further studies and work as a physician, and integration into the medical field from the perspective of first-year medical students. Such a course allows the students to gain insight into the broad spectrum of internal medicine and might increase future physicians’ interest in this field. Further studies are needed to better understand the underlying success factors of such courses.
