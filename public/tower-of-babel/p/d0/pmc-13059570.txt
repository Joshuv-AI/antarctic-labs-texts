Renal failure mortality, 1999–2022: exploring demographic and regional trends and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Mark T May, Stone Zhang, Ali Bin Abdul Jabbar, Khalid Bashir, Abubakar Tauseef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal failure, including acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), represents a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality. Previous studies of CDC WONDER data related to renal diseases have not tracked mortality into the pandemic years, and have not analyzed disparities in deaths from renal failure specifically. Our study addresses these two gaps.MethodsRenal failure-related mortality data were extracted from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database and stratified into different demographic and geographic groups. The West census region had significantly lower renal failure-related mortality rates than the other census regions.ConclusionThere were significant increases in renal failure mortality rates from 2020 to 2022, reversing nearly two decades of declining rates. While our study cannot provide causal analysis, this increase maps neatly onto the years of the COVID-19 pandemic. Several demographic and regional disparities were noted. Background

Renal failure (RF) in its two common forms—acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD)—is a source of significant morbidity and mortality across the globe. AKI is associated with high mortality rates. Following AKI, patients experience a mortality rate higher than that of the general population, with more than one in four individuals succumbing within a year, according to one study [1]. AKI can progress to CKD, and recurrent AKI confers a cumulative risk for developing CKD [2].

By 2017, RF affected more than 850 million people globally [3]. In part due to the increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus, CKD-related mortality increased by 50% in the United States from 1990 to 2014 [4]. Data from the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) show that mortality from end-stage renal disease (ESRD) plateaued from 2010 to 2019 [5].

There are recognized racial and socioeconomic disparities in the burden and mortality of RF. In the United States, while early-stage CKD shows equal prevalence across racial groups, African American, Hispanic, and Native American populations are 1.5 to 4 times more likely to progress from CKD to end-stage ESRD [6]. Poverty also increases the likelihood of developing ESRD [6]. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on the morbidity and mortality of patients with RF.

Recent studies have explored CDC WONDER data analyzing renal disease-related deaths associated with diabetes mellitus [7] and hypertension [8] from 1999 to 2020. These studies both reveal consistently higher mortality rates in males compared to females and explore several other demographic disparities. The figures in each study also demonstrate increased mortality from 2019 to 2020. This is notable given the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, but is not explored in detail in either study. Our study makes two unique contributions to the literature. First, we demonstrate demographic and regional disparities specific to renal failure as the primary cause of death. Second, ours is the first study of which we are aware to track CDC WONDER data on renal failure-related deaths into 2021 and 2022, allowing our study to explore and generate hypotheses about the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on renal failure mortality nation-wide. No previous study has reported the reversal of pre-pandemic mortality trends in renal failure using Joinpoint analysis.

Methods

Data source

We used the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database to identify RF-related deaths in the United States. The Underlying Cause-of-Death Public Use Record death certificate records were analyzed to find deaths where RF was listed as the underlying cause [9]. This database has been previously used in other studies to provide mortality data on ESRD [10]. RF-related mortality was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10) codes N17-19, RF (Supplemental Table 1). Our study was exempt from institutional review board approval because the CDC WONDER database contains anonymized, publicly available data.

Demographic stratification

We extracted data regarding RF-related deaths and population sizes from 1999 to 2022 in patients ≥ 25 years old. This age restriction was selected because RF is infrequent in patients < 25 years old. Data on demographic and regional groups were extracted, including gender, race/ethnicity, age, and census region. Racial/ethnicity groups were defined as non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, NH American Indian/Alaskan Native, and Hispanic people as identified on death certificates. Age groups were defined as young adults (age 25 to 44 years), middle age (age 45 to 64 years), and elderly (age ≥ 65 years). Regions were classified into Northeast, Midwest, South, and West according to the Census Bureau definitions [11]. Diagnosis codes were stratified into N17 (acute renal failure), N18 (chronic renal failure), and N19 (unspecified renal failure). Data cleaning and graphing were completed using R version 4.3.2. When needed, Y-axis breaks were introduced to the graphs using the ggbreak package in R [12].

Analysis

RF-related crude and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were extracted from CDC WONDER and rounded to the nearest hundredth. Crude mortality rates were calculated by dividing the RF-related deaths by the corresponding United States population. CDC WONDER age-adjusts mortality rates using the previously described 2000 United States standard population [13]. The Joinpoint Regression Program (Joinpoint version 5.0.2 available from the National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, Maryland) was used to determine trends in mortality within the study period [14]. This program identifies significant changes in annual mortality over time through Joinpoint regression. Joinpoint regression fits models of linear segments where significant temporal variation occurred. Annual percent change (APC) in AAMR with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated for the line segments linking two Joinpoints using the Monte Carlo permutation test. The weighted average of the APCs was calculated and reported as average annual percent change (AAPC) with corresponding 95% CIs to summarize the reported mortality trend for the entire study period. To maintain consistency with AAMR inputs, APCs and AAPCs have been reported rounded to the nearest hundredth below. APC and AAPCs were considered to increase or decrease if the slope describing the change in mortality over the time interval was significantly different from zero using a 2-tailed t-test. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 (represented by an asterisk ‘*’ in results and figures).

Sensitivity analysis

We compared an analysis of the overall AAMR trends from the Underlying Cause-of-Death Public Use Record to a similar analysis of the overall AAMR trends from the Multiple Cause-of-Death Public Use Record. Multiple Cause-of-Death contains data in which renal failure was listed as a contributing cause of death, rather than only as the primary case.

Overall

Overall, AAMR per 100,000 significantly decreased from 19.57 (95% CI 19.36 to 19.78) in 1999 to 19.18 (95% CI 19.01 to 19.35) in 2019, then increased to 20.82 (95% CI 20.64 to 20.99) in 2022 (Supplemental Table 2). The AAPC of AAMR from 1999 to 2022 was 0.27* (95% CI 0.14 to 0.41). AAMR increased from 1999 to 2003 with APC 3.17* (95% CI 2.16 to 4.35), decreased from 2003 to 2012 with APC − 1.11* (95% CI -2.01 to -0.83), remained stable from 2012 to 2020 with APC − 0.53 (95% CI -0.91 to 0.17), and increased from 2020 to 2022 with APC 4.11* (95% CI 2.06 to 5.28) (Fig. 1; Supplemental Table 3; Supplemental Fig. 1).

Age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 100,000 from renal failure overall and gender-stratified, 1999–2022. Error bars represent the upper and lower bounds of 95% confidence intervals on the AAMR. APC = annual percent change, * = significantly different from 0 with p < 0.05

Gender stratified

From 1999 to 2022, males had significantly higher AAMRs from RF than females. Male AAMR was 1.43 times female AAMR in 2022 (Supplemental Table 2). Temporal trends in AAMR over the study period were similar between the two groups. Both males and females had increased AAMR from 1999 to 2002-03 (APC 3.62* for males, 3.11* for females), subsequent decreases until 2020 (APC − 0.93* for males, -0.96* for females), then a significant increase during the pandemic years of 2020–2022 (APC 4.88* for males, 4.86* for females). (Fig. 1; Supplemental Table 3; Supplemental Fig. 1). Though males had consistently higher AAMR than females, RF mortality increased at a similar rate in both groups from 2020 to 2022.

Race stratified

Non-Hispanic (NH) Black or African Americans had the highest AAMR from RF among all studied racial groups, with significantly higher AAMR than all other groups each year from 1999 to 2022. NH Black or African American AAMR was more than double that of NH White and Hispanic or Latinos in 2022, and 1.58 times that of NH American Indians or Alaska Natives. NH American Indians or Alaska Natives had significantly lower AAMR than NH Black or African Americans but significantly higher AAMR than NH White and Hispanic or Latinos each year from 1999 to 2022. (Supplemental Table 4).

NH Whites and Hispanics or Latinos had similar AAMRs but distinct temporal trend behavior. NH Whites had similar temporal trend behavior to NH Black or African Americans with each paralleling the overall trend of increasing AAMR from 1999 to 2002-03, decreases of variable significance from 2003 to 2020, then a significant increase during the pandemic years of 2020 to 2022. The Hispanic or Latino and NH American Indian or Alaska Native groups diverge from the common trend behavior and do not have a Joinpoint in 2020 (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Fig. 2).

Age-adjusted mortality rate per 100,000 from renal failure by race, 1999–2022. Error bars represent the upper and lower bounds of 95% confidence intervals on the AAMR. NH = non-Hispanic, APC = annual percent change, * = significantly different from 0 with p < 0.05

Age group stratified

Among the age groups studied, elderly patients had by far the highest AAMR from RF, with significantly greater AAMR than middle-aged or young adult patients in each year from 1999 to 2022. AAMR among the elderly was 8.48 times the AAMR among the middle aged in 2022, and 54.25 times that of young adults. AAMR among the Elderly had similar temporal trends to the overall male, female, NH Black or African American, and NH White AAMRs. AAMR from RF among the middle-aged was significantly lower than AAMR in the Elderly but significantly higher than AAMR in young adults in each year from 1999 to 2022. Middle aged AAMRs mirrored overall trend behavior. Young adults had much lower AAMR from RF than other groups, with distinct trend behavior. (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table 8, Supplemental Table 9, Supplemental Fig. 4).

Census region stratified

In 1999, AAMR in the Midwest and Northeast was similar, while AAMR was lower in the South but close to that of the other two regions. Over the study period, the Northeast saw decreased AAMR compared to the Midwest and South, with statistically significant variations among both groups beginning in 2009 and remaining thereafter. The West had a significantly lower AAMR throughout the entire study period than all other studied regions. In 2022, the AAMR of the West was 0.63 times that of the Midwest, 0.75 times that of the Northeast, and 0.64 times that of the South. (Supplemental Table 6). All four census regions had significant increases in AAMR from 2020 to 2022 during the COVID-19 pandemic. The Midwest had APC 5.10* (95% CI 2.88 to 6.54) during this period. The South had APC 3.63* (95% CI 0.95 to 5.12). The Northeast had APC 2.63* (95% CI 0.01 to 4.18). The West had APC 6.65* (95% CI 2.36 to 8.88). (Fig. 4; Supplemental Table 7, Supplemental Fig. 3).

Age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 100,000 from renal failure by census region, 1999–2022. Error bars represent the upper and lower bounds of 95% confidence intervals on the AAMR. APC = annual percent change, * = significantly different from 0 with p < 0.05

Diagnosis code stratified

In 1999, unspecified renal failure (ICD-10 code N19) was the most common cause of renal failure mortality, with AAMR 9.00 (95% CI 8.86 to 9.14). From 2001 on, it persistently decreased in frequency until it became the least common cause of renal failure mortality by 2022. Chronic renal failure (ICD-10 code N18) showed persistent increases in mortality from 1999 to 2022, peaking in 2021 with AAMR 14.36 (95% CI 14.21 to 14.51). Though there was a peak in 2021, there was no distinct trend in AAMR during the 2019 to 2022 period in the Joinpoint analysis. Instead, APC from 2016 to 2022 was 1.48* (95% CI 0.88 to 1.96). Acute renal failure (ICD-10 code N17) had trend behavior most closely mirroring overall trends, with an initial increase, followed by a decrease, then a significant increase in AAMR from 2020 to 2022, with APC 25.14* (95% CI 18.83 to 29.83). (Fig. 5; Supplemental Table 12, Supplemental Table 13, Supplemental Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study revealed significant insights into trends in mortality primarily caused by renal failure in the United States from 1999 to 2022. It highlighted a consistent mortality pattern across most demographic groups: an initial increase from 1999 to 2002–2003, followed by varying declines until 2019–2020, and a significant increase from 2020 to 2022. This increase was corroborated by a statistically significant increase in AAMR from 2019 to 2022 in the Multiple Cause-of-Death data. The findings indicated that male, Black or African American, and elderly individuals experienced significantly higher mortality rates from RF compared to their demographic counterparts. Moreover, the study underscored substantial regional differences in RF-related mortality rates. Nearly every demographic group had significantly increased AAMR due to RF during the pandemic years.

Our findings parallel existing literature showing that RF mortality increased significantly during the years of the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, USRDS data showed a significant increase in ESRD mortality from 2019 to 2020 after relative stability in the 10 previous years [5]. Our study captures a similar trend, but the mortality increase in USRDS data occurred in 2020, whereas our data first showed increased mortality in 2021. USRDS data then show a relative plateau in ESRD mortality from 2020 to 2021, with a very mild increase [15]. Our study does not align with these data, instead showing that the RF-related mortality increased from 2020 to 2021 in most groups. Our study builds on existing data, adding the 2022 mortality data, showing that increasing RF-related mortality continues into 2022 in many groups. It is also the first exploration of RF-related mortality in 2021 and 2022 using CDC WONDER data. No prior study has reported the reversal of pre-pandemic mortality trends in the pandemic years using Joinpoint analysis, which has allowed us to offer quantitative trend data not previously available.

Our results establish a temporal association between the COVID-19 pandemic and increased RF mortality. Our study design does not permit us to comment on causality. RF requires regular follow up care, especially in cases of end-stage renal disease (ESRD). The pandemic caused profound limitations in healthcare access, and we propose this as a mechanism by which the pandemic could have caused the observed RF mortality increase. Furthermore, RF itself may exacerbate poor outcomes in COVID-19 cases, since RF causes a suppressed immune system. Notably, COVID-19 prognosis appears more severe in patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) at stage 2 or higher [16, 17] and in patients with CKD [18–20]. COVID-19 patients in the United States and Europe develop very high rates of AKI (> 50% of intensive care unit patients in one study) [16], demonstrating the capacity of COVID-19 itself to cause renal failure. The field would be well-served by future studies seeking a causal link between the COVID-19 pandemic and increased RF mortality. However, the pandemic is not a repeatable phenomenon, so these causal studies cannot be performed. If RF-related mortality trends down in 2023 and beyond, or returns to 2019 levels, then this would further strengthen the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and the increased RF mortality observed from 2020 to 2022. These results may offer lessons for future pandemic preparedness including the importance of maintaining access to dialysis for patients with ESRD, and the general prognostic significance of acute RF in major systemic illness.

Racial disparities in RF persist among patients with concurrent COVID-19 infections. African American patients with COVID-19 are more susceptible to developing AKI [16]. During the period from March 1, 2020, to August 1, 2020, one study revealed that 72% of excess deaths among the U.S. RF population occurred among Black and Hispanic patients. The relative increase in mortality among Black and Hispanic patients during this timeframe was over four times greater than that observed among White patients [21].

Males were shown to have higher mortality rates from renal failure as compared to females. It is known that the effects of testosterone promote oxidative stress in men, activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and contributing to a more rapid progression of chronic kidney disease. Conversely, estrogen in women inhibits these processes and is therefore protective [22]. Further, men tend to lead lifestyles which are unhealthier and which lead to an increased incidence of hypertension and diabetes, which then exacerbate kidney disease and renal failure [23].

Our results show a lower mortality rate from renal failure in the Western United States compared to the other census regions. We hypothesize that there are multiple contributing factors. One is that the western US has a lower percentage of individuals who are African American [24]. This racial group was shown to have among the highest AAMR from renal failure. Second, the West has been shown to have generally lower rates of hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease than other census regions, including the Midwest and South [24]. These co-morbidities negatively influence disease progression in renal failure.

The increase in AAMR from renal failure from 2020 to 2022 represents a continuation of increasing chronic renal failure deaths throughout the study period. It appears that the sharp increase in acute renal failure deaths from 2020 to 2022 primarily drove the pandemic-era trends.

The two most common causes of RF are diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Diabetes mellitus is the leading cause of renal failure, with nearly half of new RF cases being attributed to diabetes by 2019 [25]. The temporal trends in diabetes incidence and prevalence in the US parallel those of RF, with declines seen in the early 2000s but a steady increase from the years 2010 onwards [26]. The number of cases of hypertension-induced chronic kidney disease and RF increased substantially during the years 1990 to 2021, with hypertension accounting for 29% of new RF cases in 2019 [27].

In the Multiple Cause-of-Death data used for sensitivity analysis, there is a large increase in AAMR from 2010 to 2011, with a corresponding large decrease from 2012 to 2013. The trend of decreasing AAMR from roughly 2005 through 2020 is otherwise undisturbed. CDC WONDER updated its coding of chronic renal failure for both underlying and multiple cause of death in 2011 [9, 11]. From 1999 to 2010, deaths from chronic renal failure (N18) are coded as N18.0 (End-stage renal disease), N18.8 (Other chronic renal failure), or N18.9 (Chronic renal failure, unspecified). From 2011 on, there are no deaths coded with N18.0 or N18.8. They are replaced by N18.1-5 (Chronic kidney disease, stage 1–5, respectively), added to CDC WONDER in 2011. No similar change in AAMR in 2011 and 2012 is noted in the USRDS data [5]. Given these facts, we suspect that these outlier AAMRs reflect changes in administrative coding practices for cause of death rather than a sudden shift in population health.

CDC WONDER is a public health database and has several limitations. Factors such as social determinants of health which could have played a role in the patient’s death are not reported on the death certificate. Additionally, our analysis only utilized mortality data for RF as the primary cause of death, which means that deaths where RF may have indirectly contributed to mortality or acted as a secondary cause were not considered. (Though in the sensitivity analysis, trends looked broadly similar in the Multiple Cause-of-Death data, where RF can be an indirect or secondary cause.) The CDC WONDER database lacks details about a patient’s specific renal function (eGFR), clinical status, or detailed RF information, providing only the causes of death listed on death certificates. This also precludes stratifying patients based on comorbidities. CDC WONDER data are derived from death certificates, but these are prone to misclassification bias. It is also possible that mortality was undercounted in the early pandemic due to reporting delays. Changes in ICD-10 coding practices over the 23 years studied may also have affected the data. Our study did not include a state-by-state analysis, though we hope that geographic trends were adequately explored with census region analysis. Finally, CDC WONDER only contains United States data, so our study does not show any global mortality trends. This limits generalizability to other countries.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a significant increase in RF-related mortality from 2020 to 2022, the years of the COVID-19 pandemic, which holds across nearly all studied demographic groups. It appears this was driven primarily by increases in acute renal failure. It will be interesting to monitor for how long RF mortality remains elevated from pre-pandemic levels. Significant gender, racial, and regional disparities exist in RF-related mortality. Future studies to establish the causes of these disparities, and policies to address them, should be pursued.
