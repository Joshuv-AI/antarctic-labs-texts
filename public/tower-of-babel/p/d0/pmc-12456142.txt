Human cytomegalovirus promotes de novo PC synthesis during early virus replication
Authors: Ian Kline, Rebekah L. Mokry, Yuecheng Xi, Magí Passols Manzano, Sidnie Layesa, Nowroz Sohrab Ali, Melissa A. Moy, Felicia D. Goodrum, John G. Purdy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman cytomegalovirus (HCMV) infection reprograms metabolism, including lipid synthesis. While several metabolite-related pathways exhibit altered activity in infected cells, the alteration of lipid-related pathways by HCMV has not been examined beyond fatty acid synthesis and elongation. In this study, we addressed this lack of understanding by focusing on phosphatidylcholine (PC), a class of lipids we previously showed is increased by HCMV infection in human foreskin fibroblasts. Here, we expand upon this finding by demonstrating that HCMV infection increases the abundance of PCs in several different fibroblasts and, similarly, in endothelial and epithelial cells. Additionally, HCMV elevates PC levels regardless of the level of confluency, type of growth medium, and presence of serum. Next, we investigated if HCMV alters the activity in the three PC synthesis pathways. We demonstrate that HCMV infection promotes the activity in the de novo PC synthesis pathway using a 13C-choline isotopic tracer and liquid chromatography high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry. Infection did not alter the activity in the other two pathways. Moreover, we examined the kinetics of PC remodeling by HCMV and found that PC synthesis was promoted and the PC lipidome shifted after 24 h post-infection. Furthermore, we found that PC remodeling occurred when DNA synthesis and subsequent steps of virus replication were inhibited by phosphonoacetic acid. Overall, this work suggests that the early steps of HCMV replication promote the reprogramming of host lipid metabolism to ensure the synthesis of a lipidome necessary to support HCMV infection.IMPORTANCEHuman cytomegalovirus (HCMV) is a common herpesvirus that establishes a lifelong and persistent infection in its human host. HCMV infection in most people does not cause overt disease. However, in immunocompromised individuals, severe CMV-associated disease can lead to permanent disabilities and even death. Additionally, congenital CMV is the leading infectious cause of birth defects. Viruses have evolved to hijack host metabolic pathways to facilitate their replication cycle. In this study, we determine that HCMV promotes the activity in the de novo pathway of phosphatidylcholine (PC) synthesis. We demonstrate that the activity in the other PC synthesis pathways, the phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase and Lands cycles, is unaltered by HCMV infection. Moreover, we found that HCMV infection alters metabolic activity to increase the PC lipidome before 48 h post-infection. Additionally, our results suggest that immediate-early and early gene expression promotes changes in PC lipids. Together, our findings demonstrate that infection promotes the de novo PC pathway to increase PC lipids during the early stages of virus replication.

INTRODUCTION

Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) is a β-herpesvirus that causes disease in immunocompromised children and adults (1, 2) and developmental disabilities during congenital infection (3, 4). HCMV establishes life-long, persistent infection in part by reprogramming host metabolism to support virus replication. Infection promotes the activity in host lipogenic pathways for increased fatty acid (FA) synthesis and elongation to generate lipids needed for virus replication (5–8). Our previous lipidomic studies found that HCMV infection increases the relative abundance of phosphatidylcholine (PC) (9, 10). PC is a class of phospholipids and a major constituent of biological membranes, including the virion envelope (11). PCs function in several biological contexts, including acting as structural and barrier lipids in lipid bilayers (11–14). The plasma membrane and many organelles are enriched with PC lipids, which provide structural roles and help maintain basic cellular functions.

Structurally, PC lipids contain a phosphocholine head group and a glycerol backbone with two attached fatty acyl tails (Fig. 1). PC lipids are named according to the amount of carbon content and double bond desaturation in the hydrocarbon tails. For example, PC(46:1) contains 46 carbon atoms and 1 double bond in total among the 2 FA tails. In humans, PC lipids are made by the de novo PC synthesis pathway (15–20). The de novo pathway is also known as the Kennedy pathway. In this pathway, choline and diacylglycerol (DAG) are metabolized to generate PC (Fig. 1). Two additional pathways can contribute to PC synthesis. The phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PEMT) pathway converts phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) lipids to PC lipids. This pathway is active in the liver where more than 20% of PC is made via the PEMT pathway (21, 22). Other tissues may have low levels of PEMT activity (23). The final pathway involves the addition of a free fatty acyl chain to a one-tailed lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) to generate the two-tailed PC. Several LPC acyltransferases (LPCATs) convert LPC to PC; this pathway is called the LPCAT pathway or Lands cycle (Fig. 1). Given that HCMV infection increases the PC lipid content of host cells (9, 10), we hypothesized that HCMV infection reprograms host metabolism to induce PC synthesis. We used metabolic tracing to measure the impact of HCMV infection on the activity of each of the three routes of PC generation. To this end, we used liquid chromatography high resolution tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to measure the lipid content and metabolic activity in primary human cells infected with HCMV. We determined that HCMV infection significantly altered the levels of several PC lipids, including PC with very long chain fatty acids (VLCFAs; PC-VLCFAs).

Phosphatidylcholine lipids and their synthesis. A simplified PC lipid model emphasizing the major structural components: two fatty acyl tails, glycerol backbone, and the phosphocholine head (left). Since the tails can vary in their length (number of carbons) and degree of unsaturation (number and placement of double bonds), they are depicted as gray bars. In humans, three metabolic pathways can generate PC lipids (right). The de novo PC pathway starts with choline and uses DAG. The phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase pathway uses phosphatidylethanolamine lipids and modifies the head to generate PC. The LPCAT pathway, as part of the Lands Cycle, uses one-tailed lysophosphatidylcholine as a substrate and adds a second tail to generate PC.

Here, we show that HCMV promotes PC and PC-VLCFA synthesis through metabolic reprogramming of the canonical de novo PC pathway at an early time post-infection. Furthermore, we found that increased PC abundance caused by HCMV infection occurs when infected cells are treated with an inhibitor of viral genome replication that reduces subsequent steps of HCMV replication. The findings suggest that replication steps before viral genome synthesis, such as immediate early or early viral protein expression, promote host metabolic reprogramming to increase de novo PC synthesis that shifts the host lipidome.

RESULTS

Lytic HCMV replication reprograms the PC lipidome

While several studies have demonstrated that lytic HCMV infection promotes lipid metabolism, these studies have mostly investigated fibroblast cells grown under similar conditions (5–10, 24). HCMV infection in human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cells promotes the levels of most PCs, including some PC-VLCFA found in low abundance in uninfected cells (9, 10). This was demonstrated using two HCMV strains: AD169 and TB40/E. PC(46:1) is increased by >100-fold and >80-fold in AD169 and TB40/E infection, respectively (9, 10). PC(48:2) was increased by >100-fold in cells regardless of strain used for infection (10). To extend this finding, we initiated a study by examining a diverse set of conditions to determine if several factors influence the ability of HCMV to alter PC lipid levels. We considered cell type, cell confluency, presence or absence of serum, and the cellular source of virus used to initiate infection. First, we used LC-MS/MS to measure the relative abundance of PC lipids in HCMV-infected cells and uninfected control human foreskin fibroblast cells that are fully confluent and serum-free, which have been extensively used to study metabolism in HCMV infection (5–7, 9, 10, 25). For these experiments, confluent cells were switched from Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing fetal bovine serum (FBS) to serum-free DMEM, 24 h prior to infection. We infected cells with TB40/E-GFP at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3 infectious units per cell in the serum-free DMEM. At 1 h post-infection (hpi), the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and returned to serum-free DMEM. We observed an overall increase in the relative abundance of most PC lipids at 72 hpi, consistent with our previous observations (Fig. 2A, column 1) (9, 10).

HCMV replication is supported by host fatty acid elongases that generate VLCFAs (7, 8). While VLCFAs are defined as those with 22 or more carbons, previous studies demonstrated that the levels of fatty acids with 26 or more carbons (≥C26) are increased the most by HCMV infection (7–10). So far, the longest fatty acyl tail incorporated into a PC lipid in HCMV-infected cells is C30 (9, 10). Since we observed an increase in PC-VLCFAs during infection, we identified the tail content of PC-VLCFA lipids in HCMV-infected cells. Based on our newest results, the longest VLCFA tails identified in HCMV-infected cells are C32 (i.e., C32:1, C32:2, and C32:3) (Fig. 2B). These tails were found in PC(50:2), PC(50:3), and PC(50:4), lipids that were readily detected in HCMV-infected HFFs but below the limit of quantification in uninfected cells (Fig. 2B). We had previously demonstrated that PC with a total of 50 carbons in the tail is increased by HCMV infection (10); however, this was the first time we observed the C32 tails.

We identified several previously unreported molecular species of PC-VLCFA containing either a saturated (no double bond) or monounsaturated fatty acid (one double bond). These FA tails were paired with a long chain or very long chain polyunsaturated fatty acid (two or more double bonds), such as C20:4 or C22:6 (Fig. 2B). PC(42:4), PC(42:5), and PC(42:6) differed from most others in their VLCFA tail content, each containing a previously unreported C26:3, C26:4, C26:5, or C26:6 tail that was paired with either a C16:0 or C16:1 tail (Fig. 2B). The levels of these PC-VLCFAs were ~1.5-fold higher than in uninfected cells (Fig. 2B). In summary, HCMV infection significantly altered the levels of PC lipids with ≥40 carbons (Fig. 2C).

Next, we investigated if HCMV infection reprogrammed PC levels similarly in other fibroblast cell types. MRC-5 cells are another primary human fibroblast commonly used in HCMV metabolic studies (6, 8, 26, 27). Therefore, we measured the levels of PC from HCMV-infected MRC-5 grown in the same serum-free DMEM culture medium conditions and infected with an MOI of 3. HCMV infection in MRC-5 cells increased PC-VLCFA lipid levels, similar to infection in HFFs (Fig. 2A, column 2). Next, we examined MRC-9 human fibroblast cells using the same growth and infection conditions used for the other primary human fibroblasts. While HFF and MRC-5 cells are from a male donor, MRC-9 cells were isolated from a female donor (28, 29). Like the other fibroblasts that have been well studied by the HCMV field, MRC-9 cells support HCMV replication (Fig. 2D). PC lipids in MRC-9 cells were remodeled by HCMV infection, similar to the other fibroblast cells examined (Fig. 2A, columns 1–3)

HCMV exhibits broad cell tropism (30–33). Endothelial cells support HCMV infection in vivo (34–39). In vitro infection of endothelial cells supports lytic replication; however, the amount of infectious virus produced is lower than in fibroblasts (37, 40, 41). We investigated the effects of HCMV on the level of PCs in subconfluent human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) cultured in Endothelial Growth Medium (Lonza) with 10% FBS. Unlike the serum-starved conditions used to test fully confluent fibroblasts, we used subconfluent HUVECs grown in 10% FBS during and after HCMV infection. At 96 hpi, HCMV-infected HUVECs had elevated levels of PCs (Fig. 2, column 4). Again, PC-VLCFAs were increased by HCMV infection, albeit to a lesser degree than in fibroblasts.

Next, we examined PC lipids in epithelial cells that are infected in vivo and capable of supporting productive HCMV infection (30, 41). The retinal pigment epithelium of the eye is a site of persistent HCMV infection leading to retinitis and blindness, and retinal epithelial cells are a model for HCMV lytic replication (42–44) (45). We measured the levels of PC lipids in subconfluent ARPE-19 cells growing in 1:1 DMEM:F12 media containing 10% FBS infected with TB40/E at an MOI of 3 using a virus stock generated in fibroblasts and titered on ARPE-19 cells to improve HCMV infection efficiency (45). Like the subconfluent HUVECs, the subconfluent ARPE-19 cells were cultured in 10% FBS during and after HCMV infection. At 72 hpi, HCMV-infected ARPE-19 cells contained a greater relative abundance of PC-VLCFA lipids than uninfected epithelial cells (Fig. 2, column 5). In general, the levels of most PC lipids increased in ARPE-19 cells, including the PC-VLCFAs.

HCMV virus produced by epithelial cells can have a greater infection efficiency than virus produced by fibroblasts due to the altered ratios of glycoproteins present on the surface of the virion (46, 47). Therefore, we tested if the type of cell producing the virus alters the ability of HCMV to remodel PCs by infecting ARPE-19 cells with virus produced by fibroblast or ARPE-19 cells. A similar PC profile was observed in cells infected with virus produced in fibroblast and epithelial cells (Fig. 2, columns 5 and 6).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that HCMV lytic infection promotes the level of PC lipids, particularly PC-VLCFAs, independent of cell type, confluency, and culture medium. Furthermore, these observations suggest that HCMV infection alters host metabolism in a broad range of cell types and growth media to increase the abundance of PC lipids.

HCMV infection promotes de novo PC synthesis

Since HCMV infection promotes fatty acid synthesis and elongation (6–10) and the abundance of PC lipids (Fig. 2), we hypothesized that HCMV infection stimulates PC synthesis. PC de novo synthesis is the canonical pathway in humans (19). In this pathway, the headgroup comes from metabolism of choline, while the tails are from diacylglycerol (Fig. 1). Choline is metabolized through a series of reactions to generate CDP-choline (Fig. 3A). In the final step, CDP-choline donates phosphocholine to DAG, generating PC. To determine if HCMV infection alters metabolic activity in de novo PC synthesis, we measured PC synthesis in HCMV-infected and uninfected cells using 13C-choline stable isotope labeling. The labeled form of choline contained two 13C atoms retained at each step in the pathway, resulting in PC lipids with two 13C atoms in the head (Fig. 3A, red asterisk). Since free choline is only incorporated into PC lipids via the de novo synthesis pathway, analyses of 13C-choline-labeled PCs will enable us to observe if HCMV infection alters this pathway specifically. The other two pathways for PC synthesis do not use free choline as a substrate and will not be labeled using this strategy (Fig. 1).

To measure activity in the de novo PC synthesis pathway, HFFs were infected with TB40/E at an MOI of 3 for 1 h. The experiments were performed in serum-free conditions, avoiding the potential for unlabeled choline from serum affecting the results. At 1 hpi, the virus inoculum was removed, the cells were washed twice, and DMEM containing 13C-choline in place of 12C-choline was added to the cells. From 24 to 72 hpi, lipids were extracted, and the percentage of labeling in PC lipids was determined by LC-MS/MS following correction for the natural isotopic abundance. In uninfected cells, we observed an increase in the amount of labeled PCs from 1.9% to 12.2% at 24 h to 13.7% to 31.4% at 72 h (Fig. 3B). In infected cells, we observed an increase in the percentage of 13C-labeled PC from 1.8% to 30.2% at 24 hpi to 33.3% to 53.6% at 72 hpi (Fig. 3B). At 24 hpi, none of the PCs showed a significant difference in labeling between infected and uninfected cells (Fig. 3B and C). Most PCs, 29 of 38, had a significantly greater percentage of 13C labeling in HCMV-infected cells compared to uninfected cells at 48 hpi (Fig. 3C), demonstrating that HCMV infection promotes de novo PC synthesis between 24 and 48 hpi. By 72 hpi, 33 of the 38 measured PCs had a significantly higher percentage of labeling in infection. Since the 13C-choline labeling approach allows us to measure the activity in the de novo synthesis pathway while not labeling the other two PC synthesis pathways, our results here show that HCMV promotes de novo PC synthesis from early to late times in virus replication (Fig. 3B and C).

Next, we selected PC(46:5) as a representative PC-VLCFA to highlight the changes over time that we observed in the de novo synthesis pathway. PC(46:5) is a PC-VLCFA that has five total double bonds in the tails. We selected PC(46:5) since we have not previously focused on PCs with polyunsaturated tails, i.e., those with two or more double bonds. By 72 hpi, the abundance of PC(46:5) was increased by >3.5-fold, and the level remained elevated through 120 hpi in HCMV infection (Fig. 3D). In cells fed 13C-choline, we observed a similar level of labeling in uninfected and infected cells at 24 hpi (Fig. 3G and J). At later times, labeling of PC(46:5) in uninfected cells remained low with only 15% by 72 h (Fig. 3G and J). In HCMV-infected cells, labeling was greater at 48 and 72 hpi, with more than 40% of PC(46:5) labeled at 72 hpi (Fig. 3G and J). These data demonstrate that HCMV infection increases the relative abundance of PC(46:5) and promotes its synthesis via the de novo pathway, suggesting that HCMV promotes the de novo synthesis of PC-VLCFAs to increase their levels in infection. We further examined this possibility by performing the same analysis of PC(46:1) and PC(48:2), the PCs that were increased the greatest in HCMV infection (Fig. 2B). At 24 hpi, the abundance of these two PCs was unaffected by infection (Fig. 3E and F). Their levels were elevated by 48 hpi and significantly increased at later times. At 120 hpi, PC(46:1) and PC(48:2) were increased by ~115- and ~170-fold, respectively. In our 13C-choline feeding experiment, the labeling of PC(46:1) and PC(48:2) in HCMV-infected cells was greater than in uninfected cells (Fig. 3H and I). These observations further demonstrate that HCMV infection increases the activity of the de novo PC pathway to increase the abundance of PC-VLCFAs in infected cells.

Since we observed an increase in the labeling of most PC lipids, PC-VLCFAs and those with shorter tails (Fig. 3B), our results suggest that HCMV infection promotes the de novo synthesis of PCs regardless of tail composition. To investigate this possibility further, we examined lipids with labeled percentages that were significantly increased at 48 and 72 hpi, similar to PC(46:5). Several of these lipids contained VLCFA tails or shorter tails, including PC(28:0), the shortest PC measured in our study (Fig. 3K). Of the 38 PC lipids we identified, 29 had the same phenotype, including PC lipids ranging from PC(28:0) to PC(46:5), demonstrating that HCMV infection increases the de novo synthesis of PCs regardless of tail type. For five PCs, labeling in infected cells was increased only at 72 hpi, for example, PC(36:2) (Fig. 3L). The others were PC(32:0), PC(40:8), PC(42:4), and PC(42:6). Only four PCs had similar percentage labeling in uninfected and HCMV-infected cells from 24 to 72 hpi: PC(32:2), PC(34:0), PC(36:3), and PC(46:2) (Fig. 3M). Overall, our 13C-choline labeling results demonstrate that HCMV infection promotes the activity of the de novo pathway for most PC lipids regardless of tail composition, suggesting that this pathway contributes to an increase in the relative abundance of many PC lipids.

The PEMT pathway is inactive in fibroblasts and unaffected by HCMV

In addition to the de novo synthesis pathway, PC lipids can be synthesized via the PEMT pathway (Fig. 1). PEMT is a host enzyme that adds three methyl groups to the head of PE to generate PC (Fig. 4A) (19, 23, 48–51). PEMT expression and activity are greatest in the liver, epididymis, and adipose tissue; for example, PEMT activity generates 20% PC lipids in the rat liver (22, 52, 53). Given that HCMV infection may induce PEMT to generate PCs, we investigated if infection alters the expression of PEMT or activity in the pathway. First, we tested if PEMT protein is present in HFF cells. PEMT protein levels in uninfected primary human fibroblasts were undetectable (Fig. 4B), consistent with the known expression of PEMT (23, 54). Next, we examined if HCMV infection alters PEMT levels in fibroblasts. Similar to uninfected cells, PEMT protein was unobservable in HCMV-infected fibroblasts up to 48 hpi (Fig. 4B). When blotting for PEMT, we observed a 25 kDa band in infected samples at 72 and 96 hpi; however, a band at the expected 19 kDa size was not detected (Fig. 4B). We evaluated the PEMT antibody by exogenously expressing PEMT using a doxycycline-inducible system and used the same system to express GFP as a control. When we expressed PEMT in HFF cells, we observed a band at the expected size of 19 kDa, ensuring that our western blot approach was able to detect PEMT and that it migrates at the anticipated size (Fig. 4B). PEMT has two isoforms, PEMT-S and PEMT-L (55). PEMT-S has higher enzymatic activity than PEMT-L. PEMT-S is approximately 19 kDa. PEMT-L can be N-glycosylated and has an expected relative molecular weight of 24–28 kDa (55). We treated HCMV-infected samples with PNGase F to cleave N-linked oligosaccharides from glycosylated proteins and determined if the 25 kDa band observed at late times in HCMV infection represents glycosylated PEMT. PNGase F treatment had no effect on the 25 kDa band visualized using the PEMT antibody that we observed late in infection (Fig. 4C). As expected, PNGase F treatment reduced the size of HCMV gB, which is known to be glycosylated (56). The 25 kDa band may be O-linked glycosylated PEMT, unglycosylated PEMT-L, or could be due to cross-reactivity with a late HCMV protein or a host protein that is only expressed late in HCMV replication. We conclude that HCMV infection does not lead to the expression of PEMT-S, but we cannot rule out the possibility that HCMV may induce the expression of PEMT-L during the late steps of replication.

Since PEMT-L may potentially be expressed late, we sought to directly test if HCMV infection alters the activity in the PEMT pathway. Since the 13C-choline labeling strategy we previously used does not measure the activity of the PEMT pathway, we used a different approach. The methyl donor in the PEMT reaction is S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). SAM is generated from methionine (Fig. 4A). To measure PEMT activity, we fed cells 13C-methionine and measured labeled PC lipids that contained three labeled methyl groups using LC-MS/MS. At 1 hpi, cells were washed, and medium containing 13C-methionine was added. We first measured the amount of 13C-labeled PC lipids in uninfected cells. We found that the percentage of 13C-labeled PC ranged between 0.07% and 4% for any given species, indicating primary HFFs exhibit little to no PEMT activity (Fig. 4D). This is consistent with the lack of observable PEMT-S and PEMT-L enzymes in these cells. When we tested if HCMV infection altered PEMT activity, we observed a low level of 13C-labeled PC similar to that of uninfected cells (Fig. 4D). Our results indicate that PEMT activity is unaltered by HCMV infection.

Next, we ensured that our labeling strategy could measure activity in the PEMT pathway if it was active in HFF cells. We used the doxycycline-inducible PEMT-S HFF cells (Fig. 4E). Feeding 13C-methionine to these cells resulted in 11%–35% labeling in PCs when PEMT expression was induced with 1 µg/mL of doxycycline (Fig. 4F). In contrast, control cells with doxycycline-inducible GFP expression had PC labeling percentages of 0.32%–3.5%, levels similar to uninfected and HCMV-infected fibroblasts (Fig. 4F). Based on the data presented here, we conclude that the increase in the abundance of PC at 72 hpi following HCMV infection occurs independently of the PEMT synthesis pathway.

LPCAT activity is unaltered by HCMV infection

A third route for synthesizing PC involves generating two-tailed PCs from one-tailed LPC (Fig. 1). In this pathway, one tail of the PC comes from LPC and the other from a free fatty acyl chain (57). The conversion of LPC to PC contributes to the maintenance of biological membranes by regulating PC composition through the activity of a family of host enzymes called LPC acyltransferase (LPCAT1–4) (57–61). Of these, LPCAT1 is reported to generate saturated PCs like those increased by HCMV infection (Fig. 2A) (9, 10).

First, we measured the protein levels of LPCAT1 to initiate an investigation of the impact of HCMV infection on the LPCAT pathway. A low level of LPCAT1 was observed in uninfected samples that remained at a similar level throughout the 6–96 hpi time course examined (Fig. 5A). Beginning at 48 hpi, LPCAT1 was elevated in HCMV-infected cells and remained increased through 96 hpi (Fig. 5A and B). Second, we measured the levels of LPC at 72 hpi. We found that LPC levels were generally increased by HCMV infection (Fig. 5C).

Next, we fed cells a deuterium (d)-labeled LPC lipid and measured labeled PCs using LC-MS/MS to determine if HCMV infection impacts LPCAT activity. The d-labeled LPC(17:0) contained a single odd-chain saturated C17:0 FA tail and five deuterium atoms (d5) located in the glycerol backbone that generate a combined mass shift of Δ5.0313 m/z higher than endogenous unlabeled LPC(17:0) (Fig. 5D). In this assay, we used LC-MS/MS to track the conversion of d5-LPC(17:0) to d5-PC(17:0/X), where X represents the addition of a fatty acyl-CoA that was added to the sn2 position by an LPCAT (Fig. 5D). We began by determining the background levels of d5-LPC(17:0) and uptake of the exogenous d5-LPC(17:0) substrate in HCMV-infected and uninfected cells. Stocks of the d5-LPC(17:0) substrate were suspended in ethanol (EtOH) and incubated with 1.7 µM delipidated-BSA at a final concentration of 0.5% EtOH. In cells treated with 10 µM d5-LPC(17:0), we detected the one tail lipid at >100-fold above background, demonstrating that uninfected and infected cells take up the fed LPC at a similar level (Fig. 5E). Next, we compared the conversion of d5-LPC(17:0) to d5-PC(17:0/X) to evaluate LPCAT activity. When we examined the background levels of d5-PC(17:0/X) in EtOH-only treated cells from 24 to 72 hpi, we observed only 7 of 336 peaks in EtOH-treated samples, demonstrating that uninfected and HCMV-infected cells have little to no endogenous levels of a lipid that has a mass similar to d5-PC(17:0/X) (Fig. 5F). Since there is little to no d5-PC(17:0/X) in cells, we report the extracted ion counts instead of percentage of labeling. In d5-LPC(17:0)-treated cells, d5-PC(17:0/X) lipids were readily identified, demonstrating that LPCAT conversion of d5-LPC(17:0) to d5-PC(17:0/X) is active in these cells (Fig. 5F). Moreover, d5-PC(17:0/X) levels were similar in uninfected and infected cells, suggesting that LPCAT activity is unaffected by HCMV infection (Fig. 5F).

We further analyzed LPCAT activity via conversion of d5-LPC(17:0) to d5-PC(17:0/X) from 24 to 72 hpi for changes in individual PC species. At 24 and 48 hpi, a similar level of labeled PC in uninfected and HCMV-infected cells was observed in all identified d5-PC(17:0/X) lipids (Fig. 5G and H). At 72 hpi, we found that only 3 of the 14 d5-PC(17:0/X) lipids had a statistically higher amount of labeled PC in infected cells (Fig. 5I). For these three, the difference between infected and uninfected was between 2.2- and 3.9-fold increased. Overall, these findings show that the LPCAT pathway is active in primary human fibroblasts and that HCMV infection has little to no impact on the pathway activity.

HCMV reprograms host PC lipid metabolism during the early stage of replication

Thus far, we have found that HCMV infection increases the abundance of PCs, and their de novo synthesis is increased before 48 hpi (Fig. 2 and 3). Next, we wanted to define the time when infection alters PC levels. Therefore, we measured the relative abundance of PCs in HCMV-infected and uninfected cells from 4 to 120 hpi. We determined the fold change for each individual PC and visualized the percentage of change of each measured PC species. In this case, if 100 PCs were measured and all had the same change, then each would have a 1% change. At 4 hpi, we observed a similar change in abundance among all PC lipids, each represented <2% of the overall change (Fig. 6). At 24 hpi, the percentage of change of most PCs was similar to 4 hpi, suggesting that infection does not alter the levels of any PC species at these early times. Between 24 and 48 hpi, the levels of some PC-VLCFA lipids, including PC(44:0), PC(44:1), PC(46:1), PC(46:2), PC(48:2), PC(48:4), PC(48:5), and PC(50:7), rose precipitously, accounting for the majority of change observed (Fig. 6). At the later times, 72–120 hpi, these PC-VLCFAs continued to be the ones that changed the most in HCMV infection. These data support the conclusion that the PC lipidome is remodeled during the early stage of HCMV replication starting after 24 hpi and continues to be shifted throughout the later steps of replication.

Based on our findings that PC lipid levels and their de novo synthesis are increased starting around 24 hpi, we hypothesize that the PC lipidome is altered independent of expression of HCMV late genes. HCMV genes are classified broadly into three major kinetic classes, immediate early (IE), early (E), and late (L). Since late gene expression depends on viral genome synthesis (62), we used phosphonoacetic acid (PAA) to reduce HCMV genome synthesis and decrease the expression of late genes (63). HFFs were infected and treated with 100 µg/mL PAA or vehicle (H2O) at 1 hpi. Media containing PAA or vehicle were replaced every 24 h. As expected, the number of viral genomes increased between 24 and 48 hpi in the vehicle-treated cells, indicating the onset of viral genome synthesis (Fig. 7A). The number of viral genomes remained low in PAA treatment, demonstrating that virus genome synthesis was inhibited (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, we observed that IE1 protein and early protein pUL44 were not impacted by PAA at early times (Fig. 7B through D). However, we found that PAA treatment reduced late proteins pp28 and pp71 at 72 and 96 hpi (Fig. 7B through F). A reduction in IE and E proteins was observed at later times, as previously shown (62, 64–66). Since PAA treatment inhibited genome synthesis and late protein expression as expected, we investigated the impact of PAA treatment on PC lipid levels. At 72 hpi, the relative abundance of PC lipids was measured by LC-MS/MS. PAA treatment had little effect on PC levels (Fig. 7G). These findings demonstrate that HCMV infection alters the PC lipidome independent of viral genome synthesis and viral proteins whose expression is dependent on genome replication.

DISCUSSION

Multiple studies demonstrated that HCMV replication depends on increased lipid metabolism (6, 8–10, 25, 67). It is also known that the changes in metabolism occur temporally (5, 9, 68, 69). Since virus replication depends on changes in host metabolism, the temporal changes in metabolism may need to align with the kinetics of virus replication to generate an environment to support successful replication. Our current understanding of the temporal control of metabolism by viruses is limited. Identifying the temporal control for virus-induced changes to lipid metabolism will guide studies in defining the viral proteins, including their kinetic classes, involved in metabolic reprogramming. In this study, we utilized a lipidomics approach to identify the synthesis pathway that HCMV targets to increase PC lipids and defined when the activity in the pathway is altered.

Our work demonstrated that lytic infection of HCMV increases the levels of PC lipids independent of cell type, presence or absence of serum, and the cell type which the inoculating virus was produced (Fig. 2). Similarly, the metabolome is altered by lytic infection of HCMV and herpes simplex virus type 1 independent of cell type (69). HCMV-induced FA elongation results in VLCFA tails that can be used for PCs (8), and PC-VLCFAs were among the lipids increased most in HCMV infection (Fig. 2) (9, 10). PCs are important components of cellular membranes. While each type of membrane has a distinct lipid composition, PC lipids are a major constituent of most membranes and are the most abundant phospholipid in several cellular membranes. Nearly half of the phospholipids in the double-layered membrane of the nucleus are PC (70), while the plasma membrane PC is 20%–25% of the total lipid content and is enriched on the outer monolayer (71). Viruses remodel host membranes to support their replication. For example, poxviruses protect the site of viral DNA replication by rearranging ER-derived membranes to form a mini-nucleus in the cytoplasm called virosomes or virus factories (72). HCMV genome replication occurs in the nucleus, and assembling virus particles acquire their envelope in the cytoplasm during secondary envelopment. Their envelope membrane consists of 34% PC, 48% PE, 8% PS, 3% PI, and the remainder is composed of select PA and PG species (11). The lipid composition of the virion differs from the nuclear and plasma membranes and more closely resembles synaptic vesicles, suggesting viruses create a unique lipid environment that is used during secondary envelopment (11). Following secondary envelopment, virions must traffic out of the cells in a process that likely involves different lipids. Recent research has demonstrated that HCMV egress is cell-type dependent (73, 74). In addition to lipids supporting assembly, egress, and the virus envelope, the characteristic cytomegaly and expansion of plasma and nuclear membranes observed in HCMV-infected cells suggest a necessary increase in the levels of lipids.

In this study, we found that PC metabolism was altered by HCMV independent of the expression of the late temporal class of genes. Herpesvirus lytic replication is coordinated by a well-regulated cascade of gene expression. Conventionally, viral gene expression during lytic replication is characterized as three kinetic classes: immediate-early, early, and late. Immediate-early genes are transcribed independently of any de novo viral protein translation (75). These genes include immediate-early genes 1 and 2 (IE1 and IE2) encoded by UL123 and UL122 genes. IE1 and IE2 proteins are transcriptional activators that coordinate the expression of viral genes. Early genes require protein translation to occur after infection but do not require viral genome replication. HCMV encodes more than 100 E genes. The expression of L genes depends on viral genome synthesis. Herpesvirus genes can be further classified as genes with transcription enhanced by viral DNA synthesis (delayed-early or leaky-late) and genes with transcription enabled by viral DNA synthesis (true-late). A recent RNA-seq study led to the proposal of classifying HCMV genes into seven temporal classes (TC1–7) (65). In this system, IE genes are classified at TC1, genes with early or delayed early kinetics as TC2–3, and late genes in TC5–7. TC4 contained genes that were expressed with early kinetics but had a dependency on DNA replication. The identification of leaky-late, true-late, and TC4-7 genes involves the use of viral DNA replication inhibitors, such as PAA or phosphonoformate. In this study, we treated HCMV-infected cells with PAA to reduce viral genome replication and the levels of late proteins (Fig. 7A through F). We used this as a tool to determine that HCMV infection increased the levels of PC independent of viral DNA replication and late gene expression (Fig. 7G). These findings suggest that IE and E proteins are sufficient to promote changes in metabolism that result in PC increases during HCMV infection. While our findings demonstrate that true late gene expression is unnecessary for PC changes that occur in infection, expression of late genes may help promote metabolic changes started earlier in virus replication or may support other changes in the host lipidome.

Several viral proteins are involved in HCMV metabolic reprogramming. Of these, UL38 is the one we understand the most. Consistent with our conclusion that PC metabolism is altered with early kinetics, UL38 is an early gene. It is expressed within the first 24 h of infection in fibroblasts (76). UL38 suppresses cell death while triggering and maintaining mTOR activity (76, 77). UL38 supports fatty acid elongation that is necessary for virus replication, and it was originally proposed that HCMV does so through both mTOR-dependent and independent mechanisms (8). Subsequently, it was demonstrated that UL38 supports HCMV replication by inducing several additional metabolic activities, including increasing the consumption of glucose and some amino acids (78). While UL38 targeting of the host tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2) also drives UL38 induction of mTOR activity, UL38 modulates metabolism independent of mTOR (78). Furthermore, UL38 inhibition of TSC2 aids its metabolic control. In addition to UL38, UL37×1 supports HCMV remodeling of the host lipidome, suggesting that it facilitates metabolic reprogramming (9). UL37x1 helps promote the expression of proteins involved in fatty acid elongation that use glycolytic-derived metabolites increased by UL38, suggesting that UL38 and UL37x1 may function in a coordinated fashion to alter metabolism. UL13 is the third HCMV protein found to be involved in metabolism (79). UL13 localizes to the mitochondria where it promotes oxidative phosphorylation. Since UL38, UL37x1, and UL13 are expressed early in lytic replication in fibroblasts (65), they can allow for viral-driven metabolic changes to occur prior to viral genome replication. The observation that the known viral proteins exerting metabolic control—UL38, UL37x1, and UL13—are expressed from early genes further supports our conclusion that HCMV reprograms metabolism in the early phase of replication to increase PC lipids.

Additional evidence in the host supports the conclusion that metabolic reprogramming is initiated during the initial stages of HCMV replication. Gene expression and protein levels of host metabolic regulators and enzymes are altered within 48 hpi. ChREBP is a transcription factor regulated by glucose signaling and is activated by 24 hpi in HCMV infection (80, 81). The mature, active form of SREBP1 and SREBP2 transcription factors that promote cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis is increased at 24 and 48 hpi, respectively (25, 82, 83). SREBPs help promote the expression of fatty acid elongase 7, ELOVL7, which is increased in HCMV-infected cells by 24 hpi (8, 9, 81). HCMV infection promotes the levels of acetyl-coenzyme A carboxylase 1 (ACC1), the rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid synthesis, by 48 hpi (9, 25, 82). Similarly, fatty acid synthase is elevated by 48 hpi (25). Several host kinases involved in HCMV reprogramming of metabolism are increased by 24 hpi, including calmodulin-dependent kinase kinase (CaMKK1) and protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) (10, 83, 84). While most of the viral mechanisms responsible for altering these host factors have yet to be elucidated, the kinetics of these alterations are consistent with an increase in de novo PC synthesis starting at 24 hpi (Fig. 3) and a shift in the PC lipidome between 24 and 48 hpi (Fig. 6). During this time, new viral genomes are synthesized, which subsequently trigger the expression of HCMV late genes. We found that PC changes induced by HCMV infection occur independently of late gene expression (Fig. 7).

After observing increases in the levels of PC lipids in several cell types under differing growth conditions following HCMV infection, we used metabolic labeling to determine how infection may alter the activity in PC synthesis pathways. Stable isotopic labeling using 13C, 2H, or 15N tracers is a common approach for measuring intracellular metabolism. Most tracer studies performed to understand metabolic changes caused by virus infection have investigated metabolites or fatty acids (6–9, 69, 85). However, our study used metabolite and lipid tracers to analyze lipid labeling. We used labeling to determine which pathways are active in the fibroblast cells used for infection, which pathways are altered by infection, and when the pathways are altered. We found that HCMV infection increased the activity in the de novo synthesis pathway using 13C-choline (Fig. 3A). Moreover, 13C-choline labeling allowed us to determine when infection increased the de novo pathway. We found a marked increase in the de novo pathway starting at 24 hpi and persisting through at least 72 hpi (Fig. 3B through H).

Additionally, metabolic labeling allowed us to determine that the PEMT pathway that converts PE to PC was not active in HFFs and that HCMV infection does not promote its activation (Fig. 4). Finally, by using a labeled LPC lipid tracer, we were able to determine that the LPCAT pathway is active in the fibroblast cells used in this study and that HCMV infection had little to no impact on the conversion of LPC to PC lipids (Fig. 5E and F). We found that HCMV infection promotes the levels of LPCAT1, a member of the family of host lipogenic enzymes that convert LPC to PC. The increased levels of LPCAT1 were from 48 to 96 hpi (Fig. 5A and B). While our labeling data suggest that LPCAT activity is unlikely to be the reason for the increased levels of PC lipids that happen in HCMV-infected cells, it is possible that LPCAT1 is serving a function during the late steps of virus replication. The infection-related changes in LPC lipid levels are not as great as the changes in PC; nonetheless, we found that several LPC lipids were increased at 72 hpi (Fig. 5C). LPCAT activity at late times in HCMV replication may help maintain a more homeostatic level of LPC. Furthermore, LPCAT1 aids membrane integrity, in part by helping to replace damaged lipids, including those that could induce ferroptosis that may be triggered by infection (86–88). Based on our findings, we conclude that LPC conversion to PC lipids is insufficient to support the full level of PC remodeling that occurs during HCMV replication; however, further investigations of LPCATs may find that they play a role in HCMV infection.

Overall, our observations using several metabolic tracers support the conclusion that HCMV infection increases the concentration of PC lipids by promoting the activity in the de novo PC synthesis pathway. Our LC-MS/MS studies demonstrate that the temporal changes to PC levels in HCMV infection begin between 24 and 48 hpi and continue through late stages of infection (Fig. 3 and 6). Furthermore, our data indicate that IE or E proteins induce metabolic reprogramming to feed and promote the de novo synthesis pathway of PC lipids. Overall, our study reveals that HCMV reprogramming of metabolism that supports a shift in the lipidome occurs independent of viral genome synthesis and without the need for late viral proteins. Knowing when metabolic changes are induced will allow us to better define how these changes occur and identify possible mechanisms to suppress the metabolic reprogramming that is essential for virus replication.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells, viruses, and experimental design

HFF cells were used throughout the study. Where indicated, the study included additional fibroblasts (MRC-5 and MRC-9), ARPE-19 epithelial cells, and HUVEC endothelial cells. Fibroblasts were grown in DMEM, epithelial cells were grown in 1:1 DMEM:F12 medium, and endothelial cells were grown in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (Lonza). Lipidomic labeling and replication experiments were performed in HFF-hTERT cells that were first grown to and held at 100% confluence for 3 days in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10% fetal bovine serum, 10 mM HEPES, and penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). One day prior to HCMV infection, FBS was withdrawn from the medium. All HCMV infections were performed using TB40/E-GFP (9, 10). Cells were infected using a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3 infectious units per cell in serum-free DMEM, unless otherwise indicated. Mock-infected and uninfected cells received inoculum lacking virus particles and were otherwise treated the same in parallel with HCMV-infected cells. All infections were performed for 1 h, after which the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline. Cells were then maintained in their respective growth media, unless otherwise stated. At 48 hpi, the growth medium was replaced to refresh nutrient levels, except for the PAA treatment conditions where cells were treated every 24 h.

Experimental virus stocks were generated from a BAC-derived stock and propagated in fibroblasts at a low MOI. Extracellular virus from the supernatant was collected and concentrated to a pellet through a layer of sorbitol (20% sorbitol, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2, and 1 mM MgCl2) using ultracentrifugation at 20,000 rpm for 80 min. Virus was resuspended in serum-free DMEM with HEPES and P/S. Virus stocks and experimental samples were titered using a tissue culture infectious dose 50 assay. A starting dilution of 1:10 was used. After 2 weeks, infectious colonies were counted by the presence of two or more adjacent GFP+ cells.

LC-MS/MS lipidomics

Lipid abundances were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS and treated with cold 50% methanol. Lipids were extracted twice using chloroform and dried under nitrogen (9, 10). Data were collected using either a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive Plus (QE+) or Thermo Fisher Scientific Exploris 240 mass spectrometer. For experiments performed on the QE+, lipids were resuspended in 100 µL of a 1:1:1 solution of methanol-chloroform-isopropanol per 200,000 cells. For experiments performed using the Exploris 240, lipids were resuspended in 200 µL 1:1:1 solution per 200,000 cells. For each sample, a total of three wells were used for analysis. Two wells were used for lipid extraction (i.e., technical duplicates), and one well was used to determine the total number of cells for normalization. Samples were normalized according to the number of live cells at the time of lipid extraction. Additional wells with no cells were used as a control to determine any contaminants from the lipid extraction and LC-MS/MS. Following resuspension, lipids were stored at 4°C or 7°C in an autosampler. Lipids were separated by reverse-phase chromatography using a Kinetex 2.6-mm C18 column (Phenomenex; 00F-4462-AN). LC was performed at 60°C using a Vanquish ultrahigh-performance LC (UHPLC) system (Thermo Scientific) and two solvents: solvent A (40:60 water-methanol, plus 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (10:90 methanol-isopropanol, plus 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid). UHPLC was performed at a 0.25 mL/min flow rate, starting at 25% solvent B and ending at 100% solvent B as described previously (9, 10). After each sample, the column was washed and equilibrated. The total run time was 30 min per sample. Blank samples were run before, after, and interspersed with samples. Lipids were ionized using a heated electrospray ionization source and nitrogen gas as described previously (9, 10). Data were collected using full scan MS/data-dependent MS2 (dd-MS2) TopN mode. QE + MS1 abundance data were collected at a resolution of 70,000 over a mass range of 200–2,000 m/z, and MS2 spectra were collected using a transient time of 128 ms and a resolution setting of 35,000, with an AGC target of 1e5. Exploris 240 MS1 abundance data were collected at a resolution of 90,000 over a mass range of 200–2,000 m/z, and MS2 spectra were collected using a transient time of 64 ms and a resolution setting of 30,000, with an AGC target of 1e6. Each sample was analyzed using negative and positive ion modes. The mass analyzers were calibrated weekly, and systems were calibrated monthly.

Lipid abundance analysis

PC abundance data were analyzed using MAVEN and a reference library of empirically derived retention times to aid identification of PCs (9, 10, 89). Lipids were initially identified in MS1 in positive mode using the expected m/z and confirmed using the isotopic pattern of naturally occurring 13C carbon and retention time. MS1 identifications were done using ≤8 ppm accuracy. In addition to high accuracy MS1 and retention time, the identification of all reported PCs was confirmed using MS/MS (MS2). In positive mode, PCs were confirmed by identifying the phosphorylcholine head group (184.0733 m/z). Moreover, PCs were further confirmed in negative mode using formate adducts. In negative mode, retention time, high-resolution MS1 accuracy, and tail identification were used to confirm the identity of PC lipids. All reported FA tails were identified by MS2 in negative mode. Quantification of all PCs was done using MS1 peak information from positive ions.

LC-MS/MS metabolic labeling measurements

Stable isotope LC-MS/MS metabolic labeling experiments were performed as described above with the following differences in instrument settings and method. QE + MS1 stable isotope tracer data were collected at a resolution of 140,000 and 280,000 resolution over a mass range of 400–1,400 m/z. Exploris 240 MS1 abundance data were collected at a resolution of 180,000 over a mass range of 200–2,000 m/z. While most PCs in labeling experiments were confirmed in both positive and negative modes as described above, the PCs shown in Fig. 3H and I were only confirmed in positive mode since their low abundance precluded MS2 selection in negative mode.

Metabolic tracing

For stable isotope metabolic tracer studies, unlabeled PCs were identified using MAVEN and the predicted m/z of the precursor molecule (89). Labeled forms were exported to a csv. file from MAVEN based on the predicted m/z of the unlabeled precursor ion and mass shift produced by the isotopic tracer. MATLAB or RStudio IsoCorrectoR package was used to correct for the abundance of naturally occurring isotopes in the metabolic tracing experiments (6, 90). For de novo PC synthesis pathway analysis, the percentage of labeled PC was determined using 2-labeled 13C-choline, which produces a MS mass shift of Δ2.006 m/z relative to unlabeled 12C-choline. Therefore, only 2-labeled PC forms possessing a 2-labeled choline head group from exogenously supplied 13C-choline were included. The amount of 13C-labeled PC was calculated for each species as the fraction of 2-labeled PC relative to the total measured PC level. For analysis of the PEMT pathway, 13C-methionine labeling was used. Since the PEMT pathway involves the sequential addition of three methyl groups to form a PC from a PE, the resulting PC will have up to three 13C carbon atoms depending on the labeling rate of S-adenosylmethionine from methionine. Therefore, PCs containing 1–3 13C atoms were included in the PEMT pathway analysis. The percentage of labeling was calculated as a fraction of the sum of 1-, 2-, and 3-labeled PC relative to the total PC level. For the analysis of LPCAT activity, d5-LPC(17:0), containing five deuterium atoms in the glycerol backbone, which produces a MS mass shift of Δ5.031385 m/z relative to unlabeled LPC(17:0), was used. Therefore, only 5-labeled PC forms were included in the LPCAT activity analysis. Candidate PCs were considered newly synthesized by LPCAT activity if they produced the anticipated Δ5.0313 mass shift and contained a mass spectral peak of 269.249 m/z corresponding to the C17:0 fatty acid of exogenously supplied d5-LPC(17:0). When present, the complementary PC tail was confirmed by FA tail analysis. The data were normalized following a normality test (RStudio) due to large differences in EIC values between label-fed vs EtOH control conditions, which was expected since d5-LPC(17:0) does not exist in untreated cells. An outlier test was used to determine that the low levels of background labeled PC(33:1) and PC(35:1) observed in 5 out of 24 EtOH samples were indeed outside of the normal distribution of the data. However, these data were included in the statistical analysis as their overall impact was relatively minor when weighed against the remaining samples, which exhibited no signal. A Student’s t-test was used to determine statistical significance with a statistical threshold of P < 0.05.

Metabolic tracer studies were performed using stable isotope labeling reagents purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories Inc. and Avanti Polar Lipids. 13C-choline (1,2,-13C2, Cambridge #CLM-548-0.1) was used to measure de novo PC pathway activity. 13C-methionine (Methyl-13C, Cambridge #CLM-206-1) was used to measure PEMT pathway activity. LPC(17:0-d5) (Avanti #855679) was used to measure LPCAT activity. Product quality reported as >99.5% compound purity.

Growth medium containing either 13C-choline (4 mg/L) or 13C-methionine (30 mg/L) was generated following a published Gibco formulation (91). The solution was pH adjusted to 7.4 and filter sterilized. P/S and 10 mM HEPES were added. All labeling experiments were performed in serum-free conditions as described above. The labeling media were stored at 4°C until used.

d5-LPC(17:0) in 1:1 dichloromethane-methanol was stored at −20°C until use. The day before use, the solvent was gently evaporated under nitrogen gas and was reconstituted using 100% EtOH; this was repeated for a total of four resuspensions. An aliquot of reagent from the same EtOH stock was used as the mock-treated control. On the day of each experiment, d5-LPC(17:0) was diluted into serum-free growth medium. Lipid-free BSA carrier protein (Sigma) was used at a final concentration of 1.7 µM to conjugate d5-LPC(17:0) prior to each experiment.

PEMT cloning and overexpression

The human PEMT gene (NCBI CCDS ID #11187.1) was synthesized by GENEWIZ with a silent mutation at amino acid 27. The sequence was verified by next-generation sequencing. The 5′ sequence was engineered to contain an XbaI restriction site, and the 3′ sequence was engineered to contain a ClaI site. XbaI-HF and ClaI restriction enzymes were used to cut the PEMT gene from the GENEWIZ vector. The gene was inserted into pLV-TRE-blast expression vector (VectorBuilder). HFF-hTERTs containing rtTA were transduced with pLV-TRE-PEMT lentivirus particles or pLV-TRE-GFP lentivirus particles as a control. Following blasticidine selection, PEMT or GFP overexpression was induced with doxycycline at the indicated concentration and validated by western blot.

Protein analysis

Proteins were examined by western blot using SDS-PAGE. Proteins were resolved using Mini-Protean TGX 4-20% or anyKD gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to an Odyssey nitrocellulose membrane (LI-COR). For PEMT detection, membranes were blocked using 1% BSA in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 (PBS-T) and incubated with a rabbit antibody against PEMT (Invitrogen PA5-42383) in 1% BSA PBS-T solution at 1:500 dilution. For all other blots, membranes were blocked using 1% milk in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 and incubated with primary antibody in the presence of 1% milk-PBS-T solution. The following antibodies were used: rabbit polyclonal LPCAT1 (1:500; Cell Signaling E4V4B, #57411), mouse monoclonal IE1 (1:100; clone 1B12), mouse anti-UL44 (1:2,500; virusys), mouse anti-pp28 (1:100; clone 10B4-29), mouse anti-pp71 (1:100; clone 2H10-0), mouse anti-gB (1:50; clone 27-156), rabbit anti-tubulin (1:1,000; proteintech), and mouse monoclonal anti-α-tubulin (1:2,000; Sigma-Aldrich; #T6199). All HCMV antibodies except for anti-UL44 and anti-gB were gifts from Dr. Thomas Shenk (Princeton University). The anti-gB was provided by Dr. William Britt (University of Alabama at Birmingham) (92). Membranes probed with mouse monoclonal antibodies were incubated for 1 h at RT, while rabbit antibodies were incubated for 2 h at RT. Visualization and quantification of western blots were performed using a LI-COR Odyssey CLx imaging system.

Viral DNA synthesis inhibitor

PAA was used to inhibit viral DNA synthesis to reduce late gene expression and protein synthesis. At 1 hpi, HCMV-infected cells were treated with either 100 µg/mL PAA or an equivalent volume of H2O as a vehicle control. Media with treatment were replaced every 24 h. At 72 hpi, cells were washed three times with cold PBS, and lipids were extracted for LC-MS/MS as described above.

DNA quantification

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to determine the HCMV genome copy number relative to cellular DNA. Cellular DNA was isolated using a Zymo Quick DNA mini-Prep kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Viral genomes were quantified between 4 and 96 hpi. Viral DNA levels were measured using primers specific to the HCMV gene UL123 (5′-GCCTTCCCTAAGACCACCAAT-3′ and 5′-ATTTTCTGGGCATAAGCCATA ATC-3′). Host genomes were measured using primers specific to actin (5′-TCCTCCTGAGCGCAA GTACTC-3′ and 5′-CGGACTCGTCATACTCCTGCTT-3′). A bacterial artificial chromosome containing the viral genome sequence of HCMV FIX strain engineered to express cellular actin was used to develop a standard curve and determine the absolute quantities of viral DNA (93). qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real Time PCR system using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Statistics

Figures and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism and RStudio. Statistical significance was determined for Fig. 2B and C using a t-test with a significance cutoff of P < 0.05. Figures 3 and 7 use a two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s or Šídák’s test for post hoc analysis.
