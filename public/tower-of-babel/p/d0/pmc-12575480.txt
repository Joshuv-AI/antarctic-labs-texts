Effect of peritoneal non-closure and closure during open appendectomy on post-operative pain: a randomized, double-blinded study
Authors: Dripta Ramya Sahoo, Debasis Naik, Vishnu Prasad, Julia Sunil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClosure of the peritoneum has been a standard practice in appendectomy. However, it has been proposed that closure of the peritoneum following any abdominal surgery is more likely to give rise to adhesions and increased postoperative pain. This study was based on the hypothesis that peritoneal non-closure would result in decreased postoperative pain without causing any significant increase in complications like surgical site infection or wound dehiscence.ObjectiveThe primary objective of this study was to compare the pain scores in patients undergoing open appendectomy and peritoneal closure, with non-closure using the visual analogue scale (VAS) on postoperative day 1.ResultsMedian VAS score on postoperative day 1 was found to be 4 (3,4) in the patients where the peritoneum was left open as compared to 5 (4,5) in patients where the peritoneum was closed (p < 0.001). Similarly, on postoperative day 2, the median VAS score in the peritoneum closure group was 4 (3,4) as compared to 3 (3,5) in the non-closure group. 29 out of 69 patients (42%) in the peritoneal closure group required rescue analgesia as compared to 11 out of 71 patients (15.5%) in the group with non-closure of the peritoneum (p < 0.001). The median duration of analgesia received, across both study groups was found to be 5 days.ConclusionThis study showed that peritoneal non-closure during open appendectomy is associated with less post-operative pain. It also significantly reduces the need for rescue analgesia and decreases the total number of days of analgesia used. There is no significant increase in surgical site infection and wound dehiscence after 30 days through the above intervention, implying no difference in morbidity with the intervention.

Introduction

The lifetime risk of acute appendicitis is 7–8%, making it one of the most common surgical emergencies worldwide, and appendectomy is one of the most commonly performed emergency surgeries and contributes to a major burden on the modern health system [1]. Closure of the peritoneum has been a standard practice in appendectomy. However, it has been proposed that closure of the peritoneum following any abdominal surgery is more likely to give rise to adhesions and increased postoperative pain as a result of reaction to the suture material and tissue ischemia [2, 3].

In epidermal repair, healing starts gradually from the edges of the wound. However, in the peritoneum, multiple sites of repair are initiated by mesothelial cells resulting in simultaneous healing throughout the wound [2]. Spontaneous reperitonealization starts within 48–72 h of injury, and it takes about 5 to 6 days to complete when the peritoneum is left open [3–5]. Moreover, suturing of the peritoneum tends to cause aggravated inflammation, ischemia of tissue and foreign body reaction to the suture material. The previously mentioned factors contribute to slow wound healing, increased adhesion formation and postoperative pain [2, 3, 6]. This study was based on the hypothesis that peritoneal non-closure will result in decreased postoperative pain without causing any significant increase in complications.

Materials and methods

The primary objective of this study was to compare the pain score using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) on postoperative day 1 following open appendectomy between patients having non-closure and closure of peritoneum while undergoing open appendectomy. Secondary objectives were to compare the pain score on postoperative day 2, the requirement of analgesics in the postoperative period, the incidence of surgical site infection and the incidence of wound dehiscence in 30 days between the two groups.

This was a prospective parallel arm double-blinded randomized controlled trial conducted in a tertiary care institute in India after obtaining clearance from the Institute’s Ethics Committee for interventional studies and registering the trial at the Clinical Trial Registry of India with CTRI No. 2022/11/047615. All patients aged 18 to 65 years, undergoing open appendectomy for acute appendicitis from December 2022 to April 2024 were included in this study. Patients with perforated appendix noted intraoperatively, presence of intra-abdominal abscess, localized or diffuse purulent fluid in the abdominal cavity, patients requiring Rutherford-Morrison incision, pregnancy and patients with chronic renal failure, diabetes mellitus or known psychiatric or mental disorders were excluded from this study.

Patients in both groups received 1 g of Ceftriaxone and 500 mg of Metronidazole intravenously before the surgery. Mc Burney’s incision was placed, and after opening the abdomen and confirming the intraoperative findings, patients were randomized to either Group A (Peritoneal non-closure) or Group B (Peritoneal closure). Block randomization using a computer program with randomly selected blocks of sizes four and six was done. Patients were allocated into groups using serially numbered opaque sealed envelope (SNOSE) technique. Open appendectomy was performed as per the standard technique. In Group A, following appendectomy, at the time of closure of the incision, the peritoneum was left open. The muscle layer was approximated using interrupted 2 − 0 polyglactin suture and the external oblique aponeurosis was closed with continuous 2 − 0 polyglactin suture. Subcutaneous tissue was approximated with a 2 − 0 polyglactin suture, and finally, the skin was sutured with an interrupted 3 − 0 polypropylene suture. In Group B, after performing appendectomy, the peritoneum was closed with a 2 − 0 polyglactin suture in a continuous fashion. The rest of the incision was closed, as in Group A.

All the patients, as a standard of care, received 1 gram of Ceftriaxone 12 hourly and 500 mg Metronidazole 8 hourly intravenously, and Diclofenac sodium 50 mg 12 hourly through intramuscular injection for 24 h. On postoperative day 1, Diclofenac sodium 50 mg tablet was given twice a day if required. Pain score was assessed using visual analogue scale by resident doctor who was blinded. Rescue analgesia in the form of intramuscular injection of 50 milligrams of Tramadol hydrochloride was given when the VAS pain score was 4 or above. Patients were discharged once on an oral soft diet. Patients were followed up in the surgery outpatient department on the 10th postoperative day for surgical site inspection and suture removal and then again on the 30th postoperative day.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables that had a normal distribution computed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnoff test were defined using mean and standard deviation and analyzed using the student t-test. For non-parametric variables, median and range were used and compared using Mann-Whitney test. Qualitative variables (sex, surgical site infection, wound dehiscence) were defined using percentages and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 19.0 software. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Sample size calculation

Considering a VAS score on postoperative day 1 as 3.7 ± 0.6 in the non-closure group and 4.0 ± 0.6 in the peritoneal closure group from a previous study,7 with a confidence level of 95%, power of 80%, and precision of 5%, the sample size was calculated to be 140, considering 10% attrition using OpenEpi software v 3.01.

Results

All patients presenting to Surgery casualty diagnosed with acute appendicitis and planned for open emergent appendectomy were assessed for their eligibility. 178 patients were screened, and 150 patients were found eligible and were recruited into the study after obtaining informed written consent. 28 patients were excluded for reasons like age of less than 18 years (5 patients), pregnancy (1 patient) and diabetes mellitus (22 patients). Eligible patients were taken up for emergent open appendectomy. 10 patients were found to have perforated appendix intraoperatively and were excluded from the study. Finally, 140 patients were then randomized by block randomization using blocks of four and six into two groups. The peritoneum was not closed in 71 patients and was closed in 69 patients. No participant was lost to follow-up. None of them withdrew from the study. A schematic representation of the study as per the CONSORT 2010 (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. Demographic parameters were found to be homogeneous across the study groups, as shown in Table 1. The median age was found to be 26 years in the peritoneal closure group and 25 years in the peritoneal non-closure group. The total duration of surgery was defined as the time from placement of incision to skin closure and was recorded in minutes. The mean duration of surgery was found to be 52.5 min in the peritoneal closure group and 52.6 min in the peritoneal non-closure group. Homogeneity across the study groups was confirmed with the help of independent student t-test, and the p-value was found to be 0.97.

The pain scores on postoperative day 1 were compared between the two groups and are elaborated in Table 3. The median VAS score was found to be 4 (3,4) in the patients where the peritoneum was left open as compared to 5 (4,5) in patients where the peritoneum was closed along with other layers of the abdominal wall. Mann Whitney U test was performed as the outcome was ordinal, and the difference was found to be significant, p < 0.001. Patients with a VAS score of more than 4 were given rescue analgesia as per the protocol of this study. Twenty-nine out of 69 patients (42%) in the peritoneal closure group required rescue analgesia as compared to 11 out of 71 patients (15.5%) in the group with peritoneal non-closure. This difference in proportion was found to be statistically significant by the Pearson Chi-square test with a p-value of < 0.001. The absolute risk reduction is 26.5% and number needed to treat is 3.7.

Discussion

This study found that there was a significant reduction in postoperative VAS pain score on POD-1 and POD-2 by leaving the peritoneum open after open appendectomy. There was also a significant reduction in the need for rescue analgesia and the number of days of analgesia requirement between both groups. It had been proposed through previous studies that closure of the peritoneum would lead to an increase in post-operative pain due to ischemia of the peritoneal layer. Also, foreign body reaction to the suture material used would also contribute to postoperative pain. As a practice, closure of the peritoneum was being routinely carried out, fearing the incidence of postoperative surgical site infection and wound dehiscence. However, in this study, there was no significant increase in the incidence of SSI or wound dehiscence by leaving the peritoneum open, as feared in previous studies.

This study was conducted on patients undergoing emergency open appendectomy for acute appendicitis in the age group of 18 to 65 years. Pregnant, immunocompromised and patients undergoing muscle-cutting incisions were excluded from the study. The study population was similar to that in a randomized control trial conducted by Basarkod et al.. in 2012, Wilkinson et al. in 2018, and Huseyin et al. in 2019 [7–9]. There is more available literature in gynecological practice where the abandonment of peritoneal closure following cesarean section has resulted in lower postoperative pain [10–13].

This study showed a significant reduction in postoperative pain measured in terms of VAS score measured on postoperative days 1 and 2. This finding is similar to a randomized control trial conducted by Basarkod et al.. in 2012, where there was a reduction in postoperative pain score assessed at 24 h and requirement of post operative analgesia after 24 h in the non-closure than in the closure group [7]. However, in a study conducted by Wilkinson et al. in 2018, there was no difference in postoperative analgesia requirement [8]. In a trial conducted in 2019 by Huseyin et al., it was seen that leaving the peritoneum open resulted in a lower median VAS score as compared to closing the peritoneal layer [6]. However, this difference in medians of 3 and 4 in non-closure and closure groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.134). Kapikeri V et al., in a study conducted in 2019, found no significant difference in perception of pain as measured with VAS score in the postoperative period [14]. A randomized control study done by Farooq et al.. showed a statistically significant difference only in the number of complaints between the groups undergoing and not undergoing peritoneal closure [15]. There was a statistically significant difference in the perception of pain.

In our study, there was a significant decrease in the requirement of rescue analgesia and the duration of analgesic requirement in patients undergoing open appendectomy with peritoneal non-closure. Kapikeri V et al. found that the requirement for higher analgesics between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.0001), even though there was no significant reduction in perception of pain between the groups [14]. Similarly, Basarkod et al. found the postoperative requirement of analgesia was significantly lower in the group where the peritoneum was not closed after 24 h [7]. However, the study conducted by Wilkinson et al.., Huseyin et al. and Farooq et al.concluded that non-closure of the peritoneum at open appendectomy was associated with no difference in postoperative analgesia requirement [8, 9, 15].

In this study, there was no statistically significant difference in incidence of SSI between the study groups. Patients in both groups were followed up postoperatively for a period of 30 days, and the incidence of wound dehiscence in both groups was not found to be statistically significant. This absence of a significant difference in short-term morbidity between peritoneal closure and non-closure has been supported by various studies. The study conducted by Wilkinson et al. in 2018 concluded that non-closure of peritoneum at open appendectomy was associated with reduced operating time and early discharge from the hospital [8]. Also, there was no difference in the incidence of postoperative complications. A prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted by Hugh et al. on peritoneal closure in midline abdominal surgical wounds showed no significant difference in the requirement of postoperative opioid analgesic pain scores or incidence of wound complications [16].

In our study, there was no significant difference in operating time between the peritoneal non-closure and closure groups. Kapikeri V et al., found a statistically significant (p < 0.016) difference in operative time between the group [14]. In a study conducted by Huseyin et al., non-closure during open appendectomy had a significant decrease in the duration of surgery from a mean duration of 38.5 min in the peritoneal closure group to 31 min in the non-closure group with a p-value of 0.016. There was no change in incidence of complications between the two groups and quality of life following the surgery was also similar among the groups [9].

Strengths

Most of the studies comparing peritoneal non-closure with closure during the closure of the abdominal incision had been performed in gynecological and obstetric operations evaluating the postoperative outcomes like pain, duration of hospital stay, postoperative complications and formation of adhesions. Only a few studies comparing this aspect had been performed while performing open appendectomy, with conflicting results. Conclusion

This randomized, controlled, double-blinded trial compared the postoperative pain between patients in whom the peritoneum was closed and those in whom the peritoneum was not closed during emergent open appendectomy for acute appendicitis. This study showed that leaving the peritoneal layer open after open appendectomy for acute appendicitis results in less post-operative pain assessed using visual analogue score. It also significantly reduces the need for rescue analgesia and decreases the total number of days of analgesia used. There is no significant increase in surgical site infection and wound dehiscence after 30 days through the above intervention, implying no difference in morbidity with the intervention. Hence, non-closure of the peritoneum can be safely carried out as a routine following emergency open appendectomy for acute appendicitis.
