Cardiometabolic impact of dolutegravir as second-line therapy: secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Phyo Pyae Nyein, Mark W. Donoghoe, Nnakelu Eriobu, Blessing Uche, Richard Kaplan, Dianne Carey, Dannae Brown, Mariana Kundro, Nila J. Dharan, Josh Hanson, Kathy Petoumenos, Gail V. Matthews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To examine changes in weight after initiating different dolutegravir-based second-line antiretroviral regimens and to measure the impact of observed weight change on blood pressure and serum lipids.Design:Secondary analysis of the D2EFT trial, a randomized open-label trial in people with HIVMethods:In D2EFT, ritonavir-boosted darunavir plus dolutegravir (DTG + DRV/r) and dolutegravir with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate plus either lamivudine or emtricitabine (DTG + TDF/XTC) were compared to ritonavir-boosted darunavir plus two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (DRV/r + 2NRTIs). We used linear mixed effects models for weight, blood pressure and lipid changes and generalized estimating equations for ≥5% weight gain.Results:Eight hundred and twenty-six participants were included. Significantly greater body weight and BMI gains at 48 and 96 weeks were observed in individuals taking DTG + DRV/r or DTG + TDF/XTC than in individuals taking DRV/r + 2NRTIs. Treatment arm (DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC), female gender, Black and Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, lower baseline CD4+ count, and higher baseline HIV RNA were all associated with weight gain in multivariable analysis. There was no significant difference between treatment arms in blood pressure changes at 96 weeks after adjustment for weight gain. Both darunavir containing arms demonstrated greater increases in LDL cholesterol than DTG + TDF/XTC, with the greatest increase in the non-NRTI containing arm.Conclusions:Dolutegravir-based second-line regimens were associated with weight gain, which was further influenced by gender, ethnicity and HIV-related factors. DTG-based second-line regimens had no significant effect on blood pressure at 96 weeks; DTG + TDF/XTC was associated with a more favourable lipid profile than darunavir-containing regimens.Clinical trial registration number:NCT03017872

Introduction

With the advent of potent antiretroviral therapy (ART), HIV care has shifted from focussing primarily on viral suppression toward holistic long-term health management. As managing metabolic and cardiovascular risks becomes central to improving both survival and quality of life of people with HIV, assessing the cardiometabolic safety of antiretrovirals has become a crucial component of antiretroviral clinical trials, providing essential guidance for everyday clinical practice.

Dolutegravir (DTG), an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI), has been variably linked to weight gain, raising concerns regarding its cardiometabolic safety [1–4]. At the same time, its excellent antiviral efficacy, high genetic barrier to resistance, favourable tolerability profile, minimal drug interactions, cost-effectiveness, and availability in single-tablet combinations with nucleoside reverse transferase inhibitors (NRTIs) support its widespread clinical and programmatic use [5]. While studies in ART-naïve and virally suppressed individuals have consistently reported DTG-related weight gain [3,6–8], other studies, including NADIA, found comparable weight changes between DTG- and DRV/r-based second-line regimens in those with nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based first-line failure [9,10]. The evidence on whether DTG directly or indirectly – through weight gain – affects blood pressure or plasma lipid levels also remains inconclusive [8,11,12]. As weight gain is a part of normal aging and a common consequence of viral suppression, it is critical to determine the independent contribution of DTG-associated weight gain on cardiometabolic health, ideally through randomized trials.

The Dolutegravir and Darunavir Evaluation in adults Failing Therapy (D2EFT) trial assessed the performance of DTG-containing second-line regimens. Virological and immunological outcomes at the primary endpoint (week 48) and at extended follow-up (week 96) have been reported, with both intervention arms demonstrating virological noninferiority to DRV/r + 2NRTIs [13,14]. Greater weight gain was also observed in DTG-containing arms at weeks 48 and 96. In this analysis, we used a repeated measures approach to examine trajectories of weight change, allowing a more comprehensive assessment across the study period. We also examined the association between weight change and related cardiometabolic outcomes, including blood pressure and lipid levels.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a secondary analysis examining cardiometabolic endpoints of the D2EFT trial. D2EFT was a prospective, multicentre, phase IIIB/IV, randomized, open-label trial in people with HIV who had failed NNRTI-based first-line therapy (NCT03017872). Ritonavir-boosted darunavir plus dolutegravir (DTG + DRV/r) and dolutegravir with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate plus either lamivudine or emtricitabine (DTG + TDF/XTC) were compared to ritonavir-boosted darunavir plus two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (DRV/r + 2NRTIs). The study commenced with comparison of two arms: DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs (Stage 1). Subsequently, it evolved to include a third arm, DTG + TDF/XTC (Stage 2), enabling comparison of both DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC against DRV/r + 2NRTIs. The rationale and details of the D2EFT design adaptations have been reported [15]. The trial's primary outcome was viral suppression at 48 weeks; additional outcomes at 96 weeks have also been reported [13,14].

Study population

D2EFT recruited participants from 28 sites in 14 low- and middle-income countries, in Latin America, Africa and Asia (Argentina, Chile, Colombia, India, Malaysia, Mexico, South Africa, Thailand, Zimbabwe, Brazil, Guinea, Indonesia, Mali and Nigeria). Eligibility criteria of the trial included positive HIV-1 serology, age ≥18 years, and two consecutive HIV RNA >500 copies/ml (≥7 days apart) after at least 24 weeks of continuous NNRTI + 2NRTIs therapy. Full eligibility criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content. All participants provided written informed consent.

In this analysis, we included all randomized participants from D2EFT with available data for analyses of body weight, blood pressure and lipids. Blood pressure analyses excluded participants who had hypertension at baseline, defined by a documented diagnosis of hypertension, or systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg. Lipid analyses excluded participants who had dyslipidaemia at baseline, defined by documented diagnosis of dyslipidaemia or fasting LDL cholesterol ≥4.1 mmol/l (160 mg/dl).

Assessment and outcomes

All D2EFT participants were followed through to week 96, and clinical and laboratory assessments occurred at scheduled visits at weeks 0, 4, 24, 48 and 96. Cardiometabolic parameters including weight, height, blood pressure and serum lipids were measured at weeks 0, 48 and 96. The outcomes of this analysis included mean changes in body weight and body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, proportion of participants with ≥5% weight gain, and mean changes in SBP, DBP and lipids from baseline through week 96.

Statistical analysis

All analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) approach, including all randomized participants irrespective of treatment switches and excluding participants who were randomized but did not commence their assigned ART.

Changes in the continuous outcome measures (body weight, BMI, waist circumference, SBP, DBP, LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol) from baseline to weeks 48 and 96 were compared between arms using linear mixed effects models, which is valid under a Missing at Random (MAR) assumption [16]. Missing weight was not imputed. Time was treated as a categorical variable to allow flexibility in detecting nonlinear changes. Fixed effects included treatment, time and time-treatment interaction. Random effects included participant level intercepts and random slopes for time. Following the identification of an association between weight change and sex in the primary analysis, an additional post hoc analysis stratified by sex was conducted to further explore this relationship. For blood pressure and lipid outcomes, additional analyses of change also adjusted for weight over time. The proportion of participants with ≥5% weight gain at weeks 48 and 96 were compared using generalized estimating equations with a logit link, binomial distribution and an exchangeable correlation structure. Comparisons were made for DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs and DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs. To avoid comparison between participants enrolled at different times, the DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs comparison included participants enrolled in Stage 2 only. No adjustment for baseline characteristics was performed, as randomization was assumed to be preserved even after exclusions and baseline characteristics were comparable across the treatment arms.

To assess factors associated with weight gain, we fitted linear mixed effects model for weight gain as described above, including categorical time, treatment and categorical time-treatment interaction, and then added key covariates hypothesized to influence weight gain, and their interactions with time, to the model. Covariates were identified a priori and included stage of enrolment, age at randomization, sex, ethnicity, duration of ART, smoking status, TDF versus non-TDF containing regimen in prior ART, baseline CD4+ count, baseline HIV RNA and baseline body weight. Missing values were not imputed. Time was included as a continuous variable in the time-covariate interactions. Participants enrolled in both Stage 1 and 2 were included in the model and stage of enrolment was adjusted as a covariate.

Sensitivity analyses for weight change and BMI change were performed excluding people who are underweight at baseline, defined as BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2. Sensitivity analyses for SBP, DBP, LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol outcomes included analyses adjusted for predefined baseline covariates and analyses including participants who had hypertension (for SBP and DBP) or dyslipidaemia (for lipid outcomes) at baseline.

There was no adjustment for multiple comparisons. All P-values were two-sided and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata (Version 18.0).

Results

Participant characteristics

Between November 2017 and January 2022, D2EFT recruited 828 participants, and 826 (261 in DRV/r + 2NRTIs, 271 in DTG + DRV/r and 294 in DTG + TDF/XTC) received at least one dose of study medication and attended at least one follow-up visit, constituting the mITT population. All 826 participants were included for body weight and BMI analyses as no baseline body weight data was missing. Blood pressure and lipid analyses included 663 participants (209 in DRV/r + 2NRTIs + 226 in DTG + DRV/r and 228 in DTG + TDF/XTC) and 782 participants (252 in DRV/r + 2NRTIs + 258 in DTG + DRV/r and 272 in DTG + TDF/XTC), respectively (Table 1).

The overall median [IQR] age was 39 [33,46] years, 450/825 (54%) were female and 570/826 (69%) were Black. The median [IQR] baseline body weight and BMI were 62.7 [54.1, 72.8] kg and 23.1 [20.3, 26.8] kg/m2 respectively. Ten percentage of the overall study population were underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2) at baseline. The median [IQR] baseline SBP, DBP and LDL cholesterol were 119 [110, 128] mmHg, 78 [70, 84] mmHg and 2.5 [2.0, 3.0] mmol/L respectively. The baseline characteristics of the participants were comparable across all arms in body weight, blood pressure and lipid analyses (Table 1). At randomization, TDF served as one of the backbone agents in 49/261 (19%) in the DRV/r + 2NRTI arm and 285/294 (97%) in the DTG + TDF/XTC arm (9 participants were inadvertently enrolled whilst on an alternative NRTI-backbone; all of whom except three were switched on to TDF/XTC within a median of 18 days (IQR 8–45) after randomization).

Body weight and BMI

There was a significantly greater body weight and BMI gain in both DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC compared to DRV/r + 2NRTIs at 48 and 96 weeks. The estimated differences in mean [95% confidence interval (CI)] body weight (kg) change from baseline were 2.7 [1.8, 3.7] at week 48 and 3.5 [2.2, 4.8] at week 96 for DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs and 1.6 [0.6, 2.7] at week 48 and 1.9 [0.5, 3.3] at week 96 for DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs (Table 2 and Fig. 1). The estimated differences in mean [95% CI] BMI (kg/m2) change from baseline were 1.0 [0.7, 1.4] at week 48 and 1.3 [0.9, 1.8] at week 96 for DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs, 0.6 [0.2, 1.0] at week 48 and 0.7 [0.2, 1.3] at week 96 for DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Results of the post hoc sex-stratified analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content. A similar pattern was observed for waist circumference in DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs, but there was no statistically significant difference in waist circumference in DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs (Supplementary Table 3, Supplemental Digital Content). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who were underweight at baseline yielded similar results.

Serum lipids

Greater increases in LDL cholesterol were observed in DTG + DRV/r than DRV/r + 2NRTIs and in DRV/r + 2NRTIs than DTG + TDF/XTC at weeks 48 and 96, which remained significant after adjustment for weight gain (Table 5). Similar changes in total cholesterol were observed with greater rise in DTG + DRV/r compared to DRV/r + 2NRTIs and in DRV/r + 2NRTIs compared to DTG + TDF/XTC (Supplementary Table 4, Supplemental Digital Content).

There was no statistically significant difference in HDL cholesterol change in DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs at weeks 48 and 96. DRV/r + 2NRTIs was associated with a significantly greater rise in HDL cholesterol compared to DTG + TDF/XTC at week 48 and 96 (Supplementary Table 4, Supplemental Digital Content).

Similar results were observed in sensitivity analyses that adjusted for the baseline covariates and that included participants who had dyslipidaemia at baseline (Supplementary Table 5, Supplemental Digital Content).

Discussion

In this analysis of cardiometabolic outcomes from the three-arm randomized D2EFT trial, both DTG containing arms, DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC, were associated with a significantly higher body weight (including >5% weight gain) and BMI gain compared to DRV/r + 2NRTIs after 48 and 96 weeks. Being initiated on DTG-containing regimens (DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC), being female, Black and Hispanic/Latino versus Asian ethnicity, baseline CD4+ cell count <200 cells/mm3 versus 350 cells/mm3 or more, and baseline HIV RNA 50 000–100 000 copies/ml and >100 000 copies/ml versus <50 000 copies/ml were associated with greater weight gain in the multivariable model.

Initiating or switching to DTG has been linked with weight gain in both ART naïve and virally suppressed individuals [3,6–8]. However, the NADIA study reported comparable weight changes between DTG- and DRV/r-based second-line regimens among those who experienced NNRTI-based first-line failure [9,10]. Moreover, defining the independent contribution of DTG to weight gain in clinical studies is challenging. The impact on weight of prior, concomitant and comparator antiretrovirals, concomitant changes in backbone NRTIs when ART is switched, the possibility of pharmacokinetic interactions, and viral suppression (return-to-health phenomenon) all complicate this interpretation [17]. Notably, in our study, outcomes were compared against DRV/r, a contemporary protease inhibitor, ensuring clinical relevance, as different protease inhibitors have shown different impact on weight [8]. The use of prior TDF and efavirenz (EFV) was balanced across arms, mitigating the impact on weight by stopping antiretrovirals that can affect weight. However, TDF use by treatment arm did differ as almost all participants in the DTG + TDF/XTC arm, 19% in the DRV/r + 2NRTI arm and none in the DTG + DRV/r arm were on TDF. The greater weight gain observed with DTG + TDF/XTC compared with DRV/r + 2NRTIs indicates that concomitant TDF use did not explain the weight differences; in fact, the potential weight-suppressive effect of TDF may have attenuated the observed differences. Similarly, the weight-stabilizing effect of ZDV + 3TC might have contributed to the lesser weight gain observed in the DRV/r + 2NRTI arm, as 77% of participants in this group received ZDV + 3TC [13], which has been reported to be associated with less weight gain compared with other NRTI combinations [1].

As with other studies [1,2], weight gain in our cohort was associated with lower CD4+ cell count and higher HIV RNA at baseline suggesting that return-to-health had some role in weight gain. Both DTG + DRV/r and DTG + TDF/XTC were also found to be virologically superior to DRV/r + 2NRTIs at 96 weeks [14]. However, in the multivariable model which adjusted for baseline CD4+ cell count and HIV RNA, weight gain remained significantly associated with DTG-containing regimens. Again, there was no association between baseline body weight and weight gain in the multivariable model, and the findings were consistent in the sensitivity analyses that excluded participants who were underweight at baseline. Thus, return-to-health cannot be considered as the sole explanation for the greater weight gain observed in DTG containing arms. Greater weight gain among participants with lower baseline CD4+ cell count and higher HIV RNA also suggest the possibility of rebound and overshoot weight gain following severe catabolism, as reported in prior studies [18]. Other potential yet incompletely understood mechanisms underlying DTG-associated weight gain include stimulation of adipocyte hypertrophy, adipogenesis, and lipogenesis, reduced leptin production, and inhibition of the melanocortin-4 receptor [19–21].

Female gender and Black ethnicity were associated with DTG-related weight gain in our cohort, replicating findings from previous studies [22–25]. The substantial representation of female (54.5%) and Black participants (69%) in our study strengthens the reliability of this observation. Hispanic/Latino ethnicity was also associated with weight gain; the smaller proportion of Hispanic/Latino participants (4.1%) limits the robustness of this observation. However, these associations are not unique to individuals taking DTG or even people with HIV. Similar patterns have been observed in individuals initiating ART regardless of regimen [1,26,27], as well as in non-HIV populations [28,29]. Biological, social and environmental factors, including perceived weight status, may underline these associations [30]. Importantly, weight gain may also carry differential health risks across gender and ethnic groups, for example, there are stronger links between waist circumference and systemic inflammation in Black women than in White men [31]. Asian individuals, on the other hand, are at higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus than Black individuals at the same BMI [32,33]. Adiposity increases similar and dissimilar health risks in men (e.g. prostate cancer) and women (for example postmenopausal weight gain and breast cancer), highlighting that clinical and public health implications of DTG-related weight gain should be interpreted within the context of gender and ethnicity. Future research should also examine whether gender and ethnicity exert additive or multiplicative effects on DTG-related weight gain.

Evidence linking DTG itself or DTG-associated weight gain to increased blood pressure or hypertension remains inconsistent [34]. Observational studies have reported an association between the use of INSTIs and incident hypertension [11,35]; however, most randomised trials did not observe this with DTG [12,36,37]. At 48 weeks in D2EFT, we reported significant increases in SBP and DBP, that persisted after adjustment for weight gain, in DTG + DRV/r versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs, though the effect sizes were modest and unlikely to be clinically meaningful [38]. In this longer-term analysis, no significant difference in DBP change was observed between DTG + DRV/r and DRV/r + 2NRTIs at 96 weeks, and the difference in SBP change was not significant after adjustment for weight gain. There was no significant difference in SBP and DBP rise in DTG + TDF/XTC versus DRV/r + 2NRTIs in both adjusted and unadjusted analyses at both time points. This underscores the need for cautious interpretation of observational studies, where unmeasured confounding and channelling bias might contribute to observed associations. On the other hand, as our study is limited by the lack of comprehensive data on antihypertensive use, newly initiated – but unrecorded – antihypertensive medications might have diminished differences in observed blood pressure. In the NAMSAL trial, the use of antihypertensive therapy was minimal and blood pressure differed significantly between the DTG and EFV arms, whereas in the ADVANCE trial, where antihypertensive therapy was initiated in individuals with Grade 1 hypertension, there was only marginal long-term between-arm differences in blood pressure [39]. Overall, while ongoing clinical optimization of cardiovascular risk reduction remains essential for all individuals with HIV, neither DTG-associated weight gain nor DTG itself is likely to cause clinically significant blood pressure rise.

In our study, both DTG + DRV/r and DRV/r + 2NRTIs were associated with a significantly greater increase in LDL cholesterol concentrations compared to DTG + TDF/XTC at weeks 48 and 96, an effect that remained significant even after adjustment for weight gain. We hypothesize that the protease inhibitor component predominantly accounted for the rise in LDL cholesterol. Protease inhibitors are well known to be associated with dyslipidaemia although later generation protease inhibitors have less detrimental impact on lipid profiles compared to earlier protease inhibitors [40–42]. Prior evidence suggest that INSTI-based ART has a favourable effect on lipid levels compared with protease inhibitors [43,44]. In the NEAT022 trial, switching from a boosted ATV- or DRV-based regimens to a DTG-based regimens in people with HIV with high cardiovascular risk improved lipid parameters [45]. The contrasting effects of DTG on weight and lipid-related cardiometabolic risk highlights the importance of evaluating cardiometabolic risk in a balanced manner, that also accounts for gender, ethnicity, and other contextual influences.

Our study has some limitations. Assessment of overall cardiovascular risk was not possible as not all components of the cardiovascular risk scores were available. We also did not uniformly collect detailed history of antihypertensive and lipid lowering medications. Follow up time may not be long enough to observe the long-term impact of DTG on cardiometabolic risks. Finally, excluding participants who had hypertension or dyslipidaemia at baseline in blood pressure and lipid analyses may have introduced selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals without these conditions. However, sensitivity analyses including these participants supported the robustness of the primary findings. The strengths of the study include representation of Black and female populations, mitigation of bias by discontinuing TDF or EFV, and comparison with DRV/r, the contemporary protease inhibitor.

In summary, our study demonstrated that DTG-containing second-line regimens are associated with weight gain among individuals experiencing NNRTI-based first-line failure, underscoring the importance of gender and ethnicity considerations when translating this association into clinical and public health practice. The absence of a clinically meaningful association between DTG-related weight gain and blood pressure together with the favourable lipid profile observed in the protease inhibitor-free arm, DTG + TDF/XTC, should also be factored into a balanced interpretation of the cardiometabolic safety of second-line regimens.
