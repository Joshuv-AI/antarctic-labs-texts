Nurses experiences with an intervention enhancing skill-mix in Kenyan neonatal units with severe workforce deficits: A qualitative study
Authors: Nancy Odinga, Caroline Waithira, Justinah Maluni, Gloria Ngaiza, Vincent Kagonya, Onesmus Onyango, Abdulazeez Imam, David Gathara, Michuki Maina, Kenneth Karumba, Fred Were, Sebastian Fuller, Mike English, Edna Mutua, Sassy Molyneux, Caroline Jones, Dorothy Oluoch, HIGH-Q Hospital Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses remain critical in newborn care delivery in Kenya. However, persistent nurse shortages in newborn units limit their ability to provide optimal care. Staff shortages contribute to missed care and high workloads, negatively impacting the motivation and well-being of nurses. Evidence from Low and middle-income countries reveals strategies employed by nurses to manage high workloads. These include prioritisation of care and informal task shifting, where tasks are delegated to caregivers, student nurses, and support staff. While sharing and shifting non-technical tasks to less qualified personnel may be helpful, perceptions linked to the cadre of staff taking on extra roles and effects on the care performed and other critical relational aspects of care are often unclear or overlooked. To generate evidence on the impact of staffing interventions, we investigated the effects of workforce interventions on supporting neonatal care in hospitals in Kenya. In this paper, we report on the experiences of nurses with the intervention.MethodsWe adopted an ethnographic approach to explore nurses experiences with a staffing intervention in four Kenyan newborn units (neonatal units). Non-participant observations on neonatal units and in-depth interviews were conducted with healthcare providers. Data collection occurred in three phases: at baseline (Phase 1), after supplementing the units with additional nurses (Phase 2), and after supplementing the units with ward assistants (Phase 3). Over 1000 hours of observations and 112 in-depth interviews were conducted between January 2022 and July 2023. Drawing from the project's theory of change, we used a thematic approach in analysing and interpreting the data.ResultsAdditional nurses and ward assistants were perceived as contributing to the overall motivation and well-being of existing nurses. Additional nurses meant that there were more hands per shift, which enhanced teamwork, task sharing, and task completion. The reported benefits of having more ward assistants included nurses being able to delegate non-technical tasks to ward assistants, which reduced nurses workload and freed up their time to concentrate on critical nursing tasks. This contributed to improved nurse motivation and well-being.ConclusionsAdequate staffing is crucial for delivering optimal care quality and is central to nurses well-being. There is a need to increase nurse staffing in neonatal units. Further beneficial support for nurses may be gained by carefully designing and implementing non-technical task-sharing roles.Keywords: Neonatal units, Nurses, Ward assistants, Intervention, Experiences, Public hospitals, Task sharing

Introduction

Nurses play a pivotal role in care provision in hospital settings and are central to the efforts to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals. However, there are existing global disparities in the distribution of nurses (WHO, 2020). Out of the global shortfall of 5.9 million nurses, 89 % of these shortfalls are estimated to be in Low and Middle-Income countries (WHO, 2020). Kenya is experiencing shortages in the nursing workforce, with an estimated 8.3 nurses per 10,000 population compared with ratios in regions such as Europe and America of 79.3 and 83.4, respectively (MOH Kenya, 2015; Wakaba et al., 2014; WHO, 2020).

Shortages in nurse staffing create significant challenges in providing quality services to neonates, with studies showing associations between high nurse-to-baby ratios and missed neonatal nursing care (Gathara et al., 2020; Kaur et al., 2023; Lake et al., 2020). Missed nursing care comprises care required to be delivered that is partially done, significantly delayed, or missed entirely (Kohanová et al., 2023; Lake et al., 2020; Tubbs-Cooley et al., 2019). Missed nursing care threatens patient safety and could potentially have profound effects on the health outcomes of neonates (Gathara et al., 2020). It also affects staff job satisfaction and is associated with the intention to quit work (Alsalem et al., 2023; Blackman et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2020).

Nurses provide direct care to babies and perform indirect care that may include multiple non-technical tasks. Some of these tasks are below the skill level of nurses and do not require nursing education (Aiken et al., 2001; Bruyneel et al., 2013; Grosso et al., 2021). The performance of these tasks adds to nurses workload. As a way of managing the workload, there is increasing evidence of nurses prioritising technical tasks over bedside care and prioritising critically ill babies for care (Mbuthia et al., 2023; McKnight et al., 2020). While this strategy may seem understandable, prolonged practice can create nursing norms that prioritise disease-focused care, overlooking the needs of patients and other essential aspects of care, such as communication and support. The provision of holistic care is thereby limited (Imam et al., 2023). To achieve holistic care in neonatal units, studies suggest addressing nurse staffing, including their working environments (Gathara et al., 2020; Kim and Chae, 2022).

Other approaches, such as task shifting and task sharing, are emerging in care delivery in resource-constrained settings. As described by the World Health Organization (WHO), task shifting entails moving specific tasks to another cadre with shorter training and fewer qualifications (WHO, 2008). Task sharing entails the rational distribution of identified tasks among staff (Schaefer, 2015). While task shifting may be seen as a way of addressing human resource challenges (Deller et al., 2015), there are both positive and negative sentiments regarding the implementation of task shifting (Kagonya et al., 2023). There are concerns about quality and safety (Fulton et al., 2011; Munga et al., 2012), supervision and training (Baine and Kasangaki, 2014), threats to existing professionals (Mijovic et al., 2016), and a lack of regulatory frameworks and policies for implementation (Deller et al., 2015; Kagonya et al., 2023). More positively, the shifting of unskilled or nonclinical tasks has been supported as a means of reducing nurses workloads, helping them to concentrate on clinical tasks, and potentially improving the delivery of essential healthcare interventions (Mbouamba Yankam et al., 2023).

Although task shifting and task sharing are routinely practised in many resource-constrained settings, the scope of services shifted as described in a scoping review conducted by (Okoroafor and Christmals, 2023), includes hypertension, diabetes, mental health, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, sexual and reproductive health, surgical care, maternal and child health, medicines management and emergency care. Within the neonatal units, formal institutionalisation of task shifting and sharing remains a challenge (Munga et al., 2012; Nzinga et al., 2019; Omondi et al., 2020). Within the context of neonatal nursing in Kenya, stakeholders in policy formulation spaces agree on the concept of task shifting (Murphy et al., 2018; Oluoch et al., 2018). However, evidence of benefits or harms would help support policy decisions on staffing.

The HIGH-Q project (Harnessing Innovation in Global Health for Quality Care), described in detail in a separate paper, aimed to generate such information and was implemented in four public hospitals, which are part of the NEST 360 (Newborn Essential Solutions and Technologies) programme (Maina et al., 2025). The NEST 360 programme, being conducted in partnership with the Ministry of Health, equips newborn units with newborn technologies and provides associated training and support (NEST 360, 2023). The HIGH-Q project supplemented this intervention by adding three nurses and ward assistants in each of the four neonatal units (Imam et al., 2022). To characterise the intervention in detail, we developed a HIGH-Q project Theory of Change for the nurses and ward assistants intervention, which is captured in supplementary materials 2 and 3, as well as in a separate paper (Maina et al., 2025). This was done to inform the design and implementation of future staffing interventions and considerations of skill mix in neonatal units. This study explored how nurses in four neonatal units described their experiences following a workforce intervention. Specifically, it aimed to explore the context of care in these units at baseline and the nurses experiences after the addition of three nurses and ward assistants to each of the four neonatal units. The specific research questions included the following:

Methods

Study design and data collection methods

Using an ethnographic approach, we conducted longitudinal observations and interviews with healthcare providers as part of a wider evaluation (Maina et al., 2025). Our longitudinal approach enabled us to collect, analyse, and compare data across three phases (Molloy et al., 2002). Data were collected across four purposively selected public hospital neonatal units in Kenya. Two of the larger hospitals were located in urban areas and had broad catchment areas, while the other two were situated in semi-urban areas with smaller catchment areas. All nurses in the neonatal unit available during the project period were eligible for participation. However, for in-depth interviews, the nurses were purposively selected based on their availability and willingness to participate and share their experiences (Palinkas et al., 2015). The educational background of the nurses varied. While the minimum qualification was a certificate, most nurses across the four neonatal units possessed diplomas. There were also a few nurses with bachelor's degrees and higher diplomas with specialisation in paediatric nursing.

Data were collected by NO, CW, JM and GN, four female researchers of African descent. NO and CW are social scientists, GN is a medical doctor, while JM is a public health professional. All the researchers had experience in qualitative data collection methods. We utilised different data collection tools for triangulation and held weekly reflexive sessions to ensure rigour. We conducted three rounds of data collection: baseline (phase 1), between January and April 2022, after the introduction of the three additional nurses (phase 2), between August 2022 and January 2023, and after the addition of three ward assistants (phase 3), between May and July 2023. Cumulatively, the workforce intervention periods for nurses and ward assistants in the neonatal units were 15 and 7 months, respectively.

A total of 112 in-depth interviews with nurses were conducted, together with over 1000 hours of non-participant observations across the four hospitals. Interviews were audio recorded and transferred to a secure OneDrive programme hosted by KEMRI Wellcome Trust Research Programme and only accessible to the research team in the HIGH-Q project. Handwritten observation notes were typed into a Word document. Audio recordings were transcribed and verified for accuracy of information. The transcripts and observation notes were uploaded to NVIVO 12 software for management and analysis. Additionally, data sources such as Health Facility Assessments and Daily Bed Attendances were used to understand the context before and during the intervention.

We employed a thematic analysis to analyse the data. NO and CW jointly developed an initial coding framework using the interview guides and observation checklists. We incorporated the theory of change for nurses and ward assistants as our starting point for deductive coding. We also employed an inductive coding approach, which allowed us to explore emerging findings and generate new themes as the analysis progressed in discussions with DO, EM, CJ, and SM. In our coding, analysis, and interpretation, we examined the changes in key domains of interest related to nurses experiences with the interventions. As stipulated in our theory of change, we hoped that the addition of three trained nurses to each neonatal unit would contribute to better staff-to-patient ratios during shifts, reduce workplace stress, improve relations among nurses, and improve nurses experiences working in the neonatal units. In the ward assistants job description co-designed with nursing stakeholders including frontline nurses, nursing regulator, and professional associations, we also anticipated that the addition of 3 ward assistants (job description attached as supplementary material 1) would reduce the requirement for nurses to perform non-technical tasks, freeing up their time for professional activities and further reducing workplace stress (see attached supplementary material 2 and 3).

Ethical considerations

This study was part of the larger HIGH-Q project, which evaluated the effects of technology and workforce interventions on neonatal care (Maina et al., 2025). Ethical approval for the HIGH-Q project was received from the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) Scientific and Ethics Review Unit (SERU)-SERU number and KEMRI/SERU/CGMR-C/229/4203. Additional approvals were sought from the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) (NACOSTI/P/22/21337), the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (Reference 26-21), the counties, and the implementing hospitals. Within the hospitals, we relied on initial engagements to introduce the project. Consenting for observations was done continuously to help new mothers and healthcare providers with whom we were interacting for the first time understand our work. Written informed consent was obtained for the in-depth interviews. Throughout the data collection period, the researchers adhered to ethical principles, including maintaining the confidentiality of information, respecting the right to withdraw, and ensuring that participation was voluntary.

Results

In this section, we provide a brief description of nurses work contexts in neonatal units. The theory of change for nurses guides the reporting of the findings. These findings are essential in linking and understanding nurses experiences before and after the neonatal units were supplemented with three additional nurses and ward assistants.

Context of care before the nurses intervention

Our findings reveal variations in nurse staffing numbers, shift schedules, and the number of nurses available per shift across the four neonatal units. All neonatal units had very low nurse-to-patient ratios (Table 1), given the high patient workload (number of sick babies). The low number of nurses contributed to limitations in providing optimal care, evident through prioritisation of care (critically ill babies over other babies in the units) and challenges with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) initiation and monitoring. The handing over of uncompleted tasks to other shifts created a vicious cycle of workload pressure. This, combined with intermittent shortages of consumables, a heavy workload, and pending leave days, contributed to feelings of burnout and demotivation among nurses. Before the intervention, support provided by existing ward assistants with non-technical tasks was not standard in all the hospitals. In one of the hospitals (H3), the ward assistant was restricted to cleaning the floors, walls and surfaces. Nurses, therefore, had additional non-technical tasks, such as following up on supplies from stores, and cleaning feeding equipment (cups and syringes), compared to other hospitals where ward assistants handled such tasks.

In the subsequent section, we report on the post-intervention effects of supplementing nurses in each of the neonatal units.

Theory of change desired change 1: better staff-to-patient ratios

The addition of 3 extra nurses as an intervention resulted in only a slight improvement in nursing time available per baby per shift (Imam et al., 2025b) (forthcoming). Only some shifts had more nurses after the intervention compared with baseline, resulting in only minor changes in the ratios of sick babies to nursing staff on a shift (Table 1).

Several factors mitigated the impact of adding three nurses on shift-level staffing. These included that neonatal units lost nursing staff to retirement, existing nurses took their pending annual leave days, including terminal (retirement) leave, and in some sites, internal rotation or redeployment of neonatal unit nurses to other wards occurred (these issues of fidelity are discussed in greater detail in Imam et al., 2025a) (Fig. 2).

In general, our qualitative findings underscored that even after the nursing intervention, nurses continued to face extremely high workloads and consequently experienced high workplace stress.

“…like today, we have 16 babies in the 8 incubators. So, unless we have like 2 or 3 nurses monitoring the incubators, I don't think we can manage the workload.”

(H1)

“…shortage[nurses] has been there, and we can't say it's over. At least we are trying, we are somewhere but we can't say shortage is over because even if you are on the ground [on a shift] there are times you are overwhelmed…”

(H4)

Nurses experiences with the nurses intervention

We anticipated that increasing the number of skilled nurses would improve relations among nurses and enhance nurses experiences working in the neonatal units. This would in turn, improve their relations and we proposed, enhance motivation and teamwork, making it more likely that nurses would strive to deliver all the care babies needed.

Theory of change desired change 2: improved relations among nurses

After the intervention, nurses reported improvements in teamwork, task sharing, and task completion. These changes were attributed to the training and skills of the new nurses, their confidence in these skills, new opportunities for cooperative work, peer-to-peer learning, mentorship, and consequently, a change in attitude towards using new technology.

New nurses training and effective onboarding

As part of the HIGH-Q staffing intervention, the newly recruited nurses underwent a 1-week training on newborn care. Similar training had been provided by NEST360 to existing hospital nurses at the onset of their programme (see supplementary material 4), but only some remained working in the units. After deployment, the nurse managers in all four neonatal units organised the duty rota to allocate the new nurses to routine hospital shifts. The nurse managers reported not seeing the need for conducting local on-the-job training for the new nurses, as they felt they were competent to take on roles. This was in contrast to past burdensome experiences where resident nurses had to conduct on-the-job training for newly rotating staff to acquaint them with the skills and procedures required to deliver care in the neonatal unit.

“… it's a big burden [on the job training]…, you have to work with that staff like two weeks, so that he can know all about NBU [neonatal unit], in terms of calculation of the medicine, in terms of the care of the baby, in terms of knowing how to monitor a baby on CPAP [continuous positive airway pressure], the equipment, monitoring a baby on phototherapy, it's a journey, it's a burden…”

(H1)

“They [new nurses] came when they had the knowledge so work was going on well. It is not the way you would get three new nurses [previous experience], but they don't know anything about NBU [neonatal unit] and now you have the task of teaching them and also you are working, you see that one is very tiresome.”

(H2)

Confidence in the new nurses skill set

The pre-deployment training for the new nurses enhanced resident nurses confidence in their abilities to manage tasks within the newborn unit. In fact, combining the new and resident nurses on shifts bolstered the skillset of nurses working in the neonatal units. This facilitated workflow efficiency within the ward.

“We have seen they are so competent, they are okay. Even if we leave the two of them, we are just very fine.”

(H1)

“It [training] was of great help because they came as a package, they were trained, it was easy to work with them.”

(H3)

Co-operative working

While overall staff shortages persisted, we observed that when there were more staff on a shift, nurses had opportunities to work cooperatively. Having more hands per shift facilitated the division and sharing of work among nurses in a shift.

“Sometimes, even in the calculation of these doses, we help each other. Personally, I appreciate. The workload, like you used to go on a shift alone [before the intervention], now she [new nurse] has come, you see the workload now is shared.” (H1)

“…now [after the intervention], at least we are two on a shift, we are able to divide …we used to have a lot of work like washing cups, EBM [expressed breast milk] cups, you have to top tail [clean babies] … You still have to teach mothers how to do expressing of the EBM [exclusive breast milk], so it was so overwhelming for us…”

(H3)

Cooperation and sharing of work among nurses enhanced teamwork and task completion. This reduced the likelihood of incomplete tasks previously handed over to subsequent shifts.

“…maybe in a night we are working as two nurses, maybe we don't have students [nurses]. So that time [before the intervention] we were after priorities…so in the morning you find there are so many gaps like we've not even balanced [fluids]… But since they [new nurses] came at least in the morning… we are almost 100%, maybe 98%.”

(H2)

“…going home having seen that the baby who came very sick, because of the teamwork, at least tomorrow I'll come and see an improved baby. That gives me joy…”

(H3)

“When you are two on the shift…unless you have the students [nurses] or you don't have the student, you have been able to give adequate services. Not like before, you find sometimes there are procedures you have to delegate to the other shift because you can't do everything.”

(H4)

Peer-to-peer learning among nurses

Our findings confirmed our assumption that there would be peer-to-peer learning among nurses. During the nurses intervention period, some hospitals received new medical technologies, such as syringes and fluid pumps that the existing nurses were not familiar with. The new nurses, through peer-to-peer learning, facilitated skills transfer and knowledge sharing with the existing nurses, enabling them to utilise these technologies. For instance, hospitals like H3 had the technologies before the workforce intervention. However, they were not utilising the technologies because they felt inadequately trained to use them.

“The first day the fluid pump came, we were not taught, we had the fluid pump before NEST [organization that supplied the equipment] gave us. We had them from central government, they gave us four fluid pumps and four syringe pumps. So, when they gave us, we were not taught. The person who came just passed through it, she was in a hurry, she wanted to teach everywhere she went. Then we were left like, ‘Uh, I didn't understand anything.’”

(H3)

Improved confidence and change in attitudes towards technologies

All the hospitals had introductory NEST360 training (detailed in the supplementary materials) (NEST 360, 2023) on how to use essential technologies with ongoing internal arrangements to train resident neonatal unit nurses on technologies utilised, such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Despite these existing training arrangements, the findings reveal that not all resident nurses were trained in all the hospitals, largely attributed to rapid neonatal unit staff turnover. Furthermore, some resident nurses, even after attending training, reported low confidence in initiating technologies such as continuous positive airway pressure.

“…some of us who have not gone for training, some of us find it hard to start a CPAP because you're not sure how to go about it. It depends with personal individual confidence, to start CPAP. Personally, when I came… let me call it leave… when I came back [from leave], I found CPAP to be a lot of work but after I learnt and then I started initiating, I think, it's a procedure like any other. But there are some people up to date who are not so very comfortable with initiating CPAP.”

(H4)

Other nurses perceived continuous positive airway pressure to contribute to poor outcomes experienced through deterioration of babies' health and mortalities in some instances. Initiating and monitoring babies on such technologies was also viewed as adding to workloads. Challenges with equipment breakdown added to these negative perceptions and contributed to delays in using or initiating continuous positive airway pressure even when babies met the recommended criteria for initiation. However, with the new nurses, there was enhanced support in learning how to handle such technologies, and this promoted a change in attitude.

“… there is a big difference because with CPAP … some staff had an attitude… “Oh, these CPAPs are not working, many babies are desaturating,” but when we had the three extra nurses who were trained, they tried to mentor many other nurses in the ward to appreciate CPAP.”

(H1)

“…they [new nurses] came with a very positive attitude… even though we had been trained, these were things that were just brought, we did not start with them. So, these are new things that we were seeing, okay, so for them they came very, very competent with it.”

(H3)

Theory of change desired change 3: improved experience working in the neonatal units

As discussed above, at baseline, staffing and workload-related challenges contributed to limitations in care provision. Nurses also reported an inability to rest, which contributed to burnout and demotivation to work. The subsequent section captures nurses improved experience working in the neonatal units after the intervention.

Reduction in pending leave days

Before the intervention, resident nurses in all the hospitals had pending leave days owed to them. The shortage of nurses, heavy workload and lack of leave all contributed to burnout. However, after the new nurses were deployed, resident nurses began taking up their pending leave days in all the hospitals, an unintended consequence of the intervention. While this benefited those nurses because they were able to go on leave, it reduced the ability of the intervention to increase the number of nurses per shift (this observation mirrors what is reported in detail elsewhere) (Imam et al., 2025a). The ability to take leave allowed them to rest, promoted work-life balance, reduced burnout and enhanced their well-being.

“… personally let me tell you from experience… I didn't go for leave for almost two and a half years…when they came, we now [after the nurses' intervention] take a break to at least rejuvenate…”

(H1)

“…with the introduction of the new nurses, the staff [nurses] who were working in NBU [neonatal units] were able to get their leaves [days] and be able to rejuvenate, refresh themselves and come back to work…”

(H2)

Theory of change: intermediate outcome 1: motivation to work

While the nursing intervention did not solve the staffing shortages as discussed above, improvements in cooperative or shared work and peer support among nurses, particularly when the number of nurses per shift increased, were reported to enhance task completion, teamwork, and nurses overall motivation.

“It has motivated the other nurses because, for example, in the afternoon, we used to report one nurse in the afternoon shift. Now they report two… weekend they [nurses'] used to report one person in the morning, one person in the afternoon, nowadays they are reporting two people…it has really motivated them and with that it has improved the service delivery.”

(H2)

“…generally, the team in the newborn unit has been motivated all through, everyone wants to take care of these babies, wants just to take care of the babies, just have to take care of them.”

(H4)

Further, nurses in all four hospitals were concerned about the level of training of these existing non-professional staff. Nurses experiences with the new ward assistants

In our ward assistants theory of change, we hypothesised that after the introduction of the new ward assistants, nurses would effectively delegate non-nursing tasks to them (See job description attached as supplementary material 1). This would then reduce nurses workload, thereby improving their mood and motivation to work.

Theory of change desired change 1: delegation of non-nursing tasks to ward assistants

As part of the intervention, each neonatal unit was supplemented with three ward assistants. Our findings show that nurses in all the hospitals delegated non-nursing tasks to ward assistants. Some of the tasks delegated included portering, collecting supplies, and roles related to hygiene and sanitation, among others.

“There has been a change because before we used to take the baby but now because they [new ward assistants] are here and they were well oriented to those places for X-ray, the ultrasound. Most of them are oriented to them and so it has become easier because we just tell them to escort a baby to the department and the procedure is done and they come back with the baby and so there has been a change, and they have saved our time”

(H1)

“Since they [new ward assistants] came, …they're the ones who are doing the stores [collecting supplies from hospital stores and delivering to the neonatal units] now, before it used to be the nurses”.

(H4)

Theory of change desired change 2: reduced workload from performing non-nursing tasks

In our Theory of Change, we had postulated that nurses delegating non-nursing tasks to ward assistants would reduce their workload. Our findings support this idea. Our study could not measure how nurses utilised the time they gained, but nurses reported that they were transferring extra time to direct patient care, and they perceived the care they were providing to be of better quality.

“My view is that it has made work easier, it has made the nurses able to take care of the newborns more closely…”

(H1)

“They [ward assistants] have changed things… before, when the ward assistants were not here, if I needed something, I had to go for it, if it is at the pharmacy, or going to the store, or going wherever, I would go. You see I will leave this ward and go, when I leave and go, time is also moving, so, I would not do it, I will just leave and go search for what I can find until I get”.

(H2)

“We felt the difference when the nurse aides [new ward assistants] came. That is when we felt like at least we are delivering some quality care. We are able to focus on the kids [babies]”.

(H3)

“I can say us as nurses our workload has reduced and we are now able to concentrate on more important things”.

(H4)

Theory of change desired change 2: improved equipment cleaning and sanitation in the units

Lastly, we postulated that the new ward assistants would take up cleaning roles. Taking up these roles would then lead to improvements in equipment cleaning and sanitation in the wards. As part of the intervention, new ward assistants were stationed exclusively in the neonatal units at all hospitals. They were available to support the needs of both mothers and nurses throughout their shifts. They had also undergone training in hygiene and sanitation roles. With the above arrangements, nurses noted improvements in hygiene and sanitation, particularly the cleaning of feeding items and equipment.

“… now we have them [ward assistants] here throughout, they take their time I have realized even in their cleaning they are very keen in cleaning of those cups, cleaning of those equipment's disinfecting them they are very careful with it unlike earlier.”

(H4)

Notably, hospital H3 had never had a staff member to support the nurses with non-technical tasks. The role of casual staff was to clean the floors, walls, and surfaces, as well as to clean closed footwear provided in the unit for use by healthcare providers. As such, nurses undertook tasks such as cleaning feeding utensils and equipment, preparing formula milk for babies, setting up water for cleaning babies, processing files at the records department for discharged babies, and following up on supplies from the stores, in addition to their direct patient care. The introduction of the ward assistants relieved nurses from performing the tasks mentioned above.

“The ward assistants just give us a sigh of relief because you are concentrating on the nursing task and not concentrating on the non-nursing task.”

(H3)

Theory of change intermediate outcome 1: increased support/assistance for non-technical tasks

In hospitals H1, H2, and H3, the new ward assistants were well received. In H4, nurses initially expressed some fears about ward assistants ability to undertake roles in the newborn unit. However, these fears were quickly allayed. In H3, the nurses reported changes in how the ward assistant performed their roles after a few months and this reduced their value.

“We felt like… you know, we were being assisted, yeah. We took it positively”.

(H1)

“My view is it was a good idea to think about adding the ward assistants”.

(H2)

“Well, to be honest, in the first few months, they were helpful”.

(H3)

“Initially, we had some fear, but you find they have proven themselves to be very reliable”.

(H4)

Unintended consequences of the ward assistants' intervention

Despite the above positive experiences of the ward assistants intervention, we noted an unintended consequence in one of the hospitals (H3). A few months into the ward assistants intervention, some of the ward assistants failed to complete tasks, absconded from duty without notice, and delegated duties to student nurses. Nurses perceived such behaviours as acts of insubordination. This fuelled a frosty relationship between the nurses and ward assistants. As a way of coping, the nurse manager reorganised the ward assistants rotas and placed them on night duty. This meant that the nurses went back to performing non-nursing tasks during the day.

“You've been here [researcher], you've seen some of them don't come on night duty. Some of them leave utensils not washed. You just don't understand why you speak to them, nobody wants to”.

(H3)

“Some of them wanted to make use of the students [nurses] because sometimes we have so many students, so they think students can take some of these roles like feeding the baby”.

(H3)

“…you can ask someone to do something after you finish your work, like getting medication from the pharmacy, and they tell you they are busy. You know they can't be busy the entire twelve-hour shift, but if they tell you they are busy, you know you won't wait for them to finish being busy after twelve hours before they go, you just go…”.

(H3)

Discussion

In this paper, we describe nurses experiences and views following the supplementation of neonatal units staffing with three nurses and three ward assistants for each of four neonatal units. We used the HIGH-Q project Theory (nurses and ward assistants) and its pre-specified desired changes, assumptions and expected outcomes to examine these experiences while also identifying new insights and unintended consequences (Maina et al., 2025). Before and during the intervention, the high number of babies (patients) and few nurses emphasised how overstretched the nursing workforce is, reflecting the general challenge with human resources for health (HRH). This challenge is not unique to Kenya but is found in other settings such as Malawi, Tanzania, and Nigeria (Adegoke et al., 2015; Bream et al., 2005; Jirapaet et al., 2006; Nyamtema et al., 2008).

Throughout the intervention period patient-to-nurse ratios exceeded 15:1 as previously reported in neonatal settings in Kenya (Murphy et al., 2018). These are far from the nurse-to-baby recommended ratios of 4:1 for babies requiring only basic care, and 1:1 for critically ill babies in other neonatal settings (American Academy of Pediatrics, 2017) and likely to have an impact on the provision and outcomes of care (Mbuthia et al., 2023; McKnight et al., 2020). After supplementing the neonatal units with three additional nurses, one unintended consequence was the withdrawal of neonatal unit nurses to cover shortages in other wards within the hospitals, while others took up opportunities for missed annual leave. These dynamics contributed to only a modest impact on nurse staffing in intervention neonatal units.

Registered nurses primarily provide newborn care in Kenya (Murphy et al., 2018) with few specialised nurses in neonatal care, findings that are similar in Malawi (Kozuki et al., 2017). This means that most neonatal unit nurses gain skills through on-the-job training. In our theory of change for nurses, we assumed that peer-to-peer learning would occur following supplementation with new nurses who had received training in neonatal care. Our findings show that new nurses supported resident nurses in learning to use new technologies and changing their attitudes towards technologies, such as continuous positive airway pressure. We note that knowledge sharing, and skills transfer can foster a sense of professional growth, which can translate to improved motivation. These findings highlight the critical impact of enhancing staff numbers and skillset to improve newborn care (Han et al., 2021; Murphy et al., 2019). Skills gaps might be addressed through periodic training, but these will not solve the challenges created by very high patient-to-staff ratios.

Nurses' well-being plays a pivotal role in delivering safe and effective care to neonates (Staples et al., 2024). Inadequate staffing and high workloads may result in workplace stress and burnout which may contribute to missed nursing care (Imam et al., 2023; Mbuthia et al., 2023; McKnight et al., 2020). Missed nursing care itself has been shown to affect job satisfaction and has been associated with intentions to leave work (Alsalem et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2020; Tubbs-Cooley et al., 2019). Our findings suggest an improvement in motivation and nurses well-being after additional nurses were deployed. Although nurses taking leave following the addition of nurses was an unintended consequence of the intervention, it did seem to promote staff well-being and motivation. This is likely to have enhanced their commitment and ability to provide care. These often intangible effects of improving nurse well-being are important and can potentially contribute to the provision of quality services in newborn units.

Kenya has guidelines on task sharing between professionals and also with community health workers (currently referred to as community health promoters) however, these do not include roles for non-professional cadres such as ward assistants (Kinuthia et al., 2022; MOH Kenya, 2017). Previous literature makes proposals for well-designed shifting of tasks that do not require technical skills (Nzinga et al., 2019; Oluoch et al., 2018). Similar to other findings, task shifting for non-technical tasks to ward assistants in the neonatal units we studied was welcomed by nurses, similar to other findings reported (Mijovic et al., 2016; Oluoch et al., 2018). Following the delegation of non-technical tasks to ward assistants, our findings indicate a reduction in workload for nurses from non-technical tasks. While our study did not evaluate whether the freed-up time was used for direct patient care, we note that this may translate to a shift in focus towards more clinical tasks, thereby promoting the provision of key interventions and potentially better support to caregivers.

Our study highlights that ward assistants took on roles previously performed by nurses. For instance, cleaning of feeding items, and equipment among others. Our findings also point to the need to dedicate specific non-professional personnel to neonatal units. We note that when ward assistants roles were taken up temporarily by others not familiar with the neonatal units (for example if the ward assistants were off duty) nurses raised concerns around their knowledge and skills, training, supervision and the quality and safety of supportive work they could offer as has been reported in other studies (Barasa et al., 2017; Oluoch et al., 2018). These findings underscore the need for close supervision and well-structured training of ward assistants tailored to the specific needs of the neonatal units. Further, in one of the hospitals, nurses encountered challenges working with the intervention ward assistants. We speculate from our unpublished observations that this indiscipline and insubordination may have resulted from a lack of autonomy on the part of hospital management during the recruitment process. Similar observations have also been reported by (Barasa et al., 2017). Our findings, however, highlight the importance of clear role definition, structured recruitment processes, and well-defined lines of authority and supervision for this cadre of staff.

When ward assistants dedicated to neonatal unit work were effectively deployed, our findings reveal improvements in hygiene and sanitation. This may help in reducing the risk of infections in vulnerable newborns. Additionally, having ward assistants dedicated to the neonatal unit may reduce the need for on-the-job training and close supervision, which can, in turn, increase nurses workload. The uptake of non-technical tasks by ward assistants suggests that they may be a valuable option to optimise the performance of available human resources; however, they are not a substitute for trained nurses. They may enhance the ability of improved supplies and technologies to deliver benefits to patients and caregivers.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a nuanced understanding of nurses experiences with a staffing intervention in four neonatal units in resource-constrained settings in Kenya. Data collection at different points in time (baseline, after nurses and ward assistants' intervention) provides a comprehensive understanding of nurses experiences over time. Furthermore, the use of a combination of data collection methods (non-participant observation and in-depth interviews) together with reflexive sessions created an opportunity to compare data at baseline and after the interventions, thereby enriching the analysis. Additionally, we based our analysis on pre-specified theories of change, although remaining open to emerging themes, and as a result, were able to explore nurses experiences with the intervention in depth.

The study was not without limitations. We acknowledge the subjective nature of qualitative research. Nurses previous experience or personal biases may have influenced their experiences and views. However, we mitigated this by conducting ethnographic observations to help understand the contexts better before and after the wards were supplemented with additional nurses and ward assistants. We also acknowledge that our backgrounds (NO and CW as social scientists, GN as a medical doctor, and JM as a public health professional) may have influenced our interpretation of the data. Therefore, we engaged in reflexive discussions with senior researchers in the project to maximise our combined learning.

We are aware of other context-specific challenges beyond staffing, such as sporadic shortages of supplies across hospitals and changes in hospital management following the general election in 2022 in Kenya. These factors may have contributed to the nurses experience. Lastly, we acknowledge that the study, although longitudinal in nature, captured the experiences of nurses with the intervention over a period of only 15 months and was conducted in four neonatal units in one country. Findings may not be generalisable to other neonatal units in Kenya or elsewhere.

Conclusion

Adequate staffing remains essential for delivering high-quality services in neonatal units. The benefit of adequate staffing is not only crucial to neonates but also central to nurses motivation and well-being. While nurses play a critical role in care delivery in neonatal units, ward assistants can also play essential roles in supporting both nurses and caregivers. However, there is a need for clear supervision, training and management structures for their inputs into care to be fully realised. Although there are attempts to address context-specific challenges, such as stockouts of supplies and infrastructural issues, governments need to prioritise staffing in neonatal units if new technologies are to produce better health outcomes.
