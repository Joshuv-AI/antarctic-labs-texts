An analysis of pruning practices in soilless tomato cultivation
Authors: Aylin Çolak, Aslıhan Çilingir Tütüncü, Andaç Kutay Saka, Esin Hazneci, Harun Özer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this study is to enhance the efficiency of pruning techniques in soil-less tomato cultivation, a process that constitutes a substantial proportion of labour costs. The study evaluated several pruning strategies, including leaf pruning after harvest (T1), leaf pruning after fruit color break (T2), and single leaf pruning between clusters (T3). The study also integrated fruit thinning and sucker pruning into treatments T4 through T6. Moreover, T0 pruning was performed as part of the routine maintenance procedure.ResultsThe highest fruit number (number/cluster and total), yield, and relative profit values were obtained in the control (T0) application. The application of T1 was found to have a significant impact on various parameters, including leaf chlorophyll index, average fruit weight, firmness, SSC, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) values. The T5 treatment was found to be the most effective in increasing plant height, cluster number, stomatal conductance, photosynthesis rate, colouration rate, total flavonoid, and lycopene values. The initial colour change in fruits and the total phenolic content exhibited elevated levels in the T6 treatment.ConclusionExcessive pruning has a detrimental effect on plant physiology and yield. It has been determined that the execution of these practices must be conducted with the utmost caution, taking into account the prevailing environmental conditions. In terms of yield maximization and economic profitability, the T0 treatment was found to be the most effective pruning strategy. In terms of quality factors such as fruit weight and firmness, the T1 treatment also yielded the most successful results.

Introduction

The tomato fruit is notable for its phenolic compounds, vitamins, and minerals, particularly lycopene, and it has been demonstrated that the structure of the fruit provides significant health benefits by strengthening the immune system [1–3]. A substantial proportion of tomatoes cultivated globally are produced using the greenhouse method [4]. Protected vegetable cultivation is defined as an agricultural practice that facilitates the attainment of high yields per unit area. The development of soilless systems within controlled greenhouse environments has enabled the attainment of maximum yield levels. Soilless is a rapidly expanding practice on a global scale [5–7]. The ongoing climate change and the increasing population density in major metropolitan areas have led to a heightened demand for food, thereby prompting a shift towards soil-free farming methods. The practice of soil-free farming has been demonstrated to facilitate the attainment of high-yield production, with this capacity being exhibited throughout the year and exhibiting independence from meteorological and soil conditions [8, 9].

Pruning practices, which represent a pivotal component of vegetable cultivation, have been demonstrated to enhance yields through the optimization of quality [9–15]. Pruning and training systems represent important cultural practices that directly affect plant productivity, fruit quality, and longevity. The primary function of pruning is to preserve the plant’s youthful form, enabling the early production of high-quality products and ensuring high yields. Additionally, pruning facilitates the production of early, high-quality, and high-yield products by allowing the plant to utilize light optimally [10–15]. At the same time, it is stated that pruning contributes to disease and pest management by increasing air circulation [16, 17]. However, many infections can originate from wounds caused during leaf pruning. It is clear that pruning close to the trunk develops resistance to diseases [18].

Tomatoes are actually perennial plants, and under suitable conditions they can remain green throughout the year [19]. Plant rejuvenation is achieved by extending the greening period the extension of the period during which they are green. In the context of tomato cultivation, the strategic extension of the period during which plants remain green through the implementation of shading has been demonstrated to enhance leaf chlorophyll content, stomatal conductance, and photosynthesis rate, thereby leading to increased yields [20, 21]. Pruning diseased and aged leaves that have lost their photosynthetic capacity can also extend the period of vegetative growth in plants [11]. It is imperative to acknowledge the pivotal role of leaves in the process of photosynthesis, as they serve as the primary organs responsible for capturing light [22]. However, if leaf, side shoots, sprouting shoot and fruit thinning is not carried out, the vegetative and generative balance will be disrupted [10, 11, 15].

In contemporary tomato cultivation, specific pruning techniques are employed to address diseases, attain high yields, and enhance fruit coloration. These practices encompass a range of labor-intensive procedures, including suckering, leaf pruning, and fruit thinning. It is imperative to prune leaves in order to sustain a harmonious equilibrium between the vegetative and reproductive growth of the plants. Commercial practices entail the implementation of rigorous pruning techniques, which may involve the strategic removal of a specific number of leaves beneath the harvest clusters or the complete removal of all leaves. However, in order to reduce labour requirements and improve product performance, it is necessary to optimize the timing and amount of leaf pruning [10–13, 15]. Moreover, it has been documented that the strategic adjustment of leaf pruning in accordance with light intensity and the duration of light exposure has been proven to be an efficacious method for reducing labour costs without compromising yield and fruit quality [15]. Recent studies have examined the practice of pruning in tomato cultivation (15, 30–31, 33, 35, 37, 39, 48). However, research on soilless tomato cultivation, where production is particularly intensive, has been limited.

In the context of soilless tomato cultivation, where the practice of intensive fertilization is prevalent, the establishment of an effective pruning method for fast-growing tomato plants is imperative. Given that the practice of soil-less tomato cultivation is undertaken across extensive areas, errors during the pruning process can result in not only yield losses but also an increase in pesticide usage, consequently impacting profitability and human health. Moreover, pruning has been demonstrated to result in a substantial escalation in labor costs. In light of the aforementioned points, the present study was conducted with the objective of investigating the effects of diverse leaf, side shoot, sprouting shoot and fruit thinning techniques on yield, quality, and production costs in soilless tomato cultivation. An attempt was also made to determine how much pruning practices affected profitability.

Materials and methods

Experimental location

This research was conducted between 15 April and 31 August 2025 at the Department of Horticulture, Faculty of Agriculture, Ondokuz Mayıs University. The cultivation phase of the study was conducted within a greenhouse equipped with a state-of-the-art, fully automated fertilization and climate control system. The greenhouse has an area of 120 m² and contains six rows (6 × 18 m) of plant-growing gutters. The exterior of the greenhouse is sheathed in polycarbonate. The fruit analyses for the study were conducted in the physiology laboratory of the Department of Horticulture, Faculty of Agriculture, Ordu University.

Experimental setup

In this study, the cluster tomato variety (Solanum lycopersicum L.) suitable for soilless (Climbo F1, Syngenta, Turkey) was used as plant material. The commercial tomato seeds used in this study were purchased from a commercial company in İzmir. In the greenhouse where soilless tomato cultivation was performed, six pruning applications (T1-T6) were conducted, in addition to a control (T0) application involving the pruning of diseased and yellowed leaves alone. The study was designed as seven pruning treatments (T0–T6) with three replicates. The measurement of six plants was conducted in each replicate. Consequently, measurements were obtained on 18 plants in each treatment, yielding a total of 126 plants measured across the seven treatments. Pruning was carried out twice a week (Table 1).

The seeds of the Climbo F1 variety were sown in EPS trays with 216 cells (31 × 31 × 65 mm), a cell/root volume of 50 ml, and dimensions of 695 × 470 × 75 mm for the purpose of growing tomato seedlings. The trays were filled with peat (Klasman, KTS I, DRT, Turkey). The seeds were manually sown on 17 March 2025, after which the trays were covered with a thin layer of vermiculite. The sown trays were then transferred to seedling production benches and irrigated using a miniature sprinkler connected to an automatic irrigation system. Temperatures in the greenhouse were set at 26–27 °C during the germination-emergence period. Subsequent to emergence, temperatures were set to vary during the day (23–24 °C) and night (19–21 °C) until the seedlings reached the four-leaf stage.

The 7-litre plastic pots were prepared by filling them with cocopeat. The pots were then placed in channels (gutter) 40 cm above the ground, 18 m long, 25 cm wide, and with a 1.5% slope in the greenhouse. The seedlings of the Climbo F1 tomato variety were planted on 15 April 2025, with one seedling per growing pot, arranged with 30 cm between plants in rows and 80 cm between rows.

Subsequently, the macro and micro nutrients to be used in the fertilization process were carefully prepared as stock solutions in two tanks (A, B, and C), each with a capacity of 50 L). The nutrient solutions used in the experiment are given in Table 2 [23]. Fertilization was monitored using a fully automated soilless system.

Throughout the research period, the climatic conditions in the greenhouse environment were measured continuously. This was achieved by utilizing recording devices (Hobo, MX2301A and H21 Onset, Bourne, USA) during the cultivation period. In circumstances where the relative humidity values exceeded 70%, the ventilation system was utilized to regulate these values.

The process of fertilization was initiated at the moment of sunrise and concluded at sunset, during the period of the growing season. The fertilization and irrigation program was set to 2/15 min during midday hours when sunlight intensity increased and 2/30 min during other hours. The EC value of the irrigation water was set to 2.3 dS m− 1 and the pH value to 5.5. EC and pH were continuously measured in the water draining from the growing media from seedling planting through harvest, and when salinity was measured to be high (3.3 dS m− 1), the media were flushed with water only in 10-minute intervals. This reduced the salinity of the media.

Plant measurements and observations

Plant height (cm) was measured using a tape measure from the root collar to the growing tip. Stem diameter (mm) was measured from the root collar using digital calipers. The number of leaves was measured in terms of count, taking into account the leaves pruned at 20-day intervals.

Leaf chlorophyll Index (LCI), stomatal conductance (mmol m− 2 s− 1), and photosynthesis rate (µmol m− 2 s− 1) measurements were taken between 09:00 and 11:00 a.m. on leaves at different levels of the measurement plants. LCI was measured using a CCM-200 chlorophyll meter (Opti-Sciences, USA), stomatal conductance was measured using an SC-1 leaf porometer (Decagon Devices, Pullman, USA), and photosynthesis rate values were measured using a Mini PPM device (EARS, Wageningen, Netherlands). A total of six leaves were measured from each plant. The mean value was derived by calculating the arithmetic mean of these six leaves.

The number of clusters (number) was counted in ten-day periods. The start of fruit color break in the first fruit on the plant was recorded as the first day of color change. “Breakers” means there is a definite “break” in color from green to tannish yellow, pink or red on not more than %10 of the surface. The fruit color break day in the cluster following the color change in the first fruit of a cluster was determined. These values were measured in all clusters, and the coloration rate (cluster/day) was determined by dividing the obtained values by time.

The quantity of fruit harvested from the initial harvest to the final harvest (8. cluster) date was ascertained for each test plant and in total. Consequently, the fruit from all test plants up to the eighth cluster was meticulously weighed using a scale with a precision of 0.1 g. The measurements were calculated as the total number of fruits per cluster and per plant. The average fruit weight and the total fruit weights (yield) of all harvested fruits were calculated.

Fruit quality characteristics were assessed on 10 fruit samples from each replicate in each application. Fruit peel color (Chroma and Hue) values were determined by taking a measurement from both sides of the fruit’s equatorial section using a color measurement device (Minolta, model CR-400, Tokyo, Japan). Fruit flesh firmness was measured by removing the fruit peel from two different sides of the equatorial part of the fruit, then using a hand penetrometer (4301, Instron, USA) with a 7.9 mm tip. The force required to pierce the fruit was expressed in Newtons (N). For the soluble solids content (SSC) assay, the fruit juice obtained after grinding the fruit slices with an electric mixer through a cheesecloth. A sufficient amount of fruit juice sample was taken, readings were taken with a digital refractometer (PAL-1, McCormick Fruit Tech. Yakima, USA), and the values were expressed as a percentage. To determine the titratable acidity (TA) value, a 10 mL sample taken from the fruit juice sample obtained was diluted with 10 mL of pure water. It was then titrated with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) until a pH of 8.1 was reached, and the amount of NaOH consumed in the titration was used to express the citric acid content (g citric acid 100 mL− 1). Vitamin C was measured using the fruit juice sample prepared for TCA measurement. Measurements were performed using a Reflectoquant plus 10 device (Merck RQflex plus 10, Turkey). For vitamin C measurement, the fruit juice was first diluted 10 times with oxalic acid (5 g fruit juice sample, 50 mL oxalic acid), and then the ascorbic acid test kit (Catalog no: 116981, Merck, Germany) was immersed in the diluted solution for 2 s. The test kit was then left outside for 8 s to oxidize and subsequently placed into the test adapter of the Reflectoquant device 5 s before the end. Finally, the value read on the device was recorded and expressed in mg 100/g. A total of 10 fruit samples were collected from each test plant. The fruit samples were obtained through the harvesting of three clusters (from the third, fourth and fifth clusters) during a single harvest period. The arithmetic mean of these ten fruits was determined to be the average value.

The total phenolic compounds were determined by means of Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent, as described in the study by [24]. Initially, 400 µL of fresh plant extract was taken and 4.2 mL of distilled water was added. Subsequently, 100 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 2% sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) were added and left to incubate for 2 h. Following the incubation period, the solution, which exhibited a bluish hue, was measured at a wavelength of 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. The results were then calculated in terms of gallic acid and expressed as milligrams of GAE per kilogram of fresh weight (fresh weight). The determination of total flavonoids was conducted in accordance with the methodology outlined in the study by [25]. One milliliter of the appropriately diluted extract was made up to 5 mL with distilled water, and 0.3 mL of 5% NaNO2 was added. Subsequently, 10% AlCl₃ was added to the mixture and permitted to stand for a period of six minutes. Subsequently, 1 M NaOH was added, and the total volume was adjusted to 10 mL with distilled water. Subsequently, the absorbances were measured at a wavelength of 510 nm. The total flavonoid content was expressed as quercetin equivalent (QE), with the quantity of quercetin expressed in milligrams per kilogram of the fresh weight.

The FRAP assay buffer solution was prepared by mixing 0.1 mol/L acetate (pH 3.6), 10 mmol L-1 TPTZ, and 20 mmol/L ferric chloride solutions. Finally, 20 µL of fruit extract was mixed with 2.98 mL of the prepared buffer solution, and the resulting change in colour was measured at 593 nm using a spectrophotometer after 10 min. The results for FRAP antioxidant activity are given in mmol TE kg− 1 fresh fruit [26]. The investigation focused on the DPPH free radical scavenging activity. The methodology was adapted from [27] technique. The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) solution was utilised as the free radical. Stock solutions were transferred to test tubes to form solutions at different concentrations. A quantity of 0.5 ml of a 0.1 mM ethanol solution of the DPPH· free radical, for example, was prepared. The extract and the total volume of the standard antioxidant solution (50–500 µg mL-1) were made up to 3 ml. The mixture was then subjected to dynamic stirring and kept at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture’s absorbances were measured at a wavelength of 517 nanometres. The values of the absorbances obtained were calculated using the Trolox (10–100 µmol/L) standard calibration curve. The results for DPPH antioxidant activity are given in mmol Trolox equivalent (TE) kg-1 fresh fruit. The lycopene content (mg kg-1) was determined by adding 1 gram of fruit sample to 50 millilitres of a mixture of hexane: acetone: ethanol (in a ratio of 2:1:1) and sealing the sample container. The samples were subjected to a 30-minute shaking process utilising a shaker. Subsequently, 10 millilitres of distilled water was added to the sample and shaken again for five minutes. The 25 ml apolar portion (hexane) of the resulting solution was separated using a separating funnel. The lycopene-containing portion is then filtered through Whatman 42 filter paper and subsequently analysed against hexane at 502 nm. The formula created with the values read against the 1–5 mgL-1 lycopene standard is used in calculating the results obtained [1].

Relative profit

In the study, total production costs were first calculated to arrive at the relative profit (Eq. 1).

The variable production costs comprised cocopeat, shading powder, sticky traps, seedlings, fertiliser, pesticides, water charges, electricity, labour and interest on working capital. As the study was conducted within the confines of the university’s existing environment, depreciation was calculated for pots, hanging wire, and cluster protection clips. These items can be utilised multiple times within the production process and are thus categorised as fixed production costs. The study indicates that the primary discrepancy in production costs between diverse pruning methodologies is attributable to labour costs. The relative profit is calculated by dividing the Gross Production Value (GPV) by the total production costs (Eq. 2). GPV was obtained by multiplying the market price of tomatoes during the production period by the yield per plant (Eq. 3) Gross profit is calculated by subtracting variable costs from gross production value, and net profit is calculated by subtracting total costs from gross production value (Eq. 4; Eq. 5) [28–31].

Total Production Costs = Variable Costs + Fixed Costs (Eq. 1).

Relative Profit = Gross Production Value (GPV) / Total Production Costs (Eq. 2).

Gross Production Value (GPV) = Product Price x Yield (Eq. 3).

Gross Profit = GPV - Variable Costs (Eq. 4).

Net Profit = GPV - Total Production Costs (Eq. 5).

Statistical analyses

The study employed a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replicates to account for potential environmental gradients within the greenhouse. Six 18-meter-long gutters were divided into three spatial blocks, where seven pruning treatments (T0–T6) were randomly assigned to plots consisting of six plants each. Plants were maintained at a 30 × 80 cm spacing to minimize inter-plant interference. To prevent pseudoreplication, the experimental unit was defined as the plot mean, ensuring that statistical inferences were based on n = 3 per treatment. Statistical inferences were derived from the mean values of the plots (n = 3). Data were subjected to a two-way ANOVA, treating ‘treatment’ as a fixed factor and ‘block’ as a random factor, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05) using SPSS 21. Prior to analysis, ANOVA assumptions, including normality and homogeneity of variances, were confirmed. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the tomato traits examined in relation to pruning treatments under soilless conditions. During the process of PCA analysis, the Z-transformation was applied to the variables in order to accommodate unit differences. The PCA analysis was performed using the licensed JMP 13.2 statistical analysis software package.

Results

A range of pruning practices were found to have significant effects (p < 0.05) on plant growth, development, yield, quality, and relative profit values. At the conclusion of the study the maximum number of leaves was recorded in the control application, with a value of 38.9, followed by the T1 application, with a value of 31.9. The lowest number of leaves was measured in the T5 (13.3) and T2 (15.6) applications, respectively (Table 4).

The results obtained demonstrate that the highest LCI (71.4 CCI) was obtained in the T1 application, while plant height (300.2 cm), stem diameter (16.5), stomatal conductance (880.8 mmol m− 2 s− 1) and photosynthesis rate (56.0 µmol m− 2 s− 1) were obtained in the T5 application (Table 4).

When examining the effects of different pruning practices on production costs and economic outcomes in soilless tomato cultivation, significant differences were observed between the practices. The total production cost was calculated as 5.334 USD in the control practice (T0), while this value ranged from 7.180 to 9.948 USD in practices with increased pruning intensity. It was determined that the variation in production costs was primarily due to labor expenses. Indeed, in practices with increased pruning intensity, variable costs and total production costs increased due to the increased labor requirement. Discussion

Pruning practices have been demonstrated to induce vegetative growth and the continuous production of new leaves in plants. This process has been shown to reduce the number of old (withered) and yellowed leaves, which possess reduced photosynthetic capacity. Consequently, the plant is able to maintain a younger, greener appearance [11]. The pivotal function of leaves in the process of photosynthesis is intrinsically linked to the yield of the crop. This phenomenon can be attributed to the ability of leaves to capture light and facilitate the process of photosynthesis, which ultimately leads to the production of dry matter. Consequently, the strategic adjustment of leaf pruning methodologies in accordance with light availability has the potential to exert a substantial influence on both yield and quality [15, 22].

Soilless systems have been shown to offer a higher level of productivity by virtue of the continuous supply of plant nutrients [35]. In particular, the EC values of the nutrient solution are pivotal in this regard. Indeed, as [36] stated in their study, leaf pruning caused a decrease in plant height and total leaf area. In contrast to the findings reported by [36], the plants with the smallest number of leaves were the tallest plants (Table 4) [37]. similarly concluded in this study that the highest plant height was achieved with shoot pruning. However, it has been demonstrated that an EC value of 3.5 dSm-1 has been shown to increase the dry matter production of the applied nutrient solution by 15% [36]. In the present study, the range of EC levels was found to be between 1.8 and 2.3 dSm-1. This may have constrained the extent to which yield values could be maximized for severe pruning treatments (T4, T5, and T6). In contrast to the findings of our study, the researchers asserted that shoot pruning exerted a distinct and substantial influence on leaf chlorophyll ındex, in contrast to the effects of other pruning practices. The finding that the T1 application exhibited the highest leaf chlorophyll ındex (71.4 CCI) appears to contradict this. Nevertheless, its similarity to the T5 application supports the perspective of [11].

This phenomenon can be attributed to the observation that stomatal conductance and photosynthesis rate exhibited a marked increase in severe pruning treatments. In particular, stomatal conductance (880.8 mmol m−2s− 1) and photosynthesis rate (56 µmol m−2s− 1) reached their maximum levels in the T5 treatment. Research findings that indicate a substantial enhancement in total photosynthetic efficiency following the removal of senescent foliage align with the assertions put forth in this study. This is due to the negligible net photosynthetic output of aged leaves [18, 38].

Pruning and training are said to enhance productivity by supporting fruit quality. Despite the extant literature suggesting that pruning practices reduce the risk of disease by improving air circulation [10–13, 15, 17, 35], the present study focuses on yield and quality parameters. No direct measurement of disease resistance was conducted. Consequently, the impact of pruning on disease management should be regarded as a theoretical benefit in view of the observations concerning vegetative and generative equilibrium documented in this study. Furthermore, yield increases in tomato plants allowed to grow freely can be reduced by 31% due to quality losses [39].

[37] achieved the highest yield in an application where the number of pruned leaves was low, a finding that is consistent with the results of the present study. In the context of these applications, the removal of diseased and aged leaves from the plant was the only intervention undertaken. In contrast to the findings of our study [11], attained the maximum yield with single leaf pruning between clusters. It is evident that leaves, which are responsible for capturing light, play a fundamental role in dry matter production during photosynthesis [22]. Consequently, a strategic adjustment of leaf pruning methodologies in accordance with light availability is anticipated to exert a substantial influence on both yield and quality. The present study hypothesizes that leaf pruning based on weekly light integral (WLI) may be an important alternative to maximize the benefits of leaves, with such significant effects. While 35–42% less leaf pruning was performed based on WLI, the highest yield values were achieved in comparison to the control [15]. It is widely acknowledged that leaf pruning represents the optimal approach for the management of plants, supporting ventilation and controlling pest development. However, the impact of pruning on yield and quality has been found to be negligible under supplemental artificial LED lighting (16 h and 180 µmol s⁻¹m⁻²) in tomato cultivation [40]. Consequently, the decline in foliage has a detrimental effect on yield, given its role in determining dry matter production. However, the most effective strategy for successful tomato cultivation is the timely and precise pruning of the plant, based on the climatic factors of light and physiological conditions.

In the present study, in contrast to the findings of [37], no quality losses were observed; however, a substantial decrease in average fruit weight was noted. As indicated by [12] and [41], the decline in average fruit weight is ascribed to the absence of leaf pruning and fruit thinning. Intense fruit thinning has been demonstrated to result in the loss of potentially productive fruits. It is therefore vital to achieve equilibrium between the conditions under which the crop is cultivated and the fruit load. In cherry tomato cultivation, it has been documented that leaving 10 fruits per cluster has been shown to yield the highest total yield [13]. In the present study, the highest yield was obtained in the T0 application, which reached 7.87 fruits/cluster, while the average fruit weight (91.2 g) decreased dramatically. However, in the T1 application, which exhibited the highest average fruit weight of 126.9 g, the total yield was 4.76 kg/plant, ranking second. It is hypothesized that the total yield was constrained by fruit thinning, which involved the removal of five fruits from each cluster. Consequently, the determination of an ideal number of fruits is imperative, as previously identified by [13]. The number of fruits per cluster in tomatoes can be influenced by many factors, primarily genetic and environmental ones. A study in which fruit thinning was performed in tomato cultivation, leaving six fruits per cluster, supports this statement, as the highest average fruit weight and yield values were reported [42]. This effect was most pronounced in the T1 application, where five fruits were left on the cluster. As posited by [43], further research is required to ascertain whether fruit thinning is necessary for hydroponically cultivated tomatoes. In light of the findings of our study, it is hypothesised that the restriction of each cluster to 1–2 fruits, as opposed to the complete pruning of fruit, will enhance the probability of success. In contrast to the findings reported by [37], it was determined that shoot pruning resulted in a reduction in fruit weight when compared to alternative pruning methodologies. These results are consistent with the findings of the study, which indicated that leaf pruning reduces the total leaf area, thereby decreasing the amount of dry matter produced. However, it has been posited that the nutrient solution EC (3.5 dSm− 1) values in soilless may serve to mitigate the deleterious effects of leaf pruning on yield [36].

While the vitamin C content was high in the control treatment, the treatment where 10 fruits were left on the cluster followed this value [13]. In the context of fruit thinning pruning applications, the retention of six fruits per cluster has been shown to enhance the values of % dry matter, TSS, titratable acidity, and fruit flesh firmness [42]. The findings of this study are analogous to those of our T1 application. The distinguishing factor in our application is that five fruits were retained on the bunch. In a similar vein [11], obtained the highest fruit flesh firmness value exclusively from the application where the leaves under the harvested bunch were pruned, yielding results analogous to those of the present study. As was the case with the T1 application in our study, the highest fruit flesh hardness values were obtained. In the same study, a dramatic decrease in SSC values was observed with increasing pruning intensity. This phenomenon has been linked to a decline in dry matter production and a decrease in leaf number [44]. In this respect, it also shows similarities. The extant literature on the effects of pruning practices on bioactive compounds in tomato cultivation is limited. Consequently, data from a study employing fish waste protein hydrolysate in soilless cultivation of the same variety (Climbo F1) were examined [45]. It can be stated that similar values are measured, taking into account the use of the same diversity [45]. In another study [46], the following parameters were examined in industrial tomato varieties: fruit flesh firmness (30.02–36.99 N), soluble solids content (4.26–4.44%), vitamin C (9.33–13.37 mg 100 g− 1), lycopene content (93.82-101.58 mg kg-1), and total phenol content (44.15–49.94 mg GAE 100 kg-1).

It is imperative to exercise caution with regard to the intensity of leaf pruning. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that severe leaf pruning has been shown to promote robust and vigorous leafy growth. This disruption can compromise the equilibrium between vegetative and generative growth, consequently precipitating a decline in fruit yield [16]. The plant’s propensity to perpetually generate new foliage has the potential to induce complications in the processes of uptake and transportation of nutrients within the plant [16]. provided a comparable illustration of the calcium element. In their study, the researchers stated that severe pruning led to increased calcium allocation to leaves and shoots, decreased calcium transport to the fruit, and increased incidence of blossom end rot in pruned plants. In the study, it is asserted that instances of flower end rot were infrequent in the control treatment due to the elevated calcium uptake, a consequence of augmented leaf area and shading. The present study did not examine plant nutrient elements. However, plants have the capacity to develop antioxidant mechanisms by developing resistance to nutrient deficiencies and diseases. Furthermore, it is hypothesized that the elevated levels of bioactive compounds observed in severe pruning applications can be attributed to the stress induced by these practices.

It is well established that plants are subject to a multitude of stress factors (biotic and abiotic) during their life cycles, with such factors having a detrimental effect on their growth and development. The impact of these stress factors on plants can result in physiological and biochemical damage. In response, plants have evolved antioxidant and molecular defence mechanisms to mitigate these deleterious effects [47]. The study established a correlation between the increases in FRAP and DPPH antioxidant activities and the wound tissue formed as a result of pruning. This phenomenon may be interpreted as a defensive response initiated by the wound-responsive pathway. However, as wound signaling pathways were not directly measured in the study, this relationship remains a mechanistic hypothesis. However, the hypothesis that pruning close to the stem allows plants to develop defense mechanisms is supported by studies indicating that leaving the leaf stalk intact during leaf pruning leaves the plant vulnerable [18, 42]. The increases in total phenolic, flavonoid, and lycopene content observed in severe pruning treatments such as T5 and T6 are consistent with the accumulation of secondary metabolites reported in the literature as a result of the physiological stress induced by similar treatments in plants. This phenomenon may signify a biochemical response of the plant to severe pruning; nevertheless, the assessment of specific stress markers (e.g. hormone levels) is imperative to substantiate this hypothesis. With regard to the maximisation of yield and economic profitability, the T0 treatment has yielded the most successful results, while in terms of quality factors such as fruit weight and firmness, the T1 treatment has also yielded notable results, distinguishing itself among pruning strategies.

In consequence of the study, enterprises that undertake supplementary pruning in accordance with the T1 application will have the capacity to accrue 1.96 $ for every 1 $ they invest.The relative profitability of greenhouse tomato production in Mersin, Turkey (3.49) is consistent with the results obtained in this study [48]. In the study examining the effect of pruning on tomato yield, different pruning methods were tested on the plants, and additional pruning labor was carried out for this purpose. Consequently, in all applications where pruning was performed, total costs increased due to the rise in labor costs. The findings indicate that under the conditions studied, pruning practices increase production costs but do not proportionally increase economic returns, and have a decisive impact on business profitability (Table 5).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that pruning practices significantly affect the physiological and economic performance of soilless tomatoes. The highest fruit number, total yield, and relative profit were obtained in the control (T0) application, where only diseased and old leaves were removed. Among the pruning (T1-T6) treatments, T1 emerged as the most suitable method for improving fruit weight and firmness, although it resulted in a reduction in total yield compared to the control. Significant alterations in photosynthetic rate and antioxidant capacity were observed in response to heavy pruning of leaves and shoots (T2–T6). These findings suggest that the plant has developed physiological and biochemical adaptations to intensive pruning practices; however, the direct causal relationship between these processes and stress mechanisms must be confirmed in future studies. Based on these findings, excessive pruning should be avoided to maintain the balance between vegetative and generative growth.

Future research should investigate whether retaining 6–7 fruits per cluster, rather than the 5 fruits tested here, could better balance yield and fruit size. Additionally, while this study suggests a potential link between environmental factors and pruning efficiency, the development of dynamic pruning strategies based on light intensity warrants further experimental verification. The findings of this research are limited to the Climbo F1 variety and the specific greenhouse conditions (EC levels of 1.8–2.3 dS m− 1) under which the study was conducted. The economic results are also subject to local labor costs and market prices during the 2025 production season.
