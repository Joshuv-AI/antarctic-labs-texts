Validation of an AI-based automated Knee Inflammation MRI Scoring System for assessing bone marrow lesions
Authors: Rory Gilliland, Steel McDonald, Stephanie Wichuk, Soolmaz Abassi, Assefa Wahd, Deb Baranec, Constance Lebrun, Joel Paschke, Rod Fitzsimmons Frey, Abhilash Rakkunedeth Hareendranathan, Somayeh Raiesdana, Dan Durham, Dorian Nobbee, Maria Simona Stoenoiu, Ulrich Weber, Matthew Budak, Omar Azmat, Paul Benvenuto, Chris Rothe, Robert GW Lambert, Walter P Maksymowych, Jacob L Jaremko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesArtificial intelligence (AI) offers potential to automatically evaluate the burden of active arthritis on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) but must be reliable and practical to see routine use. We sought to validate the reliability and feasibility of iKIMRISS, an AI-automated iteration of the Knee Inflammation MRI Scoring System (KIMRISS) for bone marrow lesions (BMLs), using both quantitative and qualitative methods.MethodsEleven readers participated in a 3-part reading exercise evaluating 40 2-time-point knee MRI cases using manual KIMRISS and different levels of AI automation in iKIMRISS. BML scoring reliability between methods was assessed using agreement metrics. A subset of experts also participated in postexercise questionnaires and semistructured interviews to assess the usability, feasibility, and implementation potential of iKIMRISS.ResultsiKIMRISS’ BML scores demonstrated moderate-to-strong agreement with human reader scoring, especially human readers who were experienced KIMRISS users (mean intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.76 for both baseline and time interval change scores). Thematic analysis of survey responses and semistructured interviews revealed an overall positive sentiment towards iKIMRISS as a research tool but indicated a need for further technical and logistical improvements to increase its value in clinical settings.ConclusionsiKIMRISS greatly improves the speed and accessibility of KIMRISS BML scoring while maintaining acceptable reliability for research and clinical trial applications. However, further refinement of the tool is recommended to improve its applicability in clinical settings.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoarthritis (OA) is an insidious joint disease associated with significant pain, functional decline, and disability [[1], [2], [3]]. Its prevalence is reported as high as 7.6% globally and is expected to only increase with ageing populations, resulting in costs as high as 1 to 2.5% of gross national product in developed countries [[1], [2], [3]].

Despite the substantial burden exerted by OA on the world’s healthcare systems and economies, there are currently no disease-modifying therapeutics approved for its treatment, a shortcoming that can be partly attributed to a lack of objective, standardised endpoints suitable for quantifying its extent in clinical trials [[4], [5], [6]]. Several scoring systems have been validated for quantifying bone marrow lesions (BMLs; a key marker of active inflammation [[7], [8], [9]]) in knee OA, such as the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) OA Knee Scoring System (MOAKS [10]) and the Knee Inflammation MRI Scoring System (KIMRISS [11]). However, these methods require significant time, manual effort, and expertise to use, often with high inter-reader variability [12]. These limitations make manual scoring systems such as KIMRISS and MOAKS unsuitable for evaluating large numbers of cases in clinical and research settings.

The current limitations of BML scoring systems can be addressed with artificial intelligence (AI)-based automation. AI assistance holds promise to make methods like KIMRISS more accurate, reproducible, time-efficient, accessible, and ultimately more suitable for use by clinicians and researchers for objectively assessing knee OA [[12], [13], [14]].

The main objective of this pilot study was to evaluate the MRI knee BML assessments automatically generated by iKIMRISS, an AI-enhanced version of KIMRISS. iKIMRISS automatically segments BML in 3D on knee MRI images and uses AI-placed bone-contouring grid templates to derive KIMRISS scores. In keeping with the OMERACT (Outcome Measures in Rheumatology) Filter 2.2 [15], we aimed to evaluate iKIMRISS with respect to its truth (ie, agreement with manual methods), feasibility (ie, practicality for research and clinical use), and discrimination (ie, ability to differentiate groups of interest) as an instrument for objectively measuring the extent of knee OA, using both quantitative and qualitative methods.

METHODS

iKIMRISS models

Manual KIMRISS is a semiautomated scoring system for BML in the knee joint wherein readers place a grid template of predefined proportions that covers the 3D volumes of the femur, tibia, and patella by setting several key landmarks along the mediolateral width of the joint as anchor points. These grid templates delineate standardised subregions within the bone volume which readers can then select to indicate that they contain BML (Fig 1). The number of grid spaces selected throughout each bone’s grid template is then summed to provide the KIMRISS BML score [11,16]. This score expresses the spatial extent of BML throughout the knee and ranges from 0 to 500+ (depending on the size of the knee). iKIMRISS automates both the grid template placement step and the BML selection step by applying 2 AI models. The first automatically places the KIMRISS grid templates, and the second automatically highlights (or ‘segments’) regions of BML. The 2 outputs are combined to produce full KIMRISS scoring.

Bone-fitting grid placement model

Feedback received from readers about manual KIMRISS highlighted that the rigid grid template typically used sometimes did not match actual bony shapes well (see Fig 1 for an example), potentially decreasing the face validity of BML scoring. To address this concern, an automatic grid placement system was implemented in iKIMRISS that features a dynamic grid template that precisely fits the contours of each bone, thereby ensuring that BML can be fully scored despite unusual bone morphologies (Figure 2, Figure 3).

The automatic grid placement feature of iKIMRISS adapts a common segmentation model (Mask region-based convolutional neural network [17]) to mask the bones and cartilage in the knee joint. The femur grid is formed by projecting radial lines from the centre point (defined by least squares) to the edge of the bone mask at equal angles and is bounded superiorly by the upper margin of the cartilage. The patella grid is anchored at the posterosuperior corner of the patella’s bone mask and contours its patellofemoral surface to the inferior margin. The tibia grid is anchored at the anterosuperior corner of the tibia’s bone mask and contours its tibiofemoral surface to the posterior margin.

BML segmentation model

iKIMRISS also features a novel 3D BML segmentation model based on temporal, an in-context learning method which automatically identifies regions of predicted BML throughout the MRI scans (Figure 2, Figure 3). Technical details of this method have been published previously [18]. Segmented BML masks are then spatially compared against the automatically placed grid templates (using a 10% overlap rule) to provide the KIMRISS score.

Reading exercise

We evaluated iKIMRISS using a multireader pilot study based on prospective longitudinal data. A 3-part knee MRI reading exercise was delivered remotely to a set of experts via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), with KIMRISS scoring hosted on CARE Arthritis (CARE Arthritis Ltd., Edmonton, Canada, https://www.carearthritis.com) and Datamint (Datamint, Sonance Inc., Edmonton, Canada, https://www.datamint.io/). No clinical information aside from the deidentified MRI scans were presented to readers. Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the University of Alberta’s Human Research Ethics Board (Pro00147829).

MRI dataset

The MRI dataset consisted of fluid-sensitive sagittal knee MRI scans from 40 patients with OA, including 20 pairs of 2D turbo spin echo fat suppressed scans from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) dataset (https://nda.nih.gov/oai) and 20 pairs of proton density fat saturation scans from the Osteoarthritis of the Knee, Inflammation, and the effect of Adalimumab (OKINADA) trial [19]. For each patient, scans from 2 timepoints (baseline and 1-year follow-up for OAI; baseline and 16-week follow-up for OKINADA) were used for a total of 80 scans. These datasets represent a diverse burden of knee OA (Table 1).

Readers

The 11 readers who independently completed the reading exercise consisted of 8 musculoskeletal radiologists and 3 rheumatologists sampled by convenience from OMERACT. The average age of participants was 48.3 ± 12.9 years, 1 female and 10 male. Among the readers were 3 developers of the original KIMRISS (JLJ, WPM, and RGWL). Three other readers were experienced users of KIMRISS (defined as having used the system before), and the remaining 5 were novice users of KIMRISS (defined as having not used the system before). The average length of professional experience among the readers was 26 ± 13.0 years for experienced KIMRISS users (n = 6) and 5 ± 5.1 years for novice KIMRISS users (n = 5). More details about the readers are available in the Supplementary Methods.

Exercise protocol

The reading exercise consisted of 3 parts completed sequentially. All readers reviewed the same 80 MRI scans at each part, and were allowed to complete their review at their own pace, taking breaks as necessary. Readers completed the exercise over a period of approximately 3 months.

Part 1 asked readers to manually place (but not score BML on) KIMRISS grid templates as instructed in Maksymowych et al [16]. Part 2 prompted readers to score BML on grid templates that were placed automatically by an early version of iKIMRISS’ automatic grid placement feature, which used rigid (rather than bone-contouring) grid templates. Two readers, both developers of the original KIMRISS, also completed full manual BML scoring in part 1 of the exercise. Parts 1 and 2 were hosted on CARE Arthritis.

Part 3 presented readers with the results of the fully featured iKIMRISS, including the AI-placed bone-contouring grid template and the AI-segmented BML masks. Readers were prompted to rate the quality of both the bone-contouring grid template and the BML segmentation. Part 3 of the exercise was hosted on Datamint.

Upon completion of the reading exercise, readers were asked to provide feedback on iKIMRISS using a set of 54 subjective questions reflecting the constructs of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR; https://cfirguide.org/) and the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) framework (https://re-aim.org/), including usability, perceived value, and implementation feasibility. Questions included the System Usability Scale (SUS) [20,21], a modified Normalisation Measure Development Questionnaire (NoMAD) [22], a set of questions developed by our patient partners, and an open-ended written response (Supplementary Methods).

Quantitative analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using Python 3.7 [23]. To evaluate the reliability of iKIMRISS, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs; 2-way random effects model, absolute agreement, single measures) were calculated to estimate agreement of both baseline and 1-year-change KIMRISS scores. Comparisons were made between different iterations of iKIMRISS (ie, fully manual KIMRISS, automated grid placement only, and fully automated iKIMRISS) to capture reliability with respect to previously validated methods. Pairwise ICCs were also calculated among readers’ scores using automated grid placement to capture how human-AI agreement compares with human-human agreement. Finally, paired t-tests and Bland-Altman analysis were applied to assess the variability and bias that iKIMRISS introduces to KIMRISS scoring.

Semistructured interviews

Following the reading exercise, readers were invited by emails to participate in a semistructured follow-up interview to elucidate their perspectives on iKIMRISS. Seven readers elected to participate. By the fifth or sixth interview, few new ideas had emerged, suggesting that thematic saturation had largely been reached. Interviews lasted ∼45 minutes each and were conducted jointly by a researcher (RG) and a patient partner with lived experience with OA (DB). No relationship was established between the interviewers and participants prior to the interviews. Interviews were held virtually via a secure video meeting service, and all interviews were recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim. The interview guide (Supplementary Methods) was developed jointly by researchers and patient partners with the assistance of the CFIR interview guide tool (https://cfirguide.org/guide/app/#/) and the RE-AIM guide (https://re-aim.org/). It was pilot tested with an internal colleague.

Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis of semistructured interviews

An initial codebook was developed prior to the interviews based on the interview guide and the themes it was anticipated to elicit.

Interview transcripts were proofread for accuracy by an interviewer and uploaded to NVivo (version 15, Lumivero) [24] for further analysis. Two transcripts were selected at random and coded independently by 2 researchers (RG and SM) using a hybrid approach combining deductive coding (using the initial codebook) and inductive coding (to account for emerging ideas). The 2 coders met afterwards to review differences in coding, consider potential new codes, and refine the codebook. One researcher (RG) then used the finalised codebook (included in the Supplementary Methods) to code all transcripts using a deductive approach.

Using NVivo’s qualitative data analysis tools, coded data from the interview transcripts were used to identify recurring patterns in the participants’ perspectives towards various aspects of iKIMRISS. Six themes were constructed to capture the most prominent and relevant of these patterns as faithfully as possible. Feedback on these themes was sought from all interviewees.

Thematic analysis of open-ended survey responses

A similar qualitative data analysis approach was applied to analyse the open-ended written responses provided by the participants as part of the end-of-reading-exercise survey. Two researchers (RG, SM) independently used the codebook from the interview thematic analysis to code all written responses deductively and construct the coded data into themes. These themes were then unified with the themes identified from the semistructured interviews.

RESULTS

Quantitative analysis

MRI dataset

The MRI data used in the study are characterised in Table 1. Participants were 65 years old on average and had a moderate burden of OA (mean Kellgren-Lawrence Grade = 1.7 and mean baseline KIMRISS BML score = 31.9). Change in KIMRISS score over the follow-up periods was relatively small on average, at 16.1. Cases sourced from OAI data generally had greater baseline and absolute change in BML extent (35.3 and 18.5, respectively) than cases from OKINADA data (28.6 and 13.7, respectively).

KIMRISS BML score reliability

The results of the KIMRISS BML score reliability analysis are presented in Table 2. Overall, baseline scores produced by iKIMRISS demonstrated moderate-to-strong agreement with those produced by the 2 fully manual expert readers (KIMRISS BML score ICCs of 0.75 and 0.85) as well as the 11 readers using the automated grid placement feature (ICCs = 0.31-0.84). Interestingly, agreement with iKIMRISS was much stronger when excluding the more novice users of KIMRISS (baseline ICCs = 0.66-0.84). Agreement between manual KIMRISS and iKIMRISS scoring was also moderate to strong for time interval change KIMRISS scores (ICCs with manual readers of 0.66 and 0.75; ICCs with 11 readers = 0.51-0.90). Agreement was stronger when excluding novice readers (ICCs = 0.66-0.90). ICCs for iKIMRISS’ baseline scores in the femur were especially strong, with experienced user ICCs all exceeding 0.74; meanwhile, its time interval change patella score ICCs were considerably lower (experienced user ICCs = 0.17-0.59).

The reliability analysis findings are consistent with direct statistical comparisons (paired t-tests; Supplementary Table S1) and Bland-Altman analyses (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2) between iKIMRISS scoring and mean scoring by the 11 readers with automated grid placement. These analyses revealed that iKIMRISS introduced only minimal bias and moderate variability to KIMRISS scoring, especially when compared only to the more experienced KIMRISS users. We noted that stratification of these results between novice and experienced users of KIMRISS suggests that novice readers may have systematically overscored BML during the reading exercise, which may be the source of some of the lower ICC values reported in Table 2.

The inter-reader reliability analysis (see Supplementary Table S2-S9) highlights that human-to-iKIMRISS agreement in baseline KIMRISS BML scoring across the 3 scoring subregions (mean ICC = 0.72) was comparable to human-to-human agreement (mean ICC = 0.76), suggesting that iKIMRISS agreed with human readers to a similar degree as human readers did with each other (Supplementary Table S8).

Bone-contouring grid and BML segmentation quality survey responses

Results of the bone-contouring grid and BML segmentation quality surveys delivered during the reading exercise are presented in Supplementary Tables S10 and S11. Overall, participants viewed both iKIMRISS features favourably, with participants reporting that the grid placements and BML segmentations were at most only slightly deviated from the ideal position (with likely no impact on scoring) 80% and 67% of the time, respectively.

Postexercise questionnaire responses

Out of 11 readers, 8 readers completed the postexercise questionnaire, and 9 readers completed the SUS. Responses for each item are documented in Supplementary Table S12.

SUS scores of fully automated iKIMRISS indicated moderate usability, with a mean score across all readers of 63.1 ± 21.6 (out of 100). SUS scores for novice users of KIMRISS tended to be higher than for experienced users (mean scores of 72.5 ± 8.7 and 58.3 ± 25.2, respectively). The industry-standard benchmark value for SUS of a useful software tool is 68 [20,21]. The mean SUS score for the automatic grid placement feature of iKIMRISS alone was slightly higher, at 68.6 ± 11.9. For the iKIMRISS tool as a whole, the mean scores (out of a 5-point Likert scale) for the coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring domains of the NoMAD questionnaire were 3.84 ± 0.72, 4.06 ± 0.72, 3.42 ± 1.08, and 4.04 ± 0.81, respectively. These values are overall positive regarding the readers’ attitudes towards iKIMRISS becoming a normal part of their workflows, but the lower scores in the collective action domain highlight that more work is needed to define the tool’s role and value in practice.

Of the readers who completed the postexercise questionnaire, 6/8 (1/2 rheumatologists and 5/6 radiologists) ranked the usability of 1 or both versions of iKIMRISS higher than manual KIMRISS. Additionally, 6/8 readers (1/2 rheumatologists and 5/6 radiologists) agreed that iKIMRISS may impact their clinical decision-making process, and that 5/8 (2/2 rheumatologists and 3/6 radiologists) felt that iKIMRISS would become a normal part of their work. Notably, no reader agreed that their professional role was threatened by iKIMRISS, and all readers agreed that it is important for them to understand iKIMRISS’ decision-making process to trust it.

Time efficiency

To account for technical issues and breaks, runtime data from the reading exercise was excluded where more idle time than active scoring time was logged. Analysis of the remaining data indicated that iKIMRISS executes complete KIMRISS scoring in 14.5 seconds per case using 1 NVIDIA A100 graphics processing unit (Nvidia), for a mean total scoring time of 21.6 seconds. Meanwhile, full manual scoring, calculated by adding grid placement time from part 1 to BML scoring time in part 2, took readers an average of 813 seconds per case (413 ± 82 seconds for grid template placement and 400 ± 136 seconds for BML scoring). Thus, iKIMRISS produces KIMRISS scores 37.8 times faster than manual scoring.

DISCUSSION

In this image reading exercise-based validation study, we evaluated iKIMRISS—an AI-automated iteration of KIMRISS—using both quantitative and qualitative methods. In keeping with the OMERACT Filter 2.2, the results overall provide encouraging evidence of the tool’s truth and feasibility, with early indications that it has potential to be sensitive to change.

Regarding truth, the quantitative arm of this study compared iKIMRISS BML scores with an imaging ground truth of manually generated KIMRISS scores. The manual KIMRISS system has been validated in several OMERACT studies [11,16,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. We found that iKIMRISS scoring agreed strongly with both fully manual and semiautomated scoring methods, with the highest agreement between iKIMRISS and our expert users for both baseline and time interval change scores, a promising finding as it is desirable for iKIMRISS to score similarly to the most experienced users. Additionally, human-to-human KIMRISS score agreement was similar to human-to-iKIMRISS score agreement (mean pairwise ICCs of 0.76 and 0.72, respectively), highlighting that iKIMRISS performs within an acceptable margin of human reader consensus. These findings imply that iKIMRISS could be a valuable tool for researchers interested in quantitatively assessing OA without requiring time-intensive manual effort by experts.

One region where iKIMRISS performed more poorly was the patella, where the mean time interval change in BML score agreement with human readers was lower (mean ICC [experts only] = 0.35). The automated grid placement feature has also struggled in this region previously. This finding may be partly explained by the fact that BML extent in the patella tended to change very little, which may have artificially lowered ICC values. Otherwise, image review revealed that lower scores in this region may also have been due to technical problems with the AI identifying the borders of the patella for accurate grid template placement, which is understandable given the morphological variability in this structure across cases. Additional training data for the AI tool focused on the patella are likely to improve these results. This issue highlights that continued technical refinements to iKIMRISS are needed before its performance is acceptable for use outside of research settings.

In the qualitative arm of this study, the feasibility of iKIMRISS was assessed using a set of questionnaires and semistructured interviews. Thematic analysis of the interviews, as well as written responses to questionnaire items, revealed 6 core sentiments:

(1)iKIMRISS was quick to learn and easy to use;

(2)iKIMRISS substantially improves scoring speed, especially the automatic grid placement feature;

(3)iKIMRISS performed well overall but struggled occasionally with complex grid placement and overly sensitive BML segmentation;

In summary, these themes reflect an overall positive attitude towards iKIMRISS and its potential value in research settings but highlight the need for further refinements to improve performance and potential future clinical applicability.

One noteworthy result from the postexercise surveys is that novice users of KIMRISS found iKIMRISS more useful than experienced users (mean SUS scores of 73 and 58, respectively), a promising finding for the potential broader application of iKIMRISS among nonexpert researchers or clinicians. Additionally, responses to the NoMAD questionnaire indicated an overall positive attitude towards iKIMRISS becoming a normal part of practice; however, the lower scores in the collective action domain (mean of 3.4) suggest that more work may need to be done to clearly define how iKIMRISS integrates into workflows and modifies existing practices. Finally, survey responses indicated a need for clear communication of how iKIMRISS makes decisions. This issue may be addressed in future iterations with instructional modules or explainable AI techniques.

Our study had limitations. Although we used data from diverse sources and multiple readers, the number of cases scored was relatively small because the manual scoring task is tedious. Only 2 of our 11 readers also conducted fully manual KIMRISS scoring in this exercise, somewhat limiting our ability to compare iKIMRISS to the ‘gold standard’ of KIMRISS BML measurement. Our difficulties recruiting readers to complete manual KIMRISS scoring highlight the need for a less tedious automated process. This study is also limited to an imaging-based gold standard and has thus not fully assessed the validity of iKIMRISS with respect to clinical parameters, such as long-term arthritis outcomes.

A key priority for future work in this space is to continue refining the technical aspects of iKIMRISS to improve its performance and acceptability for clinical settings, as this tool may also have value for evaluating BML progression and response to treatment when disease-modifying therapeutics are used routinely in the future. Additionally, in alignment with the OMERACT Filter 2.2, future research should aim to more fully assess the truth, reliability, feasibility, and discrimination of iKIMRISS through a larger-scale longitudinal study comparing, eg, response to treatment between 2 groups. This will be essential to establish the performance, utility, and value of iKIMRISS in real-world translations.

In summary, this pilot study demonstrates that iKIMRISS may be an accessible, practical, and reliable system for scoring BML in knee OA while dramatically reducing overall scoring time compared with manual methods. An important implication of these findings is that, with minimal manual expert supervision, researchers could potentially use iKIMRISS as a suitable approximation of expert scoring for quantification of OA burden in clinical trial settings within just ∼20 seconds per case. Continued technical refinement of this tool and broader validation of its truth, reliability, feasibility, and discrimination will be important next steps towards establishing iKIMRISS as an OMERACT-endorsed instrument for evaluating OA in both research and clinical settings.
