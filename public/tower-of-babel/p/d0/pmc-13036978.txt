Barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of tuberculosis in Abidjan, Côte d’Ivoire: a qualitative study
Authors: Noelle A Benzekri, Eric Komena, Antoine Tohbi, Patrice Gousse, Antoine Jaquet, Raoul Moh, Timothée Ouassa, Souleymane Sidibé, Olivier Marcy, Eugène Messou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in West Africa. An understanding of the barriers to prompt diagnosis and treatment of TB is critical in order to develop strategies to improve TB outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative sub-study within the TB-Sentinel Research Network of IeDEA at the CePReF clinic in Abidjan, Côte d’Ivoire. Individuals 18 years of age who had received treatment for pulmonary TB within the preceding 12 months were interviewed. Qualitative data collection and analysis were guided by the Grounded Theory approach.ResultsInterviews were conducted with 40 participants (median age 35 [IQR 25–46] years), including 19 (47.5%) women and 9 (22.5%) people living with HIV (PLHIV). Participants experienced a substantial delay between symptom onset and TB diagnosis despite the presence of active TB symptoms and persistent care seeking. They sought care a median of 3 [IQR 2–5] times before TB testing and experienced a median diagnostic delay of 4 [IQR 3–6] months between symptom onset and TB diagnosis. The patient care pathway consisted of three distinct phases: the care-seeking phase, the diagnosis phase, and the treatment phase. Seven categories emerged across the three phases, each representing a potential barrier to the diagnosis and treatment of TB: (1) delayed TB diagnosis (sub-categories: prolonged care-seeking, repeated expenditures, and turning to sorcery), (2) fear of TB, (3) TB-associated stigma (sub-category: equating TB with HIV), (4) financial distress, (5) food insecurity, (6) enduring isolation (sub-categories: psychological distress and social setbacks), and (7) adherence anxiety.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first study to explore barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB in Côte d’Ivoire. Throughout each phase of the care pathway, individuals suffering from TB disease must continuously persevere in order to overcome enormous barriers. There is a need for strategies to alleviate their burden, to improve the early recognition and diagnosis of TB, and to address TB-associated stigma. Research to enhance community-based diagnostic testing, and the development and implementation of psychological support and social protection strategies, should be prioritized.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26711-y.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV, Diagnostic delay, Adherence, Stigma, Food insecurity, Sorcery, Traditional medicine, Mental health, Grounded theory, Qualitative research, Côte d’Ivoire, West Africa

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains among the leading causes of death globally and is the leading cause of death due to an infectious disease [1]. In 2023, an estimated 1.25 million people died from TB, including 1.09 million HIV-negative people and 161,000 people with HIV [1]. Approximately 27% of the deaths caused by TB among HIV-negative people and 70% of the deaths caused by TB among people with HIV were in the WHO region of Africa [1]. In Côte d’Ivoire, West Africa the estimated proportion of people who develop TB that die from the disease is 23%, which far exceeds the global case fatality ratio of 11.5% [1, 2]. Beyond the substantial impacts on mortality, many individuals who survive tuberculosis disease in Côte d’Ivoire face ongoing disability due to post-tuberculosis sequelae, and 45% of TB-affected households experience catastrophic costs [2, 3]. Despite the ongoing burden of TB, studies to identify barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB in Côte d’Ivoire are limited.

Barriers to the timely diagnosis and successful treatment of TB can occur at multiple stages in the patient care pathway, from initial care-seeking to diagnosis, and from treatment initiation to treatment completion [4]. In prior studies conducted elsewhere in West Africa, individual level factors that have been found to contribute to delayed care-seeking include perceived TB-associated stigma, financial constraints, and the use of traditional medicine [5–8]. Health system factors that contribute to diagnostic delay include healthcare providers not requesting TB diagnostic testing, reliance on sputum AFB smear microscopy, and receiving care outside of a hospital setting [5, 7–9]. In the treatment stage, barriers at the individual level include transportation costs, time constraints, use of traditional medicine, lack of knowledge about TB treatment, and TB-associated stigma [10]. Health system and structural factors that have been reported as barriers to treatment include distance to the treatment facility, costs of service, lack of TB expertise among healthcare personnel, attitudes of healthcare personnel, insufficient staffing, and receiving care in the private sector [10].

To our knowledge, there have been no prior studies in which qualitative methods have been used to conduct an in-depth inquiry into the barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB in Côte d’Ivoire. Grounded Theory, with its emphasis on emergence and process, is particularly well-suited to this goal. Guided by the Grounded Theory approach, this study was conducted in order to improve our understanding of the patient care pathway, to identify barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB, to understand how individuals overcome these barriers, and to develop strategies to improve TB outcomes in Côte d’Ivoire, West Africa.

Methods

This study was conducted among a subset of participants enrolled in the TB-Sentinel Research Network (SRN) observational cohort of the International epidemiological Databases to Evaluate AIDS (IeDEA) West Africa Consortium [11]. The IeDEA West Africa TB-SRN evaluates outcomes of individuals diagnosed with pulmonary TB at sentinel sites in West Africa. Upon enrollment in the TB-SRN parent study at the Centre de Prise en Charge, de Recherche et de Formation (CePReF) in Abidjan, Côte d’Ivoire, participants consented to enrollment in the qualitative sub-study. Participants were subsequently contacted via telephone by the study coordinator and invited to participate in an interview.

Data collection and analysis were guided by the Grounded Theory approach [12–18]. In-depth interviews were conducted with individuals 18 years of age who had received treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis within the preceding 12 months. Stratified purposive sampling was initially used to enroll a range of participants based upon age, gender, phase of TB treatment, and HIV status. Theoretical sampling was subsequently used to provide depth and dimension to emergent concepts.

Data collection in Classic Grounded Theory is flexible and emphasizes concept emergence [13, 14, 18]. Interviews begin with a very open, non-leading question, termed the “grand tour inquiry” [18, 19]. This prompts the participant to respond on their own terms, thus the direction of the interview is determined by what is important to the participant rather than the investigator [18].

Interviews were conducted in a private office by the first author, who is an expert in qualitative research methods and an infectious disease specialist. Of note, this investigator does not provide clinical care to individuals enrolled in this study, nor practice medicine at the study facility. Interviews explored the patient’s care pathway, beginning with symptom onset through diagnosis and treatment. Consistent with the Grounded Theory approach, interviews began with a grand tour inquiry (“Describe your journey through TB diagnosis and treatment, starting from when you first developed symptoms”) with subsequent questions framed according to the participant’s response (Additional File 1) [18, 19]. Each interview lasted approximately 60 min.

Data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively by the first author using the constant comparative method [12]. Preliminary analysis was conducted after each interview and used to guide and refine subsequent data collection. An interview guide was continually adapted as data collection and analysis progressed; the questions used in subsequent interviews were adapted according to emerging concepts. This process continued until theoretical saturation was reached, which is when additional data and the constant comparative method yield no new concepts related to the core variable, and new properties and dimensions of concepts cease to emerge [13].

Interviews were audio recorded using a digital voice recorder, transcribed, and uploaded into Atlas.ti (v23.4) to assist with data management and analysis. Initial open coding of the first few interviews was performed by hand on printed transcripts to facilitate data familiarization and immersion; subsequent open coding and selective coding were performed with the assistance of data analysis software (Atlas.ti). Theoretical coding was guided by the use of adapted theoretical coding families as described by Glaser [13–15]. Memoing, sorting, and diagramming were used throughout analysis [13, 14, 20]. Quantitative data that were elicited during the interview were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 29. Descriptive analysis was performed for demographic and clinical variables.

This study was approved by the Comité National d’Éthique des Sciences de la vie et de la Santé of Côte d’Ivoire (IRB00011917). All participants provided written informed consent.

Barrier 1: delayed TB diagnosis - participant quotes

Participants experienced a substantial delay between symptom onset and TB diagnosis despite the presence of active TB symptoms and persistent care seeking. They sought care a median of 3 times before they were tested for TB [IQR 2–5 times; range 2–8 times] and experienced a median diagnostic delay of 4 months between symptom onset and TB diagnosis [IQR 3–6 months; range 2–18 months] (Table 1).

Prolonged care-seeking

Participants presented to care with symptoms such as fever, night sweats, cough, hemoptysis, fatigue, and/or weight loss. They sought care at clinics, hospitals, and pharmacies. Upon presenting to care they were misdiagnosed and treated for conditions such as malaria, typhoid, or influenza. The vast majority sought care at symptom onset. For the few exceptions who delayed care seeking, the primary reason was concerns about the cost of care. The consequences of delayed diagnosis included increasing severity of illness, repeated expenditures despite limited resources, and loss of confidence in modern medicine.

Repeated expenditures

For each presentation to care at clinics, hospitals, and/or pharmacies they incurred costs. This included the costs of consultation, diagnostic testing, cough syrup, and other ineffective treatments, such as when their symptoms were repeatedly mis-attributed to malaria.

Turning to sorcery due to delayed diagnosis

As they repeatedly sought care and failed to improve with treatment, they assigned a spiritual meaning to their ongoing illness and viewed it as a moral judgement, a curse, or a result of sorcery. Traditional beliefs and practices played an important role when symptoms failed to improve despite repeated attempts to obtain effective treatment. At this point, some turned to sorcerers or traditional healers or purchased traditional medicine in the market.

Barrier 2: fear of TB - participant quotes

The initial reaction to receiving the diagnosis of TB was fear and distress, driven by strong, negative preconceptions about the significance of this diagnosis and a belief that “TB equals death”. This fear contributed to initial denial of the diagnosis, or delays and disruptions in treatment. This fear was further driven by the perception that if one is diagnosed with TB, then they must also have HIV, and that the diagnosis of HIV means that “life is over.”

Coping strategies

Successful transition through this phase involved overcoming one’s own negative preconceptions of TB and recognizing that treatment is the only way to regain health. In order to succeed with treatment, they had to first overcome the despair and hopelessness associated with the TB diagnosis. A core facilitator to overcoming this fear was reassurance from healthcare personnel that treatment would bring healing. Healthcare providers played an essential role in helping patients to understand and accept their diagnosis, and to recognize that treatment is available and a future is possible.

Barrier 3: TB-associated stigma - participant quotes

The TB diagnosis resulted in shame, isolation, rejection, and abandonment. The severe stigma associated with TB manifested as loss of community, abandonment by one’s spouse, separation from one’s children, rejection by family and friends, or being forced from one’s home. This experience was profoundly demoralizing and traumatizing. In some cases, the stigma was so severe that death was preferred over a diagnosis of TB.

Equating TB with HIV

These circumstances were further exacerbated by the concern that others would equate the TB diagnosis with HIV, which contributed to fear of further stigmatization. Among those living with HIV, this contributed to fear of inadvertent status disclosure.

Coping strategies

The strategy that was employed in order to persevere through this immense stigma was concealing one’s TB diagnosis.

Barrier 4: financial distress - participant quotes

Participants persevered despite profound financial distress and economic hardship, resulting from loss of employment, loss of income due to illness, or abandonment. This financial distress led to loss of housing, educational setbacks, and household hardship. It also created a barrier to adherence and retention in care due to inability to pay for transportation costs.

Coping strategies

The core strategy that enabled them to persevere despite these challenges was seeking support from others, including family members and healthcare providers. This support-seeking was associated with selective disclosure; support was sought only from those individuals that would not divulge their TB diagnosis. They economized and made decisions to conserve limited resources. Many had insufficient funds for household expenses and transportation, thus they walked to clinic despite illness, fatigue, and physical weakness. For some, it took hours for them to reach the clinic, as they needed to pause to rest periodically throughout the journey. A consequence of this dependence on others was loss of dignity.

Barrier 5: food insecurity – participant quotes

Overcoming barriers to food access was a dominant concern throughout the treatment period. Preexisting social vulnerability was exacerbated by illness and the absence of a social safety net. Loss of income due to poor health or abandonment contributed to food insecurity. Due to either physical weakness that prevented them from leaving the house to obtain food or due to isolation requirements that prohibited them from leaving their home, those without a support system had limited means of obtaining food.

Coping strategies

The primary coping strategies were food rationing and dependency on others. Food rationing included skipping meals, at times spending an entire day without eating in order to have sufficient food to take with medications, and prioritizing food for family members. The burden of not having food for one’s family and children exacerbated the stress of illness. Dependence on others for food was yet another way in which the TB experience eroded one’s sense of dignity.

Barrier 6: enduring isolation - participant quotes

Transmission prevention measures such as home isolation and imposed separation from others, contributed to severe psychological distress and social setbacks.

Psychological distress

The psychological toll of isolation could be so severe, that it led to depression and even suicidal ideation. Many remained in isolation for months, and some for the entire duration of treatment. During this period they ate their meals alone, separated from their family, with their own dedicated utensils. They slept alone, and in some cases, spent the entire treatment period isolated in a single room. Some participants were separated from their children for the duration of the treatment period. Apart from clinic appointments, they seldomly left their home and went for prolonged periods without human interaction. They didn’t attend social gatherings, religious gatherings, work, or school. This prolonged isolation and its consequences were a significant cause of grief, disruption, and psychosocial hardship.

Social setbacks

For some who were in school at the time of diagnosis, the imposed isolation caused them to either fall behind or not return to school at all.

Coping strategies

There were no formal strategies in place to assist patients with the psychological toll of isolation. They relied on prayer, encounters with other TB patients, and the support of healthcare personnel, either in clinic or during occasional household visits.

The pressure to remain adherent was a major source of anxiety. The fear of missing doses, or taking pills late, or not adhering strictly to the schedule, added to their burden of stress.

Coping strategies

Adherence anxiety was addressed through a commitment to adherence systems. The primary strategy was use of a phone alarm. Adherence was self-driven and not dependent on directly observed therapy (DOT) strategies.

Discussion

Although TB remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in West Africa, studies to identify barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB in the region are limited [1, 6–10, 21–52]. An understanding of these barriers is essential in order to develop strategies to improve TB outcomes. In this in-depth, qualitative study conducted in Côte d’Ivoire, we learned that individuals with TB disease must overcome seven core barriers across three distinct phases of the patient care pathway in order to successfully complete TB treatment and achieve cure. These barriers include, (1) delayed TB diagnosis, (2) fear of TB, (3) TB-associated stigma, (4) financial distress, (5) food insecurity, (6) enduring isolation, and (7) adherence anxiety. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use qualitative methods to understand barriers to the diagnosis and treatment of TB in Côte d’Ivoire.

Delayed TB diagnosis contributes to increased severity of disease, ongoing transmission, and mortality. As in other settings in West Africa, delayed recognition of TB symptoms and referral for dedicated diagnostic testing remains a challenge in Côte d’Ivoire [6–10, 25–28, 34–36, 42–45]. There is a need for heightened suspicion for TB among healthcare providers and pharmacists throughout the region. Individuals in this study sought care repeatedly before they were referred for TB diagnostic testing. Each of these encounters with a healthcare professional was a missed opportunity to expedite the diagnosis of TB. Furthermore, individuals initially sought care at community-level facilities where diagnostic testing is often unavailable. This represents a tremendous barrier to the early diagnosis and treatment of TB. Greater investment in the development of community-based diagnostic tools must be prioritized. Importantly, this study also highlights the potential of establishing partnerships with traditional healers and sorcerers, who can assist in the early referral of patients who present to them with symptoms of TB [53–57].

Profound stigma associated with TB remains deeply entrenched in Côte d’Ivoire, as in other countries throughout West Africa [6, 10, 21, 22, 26–31, 34–37]. TB-associated stigma can be so devastating that individuals may contemplate suicide rather than face society with this diagnosis [26, 27]. Robust efforts to ensure widespread and accurate understanding of TB, to raise awareness regarding the availability of TB treatment, and to increase knowledge about the possibility of cure, are needed to eliminate fear and de-stigmatize TB.

Many individuals living with TB in West Africa must overcome not only the costs associated with persistent care-seeking, but also the profound financial distress and devastating economic impacts experienced throughout the treatment phase [10, 22, 26–30, 33–39]. Economic impacts include loss of income and livelihood, depletion of savings, selling of assets, disruption of education, loss of housing, and household food insecurity. Without social support, the multitude of barriers encountered by individuals with TB disease may be insurmountable and likely contribute to loss to follow-up, ongoing transmission, and mortality. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the imposed isolation that occurs during the treatment phase can have profound psychological consequences among those receiving treatment for TB. In some cases, this prolonged isolation leads to depression and suicidality. Even after treatment is completed, the socioeconomic and psychological impacts of TB may persist and many never fully recover. Formalized psychosocial support and the universal implementation of social protections are needed to alleviate their burden [40, 58, 59].

Limitations

This was an in-depth qualitative study conducted among a subset of individuals receiving care for TB in Abidjan, Côte d’Ivoire. Although the findings may not be generalizable across populations, they may be transferable to similar contexts. Participants in this study were individuals who remained engaged in care. Future studies would benefit from seeking out individuals who have dropped out of care, in order to better understand the factors that contribute to treatment attrition.

Conclusion

Throughout each phase of the care pathway, from symptom onset to cure, individuals suffering from TB disease in Côte d’Ivoire must continuously persevere in order to overcome enormous barriers. There is a need for strategies to alleviate their burden, to improve the early recognition and diagnosis of TB, and to address TB-associated stigma. Research to enhance community-based diagnostic testing, and the development and implementation of psychological support and social protection strategies for individuals with TB, should be prioritized.
