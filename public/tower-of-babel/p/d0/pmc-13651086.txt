Barriers to AI-supported health management among older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions in China: implications for healthcare delivery and access
Authors: Liang Xu, Jiangning Fu, Nan Jiang, Boyu Zhai, Chunpeng Li, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly applied in chronic disease management, yet its adoption among older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions remains limited and poorly understood in China.MethodsA qualitative study using semi-structured interviews was conducted with 13 older adults recruited from two tertiary hospitals in Beijing and Harbin. Thematic analysis was performed to identify key themes.ResultsFive themes were identified: (1) preference for traditional health management over AI; (2) limited understanding of AI-supported health management; (3) perceived constraints in technological capability and affordability; (4) lack of external support from family and community; and (5) optimistic yet unrealistic expectations of AI capabilities.ConclusionsOlder adults with chronic orthopedic conditions face structural barriers to adopting AI-supported health management. Effective implementation requires age-friendly design and stronger integration within primary healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15119-4.

Introduction

With rapid population aging, chronic orthopedic conditions have become a major contributor to healthcare utilization and long-term care demand among older adults [1]. These conditions are a leading cause of disability globally and place sustained pressure on primary care services and rehabilitation systems. Osteoarthritis, lumbar disc herniation, cervical spondylosis, and related musculoskeletal disorders are highly prevalent among individuals aged 60 years and older, with incidence and care needs increasing substantially with age [2–4].

This trend is particularly pronounced in China [5], where rapid population aging and uneven distribution of healthcare resources intensify the service burden associated with chronic orthopedic conditions. Beyond increasing risks of falls and disability, these conditions contribute substantially to healthcare expenditures and long-term care demand at both household and system levels [6]. In response to growing service pressures, self-management has been promoted as a strategy to extend care beyond clinical settings and support continuity of care. Evidence suggests that structured self-management, such as medication adherence, functional exercise, symptom monitoring, and lifestyle modification, can reduce avoidable healthcare utilization such as acute medical interventions [7, 8] and delay disease progression. However, effective implementation remains challenging. Functional decline, cognitive limitations, and disparities in access to health information constrain older adults’ capacity to engage in sustained self-management [9, 10], revealing gaps in current healthcare delivery models. Traditional service approaches are often insufficient to address the complex and evolving care needs of this population.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have been increasingly proposed as tools to enhance chronic disease management and expand care capacity within resource-constrained health systems. AI-enabled applications, including automated reminders, remote monitoring, fall detection, and telemedicine support are being integrated into the management of hypertension, diabetes, and geriatric conditions [11, 12] to facilitate follow-up care and reduce avoidable healthcare utilization. However, despite rapid technological development, limited attention has been paid to how older adults, who are the primary target population for many of these interventions, perceive and engage with AI-supported health management [13].

Existing studies indicate that AI-related healthcare support models face multiple subjective and structural barriers among older adults in China [13, 14]. Although AI has demonstrated considerable practical efficiency in nursing contexts and has gradually been applied to the management of orthopedic conditions among older adults in China, such as postoperative pain management [15], these initiatives have largely operated within physician-monitored and professionally supervised frameworks. From a more ecological and long-term health management perspective, significant implementation challenges remain regarding how AI-supported health management can support relatively autonomous self-management among older adults. Key questions include identifying the primary barriers to sustainable adoption and determining how governmental and healthcare institutions can facilitate acceptance and utilization through policy design and service system optimization. These issues warrant systematic investigation.

Patients with chronic orthopedic conditions face distinct service challenges, including persistent pain, mobility limitations, fall risk, prolonged rehabilitation needs, and sustained self-care demands [16, 17], which may affect their ability to engage with digitally supported care. Unlike some chronic conditions that are primarily managed through medication adherence, diet control, or routine physiological monitoring, chronic orthopedic conditions often require daily functional exercise, safe movement, symptom observation, and rehabilitation-related decision-making [18, 19]. These disease-specific demands make AI-supported health management particularly relevant, as AI-enabled tools may potentially support home-based rehabilitation, exercise guidance, fall prevention, reminders, and remote monitoring. At the same time, pain, limited mobility, and other age- or disease-related physical limitations may also make technology adoption more difficult for this population [20]. Whether AI-enabled tools can be effectively integrated into the management of this population within routine healthcare delivery remains uncertain.

Therefore, this study focuses on older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions in China and employs qualitative interviews to explore perceived barriers and facilitators to AI-supported health management. The findings aim to inform the integration of AI-enabled tools into healthcare delivery models and support improved access to chronic disease management services for older adults.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed semi-structured interviews to explore perceived barriers to AI-supported health management among older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions in China. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [21]. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (KY2023-082), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Theoretical framework

This study was informed by the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) as a sensitizing framework for interpreting older adults’ engagement with AI-supported health management. UTAUT highlights performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions as key factors shaping technology acceptance and use [22]. In this study, UTAUT helped guide attention to perceived usefulness, ease of use, family and community support, and healthcare system resources, while the thematic analysis remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

Sample selection and recruitment

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling [23] between January and June 2025 from outpatient clinics at two tertiary hospitals located in Beijing and Harbin, China. The two sites were selected to reflect regional variation in healthcare resource distribution and digital access among older adults across different geographic and socioeconomic contexts. Efforts were made to include participants with diverse demographic backgrounds, including variation in age, gender, residential location (urban/rural), and educational level. Potential participants were first identified by clinicians in the outpatient clinics based on the eligibility criteria and were then contacted by the research team. The research team provided information about the study, confirmed eligibility, and invited eligible individuals to participate. During recruitment, the team monitored the composition of the sample to ensure variation in key characteristics, including residence, living arrangement, experience with smart devices, and type of orthopedic condition.

Eligible participants were aged 60 years or older, had a clinical diagnosis of at least one chronic orthopedic condition (e.g., osteoarthritis, degenerative spinal disorders, or cervical spondylosis), were able to communicate in Chinese, and were willing to provide informed consent. Individuals with severe psychiatric disorders or significant cognitive impairment were excluded.

Fifteen eligible individuals were approached by the research team. Two declined participation due to health-related reasons. Thirteen participants were interviewed and provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Prior to data collection, potential participants were contacted and informed about the purpose of the study. Interview time was scheduled in consultation with each participant. At the beginning of each interview, a brief study-developed structured questionnaire was administered to collect demographic and health-related information, including age, gender, place of residence, educational level, living arrangement, self-care ability, experience with smart devices, primary orthopedic condition(s), years since diagnosis, and perceived impact on daily life (see Supplementary Material 1).

The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the research team and refined through consultation with a multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of two psychological researchers, one artificial intelligence industry specialist, one geriatric care practitioner, and one clinical geriatrician. Based on expert feedback, the guide addressed the following core questions as below. The full interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Interviews were conducted by the second author, who had formal training in qualitative research and no prior relationship with participants. Due to mobility limitations, interviews were conducted via telephone at times convenient for participants. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent. Interviews lasted approximately 15–30 min on average. This duration was considered appropriate because the interviews were focused on a specific topic and used open-ended questions with follow-up probes to elicit participants’ experiences of managing chronic orthopedic conditions and their perceptions of AI-supported health management.

To enhance reflexivity, the interviewer engaged in regular discussions with the research team throughout data analysis. Data collection and preliminary analysis were conducted concurrently. After each interview, the research team reviewed the transcript and updated the coding notes to identify whether new codes, categories, or themes were emerging. By the eleventh interview, the main themes had become stable, and the final two interviews did not generate substantially new codes or themes. No formal saturation assessment tool was used; instead, saturation was assessed through ongoing comparison of interview data and team discussion during data collection.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after each interview, the researcher (X) transcribed the audio recordings verbatim. Participants were numbered according to the order of their interviews (e.g., Participant 01, Participant 02, etc.). The transcripts were carefully reviewed against the original recordings to ensure accuracy, and the final versions were confirmed by the participants.

Thematic analysis was used for coding and categorizing the data, and NVivo 15 software was employed to manage the qualitative data [24]. Two researchers (X and F) repeatedly read and immersed themselves in the transcripts to extract meaningful statements and conduct initial coding. After consolidating and organizing the codes, conceptual themes were developed. Any disagreements during this process were resolved through team discussion until consensus was reached. Finally, a research team of six members reviewed all the themes, defined and named them, and compiled the final research report accordingly.

Reflexivity

The research team was multidisciplinary, including one researcher with a background in orthopedic surgery, one with backgrounds in psychology and nursing, one in nursing, one in psychology, one in geriatric medicine, and one in intelligent engineering. The interviews were conducted by the second author, who had formal training in qualitative research and no prior relationship with the participants. Two researchers, including the first and second authors, independently reviewed the transcripts and participated in initial coding. During analysis, the researchers recognized that their disciplinary backgrounds might shape attention to healthcare access, rehabilitation needs, older adults’ psychological experiences, and the potential value of AI-supported health management. To reduce the influence of these assumptions, the team held regular discussions, compared interpretations with the original transcripts, reviewed coding notes, and resolved coding disagreements through consensus.

Results

Participant characteristics

The participants (n = 13) included nine females and four males, ranging in age from 61 to 82 years (Mean 70.23 years). On average, participants had received seven years of formal education, equivalent to primary school level. The sample included both urban and rural participants, older adults living alone or with family, participants with and without prior experience using smart devices, and individuals with different chronic orthopedic conditions, including osteoarthritis, spinal disc disorders, and cervical spondylosis. Thematic findings

Thematic analysis identified five interconnected themes that describe how older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions perceive and respond to AI-supported health management. These themes were: (1) preference for traditional health management over AI; (2) limited understanding of AI-supported health management; (3) perceived constraints in technological capability and affordability; (4) lack of external support from family and community; and (5) optimistic yet unrealistic expectations of AI capabilities. Together, these themes illustrate how established habits, knowledge gaps, personal confidence, and the availability of family and community support collectively influence older adults’ willingness and capacity to engage with AI-supported health management.

Theme 1. Preference for traditional health management over AI

Participants reported that they rarely considered adopting innovative approaches to address their health difficulties. During the interviews, they described substantial physical discomfort and psychological distress associated with their chronic orthopedic conditions. “I don’t dare to dance like I used to, especially spinning dances… If I fall again, I’ll be in serious trouble,” said Participant 09, explaining that her condition had affected her leisure activities and everyday mobility. She further noted, “…I avoid walking too fast, especially when it snows in winter—I’m afraid of slipping.” Despite these ongoing challenges, they were not inclined to seek more innovative or convenient solutions, such as AI-supported tools. Instead, caution and endurance characterized their typical response. “…I just try to bear it on my own,” said Participant 08. “Every day, I grit my teeth and prepare those two meals for myself.”

Participants, whether living in urban or rural settings, tended to manage their health in the simplest ways possible, relying on long-established habits such as taking prescribed medications and going for walks. They also depended heavily on clinicians for guidance, and this reliance appeared to limit consideration of AI-supported alternatives. “I go for a walk every day now—that counts as exercise. I don’t think it’s necessary to use any smart devices,” said Participant 01, explicitly expressing a lack of interest in new technologies. Participant 12 similarly indicated that the idea of using AI tools had never occurred to her. “I live in a rural area, so it’s not convenient to go to the hospital. I usually just buy some medicine myself—if I can manage, that’s enough. I’ve never thought about using any AI services.” Only when symptoms worsened did participants consider seeking medical consultation or undergoing examinations. “When the pain becomes very severe, I go to the hospital for an MRI or CT scan,” said Participant 04. “If I feel uncomfortable, I just ask the doctor what’s going on,” added Participant 10.

These accounts suggest that preference for traditional health management was not simply resistance to technology. Rather, it reflected trust in clinician-led care, long-established self-management habits, and a limited perceived need for AI-supported tools unless symptoms became severe or difficult to manage.

Theme 2. Limited understanding of AI-supported health management

Although AI has increasingly permeated various aspects of healthcare in recent years, the interviews revealed that older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions had little to no concrete understanding of AI or smart health technologies. For many, new technologies sounded distant or even somewhat abstract. Participant 03 stated, “That’s what smart devices are for—I just shout at my TV, and it turns off. I haven’t used anything else.” Participants often perceived AI as far removed from their daily lives. As Participant 05 noted, “I don’t know what AI is. I’ve heard about it on TV, but I really don’t know what it means.” Some participants even equated conventional therapeutic devices, such as physiotherapy machines, with smart technologies, reflecting conceptual confusion and indicating that the notions of AI or intelligent devices were largely unfamiliar to them. Participant 02 expressed satisfaction in describing what she considered to be a “smart device” for managing her chronic orthopedic condition:I’m using a smart device—a kind of heating therapy machine. I’m not sure if you’ve heard of the brand. It’s a physiotherapy machine… I also have many smart devices; my daughter bought them for me. They’re all treatment machines, for example, I also use an infrared lamp.

Together, these responses indicate that limited understanding of AI existed along a spectrum, ranging from unfamiliarity with the term itself to confusion between AI-enabled health management and conventional electronic or therapeutic devices. This conceptual ambiguity may make it difficult for older adults to judge what AI-supported health management can realistically offer or how it differs from tools they already use.

Theme 3. Perceived constraints in technological capability and affordability

Regarding self-efficacy, participants expressed concerns from multiple perspectives. Many believed that their older age, limited formal education, and declining memory made it difficult to follow instructions or recall operational steps, leading them to doubt whether they could successfully learn or continue using new technologies. Internalized age-related bias emerged as a significant barrier. Participant 05 stated that she was nearly 80 years old and felt that learning something new was “meaningless,” while Participant 11 shared a similar view: “I’m already over 70; I don’t understand anything.” Living in a rural area, Participant 05 further suggested that only people with higher education could use new technologies: “My education level is very low, so I’m increasingly unwilling to learn new things.” Perceived cognitive decline also contributed substantially to reduced confidence. Participant 02 remarked, “My memory has really declined—when I go to the supermarket to buy just two things, I have to think for a long time.” Participant 11 added, “I’m old and feel confused—I can’t understand what others are saying, let alone learn new technologies.” Participant 07 expressed a similar sentiment: “Older people have poor memory and accept new things slowly.”

Second, lack of confidence in technology was another important factor. Older adults stated that if AI could help them better manage their conditions, it would certainly be beneficial. However, many participants assumed, even before attempting to use it, that AI or smart devices would be difficult to operate. This expectation of high complexity discouraged them from making an initial attempt because they feared making mistakes or becoming stuck without assistance. They indicated that unless a device was extremely simple, required minimal effort, and could be used without repeated learning, they would prefer not to engage with it at all. Participant 04 expressed hope for such AI products and emphasized that they should better accommodate the characteristics of older adults:If this AI technology is simple and not too complicated, if it only requires me to press a button, then I think I should be fine. If there is such new technology, its design certainly should not be too complex, right? After all, it is designed for older people.

If this AI technology is simple and not too complicated, if it only requires me to press a button, then I think I should be fine. If there is such new technology, its design certainly should not be too complex, right? After all, it is designed for older people.

Another important barrier was participants’ perception that they lacked sufficient financial resources to use AI-enabled tools for managing their health conditions, a concern that was particularly pronounced in rural areas. Some participants acknowledged the advanced and potentially beneficial nature of these technologies, yet their primary consideration was whether their personal financial situation could support the costs associated with purchasing and using such devices. This pragmatic attitude of recognizing the value of AI but being unable to afford it limited its accessibility within these populations. Participant 10 from a rural area stated, “If I had extra money, I would still need to spend it on medical care. I cannot afford anything too expensive. My only source of income is a few acres of farmland. I have no savings and cannot afford anything costly.” Similarly, Participant 12 from a rural area remarked, “My financial situation is limited as well. Older people in the city have pensions. We have nothing, just a small piece of farmland.”

Theme 4. Lack of external support from family and community

The interview findings indicated that when adopting new technologies such as AI-enabled devices, older adults typically relied first on assistance from their adult children. Although most children were willing to help their parents engage with new tools, their busy schedules and limited time for visits often meant that they could only meet basic daily care needs, leaving little opportunity to provide systematic technical guidance. As a result, many older adults lacked sustained opportunities for practice or repeated learning, which reinforced fear of technology and self-doubt. “My children are not around. They don’t come back often—maybe once every one or two weeks. Even when they do return, they don’t think about these things,” said Participant 03, clearly expressing both reliance on and frustration with her children. Participant 04, who lived alone in an urban area, reflected from a broader demographic perspective: “Society is moving forward. In the future, we may all face this situation. There will be fewer and fewer people, and eventually not enough manpower to help older adults. It would certainly be good to have AI to help us, but who will teach us how to use it?”

These accounts show that the main gap was not a complete absence of family support, but the lack of sustained, repeated, and technically focused guidance. Community-based support was also limited, leaving participants without accessible sources of help when they encountered difficulties using new technologies.

Theme 5. Optimistic yet unrealistic expectations of AI capabilities

When participants were able to directly associate AI with familiar daily tasks, they were more willing to imagine integrating it into their everyday lives, even if they had little prior experience using smart devices. However, their descriptions suggested that many of the envisioned functions remained vague and were not well aligned with the actual capabilities of existing AI products. Participant 08, who experienced mobility limitations due to chronic orthopedic conditions, expressed her expectations of AI: “It could help me take care of myself. For example, sweeping the floor, cleaning the house, or even cooking for me.” Some participants believed that AI could fully replace the role of a hired caregiver. “If such technology really existed, I think it would be better than hiring someone. It should be specifically responsible,” said Participant 04. At the same time, participants demonstrated generally positive expectations toward new technologies. They believed that if the technology were easy to learn and could be smoothly integrated into their health routines, it would bring tangible benefits. Participant 02 said with a smile, “If I can learn it, it might be helpful. I have many health problems, including diabetes and coronary heart disease.” Others expressed willingness to learn and experiment with new technologies, not necessarily because they had specific expectations about AI functions, but simply as a way to learn something new and occupy their time. Participant 09 remarked, “I’m not yet 80 years old. I think trying new things is a good thing. People should have something to do.”

These expectations show that participants’ attitudes toward AI were not uniformly negative. However, their optimism was often based on broad hopes for practical assistance rather than a clear understanding of the current scope and limits of AI-supported health management.

Discussion

Based on in-depth interviews with older adults living with chronic orthopedic conditions, this study found that attitudes toward AI-based health management technologies are shaped not merely by individual interest or ability but by multi-level structural factors. Participants generally demonstrated limited understanding and cautious attitudes toward AI, reflecting not simple resistance to technology but the influence of established health management patterns, economic constraints, family support structures, and urban–rural disparities, consistent with prior research on the digital divide among older adults [25, 26]. AI has not yet gained a clear position within existing chronic disease management pathways, and its ambiguous role, combined with reliance on traditional healthcare systems, makes it difficult for older adults to perceive it as an integrated component of routine care. Thus, the promotion of AI in this context represents not only an issue of individual technology acceptance but a systemic challenge involving health service integration, resource equity, and sustainable support mechanisms.

Viewed through the UTAUT framework, these barriers reflect not only limited performance expectancy and effort expectancy at the individual level, but also insufficient social support and facilitating conditions at the family, community, and health system levels.

Although several barriers identified in this study are also relevant to older adults with other chronic diseases, the orthopedic context shaped how these barriers were experienced. For this group, AI adoption was closely linked to physical function, rehabilitation needs, and concerns about safe movement. Limited mobility may reduce opportunities to access in-person training or community-based digital support, while pain and fear of falls may reinforce reliance on familiar routines and clinician-led care. Thus, the findings should not be interpreted as barriers unique only to orthopedic conditions, but as showing how broader barriers to digital health adoption are expressed within the specific context of chronic orthopedic care.

This study further indicates that older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions understand health management as being deeply embedded within the existing healthcare system, relying primarily on physician guidance, in-person consultations, and familiar traditional therapeutic devices [27]. Within this established pathway, health management is perceived as a professionally led activity dominated by medical institutions rather than a self-management process driven by technological tools. Previous research has shown that older adults often struggle to integrate digital health technologies into their daily health decision-making and chronic disease management routines, largely due to persistent barriers related to capability, motivation, and structural support identified among older populations with chronic conditions [28, 29]. This is consistent with the findings of the present study. On the one hand, some participants confused conventional therapeutic devices with smart technologies, reflecting blurred conceptual boundaries. On the other hand, some expressed high functional expectations of AI, even imagining it as a substitute for human caregiving. Such cognitive ambiguity and role uncertainty suggest that AI has not yet achieved a clearly defined position within the chronic disease management system for older adults. Evidence also suggests that digital health interventions achieve higher acceptance and sustained use among older adults when embedded within structured health service pathways and supported by clinical and community care teams [30, 31].

The findings indicate that access to AI-supported health management is not evenly distributed but is clearly shaped by socio-economic conditions and urban–rural disparities in resource allocation. In rural areas in particular, limited income sources, inadequate pension coverage, and restricted access to healthcare services [32] mean that even when older adults recognize the potential value of AI, they often refrain from trying it due to cost concerns. This pragmatic decision of acknowledging its benefits yet being unable to afford it reflects a structural exclusion mechanism embedded in the diffusion of digital technologies. At the same time, differences in educational background and the long-standing lack of digital skills training infrastructure further widen disparities in technological capability. Rather than attributing this phenomenon solely to individual deficits, it should be understood as the cumulative outcome of unequal resource distribution and disparities in social protection systems.

This study found that older adults’ access to and use of AI-supported health management rely heavily on assistance from their children. This is consistent with previous research showing that, among older adults in China, family support is often the primary source of help [33, 34]. However, participants reported that due to busy work schedules or geographical separation, their children could only provide intermittent assistance, making it difficult to offer systematic technical guidance or sustained practice. As population ageing intensifies, family sizes shrink, and labor mobility increases, reliance on children as the main source of support is unlikely to be sustainable in the long term. These findings suggest that the promotion of AI-supported health management should not assume that families will naturally assume educational and support responsibilities; instead, institutionalized training and support pathways need to be established within public health and community service systems.

The findings suggest that AI-supported tools for self-management of chronic orthopedic conditions should adopt age-friendly design principles, with clear functional positioning and simplified interfaces, to enhance older adults’ confidence and sustained engagement.

From a healthcare delivery perspective, these findings suggest that AI-supported health management should not be promoted as a stand-alone consumer technology. Primary healthcare providers could play an important role in identifying older adults who may benefit from AI-supported tools, providing basic guidance, monitoring use, and linking digital support with routine follow-up and rehabilitation care. Community-based services could also provide accessible training, repeated practice opportunities, and troubleshooting support, especially for older adults who lack sustained family assistance. Digital literacy programs should therefore be designed around practical health management tasks, such as using reminders, understanding remote monitoring feedback, or following safe exercise guidance, rather than general technology education alone. At the policy level, subsidies, public service platforms, and support for rural and low-income older adults may be needed to prevent AI-supported health management from further widening inequities in access.

Ethical considerations are also important when deploying AI-supported health management among older adults. Because this population may have limited digital literacy and unequal access to technological resources [32, 35], implementation should include clear safeguards for privacy, data security, informed consent, and transparency in how health information is collected and used. Algorithmic bias may further disadvantage older adults from rural areas, lower socioeconomic backgrounds, or with limited digital access if AI systems are developed or validated using data that do not adequately represent these groups. Without appropriate policy support and service integration, AI-supported health management may fail to reduce existing inequities and could further widen the digital divide [36]. Ethical implementation therefore requires not only age-friendly design, but also equitable access, transparent governance, and support mechanisms within primary healthcare and community service systems.

This study has several strengths. It focuses on older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions, allowing AI adoption to be examined within a concrete chronic disease context rather than in abstract ageing populations. The inclusion of both urban and rural participants highlights socioeconomic and resource disparities affecting access. In-depth interviews further captured the complex interplay among health management practices, family support, and economic constraints, providing contextualized insight into systemic barriers to implementation.

Limitations should also be acknowledged. The regionally concentrated sample may limit generalizability. Although qualitative research does not aim for statistical generalization, the relatively small sample from two tertiary hospitals may limit the transferability of the findings. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to examine potential differences across regions, socioeconomic groups, digital literacy levels, and disease severity. Future research should also collect more detailed information on participants’ socioeconomic circumstances and digital experience to better contextualize barriers to AI-supported health management. In addition, participants were recruited exclusively from tertiary hospitals, and most had prior experience using smart devices, which may limit the transferability of the findings to older adults with less healthcare access or lower levels of digital exposure. Telephone interviews may have restricted observation of non-verbal cues, and findings were based on self-reported perceptions without behavioral validation. Potential social desirability bias should also be considered, as participants may have presented their views in ways they perceived as more acceptable to the interviewer. Future studies should broaden geographic scope, include more diverse participant subgroups, and examine whether saturation can be achieved across these subgroups. They should also incorporate observational or mixed-methods approaches to evaluate AI integration in real-world healthcare settings. Future research should also include the perspectives of family members and caregivers to better understand their capacity, burden, and training needs in supporting older adults’ use of AI-supported health management.

Conclusions

Older adults with chronic orthopedic conditions in China encounter structural barriers to adopting AI-supported health management, including limited understanding, economic constraints, and insufficient support. Effective implementation therefore requires age-friendly design and stronger integration within primary healthcare systems to promote equitable and sustainable use.
