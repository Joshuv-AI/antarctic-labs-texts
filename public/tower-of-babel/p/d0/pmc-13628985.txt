Dynamic, transition and variation of cervicovaginal microbiome and HPV infection and cervical dysplasia and cancer: a systematic review
Authors: Gbenankpon Mathias Houvessou, Nerys Wendy Antonieta Alfane, Manuel Mahoche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy in women worldwide, with approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually. The burden falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries. Although persistent infection with high-risk HPV (hrHPV) is the necessary cause, most infected women clear the virus spontaneously, implicating additional cofactors, including the cervicovaginal microbiome in determining oncogenic outcomes.MethodsPubMed was searched through September 10, 2024, to identify longitudinal studies assessing cervicovaginal microbiota in relation to HPV infection or cervical lesion outcomes at two or more time points. Methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Given the substantial heterogeneity, a structured thematic synthesis was performed across three predefined domains: (a) baseline microbiome composition and clinical outcomes; (b) community state type (CST) dynamics and temporal stability; and (c) microbiome changes following treatment.ResultsTwelve studies enrolling 1,663 women across 11 countries met inclusion criteria. NOS scores ranged from 4 to 9. Lactobacillus-dominated CSTs at baseline were consistently associated with HPV clearance and CIN regression, while Lactobacillus-depleted states showed higher transition rates and unfavourable outcomes. Prior L.iners (CST III) dominance was repeatedly linked to favourable outcomes, although evidence on this species remains conflicting. Cervicovaginal dysbiosis frequently preceded HPV persistence or lesion progression.ConclusionSustained Lactobacillus-dominated CST stability, rather than dominance by any single species, is the most consistent microbiome factor associated with favourable HPV and cervical lesion outcomes. Standardized longitudinal designs incorporating metagenomic sequencing, frequent sampling intervals, and rigorous confounder adjustment are needed to advance mechanistic understanding.Clinical trial registration numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13027-026-00777-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, accounting for an estimated 660,000 incident cases and 350,000 deaths per year [1, 2]. Its burden is disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America, where access to vaccination and organized screening remains limited [1]. Persistent infection with hrHPV, most notably HPV-16 and HPV-18, which collectively account for approximately 70% of cases, is the necessary cause of virtually all cervical cancers [3]. Despite this, most hrHPV-infected women clear the infection within two years through immune-mediated mechanisms, and only a minority progress to CIN or invasive carcinoma [4, 5]. This discrepancy underscores the role of modifying cofactors in disease progression.

Among the cofactors implicated in hrHPV persistence and cervical carcinogenesis, including smoking, hormonal contraception, immunosuppression, and genetic susceptibility [6, 7], the cervicovaginal microbiome has attracted growing attention. The vaginal tract of reproductive-age women is characteristically dominated by Lactobacillus species, principally L. crispatus, L. iners, L. gasseri, and L. jensenii, which sustain an acidic cervicovaginal pH, produce bacteriocins and hydrogen peroxide, and maintain a relatively anti-inflammatory mucosal environment [8–10].

Disruption of this dominance by anaerobic genera such as Gardnerella, Prevotella, Atopobium, and Sneathia constitutes the dysbiotic state associated with bacterial vaginosis and increased susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections [11, 12]. Cross-sectional evidence consistently links Lactobacillus-depleted communities to higher rates of hrHPV prevalence, persistence, and neoplastic progression [7, 13–15]. A systematic review and network meta-analysis of 11 studies established that CST IV (low lactobacilli) and L. iners-dominated states carry higher odds of hrHPV detection, dysplasia, and cancer relative to L. crispatus dominance; however, only three of those studies were longitudinal, limiting causal inference [15]. A subsequent review reported a temporal relationship between cervicovaginal dysbiosis and cervical carcinogenesis [16] but did not systematically analyze CST transition dynamics across serial timepoints or formally appraise study quality.

To address this gap, the aims: the temporal behaviour of the cervicovaginal microbiome in relation to HPV infection and cervical lesion evolution has not been comprehensively synthesized. To address this gap, the aims of this systematic review are: (a) to synthesize longitudinal evidence on the influence of cervicovaginal microbiome dynamics on HPV clearance or persistence and CIN regression or progression; (b) to identify bacterial taxa and CST patterns consistently associated with these outcomes across studies; and (c) to critically appraise methodological heterogeneity and its implications for future research design.

Methods and materials

Search strategy

PubMed was searched from inception through September 10, 2024, using the following MeSH terms and free-text keywords: “cervicovaginal microbiota”, “vaginal microbiome”, “human papillomavirus”, “HPV”, “cervical intraepithelial neoplasia”, “cervical dysplasia”, “cervical cancer”, “longitudinal study”, “cohort”, and “community state types”. The search was restricted to English-language publications in humans. Reference lists of eligible articles were manually screened to identify additional studies. Restriction to a single database (PubMed) is acknowledged as a limitation.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) assessed cervicovaginal microbiota using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing or an equivalent culture-independent method; (2) measured microbiota composition and HPV infection status or cervical lesion grade at two or more timepoints in the same participants; and (3) employed a prospective or retrospective longitudinal cohort design. Studies with a single microbiota assessment, purely cross-sectional designs, and non-human studies were excluded. One potentially eligible study was excluded because outcomes were reported only at aggregate level by cumulative HPV viral load, precluding individual-level clinical classification [17].

Data extraction and screening

Title, abstract, and full-text screening were performed by one reviewer, with supplementary screening of reference lists. Extracted variables included: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, population characteristics, follow-up duration and sampling intervals, HPV classification method, cervical lesion staging, microbiota characterisation technique and sequenced 16 S rRNA region, alpha-diversity metrics. Single-reviewer screening represents a methodological limitation and is acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of all included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. The NOS assesses three domains: selection (maximum 4 stars), comparability (maximum 2 stars), and outcome ascertainment (maximum 3 stars), yielding a total maximum score of 9. Studies scoring 7 or above were classified as high quality, 5–6 as moderate, and 4 or below as low quality (Table S2).

Data synthesis

Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity in study populations, follow-up intervals, CST classification schemes, sequencing methods, and outcome definitions, meta-analysis was not feasible. Findings were synthesized using a structured thematic approach organized around three predefined themes aligned with the review objectives: (1) baseline microbiome composition and clinical outcomes; (2) CST dynamics, transition patterns, and temporal stability; and (3) microbiome changes following cervical lesion treatment. Within each theme, the consistency of associations is summarized semi-quantitatively in Table S1: ++ denotes a consistent association across three or more studies; + in one to two studies; +/- denotes conflicting evidence; and - denotes absent or negative association. Given its recurring and contested associations across all three themes, the role of L. iners is addressed in a dedicated subsection.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

The PubMed search retrieved 1,430 records. Titles and abstracts screening led to 60 full-text assessments and 12 studies meeting inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). One additional potentially eligible study was excluded due to lack of individual-level outcome data, with individual study samples ranging from 14 to 448 participants. Seven studies examined the natural history of HPV infection or cervical lesions [18–25], and sevem assessed microbiome changes before and after cervical lesion treatment [26–29]. Study characteristics are summarised in Table S1, whereas detailed methodological features and principal findings of the included longitudinal studies are presented in Table 1. Key descriptive features are presented in Figure S1. Studies were conducted in the USA (n = 3) [19, 20, 25], China (n = 3) [23, 26, 29], and one each in the Netherlands [22], Sweden [18], South Africa [24], Costa Rica [21], the United Kingdom [27], and Italy [28]. Follow-up durations ranged from 16 weeks [19] to three years [23]; one study did not report this parameter [29]. Alpha-diversity was quantified using the Shannon index in sevem studies [18, 21, 23, 26, 29] and species richness or Chao1 in one [27]; beta-diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity in three studies [18, 22, 25].

Quality assessment results

NOS scores ranged from 4 to 9 (Table S2). Four studies achieved high quality (score 7 or above) [20, 23, 25, 27], reflecting adequate cohort definition, clear outcome ascertainment, and adjustment for at least one key confounder. Seven studies scored 5–6, indicating moderate quality, primarily reflecting limited confounder adjustment. One studies scored 4 or below [29], mainly due to inadequate reporting of follow-up procedures and absence of confounder control.

CST classification heterogeneity

Studies classified cervicovaginal communities into 3 to 7 CSTs. The most commonly applied scheme (four studies) [20, 23, 25, 27], distinguished three categories: CST I (L. crispatus dominant), CST III (L. iners dominant), and CST IV (Lactobacillus-depleted). Four-CST and five-CST schemes were each used in two studies [18, 21] and [22, 28], respectively; six- and seven-CST classifications appeared in one study each [19, 26]. This granularity variation, compounded by differing sequenced 16 S rRNA regions, limits direct cross-study taxon-level comparisons.

Theme 1: Baseline microbiome composition and clinical outcomes

Lactobacillus dominance and favorable outcomes. Lactobacillus-dominated CSTs at baseline were consistently associated with HPV clearance and CIN regression ( + + evidence for both outcomes). Low alpha-diversity and high L. crispatus abundance at baseline and follow-up were associated with a reduced risk of SIL progression in hrHPV-positive women [22]. Women with CIN2 whose baseline microbiota was Lactobacillus-dominated were more likely to achieve regression at 12 and 24 months compared to those with Lactobacillus-depleted communities [20, 21, 24]. Furthermore, women with incident or persistent CIN2 + displayed higher anaerobic dysbiosis markers at both baseline and endpoint compared to hrHPV-positive and persistently HPV-negative groups [24].

Specific dysbiotic taxa were associated with adverse outcomes across multiple studies. Baseline colonization with Gardnerella vaginalis and Prevotella amnii predicted subsequent CIN2 progression in the Costa Rica cohort [21]. In a Chinese prospective cohort, Atopobium, Sneathia, and Enterococcus predicted persistent HPV infection [29], while Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas, and Klebsiella were associated positively with CIN progression; conversely, Lactobacillus spp. and Streptococcus spp. were associated with CIN regression in the same cohort [29]. These findings suggest that the prognostic signal from the baseline microbiome extends beyond the broad Lactobacillus/non-Lactobacillus dichotomy to specific anaerobic taxa with distinct clinical correlates.

Alpha-diversity findings were heterogeneous (+/- evidence). Studies found no baseline diversity difference between HPV-positive and HPV-negative states [18, 20] or between CIN progressors and non-progressors [22]. In contrast, elevated Shannon diversity at the second visit was significantly associated with CIN2 + progression in Costa Rica (p = 0.027) [21], and serial reductions in alpha-diversity characterised the CIN regression group in a Chinese cohort [23]. In treatment studies, one study found no richness differences before versus after LEEP [27], while another reported lower post-treatment diversity [26], and elevated pre-treatment diversity predicted disease recurrence after concurrent chemoradiotherapy [29].

Theme 2: CST dynamics, transition patterns, and temporal stability

Lactobacillus-dominated CSTs consistently exhibited greater temporal stability, while Lactobacillus-depleted communities showed higher transition frequencies ( + + evidence). In hrHPV-positive women progressing to SIL, microbiome composition was significantly less similar between baseline and follow-up than in women who remained SIL-negative [22]. Women with HPV-16 persistence and CIN2 + exhibited significantly more inter-cluster transitions over 18 months than those who cleared the infection [18]. Moscicki et al. [25] demonstrated that HPV-16-infected women were significantly more likely to transition between CSTs and showed a higher probability of remaining in non-Lactobacillus-dominated states compared to HPV-negative controls. Critically, the directionality of transitions diverged between clinical outcome groups: CIN2 regressors most frequently transitioned from CST IV (Lactobacillus-depleted) to CST III (L. iners-dominated) at both follow-up intervals, whereas non-regressors predominantly transitioned in the reverse direction during the same periods [20]. This divergence in transition direction is among the most informative longitudinal findings in this review.

Van de Wijgert et al. [24] found that L. iners-dominated, mixed Lactobacilli/BV-anaerobes, and BV-anaerobe-dominated VMB types were the least stable in women living with HIV. Brotman et al. [19], using biweekly self-sampling over 16 weeks, found that L. crispatus and L. gasseri-dominated CSTs showed fewer transitions than other types; women with CST IV-B (Atopobium-enriched) microbiota frequently transitioned to CST III (L. iners), though rarely to CST I (L. crispatus). Collectively, these data support L. crispatus dominance as the most stable cervicovaginal state, with CST III serving as a frequent transitional state.

Theme 3: Microbiome changes following treatment

Five studies evaluated microbiome dynamics before and after cervical lesion treatment [26–29]. In CIN patients undergoing LEEP, 56% maintained the same CST between the treatment date and six-month follow-up; among those who transitioned, the majority shifted between Lactobacillus-dominant states (56%), while (24%) moved from CST IV to Lactobacillus-dominant and 20% transitioned in the opposite direction [27]. In Chinese patients receiving cervical excision or interferon-based therapy, CIN I-III groups transitioned predominantly from CST IV to CST III (L. iners-dominated), and SCC patients shifted from CST II (Burkholderia-dominant) and CST IV toward CST III [26].

In Italian patients, L. crispatus abundance increased significantly after LEEP in both CIN2 and CIN3 groups, with a proportionally greater rise in CIN3 (from 16% to 29%) than in CIN2 (from 39% to 45%) [28]. Women without disease recurrence after concurrent chemoradiotherapy maintained significantly more stable microbial communities during follow-up, while L. iners was the most abundant taxon in the recurrence group before treatment [29]. Taken together, treatment appears to facilitate a partial short-term shift toward Lactobacillus-enriched communities, though whether this represents sustained ecological restoration or a transient response to tissue repair remains unresolved.

The contested role of L. iners across outcomes

Prior L. iners dominance was positively associated with HPV clearance in the Costa Rica cohort [21] and with the highest CST stability in treated CIN patients [27]. In women with HPV-16 infection, L. iners relative abundance increased during the pre-infection period, declined during active infection, and remained lower than in HPV-negative controls at post-clearance visits [25]. Additionally, Brotman and colleagues [19] evaluated the HPV incidence and showed that CSTs with the greatest proportion of HPV-positive samples were the low-Lactobacillus groups (CST IV) and the L. iners–dominated (CST III), when compared to CSTs dominated by L. crispatus and L. gasseri [19]. Post-treatment, L. iners dominance was the most common endpoint state across multiple studies [26, 28, 29]. In contrast, cross-sectional meta-analyses link L. iners-dominated CSTs to elevated HPV risk relative to L. crispatus [15], and van de Wijgert et al. [24] identified L. iners-dominated VMB types among the least stable in HIV-positive women. Evidence consistency for all microbiome-outcome relationships is summarized in Table S2 and Figure S2.

Discussion

This systematic review of 12 longitudinal studies across 11 countries provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of cervicovaginal microbiome dynamics in relation to HPV infection and cervical lesion outcomes. Three overarching patterns emerge from the evidence. First, Lactobacillus-dominated CSTs, particularly L. crispatus-dominant states, are consistently associated with HPV clearance, CIN regression, and temporal microbiome stability across multiple cohorts and geographic settings. Second, Lactobacillus-depleted communities show higher transition rates and unfavorable clinical outcomes. Third, cervicovaginal microbiome shifts appear to precede, rather than merely accompany, changes in HPV status or cervical lesion grade in several cohorts, lending temporal plausibility to a modulating rather than merely reactive role.

The role of L. iners is one of the most contested questions in this field, and the present synthesis does not fully resolve it. Cross-sectional meta-analyses associate L. iners dominance with elevated HPV risk relative to L. crispatus [15], yet several longitudinal studies in this review identify positive associations between prior L. iners abundance and HPV persistence or incidence or CIN recurrence/progression [19, 20, 25, 28, 29]. These observations are not inherently contradictory. Ecologically, L. iners occupies an intermediate position: it survives under anaerobic and pH-variable conditions more readily than L. crispatus, enabling it to colonise the cervicovaginal niche after disturbance and potentially bridge the transition toward more stable Lactobacillus dominance [30, 31]. The observation that CIN2 regressors characteristically transition from CST IV to CST III before potential consolidation in CST I is consistent with this stepping-stone model [20] (Figure S2).

Nonetheless, L. iners has also been identified as a marker of ecological instability in other contexts [24, 32], and recent functional evidence suggests it may suppress host defence peptides that support L. crispatus survival under specific mucosal conditions [33]. Resolving this question will likely require strain-level metagenomic analysis, given that L. iners encompasses genomically diverse lineages with potentially divergent metabolic capacities [34]. The present evidence reflects genuine biological ambiguity: the ecological role of L. iners appears context-dependent, varying with host immune status, concomitant HPV genotype, and surrounding community composition.

Lactobacillus-produced lactic acid maintains vaginal pH below 4.5 and has been shown in vitro to suppress HPV E6/E7 oncoprotein expression [35]. Bacteriocins and hydrogen peroxide secreted by L. crispatus and L. jensenii further limit anaerobic colonization. Conversely, BV-associated species produce sialidase and proteases that degrade cervicovaginal mucus and reduce secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI), generating a pro-inflammatory cytokine milieu, characterized by elevated IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-alpha, that may impair innate immune clearance of HPV-infected epithelium [33, 35]. Biofilm formation by Gardnerella may additionally protect HPV virions from immune effectors while sustaining local mucosal inflammation [33]. Functional metagenomic profiling of the CC-associated microbiome has identified enrichment in pathways related to fatty acid biosynthesis, folate metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation [16], suggesting that dysbiotic communities may confer metabolic advantages to transformed epithelial cells that extend beyond their pro-inflammatory effects.

The HPV-microbiome relationship is increasingly recognized as bidirectional. HPV itself may suppress Lactobacillus dominance by interfering with host defence pathways: the HPV E7 oncoprotein has been shown to disrupt NF-kappaB and Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, reducing expression of host defense peptides that support Lactobacillus survival [33]. This feedback mechanism, whereby dysbiosis favours HPV persistence which in turn perpetuates dysbiosis, may help explain why microbiome changes precede progressive disease in several cohorts reviewed here [21, 24, 29].

The substantial heterogeneity across included studies directly constrains the strength of this synthesis. CST classification granularity ranged from 3 to 7 subtypes, collapsing or subdividing potentially distinct ecological states in non-comparable ways: three-CST schemes aggregate all Lactobacillus-depleted states into a single CST IV category, masking distinctions between Gardnerella-dominated and highly diverse anaerobic communities. Variable 16 S rRNA sequencing regions (V1-V3, V3-V4, V4) further compromise taxon-level cross-study comparisons, as different regions yield different resolution profiles for key genera.

Sampling interval heterogeneity additionally limits temporal inference. Studies with annual sampling [20, 21, 23] may miss intermediate transitions captured by biweekly self-sampling [19]. The single HIV-positive cohort [24] introduces confounding by systemic and mucosal immune dysregulation, which profoundly shapes both microbiome composition and HPV clearance capacity. Most critically, only two studies formally adjusted for key confounders such as ethnicity, sexual activity, and antibiotic use [19, 24]. Given established ethnicity-related variation in cervicovaginal microbiome composition [36] and the strong behavioral determinants of both microbiome and HPV outcomes, residual confounding remains a plausible alternative explanation for the associations observed in most included studies.

Based on the identified evidence patterns, future longitudinal studies should: (1) adopt standardized sampling intervals of three months or less to capture clinically relevant microbiome dynamics; (2) employ shotgun metagenomic sequencing to enable strain-level identification and functional pathway analysis beyond 16 S rRNA compositional profiling; (3) systematically report and adjust for key confounders, including sexual activity, antibiotic exposure, hormonal contraception, ethnicity, and HIV status; (4) adopt a harmonized CST classification framework to enable cross-study comparison and eventual meta-analytic synthesis; and (5) design large, multi-ethnic cohort studies to improve generalizability and allow meaningful subgroup analyses.

Regarding clinical translation, the current evidence does not support microbiome-targeted interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, or microbiome transplantation, as adjuncts to HPV management or CIN surveillance outside of clinical trial settings. Although mechanistic and early-phase trial data demonstrate biological plausibility for Lactobacillus-based interventions [33, 37], randomized controlled trials with validated cervical lesion endpoints are absent, and translational claims should be framed accordingly.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. The literature search was restricted just to PubMed; EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were not searched, introducing potential selection bias. A single reviewer, increasing the risk of inadvertent exclusion, performed title and abstract screening. Meta-analysis was not feasible due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity, precluding quantitative effect pooling. All included studies relied on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, which lacks the strain-level resolution and functional inference capacity of metagenomic approaches. Primary study quality was heterogeneous (Table S2), and most studies did not adjust for key confounders, constraining causal conclusions. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the synthesized evidence.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that cervicovaginal microbiome composition is temporally associated with HPV infection and cervical lesion outcomes. In response to the stated objectives: (a) sustained Lactobacillus-dominated CST stability is the most consistent microbiome factor associated with HPV clearance and CIN regression across studies; (b) among specific taxa, L. crispatus dominance shows the strongest and most consistent protective signal, while Gardnerella, Prevotella, and Atopobium are recurrently associated with adverse outcomes; and (c) methodological heterogeneity, particularly in CST classification schemes, sampling intervals, and confounder adjustment, precludes definitive causal conclusions and highlights the need for standardized designs. Microbiome dysbiosis appears to precede HPV persistence and CIN progression in several cohorts, supporting a temporally antecedent modulating role. Advancing from the current associative evidence to mechanistic understanding and clinical application will require interdisciplinary collaboration combining rigorous longitudinal epidemiological designs with next-generation metagenomic technologies and prospective evaluation of microbiome-targeted interventions in well-powered randomized trials.
