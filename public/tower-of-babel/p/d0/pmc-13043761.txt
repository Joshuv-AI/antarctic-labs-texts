Disordered light-harvesting aggregates can host functional vibronic couplings at room temperature
Authors: Asha S Thomas, Camelia Roy, Indranil Roy, Vivek N Bhat, Sayan Ghosh, Vivek Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotosynthesis relies on a network of chlorophyll-like molecules which together lead to efficient long-range energy transfer. Evidence at cryogenic temperatures suggests that mechanistic details of energy/charge transfer must invoke coupled vibrational-electronic (vibronic) states. Whether these survive at physiological temperature in large photosynthetic aggregates is an open question. Parallel research on artificial templates has relied on cyanines which are unlike chlorophylls. Here we report time-resolved optical spectra of porphyrin nanotubes where we selectively probe coupled Qx − Qy states through polarization control. Early time cross peaks between electronic states, their rapid broadening and survival of quantum beats with anisotropic beating amplitude conclusively demonstrate that overlapping vibrational-electronic bands of photosynthetic aggregates indeed host functional vibronic couplings at room temperature. Calculations reveal that disorder is the vital ingredient that dramatically enhances intraband vibronic couplings across the entire Q band. The parameter regime where energetic disorder is of the order of dense Raman-active vibrations with weak reorganization energies may be the key design principle.Subject terms: Energy transfer, Chemical physics, Optical spectroscopy

Introduction

Internal conversion through overlapping vibrational-electronic manifolds1 dictates the fate of photoexcitations in natural and artificial light harvesting. In case of natural photosynthesis2, delocalized photoexcitations created in pigment-protein complexes (antennas) survive lossy internal conversion channels up to several hundred picosecond timescales, diffuse across tens of nanometers (nm) lengthscales across a network of such antenna proteins to ultimately be harvested with near unity quantum efficiency as a stabilized charge-separated state. Cryogenic experiments and theoretical proposals have suggested a functional role for non-classical vibrations3,4, which can promote mixed vibrational-electronic (vibronic) states and nested energy funnels within these proteins. However, decoherence5, defined by the decay of coherence density matrix elements and caused by the time-dependent loss of overlap between the bath wavefunctions at the donor and acceptor sites, can destroy coherent vibrational-electronic admixtures and prevail at room temperature because vibronic couplings in photosynthetic aggregates are weak to begin with. Consequently, whether the mechanistic principles learnt from cryogenic studies survive decoherence at room temperature in large aggregates remains a hotly debated question6,7, inspiring several design strategies7,8 to understand and mimic photosynthetic energy transfer. Probing this question in the context of large photosynthetic aggregates at room temperature is the subject of this manuscript.

Artificial light harvesting templates aiming to mimic the natural design principles have largely included covalently linked dimers9, self-assembled nanotubes10, and DNA templates hosting molecular networks with precise control11 over position, orientation and the resulting excitonic couplings. Among these, self-assembled light-harvesting nanotubes12 (LHNs), specifically cyanine LHNs, represent a distinctly different approach where energy transfer across nanotube layers13,14, robust excitons with estimated coherence length over 1000 molecules and extraordinary micron-scale exciton diffusion lengths10 have been demonstrated.

An interesting feature that differentiates all the above approaches for designing artificial light-harvesting antennas from the naturally occurring photosynthetic nanotubes and bundles (chlorosomes15) is that the constituent molecules of the latter rely on chlorophyll (Chl) or closely related molecules that share a unique electronic structure with Qx and Qy bands and Bx and By bands in the visible and ultraviolet regions, respectively. Here, the subscripts x and y denote directions of electronic transition dipole moments. Such an electronic structure is well known to be sensitive to substituents and coordination of ring nitrogens16, undergoes fast 100–200 fs internal conversion17 between Qx and Qy bands as well as between the B and Q bands18, and hosts a dense spectrum low-frequency Raman active vibrations19 that undergo negligible Franck–Condon (FC) displacements (dimensionless displacement d < < 1) upon photoexcitation20. Small FC displacements maximize the range of nuclear coordinates over which non-adiabatic coupling between vibrational and electronic wavefunctions is effective, leading to nested energy funnels21,22 in several photosynthetic proteins22. In contrast, in the case of large nuclear displacements, non-adiabatic vibronic coupling between the donor-acceptor excitons only occurs in a limited range of nuclear coordinates23 thereby limiting the efficacy of nested energy funnels. Thus, artificial dimers, nanotubes, or DNA templates based on cyanine derivatives likely operate in parameter regimes distinctly different from natural photosynthesis23. This lack of similarity between artificial aggregates and natural photosynthetic antennas motivates study of those artificial LHN24 templates where the mechanistic details of photosynthetic energy and charge transfer can be reproduced and understood. The schematic in Fig. 1a illustrates the similarity in the four frontier orbital electronic structure16 of the constituent porphyrin molecules employed in this work with that known in chloropylls. Similar to the 100-200 fs internal conversion known17,25 in chlorophylls, sub-100 fs internal conversion18,26, between B − Q and Qx − Qy bands is known in porphyrins as well. Fig. 1b shows the Q bands of the porphyrin nanotube aggregate investigated here. The theoretical predictions27 of the current vibronic exciton models for nanotube aggregates formed from these constituent porphyrins suggest that the main band is Qx in character and polarized along the cylindrical axis while the first shoulder is Qy in character and polarized perpendicular to the cylindrical axis. The lower panel of Fig. 1b shows the dense Raman active vibrational spectrum28 of the porphyrin nanotubes, which closely mimics those of photosynthetic pigments. Similar to energy funnels in photosynthetic proteins22, the resulting overlapping vibrational and electronic bands suggest that vibrational excitations in the lower electronic state may lead to near-resonant vibronic couplings with the upper electronic states within the main Q band as well as between the main Q band and its first shoulder. The absorption and Raman spectra (Fig. 1b) together illustrate the central question that this work aims to address, that is, whether such near-resonant vibronic couplings survive at room temperature to play a functional role, that is, drive ultrafast energy transfer in large, disordered photosynthetic aggregates?

Such a role may not be expected in large aggregates because –1. for photosynthetic-like pigments such as porphyrins where the dimensionless displacement d ≪ 1, resonant vibronic couplings29,30 due to an intramolecular vibration are already expected to be weak. The vibrational-electronic admixtures resulting from such a coupling will be quenched by the time-dependent (loss of) overlap between the bath wavefunctions and, the effect increases when the vibrational bath is thermally excited at room temperature, and 2. due to weak vibronic couplings, the resonance widths over which vibronic couplings in photosynthetic dimers are effective at mixing states are only a few tens of wavenumbers and therefore not robust. Note that vibronic coupling refers to a static picture. In a time-dependent description, coupling between sites leads to mixing on a timescale related to the inverse of the coupling, and competes against decoherence between the sites.

Two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy31 (2DES) has provided unprecedented insights22,32 into the dynamics that emerge from overlapping vibrational and electronic bands due to its fast temporal resolution and spectral decongestion along excitation, detection and coherence axes. As shown in Fig. 1c, d, in our partially collinear pump-probe33 2DES experiment, a pair of pump pulses create absorptive changes in the sample as a function of their mutual delay τ. A delayed probe pulse measures these changes as a function of pump-probe waiting time T and the probe detection wavelength λt resulting in the differential probe spectrum S(τ, T, λt). Division by the probe spectrum S(λ) with no pump, Fourier transformation along τ and Jacobian conversion to ωt leads to 2D contour maps that correlate detection and excitation frequencies of a given system as a function of T. Extensions of concepts from electronic polarization anisotropy to 2DES have also led to polarization-controlled 2DES34 where 2D cross-peaks35,36, as well as vibronically enhanced quantum beats37–39, can be uniquely isolated.

So far, all demonstrations22 of polarization-controlled 2D in uniquely isolating vibronically mixed states in photosynthetic proteins have been conducted at cryogenic temperatures on small aggregates. Here we present polarization-controlled 2DES experiments on the Q band of self-assembled porphyrin nanotubes, revealing the rich complexity of overlapping vibronic bands that emerge in large self-assembled and disordered aggregates. Our observations demonstrate that the overlapping vibronic bands in porphyrin nanotubes closely mimic all dynamical features of naturally occurring chlorosome nanotubes. These observations are not evident in any prior pump-probe experiments40,41 and include features such as 2D cross peaks (CPs) at T as early as 30 fs, their broadening within 150 fs indicating ultrafast delocalization of initial excitation, and anisotropic vibrational quantum beats that are unique38,39 reporters of mixed Qx − Qy states arising from near-resonant vibronic couplings. Our refined vibronic exciton model for the nanotube suggests that disorder is the vital ingredient that promotes Qx − Qy electronic mixing across the entire Q band. Given this unavoidable electronic mixing, intramolecular vibrations of porphyrins that tune energy gaps ensure that the energetically distinct Q-band and its overlapping vibronic shoulders are strongly mixed. The evidence for intraband vibronic couplings surviving in large aggregates at room temperature suggests that the mechanisms underlying energy transfer in photosynthesis may be surprisingly robust, and disorder-induced enhancement of vibronic couplings may be a vital part of the photosynthetic design.

Results

2DES on porphyrin nanotubes reveals intra-Q band cross peaks

The 2DES experiment generates a non-linear third-order polarization, which then radiates a 90° phase-shifted signal field. In the non-linear response function formalism, the four light-matter interactions are treated perturbatively and each term in the perturbative expansion of the response function can be represented42 by a corresponding diagrammatic representation (wavemixing diagrams). The matter part of the non-linear response functions can be represented31 as Rμ~⟨(μ^⋅E^a)(μ^⋅E^b)(μ^⋅E^c)(μ^⋅E^s)⟩ where the field polarization vectors E^a−c in the lab frame interact with the system to generate the radiated signal field E^s, which is then detected by interference with a known field, in our case E⃗c. μ^ represents the transition dipole vectors in the molecular frame between the initial and final system eigenstates involved at each interaction. ⟨…⟩ denotes orientational averaging between the molecular and lab frames.

Figure 2a shows a series of PE-2D spectra of porphyrin nanotubes, where PE denotes the perpendicular pulse sequence (90°, 90°, 0°, 0°), with orthogonal relative polarizations of the pump and probe electric fields. For waiting time T as early as 30 fs, we observe two well-resolved CPs between the main Q band and its two higher energy, weaker intensity shoulders seen in Fig. 1b. The two lower CPs (CPL) in the V2 and V3 bands, each correspond to the two vibronic shoulders in the absorption spectrum, and suggest that Qy polarized states may be strongly coupled to the Qx polarized main Q band. Interestingly, each absorption band and the corresponding CP is also accompanied by a distinct ESA band, suggesting distinct transitions to the 2-quantum electronic manifold. The weak diagonal peaks (DP) corresponding to the vibronic shoulders in the absorption spectrum as well as weak upper CPs (CPU, seen in the V1 band) are overwhelmed by the ESA bands.

Note that shared ground state correlations between coherently coupled molecules predict ground state bleach (GSB) type upper and lower CPs between the Qx and Qy bands of the nanotube at T = 0 fs, where bleach of one transition affects the other. GSB-type peaks are expected to decay slowly. A weak CPU does indeed persist and is seen clearly in the integrated V1 area in Fig. 2b. In comparison, the dynamics in the CPL region, main band in the integrated V2 area in Fig. 2b, are dominated by the excited state emission (ESE) signal. This is evident from ~10× imbalanced CPL amplitude compared to CPU as early as T = 30 fs, its significant decay on a 238 fs timescale and rapid peak broadening in the CPL region which likely overwhelms any GSB type CPs. Significant broadening of these features, including the distinct ESA bands, within 250 fs supports the idea of strong intraband couplings causing ultrafast delocalization of initial excitation. Similar rapid broadening of 2D peak shapes has been observed in chlorosomes from green sulfur bacteria43 and attributed to rapid intraband electronic delocalization. The resulting ultrafast loss of frequency-frequency correlations is reported directly by the 2D lineshapes. It is interesting to note that compared to cyanine nanotubes, both systems, the porphyrin nanotubes studied here and chlorosomes studied previously43, display significantly faster electronic delocalization and peak broadening (compare Fig. 2 of current work and ref. 43 with Fig. 5 and Fig. 2 of ref. 13 and ref. 44, respectively).

The T kinetic fits are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7. In V1, the lower diagonal peak (DPL) undergoes a rapid decay along with a 2.9 nm blue shift within 2.5 ps. Selecting Qx − Qy coupled states through polarization control

Following our earlier works38,39, the transition dipole product for four light-matter interactions Rμ can be represented in a convenient notation. For example, an all x or y polarized transition dipole pathway, that is, only the transition dipole vector μ^x or μ^y is involved in all four light-matter interactions, can be denoted as ⟨xxxx⟩ and ⟨yyyy⟩, respectively, with a suppressed notation for the electric field polarizations. The all-parallel (PA) electric field polarization sequence is defined as (0°, 0°, 0°, 0°), and the isotropic or magic angle (MA) sequence as (54. 7°, 54. 7°, 0°, 0°). With these definitions, orientational average48 between the lab and molecular coordinates yields ⟨xxxx⟩PA = 1/5 and ⟨xxxx⟩PE = 1/15 for an all x polarized pathway. The same contributions also arise from an all y polarized pathway. When Qx − Qy coupled states are involved, that is, the states are admixtures of Qx and Qy electronic characters, the transition dipole involved at each light-matter interaction is a linear combination of μ^x and μ^y. In this case, Rμ involves non-linear signals from the isotropic terms— ⟨xxxx⟩ and ⟨yyyy⟩ terms, as well as from the anisotropic terms such as ⟨xyxy⟩, ⟨xxyy⟩, etc. Only the anisotropic terms are specific to Qx − Qy coupling. The P polarization sequence, defined as (0°, 0°, +60°, −60°) (Fig. 1c), eliminates the isotropic terms. This can be seen by representing the P sequence35,38,39 as (PA − 3PE)/4, from which it follows that ⟨xxxx⟩P=⟨yyyy⟩P=0, such that only the anisotropic terms are selectively probed. The P polarization sequence has been recently proposed by Zanni and co-workers35 in the context of isolating cross peaks in 2D-IR spectroscopy. In contrast, our simplified argument above, and a more detailed discussion in our earlier works38,39 in the context of isolating vibronically enhanced anisotropic quantum beats from spectator vibrations, shows that non-linear population and coherence signals arising from Qx − Qy coupled states need not arise exclusively in the CP region—depending on the first and last light-matter interaction, coupled Qx − Qy states can lead to signal pathways in the CP and DP regions of a 2D spectrum. See Section S4 of ref. 38 and Section III.A of ref. 39 for a more detailed discussion on this aspect and Fig. 3 which experimentally illustrates this point. Note that the application of P polarization sequence relies on the fact that physical rotation of the nanotubes will be significantly slower than the typical hundreds of picosecond timescale of physical rotation in isolated molecules in solution, and therefore the effects we see in the experiment arise from electronic reorientation instead. Below, we apply these ideas to investigate the overlapping vibrational and electronic bands of the porphyrin nanotube.

The polarization settings are described in detail in Supplementary Note 1.3. We first tested our polarization settings on a D2h symmetry pthalocyanine molecule (29H,31H-phthalocyanine, H2Pc), with non-degenerate Qx and Qy polarized transitions. Figure 3(left) shows the observed suppression of the DPL relative to CPL due to the elimination of isotropic ⟨xxxx⟩P type terms. The ratio of DPL/CPL changes from 2.9 to 0.1 from PA to P-2D case, indicating a 29 × suppression of the diagonal peak.

Figure 3 (right) contrasts the PA-2D and P-2D spectra of the porphyrin nanotube after T = 120 fs when the peak broadening overwhelms any 2D cross-peak amplitude in the CPL region. The SNR in the P-2D case, calculated using the time-domain data49 is 3.1, compared to 10.6 in the case of PA-2D. The PA-2D shows a strong diagonal peak at both 120 fs and 1 ps, which overwhelms the CPL region. In contrast, when mixed Qx − Qy states are selected in P-2D, partial suppression of the DPL region reveals distinct signal amplitudes in the CPL region near the Q band shoulder, as well as the DPL region. The DP:CP intensity ratio for the PA and P-2D cases was quantified by integrating the DPL and CPL areas and taking their ratio. The ratio of the DPL:CPL signal is 3.2 in PA versus 0.8 in P-2D, that is, ~4× suppression of the diagonal peak. Distinct DPL and CPL amplitudes obtained from the P sequence were confirmed across multiple nanotube sample preparations. By comparing the H2Pc system with porphyrin nanotubes, Fig. 3 illustrates the earlier point that when Qx − Qy states are coupled, the cross-peak selective P sequence will lead to surviving Qx − Qy amplitude in the DP as well as CP regions.

Taken together, the observation of ~10× imbalanced CPL at early T, its rapid broadening, and the surviving Qx − Qy amplitude in P-2D, together establishes the presence of coupled Qx − Qy states across the main Q band and its shoulders. However, this observation is not in line with the predictions27,50 from the existing vibronic exciton models for the porphyrin nanotube which predict almost no Qx − Qy coupling between them. Our initial simulations of porphyrin nanotube linear spectra were also consistent with these theoretical predictions. The question then arises as to what explains our experimental observations of states with mixed Qx − Qy electronic character? We will return to this question during the discussion of vibronic exciton model for the porphyrin nanotube.

Selection of mixed Qx − Qy states also suggests that one could simply do a polarization-controlled spectrally-resolved pump-probe experiment35 (P-SRPP) and still be able to selectively probe the mixed Qx − Qy states. Figure 4a compares the SRPP spectra recorded for the isotropic (MA) and Qx − Qy specific (P) experiments at T = 1 ps. The full SRPP datasets are shown in Supplementary Figs. 9, 10. The P-SRPP spectrum shows a ~3 nm redshift in the main GSB/ESE band and a reduced (increased) ESA contribution to the red (blue), but with a similar overall shape as MA-SRPP. Figure 4b compares the kinetics in the MA-SRPP and P-SRPP cases in the marked 10 nm GSB/ESE band. A markedly absent decay is seen in the P-SRPP trace where the signal primarily arises from the CPL region (Fig. 3). The observed lack of decay amplitude in the CPL region of the PE-2D spectra Fig. 2b is consistent with the lack of decay amplitude seen in the integrated GSB/ESE band. Supplementary Fig. 11 shows that when both the traces are normalized at longer T (200–220 ps), the signal evolution after ~30 ps is comparable between the MA and P schemes. Not surprisingly this timescale is in approximate agreement with the timescale of polarization anisotropy decay in chlorosomes reported by Struve and co-workers51.

Figure 4c, d presents the global analysis, the resulting decay associated spectra (DAS), and the comparison of early T dynamics between the MA and P-SRPP signals. As described in Supplementary Note 3, the MA signal has been fitted with a 5-exponential model with freely floated initial parameters. The resulting timescales of 238 fs, 1.34 ps, 12.94 ps, 122.23 ps and 3.37 ns are in close agreement with the reported timescales52. The P-SRPP data were then globally fitted with the same time constants that were obtained from the MA fits. The full datasets are presented in Supplementary Fig. 10. Interestingly, compared to MA-DAS, the P-DAS of the fastest two time constants shows a dispersive line shape in the GSB/ESE positive SRPP band. This striking change is easily visualized in Supplementary Fig. 13 by integrating the signal in a 10 nm bandwidth in the blue and red sides of the GSB/ESE band, marked as blue and red lines in Fig. 4c, d.

For the case of 238 fs P-DAS, the dispersive lineshape denotes energetic relaxation of the stimulated emission (SE) signal within the main Q band. Based on the separation between the positive and negative lobes of the dispersive P-DAS for the 238 fs time constant, we estimate the energetic separation to be ~16 nm or 315 cm−1. The separation is estimated from the 238 fs DAS by assuming two Gaussians with different amplitudes and positions. The analysis is shown in the Supplementary Fig. 12. The separation is less than the separation between the main Q band (709 nm) and the first vibronic shoulder(669 nm) (40 nm or 840 cm−1) and between the first (669 nm) and the second shoulder (637 nm) (32 nm or 750 cm−1). Overlaying the 238 fs DAS with the Q band absorption spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 13) suggests that both the states lie within the main Q band, with the acceptor level ~315 cm−1 below the main Q band. It should be emphasized again that the above contrast between the MA and P polarization experiments is made possible by selectively probing the mixed Qx − Qy pathways.

As shown in Fig. 4d, the slower time constant in the P-SRPP experiments also shows a dispersive DAS lineshape that is again distinct from the MA experiment and oppositely signed compared to the 238 fs DAS (with a blue rise and a red fall). The corresponding full P-SRPP data is shown in Supplementary Fig. 10 and suggests that the positive maxima of P-DAS is close to the emission peak (720 nm versus 717 nm, Supplementary Fig. 13). We therefore assign it to the decay of the ESE signal. The decay of the ESA band, which appears as a dip at 687 nm in the P-SRPP experiment is seen as a prominent negative lobe in the P-DAS in Fig. 4d (compare brown versus green curve in Fig. 4a). We therefore attribute the dispersive DAS lineshape of the 1.34 ps time constant to a decaying, blue-shifted ESA signal superimposed with a decaying red-shifted ESE signal. This decay likely corresponds to the decay of the population to the low oscillator strength, or ‘dark-like’ states53 below the main Q band which then contribute to a >3 ns long-lived ESA signal (Supplementary Fig. 9). The origin54 of these dark-like states is related to intensity borrowing by otherwise symmetry forbidden dark states near the band bottom and is discussed during the presentation of the vibronic exciton model for the nanotubes. The rapid decay of ESE signal to the dark-like states with a long-lived ESA signal is akin to that reported53 for the case of photosynthetic chlorosome nanotubes by Jansen and co-workers, where such states were proposed to play a functional role in the photosynthetic design by prolonging the excited state population lifetime. Our observations suggest that similar principles may likely be operative in the case of porphyrin nanotubes as well.

As we noted earlier, the CPL signal in Fig. 2 shows fast changes with T – ~ 10 × imbalanced CPL at 30 fs, its rapid broadening (Fig. 2) and population relaxation (Fig. 4). If CPL was solely due to a common ground state between the Qx − Qy transitions shared across several molecules in the nanotube, CPL and CPU are expected to be of equal strength, given by a product of ∣μQy∣2∣μQx∣2 transition dipoles. Rather, a ~10× stronger CPL implies that the first shoulder is a mixed Qx − Qy band that manifests as fast unresolved electronic relaxation between the first shoulder and the main Q band. This is illustrated in the GSB and ESE signal pathways (wavemixing diagrams) in Fig. 5b, both of which contribute as incoherent population signals at the CPL location. In case of the ESE pathway, a fast unresolved energy transfer process explains the imbalanced CPL amplitude at 30 fs. A slower 238 fs relaxation of the ESE signal within the main Q band, between the QxU − QxL levels separated by 315 cm−1, as estimated from the P-DAS in Fig. 4c is also shown. The picosecond decay of the ESE signal to the low oscillator strength dark states below the main Q band is shown as well.

Anisotropic out-of-plane vibrational quantum beats revealed by polarization control

In the context of unambiguously identifying promoter vibrational motions which mix electronic states and promote energy transfer versus spectator motions which do not, our recent works38,39 show that the P polarization sequence can also eliminate isotropic vibrational quantum beats arising from spectator motions and isolate the anisotropic vibrational quantum beats which uniquely report the presence of vibronically mixed Qx − Qy states. This is because the coupled Qx − Qy states lead to signal pathways such as ⟨xxxx⟩P and ⟨xyxy⟩P, where the former isotropic term leads to quantum beats that do not involve a change in electronic character and is eliminated, whereas anisotropic quantum beats from the latter term only arise in the presence of Qx − Qy vibronic coupling and should survive. Anisotropic vibrational quantum beats arise from non-totally symmetric vibrations, which tune the relative energy gaps and lead to vibronic coupling matrix elements that are akin to the Albrecht B-term55 in Raman scattering.

In the above context, it is interesting to note that a striking ~30× intensity enhancement for the low-frequency Raman active vibrations has been reported56 upon formation of nanotube aggregates (see the resonance Raman spectra for the 241 and 317 cm−1 peaks in Fig. 5 of ref. 56). Similar modes have been implicated57 in B − Q Herzberg-Teller coupling. Whether these modes promote Qx − Qy coupling is unequivocally reported by the presence (or absence) of anisotropic vibrational quantum beats in the P polarization sequence.

Figure 6a shows the quantum beat amplitude as a function of detection wavelength obtained by removing the incoherent population background from the PA-SRPP (left) and P-SRPP (right) spectra. Strong beating amplitudes are immediately seen at 236 cm−1 and 309 cm−1, as may be expected from the already known 30x enhancement56 of Raman intensities for these vibrations. A comparison of quantum beat spectra between the PA- and P-SRPP experiments (Fig. 6a, b) reveals some surprising insights. When only the transitions involving mixed Qx − Qy states are selected (P-SRPP), a remarkable suppression of the 236 cm−1 and 309 cm−1 beat is seen, immediately ruling out the role of these low-frequency ring deformation motions in Qx-Qy coupling. A quantum of excitation along the 317 cm−1 deformation mode is near-resonant with the estimated QxU − QxL energy gap (Fig. 5b). It may therefore be likely that the rapid QxU → QxL electronic relaxation is driven by low-frequency ring deformations which do not promote any Qx − Qy coupling.

Curiously, the P-SRPP data show two prominent quantum beats—432 cm−1 and 880 cm−1 that survive the P sequence. Figure 6b plots the integrated quantum beat amplitude for the PA and P experiments on the same scale. Both of the surviving quantum beats are obscured by the large amplitude lower frequency beats in the PA-SRPP case and appear as very weak peaks in the ground state Raman spectrum28 (Supplementary Fig. 14). Note that the elimination of the prominent 236 and 309 cm−1 quantum beats and the survival of 432 and 880 cm−1 beats was confirmed across multiple trials and shown as error bands in Fig. 6b. Both surviving quantum beats are known28 in the porphyrin nanotube and assigned to out-of-plane deformations of the pyrrole rings. Interestingly, both modes are not reported at higher pH where the porphyrin macrocycle is unprotonated, planar and of D2h symmetry (see Table 1 of ref. 28). Exactly similar trends—absent versus prominent vibrational modes are reported58,59 for unprotonated and protonated porphin macrocycles, respectively, around 440–480 cm−1 and 860–880 cm−1. It is well known60 that upon protonation, the porphyrin ring undergoes a D4h → D2d ruffling deformation. Such a deformation has been implicated58,59 in the activation of out-of-plane modes in protonated porphins observed in Raman58 and fluorescence line-narrowing59 and phosphorescence61 experiments. Resonance enhancement of out-of-plane vibrational modes upon excitation of out-of-plane electronic transitions has also been reported62 in strongly coupled μ-oxo porphyrin dimers through the presence of αzz component of Raman polarizability. The survival of anisotropic quantum beats from out-of-plane porphyrin deformation modes provides direct evidence for their participation in Qx − Qy coupling and complements the previous reports on the activation of out-of-plane modes upon aggregation28.

In order to decipher whether these surviving wavepackets are on the ground or an excited electronic state, Fig. 6c analyzes the wavemixing signal pathways at the CPL location that are most consistent with the observation of 880 cm−1 vibrational quantum beats. Recall that with the P polarization sequence only the ⟨xxyy⟩, ⟨xyxy⟩ etc. pathways survive38. The left panel describes a density matrix element ρg0,g1, that is, a ground state vibrational coherence along the 880 cm−1 surviving mode that proceeds through a mixed Qx − Qy state on the first Q band shoulder. The location of this signal pathway along the detection axis (length of the last interaction) is consistent with the appearance of this quantum beat under the main Q band. Figure 6c(right) considers a similar pathway for the ESE signal in which the pump pulses create an excited state vibronic coherence ρQxy0,Qxy1. Such a pathway necessarily depends on the survival of vibronic wavepackets during ultrafast energy transfer and their coherent downhill transfer within the Q band. Survival of vibronic wavepackets during electronic relaxation is unlikely for the following key reasons—1. Multilevel Redfield simulations of electronic curve crossing from Jean and Fleming63 illustrate that this is typical of spectator vibrational wavepackets64 because they do not sample the mixed vibronic (anharmonic) coordinates and therefore are not susceptible to rapid dephasing. In comparison, vibronic wavepackets sample anharmonic potentials to dephase rapidly. And 2. Vibronic coherences associated with wavepacket motions will dephase simply by ensemble averaging22 of the signal over a distribution of energy gaps. This is experimentally exemplified for the case of a photosynthetic antenna protein in cryogenic pump-probe and 2DES experiments. See Figs. 1, 2 of ref. 65 and Fig. 3 of ref. 66. Even if vibronic wavepackets were assumed to survive until picoseconds at 300 K, an ESE signal pathway for vibronic coherences under the secular Redfield approximation, which maintains the frequency of transferred coherences, will contribute at the very tail of the Q band (~880 cm−1 below the main Q band) making it inconsistent with the experimentally observed location under the main Q band maxima. Instead, Fig. 6c invokes a non-secular ESE pathway whose spectral position, set by the final light-matter interaction, is adjusted to match the experimental observations. Such a pathway, however, is strongly oscillatory—an initially prepared coherence ρQxy0,Qxy1 must be converted into a population ρQxy0,Qxy0. Given the rapid dephasing expected under ambient conditions, the survival of such non-secular coherence–population transfer at room temperature is highly unlikely and arguably unphysical. Based on the above arguments, we assign the surviving anisotropic quantum beats to ground state vibrational wavepackets corresponding to the out-of-plane porphyrin macrocycle deformations. Similar arguments are valid for the 432 cm−1 vibration as well which we also assign to the ground electronic state. Given the ~410 cm−1 FWHM of the main Q band, it is likely that the GSB pathway for the 432 cm−1 vibrational quantum beats proceeds through vibronically mixed Qx − Qy excited states that are within the main Q band. The vibronic exciton model for the porphyrin nanotubes confirms this argument.

It is quite interesting to note that a quantum of excitation along the 880 cm−1 surviving mode is quite close in resonance with the energy gap between the main Q band and the first shoulder that is estimated directly from the 2D spectra in Fig. 3. Jonas and co-workers have proposed21,29,30 a functional role for near-resonant vibrations that tune the donor-acceptor energy gap, in energy transfer through a non-adiabatic energy funnel. Our recent theoretical work predicts38,67 that such vibrations are anisotropic and should survive the P polarization sequence. Rapid electronic delocalization within the Q band (Fig. 2), mixed Qx − Qy states in the CPL region (Fig. 3), and prominent anisotropic vibrational quantum beats at near-resonant frequencies (Fig. 6), together provide the first direct experimental evidence for functional vibronic couplings in large disordered photosynthetic aggregates at room temperature.

Disorder enhances intraband vibronic couplings

The above arguments still leave a central question unanswered as to what causes the rapid intra-Q band electronic delocalization. We elaborate on this question below. Vibronic exciton models for cylindrically symmetric porphyrin nanotubes, including our own simulations discussed below, predict negligible Qx − Qy electronic mixing—the main band is dominantly Qx in character and the first shoulder is dominantly Qy in character, where Qx and Qy refer to the molecular frame electronic transition dipoles16 in porphyrins. Theoretical work on vibronic dimers29,30 shows that the vibronic coupling matrix elements for near-resonant vibrations scale as (q^A−q^B)ωdsin(2θd), where ω is the vibrational frequency, d is the FC displacement, and q^A,B are vibrational coordinate operators on the respective site. Here q^A−q^B is the energy gap tuning coordinate. θd is the electronic mixing angle between the sites, given by 12tan−1(2J/Δ), where J is the intersite Coulomb coupling and Δ is the site energy gap. Electronic coupling is a necessary pre-requisite for vibronic coupling to exist within the overlapping vibrational-electronic bands. Therefore, the absence of Qx − Qy electronic coupling precludes any role of Qx − Qy vibronic coupling in promoting electronic delocalization. We therefore decided to revisit the vibronic exciton models for the Q band of porphyrin nanotubes to understand whether the overlap between the dense vibrational-electronic bands of the aggregate is indeed inconsequential.

We model the porphyrin nanotube with a vibronic exciton Hamiltonian with an explicit quantum vibration as part of the system Hamiltonian to fully capture the near-resonant non-adiabatic mixing between vibrational and electronic states. Only nearest neighbor electronic interactions are assumed. To restrict the basis sets size in case of the nanotubes with total 5300 molecules, we also assume one-particle approximation (1PA) which underestimates68 resonant vibronic coupling effects by constraining ground state vibrational excitations to be only on the site of electronic excitations. The full details of the Hamiltonian and the experimentally constrained parameters are given in Supplementary Note 5. Briefly, analytical ultracentrifugation experiments69, and our own fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 16) and pump–probe measurements (Supplementary Fig. 15) show that the diacid monomer (Fig. 1a) does not exist as a monomer at pH 4. Instead, it should be a small aggregate (proposed69 as ‘ring’) of ~ 26 molecules. Thus, compared to previous works27 which assume the ‘ring’ aggregate to be the starting diacid monomer and float the nanotube structural parameters to obtain the best fit linear spectra, we adopt the following significantly more constrained approach. We first determine Huang-Rhys (HR) factors and transition dipole strengths by fitting the linear spectra for a known69H aggregate porphyrin dimer (Supplementary Fig. 1, 19). This is followed by floating the structural parameters to fit the experimentally obtained ‘ring’ absorption spectrum (Supplementary Figs. 1, 20). These include the tangential and axial angles α and β for Qx and Qy transition dipoles, respectively, as depicted in Fig. 7a. The net Qx − Qy angle is fixed at the experimentally determined17 angle of 75°. Having fixed the structural parameters, we then construct stacks of rings such that the nanotube diameter is experimentally constrained69 to be 160 Å with previously estimated27 center-to-center distance of 9.5 Å.

Figure 7b (top) shows the purely electronic spectrum of the nanotube where red (blue) denotes Qx (Qy) character of the main Q band and its first shoulder, respectively. No Qx − Qy coupling is evident from the electronic characters of the two bands. The nanotube energy bands and the resulting oscillator strengths are a competition between J and H type couplings shown in Fig. 7a. Unlike a J-aggregate where the oscillator strength is concentrated at the band bottom, the additional presence of H- type couplings implies that the oscillator strength is still concentrated towards the band bottom but not necessarily at the band bottom. In a simplified description, the resulting spectra can be understood as follows. In a purely electronic picture with no energetic disorder, the lowest band is Qx polarized along the nanotube axis. This is so because the Qx dipoles in the molecular frame, which have major projections along the nanotube axis and minor projections orthogonal to it, add as dominantly J-type aggregate and lead to oscillator strength concentration towards the band bottom, as expected from J-type aggregation. However, note that even in such a case, there will be symmetry-forbidden dark states at the band bottom below the main oscillator strength region. For example, for the ∣k=0⟩ exciton, where k is the wavevector, resulting from the minor projection of Qx dipoles on the cross-sectional plane, the resulting states are optically dark because dipole projections on the cross-sectional plane will interfere destructively. In the same fashion, the Qy dipoles with large projection on the cross-sectional plane add like H-aggregates, and polarized orthogonal to the nanotube axis with the oscillator strength concentrated towards the top of the band near the 1st shoulder of the Q band.

Single-particle studies on chlorosome nanotubes70 from green sulfur bacteria suggest that disorder in relative orientation and distance between the transition dipoles, transition energies, and the nanotube radius is quite typical and ensemble averaged energetic disorder can range up to ~200 cm−1 or even more (see Section S6 of ref. 70). Previous vibronic exciton models27 of porphyrin nanotubes have incorporated an energy dependent Gaussian linewidth of σ = 242–726 cm−1 to phenomenologically model energetic disorder within the Q band. Instead, we directly introduce energetic disorder in the electronic Hamiltonian (Supplementary Equation S5). The picture in Fig. 7(b) becomes dramatically different even when a nominal value of Gaussian energetic disorder (σ = 200 cm−1) is introduced while maintaining the Qx − Qy degeneracy on the individual sites. Electronic mixing is now seen across the entire Q band with oscillator strength weighted Qy electronic character of ~25% and ~58% in the two main higher and lower energy regions of oscillator strength, respectively. The electronic character is quantified using Supplementary Equation S7 and summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Introduction of energetic disorder breaks electronic symmetries, and the symmetry-forbidden dark states now attain weak oscillator strength through intensity borrowing. These dark-like band bottom states contribute as a red tail in the lowest Q band in Fig. 7(c). Emergence of weak oscillator strength states at the band bottom upon introduction of diagonal energetic disorder in symmetric aggregates was also reported in the early work54 from Wiersma and co-workers. For example, see Fig. 1a of ref. 54 where the net absorption lineshape is a statistical property of the disordered ensemble and no longer a convolution of homogeneous and inhomogeneous lineshapes. In the context of photosynthesis, a similar role for energetic disorder has also been reported71 by Scholes and Fleming to promote electronic energy transfer rates in the LH2 antenna by enhancing donor-acceptor spectral overlaps. However, note that the above models provide an adiabatic description22 of energy transfer without an explicit role of vibrations in the system Hamiltonian. While they recognize the role of disorder in breaking electronic symmetries, its key effect in enhancing vibronic couplings is not recognized. We next show that disorder seems to be the vital ingredient, not just because it breaks electronic symmetries between eigenstates and allows for Qx − Qy electronic mixing, but more importantly because it provides the prerequisite condition for intramolecular vibrations to contribute through vibronic coupling.

The next question is whether near-resonant vibronic coupling that is well understood22 in dimers, and now made possible in porphyrin nanotubes because of disorder-induced Qx − Qy electronic mixing, can survive in large disordered aggregates? Our previous work72 on vibronic coupling in aggregates larger than dimers suggested that constructive interference effects between vibronic couplings scale up with aggregate size with vibronic splittings (and resonance widths) scaling as N2. However, energetic disorder may be expected to suppress any such constructive interference30 effects in large aggregates. Figure 7c calculates the oscillator strengths for the vibronic Hamiltonian for different known vibrational frequencies of the nanotube aggregate. Since the electronic character is now significantly more complicated than a dimer, we use the idea that resonant couplings allow for quantum admixtures of ∣v=0⟩−∣v=1⟩,∣v=1⟩−∣v=2⟩, etc. states with each part of the mixture carrying a different electronic character. This allows us to ascertain the amount of Qx − Qy vibronic mixing by tracking the oscillator strength weighted vibrational character of any given state. The vibrational character is quantified using Supplementary Equation 8 and summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Figure 7c presents vibrational frequency dependent linear absorption spectra simulations. In the top panel in Fig. 7c (no disorder case), a prominent28 high frequency C-C/C-N in-plane stretching mode at ~1250 cm−1 produces only negligible vibronic mixing – compare the states with mixed characters from v = 0 states (yellow) and v = 1 states (maroon). This mixing progressively increases as we move to lower frequency modes but always remains localized within a small spectral range because the oscillator strength is also localized in a perfectly symmetric aggregate. Upon introduction of disorder (Fig. 7c, bottom panel), just like redistribution of oscillator strength across a broad spectral range in Fig. 7b, the spectral range of 0-1 vibronic coupling is also dramatically enhanced. For example, the 1250 cm−1 mode produces negligible vibronic mixing (3%) without or with disorder. In contrast, the out-of-plane mode frequency of 880 cm−1 produces 5% 0–1 mixing which enhances to 19% in the presence of disorder. Notice the increased tilt of the 0-1 boundary. The 0–1 mixing increases to 20% for the lower frequency 440 cm−1 vibration and enhanced to 55% in the presence of disorder, even within the main Q band. Range of resonance30 for a vibronic dimer roughly depends on ωd.sin(2θd). For typical values for a dimeric bacteriochlorophyll a (BChl a) pair in FMO, this range is estimated to be merely 29 cm−1—see Fig. 3 of ref. 30. The results in Fig. 7c demonstrate that 0–1 vibronic couplings not only survive in a large aggregate but are dramatically enhanced in their spectral range by energetic disorder. This is a non-trivial effect because competing couplings, such as mixed J- and H-type aggregation in the nanotubes, can also interfere destructively, for example, in non-Kasha aggregates proposed73 by Spano and co-workers. Note also that in the presence of energetic disorder the optically dark states red of the main Q band now contribute to the Q band tail by gaining some oscillator strength to become dark-like, as also reported53 recently by Jansen and co-workers in the case of chlorosomes. For example, see Fig. 5a of ref. 53 for the band bottom dark-like states in chlorosomes. It should also be emphasized that the disorder induced enhancement of vibronic coupling range suggested by the above models is expected to increase68 significantly when unrestricted vibrational basis sets (compared to 1PA), hot ground states and multiple vibrations30,38 are treated explicitly in the system Hamiltonian.

The enhanced vibronic mixing range for the lower frequency vibration explains how the prominent off-resonant 432 cm−1 anisotropic quantum beat can promote vibronic couplings even within the main Q band. The simulation results on the porphyrin nanotubes are consistent with the predictions from previous dimer models30,38 that energetic disorder can indeed enhance the width of resonant vibronic coupling. Note that we have also tested other aggregate geometries and the central conclusion about the role of disorder in promoting vibronic coupling is model agnostic (see Supplementary Note 5.5). It should also be noted that the porphyrin template, where Qy and Qx oscillator strengths are equal, provides an ideal model system where coupled Qx − Qy states could be isolated by leveraging polarization control. However, the essential idea of disorder-induced enhancement of intraband vibronic couplings is more general. For example, enhanced spectral range of 0–1 vibronically coupled states is seen even if Qy oscillator strength is made zero (Supplementary Fig. 22). It is important to point out that in the above model we have assumed that all modes can tune relative donor-acceptor energy gap and therefore equally participate in vibronic coupling, which need not be true for all vibrational motions. The survival of only specific out-of-plane anisotropic vibrational modes does indeed point to this possibility. In this regard, detailed structural characterization of porphyrin nanotubes with cryo-electron microscopy, similar to that conducted74 for cyanine nanotubes by Deshmukh et al., can provide the structural insights necessary for further refinement of the existing vibronic exciton models.

A striking enhancement of Qx − Qy vibronic coupling range upon introduction of energetic disorder in porphyrin nanotubes also suggests a similar functional role of disorder in chlorosome nanotubes. Single particle experiments and modeling70 of chlorosomes have focused on the main Q band of chlorosomes. Building upon this extensive work, our preliminary simulations presented in Supplementary Note 5.6, which start from fitting the BChl c monomer spectrum to Qy, as well as to the ~5x weaker and previously neglected70 Qx electronic state, suggests that significant Qx − Qy vibronic mixing indeed persists across the Q band shoulder of the chlorosomes. This motivates revisiting chlorosomes with polarization-controlled 2DES experiments and vibronic exciton models which fully incorporate the Qx state in order to correctly gauge the extent of Qx − Qy coupling in these systems. A recent polarization-controlled 2D study on LHC II, the major light-harvesting complex of plants and algae, at cryogenic temperatures indeed reports75 a vital functional role of mixed Qx − Qy vibronic states in harvesting green light. Note that the exciton coherence length in disordered aggregates such as chlorosomes70 and porphyrin nanotubes is theoretically estimated41 to be only ~49 molecules at room temperature, making the extent of electronic delocalization significantly smaller than the overall size of the aggregate and quite comparable to the widely studied photosynthetic antennas such as the LHC II and the LH2 antenna complexes.

Discussion

This is the first instance where previously hidden details of Qx − Qy vibronic coupling and fast intraband population relaxation within the Q band of porphyrin nanotubes are revealed by a combination of polarization-controlled 2DES and pump-probe spectroscopy. Our experimental and theoretical observations strongly suggest that intraband energy transfer across the overlapping vibrational-electronic bands of large disordered aggregates is driven by robust vibronic resonances whose widths are dramatically enhanced by energetic disorder. Spectroscopically, this phenomenon manifests as rapid loss of 2D frequency correlations and anisotropic vibrational quantum beats in the data. The rich dynamics arising from intraband Qx − Qy vibronic couplings is likely overwhelmed in the isotropic experiments by the stronger and more number of transitions between Qx − Qx and Qy − Qy manifolds. Wavelength dependent relaxation76, low-energy dark states53 and rapid electronic delocalization77 have all been reported for chlorosomes and similar observations in porphyrin nanotubes motivate artificial light-harvesting templates that are based on chlorophyll-like chromophores, which may provide the right parameter regime for mimicking photosynthetic design principles.

Our findings about the survival of resonant vibronic couplings in large chlorosome-like aggregates at room temperature also carry direct implications for the active debate6,7 on the functional role of vibronic coupling in driving photosynthetic energy transfer and complement the recent evidence32 for vibronic coherences in cyanobacterial protein supercomplexes from Beck and co-workers. Enhanced electronic delocalization78 resulting from near-resonant vibrational-electronic couplings in photosynthetic dimers has been reported at room temperature. The dual role of energetic disorder - first enabling electronic coupling, and then enhancing the energetic range of vibronic coupling across the entire Q band—is quite striking and suggests that disorder may be a vital design principle for energy transfer in large and disordered photosynthetic aggregates. As opposed to the localizing nature of disorder in quantum transport and non-radiative quenching of excitons79 by high-frequency vibrations, our observations suggest that vibronically enhanced quantum transport may be possible in a parameter regime where energetic disorder is of the order of dense low-frequency vibrations with weak reorganization energies. Disordered aggregates such as chlorosome nanotubes are predicted80 to operate in the more robust two-dimensional limit of quantum diffusion where a fine balance of electronic couplings, dephasing, and energetic disorder is crucial to attain optimal transport. The survival of resonant vibronic couplings at room temperature adds yet another ingredient and motivates refined quantum transport models with a fully quantum vibrational bath, using for instance, effective modes81 or a path-integral description3, to adequately capture electronic delocalization beyond the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.

Methods

Sample preparation

Here we briefly describe the procedure for the preparation of tubular aggregates of meso-tetra(4-sulfonatophenyl) porphyrin (TPPS). Further details about the purity of the isolated tubes and bundles are described in Supplementary Note 1.1. TPPS was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology as dihydrochloride salt. A dark green 200 μM TPPS stock solution is made in deionized water (DI), and the pH is adjusted to 3 by adding 1 N HCl. For nanotube preparation, this solution is then added to equal volume of 1.5 M HCl/DI solution in parts, which causes the color to shift from dark green to light green. The resulting 100 μM solution is left undisturbed in the dark for multiple days after which the 490 nm absorption peak was seen to not rise further. This sample exhibits a clear absorption spectrum without contamination from the ring peak at 434 nm. The sample has a peak Q band OD of 0.405 at 709 nm measured in a 500 μm pathlength cuvette.

Optical setup

The detailed schematics of the partially collinear white-light 2DES and PP setups are reported elsewhere49,82 and described in Supplementary Note 1.2. Briefly we conducted impulsive pump-probe (PP) and 2DES experiments with a 100 kHz shot-to-shot detection approach. The input to the pump and probe arms consists of a YAG generated white light continuum (WLC). The pump and probe spectra at the sample location are overlaid with the Q band of the porphyrin nanotube in Fig. 1b. In the 2DES experiment, the pair of phase-locked pump pulses are generated using a birefringent wedge based common path interferometer (CPI)83. A combination of group delay dispersion compensated chirped mirrors and glass wedges are used for fine tuning the optical dispersion. The pump pulse duration of ~ 10 fs, obtained after maximizing the two-photon interferometric autocorrelation generated by focusing into a SiC photodiode, is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2a,b and is sufficient to routinely excite vibrational coherences up to ~1450 cm−1. A global fit of the spectrally resolved pump-probe (SRPP) data across the probe WLC bandwidth shows an instrument response function (IRF) full width at half maximum (FWHM) of ~37 fs. The dispersion in signal rise time as a function of probe wavelength is only ~27 fs over ~ 200 nm bandwidth in the main signal region (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Field autocorrelation of the pump-pulse pair was routinely conducted to ensure the identical nature of the pump pulses – real and absolute value Fourier transformed spectra showed deviations of ~2%, thus minimizing phasing artifacts that can arise from non-identical pump pulses.

In the partially collinear pump–probe geometry adopted in our setup, the pump and probe polarizations can be controlled independently. The optical layout of the polarizers and waveplates and the resulting characterization of polarization purity is described in detail in Supplementary Note 1.3. At the sample location, the polarization extinction ratios for the pump and probe pulses are 403 and 3711, respectively. The pump extinction ratio in the P-SRPP experiment is 4498. The measured probe power ratios through the analyzer for a 120° relative angle (+60° polarizer and −60° analyzer) were the same as the theoretical expectation of 1/4 to within the error bar of the measurement. A 100 kHz shot-to-shot rapid scan approach49,82 has been implemented for data collection and described in Supplementary Note 1.4. The sample % transmission was confirmed to be linear across the range of pulse energies (Supplementary Fig. 3) used in the experiments.

In the PA-SRPP scans, spikes due to pump scatter are likely. Due to such spikes, we find that averaging in the time domain and then Fourier transforming leads to poor peak prominence in the frequency domain, especially for the low-amplitude quantum beats. Instead, we first Fourier transform individual residuals from all the T scans within a given trial, and then average the resulting spectra in the frequency domain. Note that the quantum beat spectra, derived from the Fourier transformation of the residual SRPP spectra, is obtained from interval 1 rapid scan up to T = 1.66 ps. For the quantum beat spectra in Fig. 6, the T resolution without zero-padding is ~20 cm−1. Further details of data collection and analysis are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Vibronic exciton model for simulations of linear spectra

Details of the vibronic exciton Hamiltonian and experimentally constrained model parameters for the H-dimer, ring and tube aggregate are provided in Supplementary Note 5. Our vibronic exciton Hamiltonian, which has been detailed in previous works38,68 in the context of vibronic exciton dimers, treats one vibration explicitly in the system Hamiltonian to allow for numerically exact treatment of vibronic coupling along that vibrational coordinate without any Born-Oppenheimer approximation for the aggregate. A fully quantum treatment is necessary68 to adequately describe near-resonant vibronic coupling effects such as resonance width, intensity borrowing and energy transfer rates. The effect of other prominent intramolecular Raman-active vibrations56 is modeled as Gaussian lineshapes with energy-independent FWHM. Such a necessity arises because the exact basis set size increases exponentially as N×(ne,vib)V×(ng,vib)V(N−1), where N is the number of electronic states – 2 per molecule in case of Qx, Qy states, ng(e),vib denotes the total number of vibrational quanta on the ground (excited) electronic states and V denotes the number of explicit vibrational modes in the system Hamiltonian. For larger aggregates such as rings (number of sites M=26 and N = 2M) we make the restricted 3-particle approximation (3PA, Supplementary Note 5.4). For nanotubes with total 5300 molecules (M = 100 stacked rings with 53 molecules/ring, Supplementary Note 5.5), the basis set is significantly larger, and therefore we have to make the one-particle approximation (1PA) where the vibrational excitation and electronic excitation are constrained to be on the same site, that is, ng,vib = 1. Similar to previous works, the simulation of the nanotube necessitates such approximations. Our previous work68 has shown that when it comes to describing near-resonant vibronic coupling effects in the optical spectra, 1PA works much better in J aggregates compared to H aggregates. Supplementary Fig. 17 compares the effect of 3PA versus 1PA on the ring aggregate where a noticeable change in oscillator strengths is seen within the first vibronic shoulder. The electronic couplings are estimated using the method of Knoester and co-workers27 by assuming extended dipoles with ±q charges separated by 5 Å. The electronic and vibrational characters for the nanotubes are estimated using Supplementary Equations S7–S8. The presence of vibronic coupling effects arising from low- or high-frequency vibrations is then ascertained by tracking the Qx − Qy electronic character and the 0-1 vibronic character in the absorption sticks within the Q band. Note that the constrained approach to fitting the linear spectra is significantly more constrained than previous approaches in that we do not attempt to obtain a dedicated27 model for the nanotubes by freely floating structural parameters such as the diameter, number of molecules spanning the circumference, and axial and tangential angles, etc. and in treatment of energetic disorder which is included directly into the system Hamiltonian.
