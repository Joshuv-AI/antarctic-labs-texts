Corneal biopsy as a primary diagnostic tool in microbial keratitis patients on poly-antimicrobials
Authors: Lavanya Gandepalli, Aafreen Bari, Prafulla K. Maharana, Seema Kashyap, Nishat Ahmed, Pranita Sahay, Dewang Angmo, Tanuj Dada, Namrata Sharma, Jeewan Titiyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:To evaluate the role of corneal biopsy in nonhealing keratitis patients on multiple antimicrobials.METHODS:In a randomized, prospective, interventional study conducted at a tertiary eye center in India, 44 patients with infectious keratitis were recruited from February 2022 to October 2023. The cases that were on ≥2 topical antimicrobial drugs belonging to 2 different classes for ≥1 week with no signs of healing and corneal ulcer size of ≥4 mm were included. They were randomized into two groups: the corneal scraping group and the corneal biopsy group. The primary outcome measure was culture positivity rate, while the secondary outcome measures were smear positivity rate and complication rates in both groups.RESULTS:The culture positivity rate of the corneal scraping group was 59.09% and the corneal biopsy group was 77.27% in cases of bacterial keratitis (P = 0.32). However, in cases of fungal keratitis, the culture positivity rate of the biopsy group was better than the scraping group (P = 0.012). The sensitivity and specificity of the Gram stain of the corneal scraping sample were found to be 55.56% and 92.31%, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity of the potassium hydroxide stain of the corneal scraping sample were found to be 50% and 94.4%, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity of histopathological samples obtained from biopsy to detect fungal elements were 66.6% and 90%, respectively. No complications were noted in both the groups during the procedure.CONCLUSION:In nonhealing keratitis with suspected fungal cause, corneal biopsy may be used as a primary diagnostic modality in a referral center.

INTRODUCTION

Microbial keratitis is defined as the proliferation of microorganisms and associated inflammation within the layers of the cornea and surrounding tissue.[1] Microbial keratitis poses a threat to vision, and accurate diagnosis with timely management is crucial to prevent complications. Common investigations to establish a microbiological diagnosis include microbiology of samples obtained from conjunctival swabs, corneal scraping, corneal biopsy, anterior chamber exudates through paracentesis, and vitreous biopsy. Confocal microscopy offers a noninvasive method of early provisional diagnosis of microbial keratitis. Newer techniques, such as immunohistochemistry, enzyme immunoassay, and molecular biology, are useful tools to aid in diagnosis and species identification.

A nonhealing corneal ulcer is defined as an ulcer showing no signs of improvement in terms of reduction in size of epithelial defect or infiltrates, even after 48 h for bacterial keratitis and 7 days for fungal keratitis after initiation of specific antimicrobial treatment.[2] Causes of non-resolving keratitis include many factors out of which wrong diagnosis is one of the most commonly observed factors. Other causes include patient factors like immunocompromised state and diabetes, organism factors which include virulent and drug-resistant microorganisms, treatment factors which include wrong diagnosis and treatment, along with poor patient compliance.[3] Thus, one should know the accurate diagnosis of the causative microorganism so that targeted therapy can be given for early restriction of infection, which can further improve the prognosis.

Accurate diagnosis in cases of microbial keratitis requires a good microbiological setup, which may not always be available. Thus, many times the patients are started on a cocktail of therapies, including antimicrobials from various classes. In most such cases, the smear report comes as negative. The concept of a drug holiday is that, during this period, the deeper organisms may repopulate the superficial layers of the cornea, and the subsequent corneal scraping would yield a positive microbiology report. However, a potential limitation of this approach is the risk of disease progression during the antibiotic-off period.[4]

A corneal biopsy is a useful diagnostic tool as the procedure involves obtaining tissue from a deeper plane. Conventionally, it is indicated in cases of nonhealing keratitis or chronic keratitis where ≥2 consecutive corneal scraping reports are negative. Since a corneal biopsy obtains tissue from a deeper plane, it could be used as a diagnostic tool for nonhealing corneal ulcers without the need for a drug holiday period, thus avoiding the potential risk of deterioration of keratitis during the antibiotic off period.

The current study was done to compare the usefulness of corneal biopsy as a primary diagnostic approach in cases of nonhealing keratitis compared to corneal scraping following a drug holiday period.

METHODS

A prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in North India. Cases of nonhealing keratitis, on two or more antimicrobials, were enrolled in the study. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance was obtained from the institute ethics committee (IECPG-54/27-1-22). The clinical trial was registered at The Clinical Trials Registry-India registration was done (CTRI/2022/10/046809).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the positivity rates of biopsy and scraping from a previous study by Kompa et al.[5] The study power was assumed to be at least 80% and the level of significance (type-I error) to be < 0.05. The estimated sample size was 40 cases. Considering the proportion of cases that would be excluded or would not follow-up to be 30%, 57 patients were screened. Forty-four patients met the inclusion criteria [Figure 1].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Case of infectious keratitis patients on more than or equal to two antimicrobials belonging to two different classes for more than or equal 1 week with no signs of healing were included in the study. The inclusion criteria included corneal ulcers with a size of more than or equal to four millimeters, age of more than 18 years, no prior microbiological report, and willing to give consent. The cases with healing keratitis, stromal thinning of more than 50%, and clinical suspicion of viral keratitis were excluded.

Randomization and blinding

The patients were randomized into two groups in a 1:1 format using computer-generated numbers. Group 1 included 22 patients who underwent corneal scraping and Group 2 included 22 patients who were allocated to the corneal biopsy group. It was an open-label study. The demographic details, including age, sex, history of trauma, any prior intervention, and history of systemic illness like diabetes mellitus, were documented for all cases.

A 24-h antimicrobial drug holiday was implemented prior to specimen collection for all cases of Group 1 to improve microbiological yield while minimizing the risk of clinical worsening. This duration was chosen as a balance between diagnostic benefit and patient safety. After explaining the procedure and taking consent, proparacaine 0.5% was instilled, and corneal scraping was done using 26G needle. Any surrounding debris was removed, and scraping was done from the base and leading edge of the ulcer. The material was transferred onto two glass slides for Gram’s stain and potassium hydroxide (KOH) mount. The smear was heat-fixed and analyzed subsequently. Material obtained from scraping was also transferred to culture media-nutrient broth and Sabouraud’s dextrose agar under aseptic conditions.

All cases in Group 2 underwent corneal biopsy in the minor operating room after explaining the procedure and obtaining consent. The patient was made to lie supine, and topical proparacaine 0.5% was used for anesthesia, some patients required peri-bulbar anesthesia. Under strict aseptic conditions, the patient’s eye was cleaned and draped, and the speculum was introduced carefully. Edges and base of the ulcer were examined properly, and mucus and debris were removed if any. The area for trephination was selected, preferably, away from the central visual axis. A round 3 mm sterile dermatological trephine was advanced into the superficial anterior corneal stroma, including both the clinically infected and the adjacent normal cornea. The base was undermined with a surgical crescent blade to complete a superficial lamellar keratectomy.

The sample obtained was divided into three parts and was sent for bacterial culture, fungal culture, and histopathological examination. For histopathological examination, the sample was transported in 10% formalin to ocular pathology, where gross examination was done, following which the sample was put in a tissue processing machine (Carousel tissue processor, MTP, SLEE medical GmbH, Germany), which has 10% formaldehyde, absolute alcohol 70%, 80%, 90%, acetone, and xylene, etc., Tissue was embedded with paraffin wax, and was trimmed, and transferred to albumin-coated slides and heat fixed. The slides were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin mounted by dibutylphthalate polystyrene xylene and observed. Silver methenamine stain was used to stain for fungus, periodic acid–Schiff, and Gram’s stain was used for bacteria.

Microbiological and histopathological evaluations were performed by investigators who were masked to the group allocation to minimize observer bias.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was bacterial and fungal culture positivity. The secondary outcome measures were stain positivity (Gram’s stain and KOH mount) and the rate of complications. The sensitivity and specificity of Gram and KOH stains were calculated using culture as the gold standard.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were entered in a Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, USA) and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS software version 24; Chicago, IL, USA). The continuous variables were presented with mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented with frequency and percentage. Appropriate parametric and nonparametric tests of significance of difference or correlation were applied to respective qualitative or quantitative parameters. Independent t-test and Chi-square test were used for statistical analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Both groups were similar in baseline characteristics in terms of mean age of presentation, gender distribution, mean duration of prior antibiotic therapy, and prevalence of diabetes [Table 1]. There was no significant difference in terms of mean area of ulcer (25.53 ± 8.32 mm2 in Group 1; 30.29 ± 7.45 mm2 in Group 2) and mean area of infiltrates (20.72 ± 8.08 mm2 in Group 1; 19.62 ± 8.09 mm2 in Group 2). However, the history of trauma was significant in Group 2 (50%) compared to Group 1 (36.6%) with a P = 0.02.

In Group 1, bacterial culture positivity rate was 40.9% (n = 9/22) and fungal culture positivity rate was 18.2% (n = 4/22). No micro-organisms were obtained in the remaining 9 of 22 samples. Among the nine culture-positive bacterial cases, Staphylococcus epidermidis accounted for 66.66% (n = 6/9), Streptococcus sp. for 22.22% (n = 2/9), and Staphylococcus aureus for 11.11% (n = 1/9). Out of the 4 culture-positive fungal cases, 25% (n = 1/4) were Fusarium sp., 25% (n = 1/4) were Aspergillus flavus, 25% (n = 1/4) were Cladosporium sp. and 25% (n = 1/4) were Aspergillus fumigatus.

In Group 2, bacterial culture positivity rate was 27% (n = 6/22) and fungal culture positivity rate was 54.5% (n = 12/22). No microorganisms were obtained in five samples, and one sample showed mixed infection. Among the six culture-positive bacterial samples, 66.66% (n = 4/6) were Pseudomonas sp., and 16.66% (n = 1/6) were S. epidermidis, and Streptococcus sp. each. Among the 12 culture-positive fungal samples, 50% (n = 6/12) were Fusarium sp., 16.66% (n = 2/12) were Cladosporium sp., and 8.3% (n = 1/12) were A. flavus, Candida sp., Curvularia sp., and Bipolaris sp. each. One biopsy sample exhibited mixed infection of Pseudomonas sp. with Fusarium sp.

When Group 1 and Group 2 were compared, there was no statistically significant difference in bacterial culture positivity rate (P = 0.34) [Table 2]. However, Group 2 showed significantly better fungal culture positivity rate than Group 1 (P = 0.02) [Table 3]. The total culture positivity rate of corneal biopsy was found to be 77.27% (n = 17/22), which was significantly higher than that of corneal scraping (59.09%; n = 13/22) (P = 0.2).

The histopathology report of biopsy samples identified mycotic elements in eight of 12 culture-positive fungal samples. Histopathological examination revealed fungal hyphae in one case, which had a negative fungal culture. The sensitivity and specificity of histopathology were found to be 66.65% and 90%, respectively, with a positive predictive value of 88.89% [Figure 2].

The Gram’s staining of the scraping sample was found to be positive in 27.27% (n = 6/22) and the KOH stain was positive in 13.63% (n = 3/22). The sensitivity and specificity of Gram’s staining were 55.56% and 92.31%, respectively, with positive and negative predictive values of 83.33% and 75%, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity of the KOH stain were 50% and 94.4%, respectively, with positive and negative predictive values of 66.6% and 89.4%, respectively.

Patients were monitored for procedure-related complications, including corneal perforation, progression of stromal thinning or scarring, and secondary infection. No complication was observed in either of the groups during the procedure. The cases that ultimately underwent keratoplasty were 63.63% (n = 14/22) in Group 1 and 54.54% (n = 12/22) in Group 2. However, the rate of keratoplasty between both the groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.539).

DISCUSSION

The choice of antibiotics prescribed in a case of infectious keratitis depends on the clinical judgment of the physician and microbiology reports. A targeted treatment helps in the early resolution of infiltrates and the healing process. In most cases of nonhealing keratitis, the patient is on multiple antimicrobials, and it is often difficult to isolate the causative organism by corneal scraping, even after a drug holiday period of 24–48 h. In this study, we explored whether corneal biopsy could be a useful tool in these cases as a primary diagnostic tool.

The mean age of presentation in our study was 50.41 ± 15.4 years in the scraping group and 56 ± 16.67 years in the biopsy group, which is comparable to the mean age in previous studies (51–61 years).[678] Gender distribution in both groups showed a higher incidence in male patients, which was consistent with the study by Alexandrakis et al.[6] The mean largest dimension of ulcer in this study was 5.62 ± 0.82 mm and 5.08 ± 1.1 mm in the biopsy and scraping groups, which is comparable to 5.1 ± 2.3 mm in the study by Younger et al.[8]

The corneal biopsy culture positivity rate was 77.77%, which is comparable to 82% (Alexandrakis et al.) and 83% (Kompa et al.).[56] However, it was found to be better than 39% and 42% reported by Robaei et al. and Younger et al., respectively.[78] The positivity rate of corneal scraping was found to be 59.09%, which is significantly higher than the previously reported 36% and 38% by Younger et al. and Kompa et al.[58] This could be explained by the drug holiday period of 12–24 h before corneal scraping, as followed in this study. While a short drug holiday was utilized, it is possible that longer withdrawal periods may further improve microbiological yield; however, this must be balanced against the potential risk of disease progression.

The overall culture positivity rate of 77.7% in the corneal biopsy group was higher than 59.09% in the corneal scraping group. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.2). Bacterial culture positivity rates were found to be similar and statistically nonsignificant between both the groups. However, the culture positivity rate of biopsy was found to be significant compared to scraping in fungal keratitis (54.4% and 18.2%, respectively, and with a P = 0.02). This could be because fungal organisms generally penetrate into the deeper layers of the cornea and it is unlikely to be missed when a corneal biopsy is done compared to scraping, which usually involves sampling from the superficial layers. However, in bacterial keratitis, the organisms are generally present in superficial layers, scraping can detect organisms as good as biopsy.

It is also important to note that a higher proportion of patients in the biopsy group had a history of trauma, which may have contributed to the increased fungal yield observed, as trauma is a well-recognized risk factor for fungal keratitis. This represents a potential confounding factor while interpreting the results. A subgroup analysis based on trauma history was not performed due to limited sample size, which may have restricted a more detailed assessment of its impact.

The sensitivity and specificity of Gram staining were found to be 55.56% and 92.31%, respectively, which is comparable to 45.25% and 92.75% as reported by Panda et al.[9] The sensitivity and specificity of KOH staining were found to be 50% and 94.4%, respectively, which is comparable to 61% and 99%, as reported by Sharma et al.[10]

The results of our study revealed that the most common bacterial organisms in Group 1 were S. epidermidis, while Pseudomonas species was predominant in Group 2. This finding aligns with the retrospective study conducted by Ahmed et al. to ascertain the spectrum of bacterial keratitis in North India, where the most prevalent organisms were identified as Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci, followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[11] Furthermore, our study indicated Fusarium species as the most common fungal organism in Group 2, consistent with previous findings reported by Tawade et al. and Satpathy et al.[1213] Both of these studies identified Aspergillus species and Fusarium species as the primary isolates in mycotic keratitis.

Our results are also consistent with a study conducted by Hudson et al., showing the most common bacterial organisms as S. aureus and Pseudomonas species, and the most common fungal organism as Fusarium.[14]

Patients were specifically monitored for complications such as corneal perforation, progression of stromal thinning or scarring, and secondary infection, none of which were observed in our cohort. Although corneal biopsy improved the culture positivity rate, no significant difference was found in the need for keratoplasty between the two groups. A total of 54.54% biopsy cases and 63.63% scraping cases underwent penetrating keratoplasty with a P = 0.539. The keratoplasty rate of the biopsy group was comparable to 47%, as in the study by Robaei et al.[7]

The histopathology report of biopsy samples identified mycotic elements in 8 out of 12 culture-positive fungal samples. The sensitivity and specificity of histopathology were found to be 66.65% and 90%, respectively. Histopathology identified a fungal pathogen in 1 sample, which had a negative fungal culture report. This could have been missed if the sample had been sent only for fungal culture. Thus, histopathological examination of corneal samples may be useful in cases where culture yields no fungus, and there is a strong suspicion based on clinical findings.

The results of our study suggest that corneal biopsy offers no significant advantage in microbiological yield in nonhealing keratitis patients suspected of bacterial keratitis. In suspected cases of fungal cause, corneal biopsy may be performed without waiting for a drug holiday period of 24–48 h. Corneal biopsy, by taking samples from deeper corneal layers, helps to identify organisms more accurately. However, biopsy, being an invasive modality, should be reserved for nonhealing cases who have already been started on poly–antimicrobial therapy, in which scraping samples generally yield negative results.

CONCLUSION

To conclude, corneal biopsy appears to offer an advantage in improving fungal detection, while the yield for bacterial pathogens remains comparable to corneal scraping. Samples obtained from a corneal biopsy can be sent for histopathological analysis, which yields results faster than fungal culture. Histopathology can also identify fungal pathogens, which could be missed in fungal culture.
