Automated Melanocytic Lesion Classification: Capsule Networks Trained With Synthetic Images Can Outperform Networks Trained With Real Images
Authors: Hayley Chai, Stephen Gilmore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground/ObjectivesConvolutional neural networks (CNNs) are known, due to inherent flaws in their design, to be subject to classification error. Many of these shortcomings in classification performance were addressed in 2017 with the introduction of capsule networks (CNs). The objective of this investigation is to determine the classification performance of a CN with respect to dermoscopic images of benign and atypical melanocytic lesions. We implement two kinds of training data for comparison: first, real images; and second, by utilising the CNs' autoencoder, a large number of synthetic images.Methods500 melanocytic lesion images (250 benign and 250 sufficiently atypical to warrant excision) were obtained from ISIC and PH2 Datasets. Images were randomly split equally for training and testing, and equally split between the classification categories. A CN was developed, trained and tested on these data. The CN was then trained on 3000 synthetic images (with an equal number of images in each classification category). The newly trained CN was then tested on the original test images.ResultsThe CN trained on the real images yielded a DOR (diagnostic odds ratio) of 51.0, a result which is comparable and, in many cases, superior to those reported for CNNs. The same CN trained on the synthetic images yielded a DOR of 71.3, a result which is significantly higher than the result obtained with real‐image training.ConclusionsSince the effectiveness of automated melanocytic lesion image classification is limited by the difficulty in obtaining large numbers of high‐quality training data, our results support the idea that training a classifier utilising synthetic images—where large numbers of quality images can be easily generated—is inherently advantageous. Given its advantages over a CNN, and trained on a very large number of synthetic images of melanocytic lesions, a CN classifier thus has the potential, only limited by computer resources, to yield previously unseen levels of generalisation performance.

Introduction

Although convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been the gold standard in machine learning for many years, there exist problems associated with their implementation [1]. Within the context of image recognition, these problems are as follows: (i) Lack of rotational invariance. Images rotated by arbitrary amounts can confuse CNNs and lead to false classifications. An example is shown in Figure 1A where the CNN may not recognise the object as a house. (ii) Lack of spatial integration. This problem is otherwise known as the Picasso problem. In Figure 1B the CNN may identify the image as that of a face, even though there is no face, only parts of faces distributed in a non‐coherent manner. (iii) Lack of transformation invariance. Figure 1C shows that a simple reflection of an image of a melanoma may yield an alternative and incorrect diagnosis [2]. (iv) Lack of recognition of hierarchical relations within images. Figure 2A–D shows the scaling relations between dermoscopic images of benign and dysplastic naevi, and melanoma, revealing the multi‐fractal nature of the latter [3]. CNNs may fail to identify such hierarchical scaling, resulting in unreliable classifications. (v) Lack of robustness to adversarial attack. Figure 2E,F demonstrates that a simple one‐pixel change to an image, unrecognisable to an observer, results in a melanoma—previously correctly classified as malignant—now classified as benign by the CNN [2]. (vi) Loss of image low‐level resolution. The max‐pooling layers of CNNs destroy image information, and in many cases, such information may be a defining component of an image.

Problems associated with classification tasks using CNNs. In (A), rotational invariance may fail yielding a false negative with respect to the identification of a house. In (B), the Picasso problem is apparent—here the CNN may yield a false positive where a face is identified where none exists. Finally, in (C) and previously shown [2] by one of us (SG), a CNN may fail to identify melanoma in a simply reflected image.

Further problems associated with CNNs. One of us (SG) has previously shown [3] that pigmented lesions may exhibit a hierarchy of complexity, from simple (A) to complex (B–D). In particular, melanoma can exhibit a multi‐fractal structure, demonstrated in (D) by the curved nature of its lacunarity vs. box size plot. CNNs do poorly in the recognition of hierarchical structure within images. CNNs are less robust to adversarial attack in comparison with CNs. One of us (SG) has previously shown [2] that a simple one‐pixel change to an image (E, F) can change a CNN classification from melanoma (correct, E) to non‐melanoma (F).

Capsule networks (CNs), first described in 2017 by Hinton et al. [4], were designed to address many of the shortcomings noted above. CNs thus overcome many of the limitations associated with CNNs, operating in a manner more akin to human pattern recognition. CNs are neural networks with an architecture that differs from traditional CNNs. Briefly, the major differences are: (i) There are no max‐pooling layers. As noted above, max‐pooling layers in CNNs result in low‐level information loss. (ii) Autoencoding. High‐fidelity image reconstruction ensures preservation of low‐level information. (iii) Vector representation of data. Instead of representing data as scalars (as per CNNs), CNs represent data as vectors and groups of vectors as capsules. The length and direction of each vector representation in a low‐level capsule code for the features of image sub‐parts: for example, edges, shapes, orientation and hue. (iv) Dynamic routing. Higher level capsules code for higher‐level features derived from lower‐level capsules via an agreement process. Further technical details are beyond the scope of this paper but may be found in the references herein [5, 6]. In the following we implement a CN in the classification task with respect to benign and atypical melanocytic lesions. In addition, by leveraging its utility as an autoencoder, we train a CN on a large number of synthetic images.

Methods and Results

We obtained images of melanocytic lesions from the publicly available repositories PH2 datasets [7] and ISIC [8]. One of us (SG) classified images into two numerically equal categories, each comprising 250 images: benign lesions and lesions sufficiently atypical to warrant excision. Note that in many of the images of lesions sufficiently atypical to warrant excision (including dysplastic naevi, atypical naevi and melanoma) histopathological diagnoses were available. For a discussion regarding the merits of this clinically motivated classification scheme see Gilmore et al. [9] We then randomly divided this dataset into 250 images for training and 250 images for testing, with the constraint that both categories of images were divided in equal proportion. All images were then cropped to a square, segmented and rescaled to a resolution of 256 x 256 pixels. A CN was developed with the architectural features noted above where separate RGB data (with respect to each image) was integrated in the same CN pipeline. Code was developed and implemented in Mathematica 12.3 (Wolfram, Illinois, USA) and run on a MacBook Pro M1 (Apple, California, USA) with 16GB of RAM.

Following training, we subjected the 250 test‐set images to the CN classifier, obtaining a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 51.0. The DOR is a single metric [10] that combines both sensitivities and specificities, and a value of 51.0 represents excellent diagnostic performance. We then developed an algorithm that is able to generate synthetic images of melanocytic lesions, utilising the output of the CN's autoencoder. An autoencoder is a network sub‐algorithm—with respect to the CN—that compresses image data into a vector of image information that is then fed into the CN output sub‐routine to re‐create the original image. As noted above, the architectural characteristics of the CN, combined with its associated trained parameters, allow for high‐fidelity image reconstruction.

To generate synthetic images, we first create a data matrix of size 250 x 14, which represents the compressed values of all training images. Compressed single image data has thus been reduced to 14 values. With respect to both the clinical sub‐type and each position in these 14‐length seed vectors we analyse the distribution of values. In all positions on the compressed vectors, either the Normal or Poisson distributions well‐fitted these data. Using a parametrisation of best fit associated with each of 14 distributions, we then generate random values for its corresponding position in the seed vector, substituting this new value. This is then done for one or more positions in the randomly generated vector. We thus generate alternative vectors that differ by one or more values from the seed vector. The new vector is then fed into the efferent part of the autoencoder, generating an image. Hence, some randomly generated synthetic images will be generated by vector values that differ by one position with respect to its seed vector, while others will have many values that differ. In principle, this process can be repeated an unlimited number of times, generating synthetic images that will all be different. In general, the randomly generated synthetic image can resemble its seed image or may not be recognisable with respect to its seed image. Surprisingly, in some cases the simple substitution of a new random value at one position of the seed vector can radically change the appearance of the synthetic image, while in other cases multiple substitutions only change the appearance of the synthetic image slightly. Examples of synthetic images used in this analysis. In (A) 50 benign lesions are shown, demonstrating a range of realistic morphologies. In (B) 50 dysplastic or atypical lesions are shown, once again demonstrating a wide variety of realistic morphologies.

The main caveat with respect to our method for generating synthetic images is that the seed images must exhibit a reasonable range of morphologic diversity such that the generated synthetic images capture a wide range of real‐life variation. Yet given the combinatorial nature of the synthetic image generating process, where novel morphologies can arise, it is clear that the number of seed images required will not need to be extraordinarily large. Although the synthetic images generated are automatically assigned a category (based on the category of the seed images), potential exists for misclassification. However, if large numbers of synthetic training images are generated, the size of these data will overwhelm any small number of misclassifications that may occur, thus eliminating the need for the synthetic image set to be meticulously curated. Note that all our images were segmented—it is currently an open question as to whether this should be a requirement for synthetic image generation.

For the purposes of re‐training the CN, we limit the reconstructions to 3000 synthetic images, with 1500 in each clinical category. Following training, we then subject the original 250 image test‐set to the CN, obtaining a DOR of 71.3. Note that the DOR when the CN is trained on the synthetic images is significantly higher than the DOR obtained when training the CN on real images.

Discussion

In dermatology, artificial intelligence shows much promise as a stand‐alone diagnostic tool or as an adjunct to diagnosis. Previous investigations have largely focused on the utility of CNNs in melanocytic lesion diagnosis. Sophisticated and successful algorithms include, for example, ResNet [11], AlexNet [12], DenseNet [13] and VGGNet [14]. In their investigations, Fink et al. [15] and Pham et al. [16] showed that CNNs can outperform dermatologists. Girdhar et al. [17] described a CNN, DenseNet‐II, which achieved high sensitivities and specificities with respect to melanoma diagnosis using the HAM10000 dataset. Although trained and tested on a relatively small number of images, our investigation with a CN classifier (trained on real images) yields a DOR of 51.0, a result comparable and in many cases superior to those reported with CNNs.

In Australia, where melanoma rates are among the highest in the world, the early detection of melanoma is considered critical in reducing both morbidity and mortality; hence, new diagnostic tools such as CNs—with their theoretical advantages over CNNs—are welcome. But do the potential advantages of CNs over CNNs confer better outcomes? Satapathy et al. [18] compared the classification performance between a CN and other state‐of‐the‐art CNNs (trained using the Ham10000 image dataset) and found that the CN achieved higher accuracy in detecting malignant lesions and had lower false positive rates. While CNNs generally need to be trained on a very large number of images in an attempt to overcome their limitations (noted in the Introduction), CNs require smaller datasets. For example, Mocanu et al. [19] performed a rigorous analysis comparing the classification performance of a CN against the Deep‐CAPTCHA CNN on CAPTCHA images. While Deep‐CAPTCHA outperformed the CN when the number of training samples was sufficiently large, the CN achieved equivalent diagnostic accuracy with a much smaller number of training samples. In addition, the CN outperformed Deep‐CAPTCHA in tasks where images were rotated. Finally, Perez et al. [6] proposed a novel neural network which combined convolutional blocks and a CN architecture in order to extract the richer abstract morphologic features found in melanoma (see Figure 2D). In the classification task, it was shown that their model significantly outperformed state‐of‐the‐art CNNs. Taken together, the evidence above, including our results, demonstrate that CNs show much promise in the evaluation and classification of melanocytic lesion images.

While we did not directly compare the performance of our CN with a CNN, a major focus of our investigation was to investigate the utility of training a classifier with synthetic images of melanocytic lesions, utilising the CN's autoencoder. We are motivated by two observations: first, the effectiveness of machine learning is limited by both the quality and quantity of its training data [20, 21]; and second, there is a lack of literature that reports on CN training and automated melanocytic lesion classification utilising synthetic images generated by a CN's autoencoder. Problems with training data can include the following: limited numbers of samples, poor quality images, lack of consistent photographic conditions (including, for example, white balance or exposure inconsistencies) or the presence of confounders (such as hair or significant actinic damage). Many datasets also require extensive pre‐processing before analysis, such as noise removal, brightness corrections, contrast enhancement and data augmentation. Finally, many datasets are biased: that is, the number of benign lesion images can overwhelm the number of their atypical or malignant counterparts.

Motivated by these aforementioned shortcomings, investigators have developed a neural network architecture known as a generative adversarial network (GAN). GANs generate synthetic images—including melanocytic lesion images—that can be used in machine learning training, and the results using this methodology have shown promise [22]. For example, Cho et al. [23] generated 5000 synthetic images of melanocytic lesions, the GAN5000 Data Set, from unstandardised online clinical photographs, and used these data to train the EfficientNet Lite CNN. It was shown that the CNN trained on synthetic images outperformed the same network trained on preexisting public datasets. Perez et al. [24] proposed a customised generative model using a GAN which has the ability to generate large high‐quality images of skin lesions, and used two networks (ResNet and DenseNet) to train the model. It was shown that the CNN which was trained on the synthetic images generated by the GAN outperformed five other CNNs trained with state‐of‐the‐art data augmentation techniques. Taken together, the evidence above, including our results—where we have also demonstrated superior classification performance with synthetic data (DOR 71.3 v 51.0)—highlights the potential of utilising synthetic data in training melanocytic lesion classifiers. Finally, note that while our results—utilising an autoencoder to generate synthetic images—are encouraging, directly comparing the classification performance of a CN or CNN utilising autoencoder versus GAN generated synthetic images of melanocytic lesions would represent an important advance in the field.

Conclusions

The advantages of implementing CN machine learning as described are due to its classification performance potential and its state‐of‐the‐art autoencoding capabilities. While the former suggests that CNs can play an important future role in automated decision‐making with respect to melanocytic lesion management, the latter facilitates the generation of realistic and morphologically diverse synthetic images—hundreds of thousands if required—which can then be used as training data. The evidence presented here gives impetus to the notion that the generalisation performance of any CN‐based melanocytic lesion classification model trained on a very large number of synthetic images—perhaps many orders of magnitude greater than the 3000 images used here—can improve without bound. Effective large‐scale machine learning is not then limited by the acquisition of large amounts of quality training data and their associated ethical constraints (the difficult parts) but by computing resources.
