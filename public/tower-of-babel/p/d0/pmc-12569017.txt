Estimating of contact area among carbon nanofibers in nanocomposites by the features of network, tunnel and interphase
Authors: Yasser Zare, Muhammad Naqvi, Kyong Yop Rhee, Soo-Jin Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA larger contact area (S) among the nanoparticles significantly enhances electron transfer within nanocomposites; however, S remains an indeterminate factor. In this paper, two innovative models for the conductivity of carbon nanofiber (CNF) polymer samples (PCNFs) are integrated to express S as a function of various parameters, including CNF concentration, waviness, percolation onset, CNF dimensions, interphase depth, network fraction, and tunneling diameter. Extensive experimental conductivity data substantiate the robustness of the proposed models. Moreover, the influence of each parameter on S is systematically analyzed to validate the developed equation. The highest contact area is achieved by the maximum CNF concentration, the thinnest and longest nanofibers, minimal waviness, the densest interphase, and the highest network fraction. Specifically, S value of 1400 nm2 is observed with a contact number of 200 and a percolation onset () of 0.002, whereas the contact area diminishes to zero at = 0.03. Thus, a greater number of contacts and a lower percolation onset are favorable to increase the contact area, while minimal contacts and a higher percolation onset result in the absence of contact area.Keywords: Carbon nanofiber (CNF), Polymer, Electrical conductivity, Contact area, Interphase networkSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Many types of carbon-based nanoparticles were studied in the earlier studies1–7. Among them, carbon nanofibers (CNFs) are highly graphitic, electrically conductive, mechanically rigid, and exhibit remarkable compatibility with a wide range of polymer matrices8–13. Additionally, CNFs possess unique surface characteristics that facilitate surface modifications, such as functionalization, enhancing their compatibility with polymer systems14–16. The key difference between carbon fiber (CF)-polymer composites and polymer CNF composites (PCNFs) is the size of the CNFs compared to CFs17–19. CF has several micrometers in diameter, while the diameter of CNFs does not exceed 200 nm. CNFs with nanometer size provide the strong interfacial bonding with the polymer matrices. Moreover, CNFs are distinguished by their high aspect ratio, attributed to their substantial length and minimal radius, which reduces the percolation onset, since the percolation onset inversely links on the particle’s aspect ratio20. CNFs demonstrate impressive electrical conductivity, approximately 104 S/m20, enabling even low concentrations of CNFs to form extensive and conductive networks within PCNFs, thereby significantly improving their electrical conductivity. The conductive and strong PCNFs can be used in aerospace, defense, sports, and automotive industries. PCNF also belongs to porous media, in which the theory of fractals is an effective method to study the transport mechanisms and network structure21–23.

Significant research has been devoted to PCNFs to develop multifunctional materials with superior performance attributes12,24–26. For instance, Ladani et al.25 observed that incorporating just 1.6 wt% of CNFs increased the electrical conductivity of epoxy system to 0.01 S/m. Similarly, Bhawal et al.26 reported a percolation threshold of 1.4 wt% in poly(ethylene methylacrylate)/CNF composites produced through melt blending, whereas solution mixing resulted in a higher percolation threshold of 1.8 wt%.

The formation of an interphase within nanocomposites arises from the nanofiller huge surface area and the strong interactions of the polymer matrix with the nanoparticles27–32. The key efficacy of the interphase in nanocomposites has been well-documented in earlier studies33–40. The interphase contributes to the CNF network, lowering the percolation threshold and facilitating charge transport within PCNFs20. Consequently, the interphase exerts a positive influence on the network and sample conductivity, though the percolation behavior of the interphase has been largely neglected in PCNF conductivity models.

Electron tunneling predominantly manages the nanocomposite electrical conductivity, as electrons can move thorough the small tunnels of adjacent nanoparticles41–48. The tunneling properties are critical to determine the conductivity, given that electron tunneling is the primary mechanism for charge transport in PCNFs. Despite this, the role of tunneling properties in PCNFs has been inadequately explored. Tunneling resistance is managed by factors like tunneling distance, contact zone, and polymer tunneling resistivity20. Among these, the contact area (S) plays a particularly crucial role, as a smaller contact area limits interactions between nanoparticles, thereby reducing electron transferring46. Although contact area is effective on the charge transport through tunnels, it remains an ambiguous and indeterminate parameter. In other words, the factors influencing S are not clearly defined, limiting the optimization of tunneling resistance necessary for maximizing the conductivity in PCNFs.

The innovation of this paper lies in its comprehensive modeling approach that integrates multiple factors influencing the contact area (S) in polymer carbon nanofiber composites (PCNFs). Unlike the preceding studies that frequently ignored the combined effects of tunneling mechanism, interphase characteristics, and CNF properties, this work formulates an equation that accounts for CNF concentration, waviness, percolation onset, CNF size, interphase depth, network fraction, and tunneling diameter to predict S. The developed models for PCNF conductivity are validated against extensive experimental data. Moreover, this article introduces a detailed analysis of how each parameter, particularly tunneling diameter and interphase depth, governs the contact area. This analysis provides new insights into optimizing material performance for enhanced electrical conductivity for many applications such as sensors, energy devices, actuators, and electronic components12,13,49.

Scrutiny of contact area (S)

The influence of several factors on the contact area S is analyzed using Eqs. 11, 21, and 22. Three- and two-dimensional designs explain the impacts of two parameters on S, though others remain constant. The average and fixed values are: ϕf = 0.02, R = 60 nm, t = 20 nm, l = 50 μm, λ = 5 nm, u = 1.3, m = 100 and d = 20 nm.

Figure 3 shows the relationship between S and both the amount and length of CNFs. The maximum S of 4000 nm² is observed at ϕf = 0.04 and l = 100 μm, whereas ϕf = 0.01 and l = 10 μm yield S = 0. Accordingly, larger quantities of longer CNFs lead to a greater contact area, while smaller amounts of shorter nanofibers fail to produce a significant contact area. Both CNF amount and length directly govern S.

Dependence of contact area on CNF amount and length, using (a) 3D and (b) contour representations.

An increase in CNF quantity reduces the spacing between nanoparticles, creating more contact zones. This implies that a higher CNF concentration results in a larger contact area in PCNFs. In contrast, fewer CNFs increase the distance between fibers, decreasing the contact area. A smaller amount of CNFs results in larger tunnels with reduced contact area, confirming that CNF quantity directly influences S, as predicted by the developed model.

Larger CNFs reduce the distance between fibers, thereby increasing the contact area. In particular, longer CNFs cluster together, increasing both the number and area of contacts. On the other hand, shorter CNFs generate greater distances between fibers, leading to a smaller contact area. As predicted by the model, shorter CNFs reduce the number of contacts and diminish S.

Figure 4 exemplifies the impressions of CNF radius and waviness parameter on S. The maximum S of 1800 nm² occurs at R = 40 nm and u = 1 (straight CNFs), whereas R > 90 nm and u > 1.3 (l > 1.3leff) reduce S to 0. Thin CNFs with minimal waviness provide the large contact area, while thicker and more wavy CNFs lead to no contact area. These results reveal that both CNF radius and waviness inversely affect S.

Contact area predictions by CNF radius and waviness factor, using (a) 3-D and (b) 2-D diagrams.

Thinner CNFs result in a lower percolation onset and a larger interphase, as shown in Eqs. 14 and 15, which contribute to the development of a grander network within the system (Eq. 17). Consequently, thinner CNFs expand the interphase and network, leading to an increased contact area, as networked CNFs possess a greater number of contacts. Conversely, denser CNFs elevate the percolation inception and condense the interphase, thereby weakening the contact area. As a result, thicker CNFs create more space between nanofibers, which diminishes the contact area. This observation confirms that Eq. 21 appropriately links S to CNF radius.

Wavy CNFs are spaced farther apart because the waviness reduces their effective length, increasing the distance between them. As a result, wavy CNFs exhibit greater distances, reducing the contact area. In contrast, straighter CNFs shorten the space between fibers, thus enlarging the contact area. Straighter CNFs create more contacts, leading to a larger contact area. Therefore, Eq. 21 reasonably expresses the direct relationship between S and waviness factor.

Figure 5 illustrates the contact area at various levels of contact diameter and interphase depth. The maximum S of 4500 nm² is shown at d = 50 nm and t = 40 nm, while S is minimized to zero at t < 10 nm. Larger contact diameters and thicker interphases produce the larger contact area, while smaller contact diameters and thinner interphases fail to generate substantial contact area. Thus, both contact diameter and interphase depth directly govern the contact area.

Influence of contact diameter and interphase size on the contact area, presented in (a) 3-D and (b) 2-D plots.

It is clear that contact width directly influences the contact area. A greater contact diameter increases S, while a smaller diameter reduces it. Therefore, a direct relationship exists between contact diameter and contact area, confirming the validity of Eq. 11. A small contact diameter decreases the contacts among the nanofibers reducing the charge transferring. The CNF alignment plays a main role in the contact diameter, since the nearby and aligned nanofibers produce the maximum contact area in the samples.

A thicker interphase reduces the space between CNFs, because the interphase covers the nanofibers thereby reducing their distance and enlarging the contact area. A thicker interphase around the CNFs fosters more contacts, enhancing the contact area. In contrast, a thinner interphase increases the distance between CNFs, thereby reducing the contact area and charge transferring. Consequently, Eq. 21 accurately captures the relationship between contact area and interphase depth.

Figure 6 reveals the relationship between S and both contact number and percolation onset. The maximum S of 1400 nm² is achieved at m = 200 and ϕp = 0.002, while S diminishes to zero at ϕp = 0.03. A larger number of contacts and a lower percolation onset result in a greater contact area, whereas a smaller number of contacts and a higher percolation onset fail to produce any contact area. Therefore, increasing contact numbers and reducing percolation onset are crucial for achieving larger contact areas in PCNFs.

Correlation between contact area, contact number, and percolation onset, depicted in (a) 3D and (b) 2D images.

The contact number is directly related to the contact area, because a higher number of contacts produce a larger contact area. In contrast, a lower number of contacts reflect a greater distance between CNFs, leading to a smaller contact area. Accordingly, the direct link between contact number and contact area validates the accuracy of the developed model.

A lower percolation onset signifies a denser CNF network, resulting in more contacts. Conversely, a higher percolation onset indicates greater spacing between CNFs, as it corresponds to the minimum volume portion of CNFs compulsory to found a network63,64. A higher percolation onset leads to increased distances between CNFs, reducing the contact area. Therefore, the inverse relationship between percolation onset and contact area confirms the validity of Eq. 22.

Figure 7 exposes the extents of contact area at several levels of network fraction and polymer tunneling resistivity. f = 0.5 maximizes the S to 1700 nm2, but f = 0.1 produces the minimum S = 340 nm2. Higher network fraction enhances the contact area, while less network portion produces the shorter contact area. This means that the network fraction directly manages the contact area, but polymer tunnel resistivity cannot govern the contact area. In fact, numerous values of polymer tunneling resistivity do not change the contact area in PCNFs.

Dependence of contact area on the network fraction and polymer tunneling resistivity, using (a) 3-D and (b) contour plots.

A higher network fraction enables CNFs to form more extensive networks, leading to a larger contact area. Conversely, a lower network fraction results in more dispersed CNFs, reducing the contact area. The larger distances between dispersed CNFs reduce the number of contacts compared to networked CNFs. Consequently, the network fraction directly influences the contact area, supporting the validity of the developed model.

Polymer tunneling resistivity has no impact on the contact area, because it depends on the properties of the insulating polymer layer within the tunneling region. While polymer tunneling resistivity relates to the characteristics of the polymer film inside the tunnels, the contact area is determined by the properties of the CNFs surrounding the tunnels. Since Eq. 21 does not include polymer tunneling resistivity, ρ does not control the contact area. Therefore, the contact area is independent of polymer tunneling resistivity, further supporting the validity of the developed model.

Conclusions

The contact area (S) was formulated based on CNF concentration, waviness factor, percolation onset, CNF size, interphase depth, network fraction, and tunneling diameter. Additionally, the influences of all these factors on S were analyzed, confirming the accuracy of the proposed equation. A maximum S of 4000 nm² was achieved at ϕf = 0.04 and l = 100 μm, while ϕf = 0.01 and l = 10 μm resulted in S = 0. Accordingly, the largest contact area is provided by the highest concentration of the longest CNFs. Moreover, the largest S of 1800 nm² was observed at R = 40 nm and u = 1 (straight CNFs), while R > 90 nm and u > 1.3 reduced S to 0. The thinnest and straight CNFs afford the largest contact area, whereas the thickest and most wavy CNFs cause no contact area. The maximum S of 4500 nm² was recorded at d = 50 nm and t = 40 nm, while S dropped to 0 when t < 10 nm. A larger contact diameter and thicker interphase yield the greater contact area, whereas minimal values of these parameters result in no contact area. Similarly, the highest S of 1400 nm² was yielded at m = 200 and ϕp = 0.002, while S reduced to zero at ϕp = 0.03. An upsurge in contact number and a reduction in percolation onset enhance the contact area, while a reduction in contact number and an increase in percolation onset eliminate the contact area. When f = 0.5, S reached a maximum of 1700 nm², but at f = 0.1, S was minimized to 340 nm². A higher network fraction increases the contact area, whereas polymer tunneling resistivity has no impact on S. Among all factors, tunneling diameter and interphase depth were identified as the most significant influence on the contact area. The outputs from the developed equation can develop the design and optimization of polymer nanocomposites for specific applications requiring high electrical conductivity such as biosensors, energy devices and actuators. Future work may use the present formulation for the optimization of charge transferring through tunneling region in the PCNFs. This challenge is valuable to improve the performance of conductivity PCNFs for special applications in the industries. Also, this equation may be developed for other nanocomposites containing carbon nanotubes or graphene nanosheets.
