Unveiling the Electrochemical Reversibility of Multielectron Redox in s‐Tetrazine Derivatives for Large‐Capacity Electrode Materials
Authors: Seunghwan Min, Dong Hyeon Jo, Hye Ryung Byon, Ji Eon Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (s‐tetrazine) is a promising redox center for organic electrode materials due to its low molecular weight and ability to undergo two‐electron redox reactions. However, in practice, the irreversibility of the second‐electron transfer has often limited the specific capacities of the s‐tetrazine‐based electrodes in metal‐organic cells. Herein, electrochemically reversible two‐electron‐transfer processes from the s‐tetrazine core are demonstrated by introducing electron‐withdrawing pyridine rings at 3‐ and 6‐positions. Comparative analysis of three s‐tetrazine derivatives, 3,6‐diphenyl‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (Ph‐Tz), 3,6‐di(4‐pyridinyl)‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (4‐Py‐Tz), and 3,6‐di(2‐pyridinyl)‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (2‐Py‐Tz) reveals that both electron‐withdrawing effect and Li‐ion coordination play critical roles in redox reversibility. Among them, 2‐Py‐Tz exhibits the highest electron‐transfer rate with Li+ diffusion coefficient attributed to the ortho‐positioned pyridine nitrogen atoms, which strongly stabilize the dianion state through synergistic electron‐withdrawing and Li‐ion locking effects.Keywords: lithium‐ion batteries, multielectron redox, organic electrodes, s‐tetrazines

Introduction

Recently, mobile electronic devices and electric vehicles have become an integral part of everyday life, making portable energy storage devices essential. Lithium‐ion batteries relying on transition metal‐based electrode materials predominate the current energy storage market, as they provide high energy density and cycle stability.[ 1 ] However, concerns about resource scarcity and limited rate performance in these inorganic electrode materials have encouraged the search for alternatives.[ 2 ]

As a promising alternative, redox‐active organic molecules (ROMs) are appealing because they are abundant, cost‐effective, and structurally tunable.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] Over the past few decades, numerous ROMs have been explored for use as battery electrodes, including carbonyls,[ 6 ] imines,[ 7 ] and nitroxide radicals.[ 8 ] Still, organic electrode materials often suffer from rapid capacity decay, poor electrical conductivity, and low practical specific capacity.[ 9 ]

However, introducing multiple charges within a small molecular structure often destabilizes it, promoting side reactions and decomposition.[ 11 ] Therefore, developing stable ROMs capable of multielectron redox is vital for large‐capacity organic electrodes.

Previously, we reported 1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (s‐tetrazine) as a promising redox center for high‐performing electrode materials in Li cells.[ 12 ] s‐tetrazine consists of four sp2‐hybridized nitrogen atoms in a six‐membered aromatic ring, featuring the most electron deficient character among the nitrogen‐rich heterocycles (Figure S1, Supporting Information).[ 13 ] This significant electron deficiency helps stabilize the negative charges formed upon reduction, making its first‐electron redox reaction electrochemically quasireversible and chemically reversible even within the small molecular structure.[ 14 ] However, the second‐electron reduction of s‐tetrazine is known to be irreversible, because the resulting dianionic state is highly nucleophilic, reacting with a trace proton to form a hydrotetrazine anion (TzH−). The sluggish kinetics of its reoxidation back to the radical monoanion hinder utilizing the second‐electron redox of s‐tetrazine in batteries (Scheme 1a). Thus, most prior works explored only its single‐electron‐transfer process in nonaqueous metal‐ion cells, capping the theoretical capacity below 120 mAh g−1.[ 12 , 15 , 16 ]

But, very recently, Walter et al. examined 3,6‐di(phenyl)‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (Ph‐Tz) as an electrode for aqueous Zn‐ion batteries.[ 17 ] They found that coinsertion of Zn(OTf)+ and H+ into Ph‐Tz upon reduction enabled a single‐step, two‐electron redox reaction of Ph‐Tz in the aqueous electrolyte. However, since this approach is only applicable in a specific aqueous electrolyte, tailored molecular designs that can facilitate multielectron transfer in nonaqueous metal‐ion batteries are required.

While this manuscript was in preparation, Xiao et al. reported an s‐tetrazine‐based electrode material capable of multielectron transfer in Li‐ion cells.[ 18 ] In their work, an s‐tetrazine core was linked to two peripheral 2,2′‐bipyrimidine rings, whose strong electron‐withdrawing nature stabilized the negative charges formed upon multielectron reductions. Although the importance of π‐conjugation for achieving multielectron redox was emphasized, the potential role of intramolecular locking through Li‐ion coordination with the peripheral rings was overlooked.

In this study, we investigate how Li‐ion coordination affects the electrochemical reversibility of multielectron redox in s‐tetrazine. To this end, we designed two s‐tetrazine derivatives, 3,6‐di(2‐pyridinyl)‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (2‐Py‐Tz) and 3,6‐di(4‐pyridinyl)‐1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (4‐Py‐Tz), which bear electron‐withdrawing pyridine rings at the 3‐ and 6‐positions of a s‐tetrazine core (Scheme 1b). Note that the pyridine rings in the two derivatives have a different N‐position to the s‐tetrazine core. Interestingly, despite the enhanced delocalization of negative charges through π‐conjugation, 4‐Py‐Tz exhibited an electrochemically irreversible second‐electron‐transfer in Li‐ion cells. In contrast, 2‐Py‐Tz underwent a quasireversible second‐electron reduction, facilitated by the intramolecular locking through Li‐ion coordination. To elucidate their difference in electrochemical reversibility, we performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, followed by various spectroscopic analyses, including electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

Results and Discussion

Molecular Design and Synthesis

We hypothesized that the irreversibility of second‐electron redox in s‐tetrazine arises from the instability of its dianion state. In 2022, Kong et al. reported a strategy enabling two‐electron redox in a p‐type organic material pyrene.[ 19 ] In that work, substituted electron‐donating groups not only favored the electron removal from the pyrene redox center but also reduced the positive charge density in the oxidized molecules, facilitating two‐electron transfer of pyrene. Inspired by this approach, we introduced electron‐withdrawing groups (EWGs) into s‐tetrazine to stabilize its dianion state after n‐type redox via charge delocalization. It is also anticipated that the EWGs would lower the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level, increasing the tendency to be reduced as well as elevating the discharge voltage.[ 15 , 20 ] We chose pyridine for the EWG substitution because of its planarity, aromaticity, and structural similarity to the phenyl ring (i.e., shape, size, and molecular weight) as well as strong electron‐withdrawing ability.

As shown in Scheme 1b, the peripheral pyridine rings can be substituted to have different regio‐isomeric N‐positions to the s‐tetrazine core (i.e., para‐ and ortho‐position for 4‐Py‐Tz and 2‐Py‐Tz, respectively). The substituted pyridine rings are expected to pull the electrons from the s‐tetrazine core via the inductive effect of their sp2‐hybridized N atoms. In addition, 4‐Py‐Tz and 2‐Py‐Tz can form a resonance structure after two‐electron reductions, further stabilizing the generated negative charges via mesomeric effect (Scheme S1, Supporting Information).

DFT calculations predict the energy levels of these s‐tetrazine derivatives. As expected, both 2‐Py‐Tz (ELUMO = −3.17 eV) and 4‐Py‐Tz (ELUMO = −3.42 eV) showed more stabilized LUMO energy levels than Ph‐Tz (ELUMO = −3.10 eV) due to the EWG effect of the pyridines (Scheme 1c). Interestingly, the LUMO level of 4‐Py‐Tz was found to be significantly lower than those of 2‐Py‐Tz, although the pyridine nitrogens in 4‐Py‐Tz are located farther from the s‐tetrazine core, implying a weaker inductive EWG effect. This is attributed to the destabilization caused by repulsion between the lone–pair electrons on the pyridine nitrogens and the tetrazine core in 2‐Py‐Tz.

In the optimized geometries, Ph‐Tz and 4‐Py‐Tz have coplanar structures, while 2‐Py‐Tz has a slightly twisted structure with a dihedral angle of 16.5° between the peripheral pyridines and the tetrazine core, due to the repulsive interaction. These computationally generated geometries are consistent with the reported crystal structures,[ 21 ] although 2‐Py‐Tz had slightly larger dihedral angles (≈19.6°) in crystallography (Figure S2, Supporting Information).

The designed s‐tetrazine derivatives were synthesized through the Pinner method. We used corresponding 4‐substituted benzonitrile as a starting material for each s‐tetrazine derivative (Scheme S2, Supporting Information),[ 22 ] and the target molecules were prepared after simple two‐step synthetic processes, respectively, which were characterized by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopies (Figure S3–S5, Supporting Information). Details of the synthetic procedures are found in the Supporting Information.

Electrochemical Properties

CV was examined to identify and compare redox events of the three s‐tetrazines relying on their substituents. All these species, Ph‐Tz, 4‐Py‐Tz, and 2‐Py‐Tz, showed two pairs of redox peaks in a 2 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) of 1,3‐dioxolane/1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME) (1/1, v/v) electrolyte solution (Figure 1a‐c). For Ph‐Tz, half‐wave potentials (E1/2) were 2.27 and 1.73 V (vs. Li/Li+) for the first ( c 1 and a 1 for cathodic and anodic peak, respectively) and second ( c 2 and a 2 ) electron‐transfer processes (Figure 1a), undergoing Ph‐Tz/Ph‐Tz •− and Ph‐Tz •− /Ph‐Tz 2− reaction, respectively. In addition, the peak‐to‐peak separations (ΔE pp) between the cathodic and anodic waves were 170 and 535 mV for each redox couple, respectively (Table S1, Supporting Information). The significantly large ΔE pp for the second redox process ( c 2 and a 2 ) indicates a very sluggish reaction in the presence of the phenyl rings.[ 23 , 24 , 25 ]

The electrochemical properties of the tetrazine derivatives. CV profiles for a) Ph‐Tz, b) 4‐Py‐Tz, and c) 2‐Py‐Tz. The scan rate for the CV measurements was 1 mV s−1. The arrows indicate the scan direction. d) Heterogeneous electron‐transfer constant (k 0, bar graph) and ΔE pp (filled circles) for the redox processes of Ph‐Tz (black), 4‐Py‐Tz (orange), and 2‐Py‐Tz (green).

In comparison, 4‐Py‐Tz exhibited E1/2 at 2.45 and 1.69 V (Figure 1b). Its first redox peak shifted more positively compared to that of Ph‐Tz, attributed to the EWG effect of 4‐pyridine rings. However, ΔE pp for its first redox process was wider (306 mV) than Ph‐Tz, reflecting the slow formation of 4‐Py‐Tz •− radical, while ΔE pp for its second redox process narrowed to 280 mV. Interestingly, the second redox process had a lower current density (J) than the first one, suggesting a partial conversion of 4‐Py‐Tz •− to 4‐Py‐Tz 2− dianion. Therefore, the slowest first redox process and the low J for the second redox events are challenging for 4‐Py‐Tz.

In contrast, 2‐Py‐Tz exhibited far more positive redox potentials at 2.64 and 1.88 V than the other two s‐tetrazines (Figure 1c), which is a discrepancy from the predicted LUMO energy levels in the DFT calculations (Scheme 1c). It suggests the inclusion of an additional factor to promote its reactions, such as Li+ ion coordination during the redox events. This process will be discussed below. It should also be noted that ΔE pp for its first (70 mV) and second redox processes (140 mV) were far narrower than the ones for Ph‐Tz and 4‐Py‐Tz. This reveals that the 2‐pyridine EWGs induced faster redox kinetics of 2‐Py‐Tz. In addition, the J value for its second redox was similar to its first one, drawing that most of 2‐Py‐Tz •− turned into 2‐Py‐Tz 2− . All these CV characterizations clearly suggest that 2‐Py‐Tz is able to undergo reversible two‐electron‐transfer processes.

To get a deeper insight into the electron and ion transfer of the s‐tetrazine molecules, further electrochemical characterizations were conducted. Tafel plots in Figure S6, Supporting Information show the electron‐transfer rate constants (k 0) for the redox reactions based on the Butler–Volmer equation.[ 8 ] The calculated k 0 values were 3.05/1.38 × 10−5 for Ph‐Tz, 3.34/2.40 × 10−5 for 4‐Py‐Tz, and 8.46/4.32 × 10−5 cm s−1 for 2‐Py‐Tz at the first/second redox reaction, respectively (Figure 1d and Table S2, Supporting Information). Notably, 2‐Py‐Tz showed the most rapid kinetics of the others, consistent with the narrower ΔE pp.

Interestingly, all s‐tetrazines exhibited a slower second redox process than the first one, due to the enhanced electrostatic repulsion for the second cathodic process. However, this repulsive force can be mitigated by ion pairing with Li+. The coordination of Li+ ion can stabilize the reduced s‐tetrazine molecules through the Lewis acid/base and electrostatic interactions.

Thus, we also measured the diffusion coefficient (D) of Li+ ion. The D measured using Randles–Sevcik plots exhibited 4.84/2.05 × 10−9, 7.41/1.62 × 10−9, and 11.6/4.81 × 10−9 cm2 s−1 for Ph‐Tz, 4‐Py‐Tz, and 2‐Py‐Tz, respectively, during the first/second cathodic reaction (Figure 1d, Figure S7, and Table S3, Supporting Information). As expected, the 2‐Py‐Tz electrode provided the most rapid Li+ ion diffusion. A similar trend of D was also observed during the second/first anodic reaction, showing 0.87/6.35 × 10−9, 4.39/9.05 × 10−9, and 6.23/12.3 × 10−9, respectively. This difference in the Li+ ion diffusivity should be related to the coordination with the reduced s‐tetrazine molecules.

Next, we examined galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) tests for the three s‐tetrazines at a rate of 0.1C. During the GCD processes, two notable voltage plateaus appeared for all s‐tetrazine electrodes, demonstrating the two‐step redox reactions under the constant current mode (Figure 2 ). Further, their voltages were in line with the CV profiles. The discharging capacity of Ph‐Tz was 229 mAh g−1, virtually identical to its theoretical one (228.8 mAh g−1), where two plateaus equally contribute. Its charging capacity was, however, lower at 198 mAh g−1 with a Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 83%, likely due to dissolution of Ph‐Tz 2− after the second reduction process. The voltage gaps (ΔV cd) between the charge and discharge curve were 60 and 250 mV for its first and second plateaus, respectively. It delivered a specific energy of 455 Wh kg−1 with an energy efficiency (EE) of 93%.

Galvanostatic discharge/charge profiles for the s‐tetrazine electrodes at 0.1C: a) Ph‐Tz, b) 4‐Py‐Tz, and c) 2‐Py‐Tz. The vertical dashed lines indicate their theoretical specific capacities.

As expected, 4‐Py‐Tz electrode displayed its first voltage plateau at a slightly higher potential, while its second plateau appeared at a voltage comparable to that of Ph‐Tz (Figure 2b). ΔV cd for its first plateau was larger (≈90 mV) than that of Ph‐Tz, but its second plateau exhibited a significantly lowered ΔV cd of 160 mV. In addition, the specific capacity for the second plateau (51 mAh g−1) was far lower than the first one (112 mAh g−1), which is in line with the CV characterization. Compared to Ph‐Tz, it delivered lower specific energy (≈350 Wh kg−1). It also exhibited significantly low CE and EE of 78% and 81%, respectively.

In contrast, the 2‐Py‐Tz electrode showed a discharging capacity of 190 mAh g−1 with two plateaus at further higher voltages (Figure 2c). Notably, it exhibited smallest ΔV cd among the three tetrazines (60 and 90 mV for its first and second plateau, respectively), attributed to the rapid Li+ diffusion and fast electron‐transfer kinetics. It should also be noted that its CE was as high as 98%, implying its high reversibility. It delivered a specific energy of 407 Wh kg−1 with an EE of 112%.

Then, the rate capability of the s‐tetrazine electrodes was evaluated at current densities from 0.1 to 5C. The 2‐Py‐Tz electrode exhibited superior rate performance over the other two s‐tetrazine electrodes (Figure S8, Supporting Information), attributed to its rapid Li+ ion diffusion and faster electron‐transfer rate. Compared to 0.1C, the capacity retentions at 5C were 27%, 10%, and 46% for Ph‐Tz, 4‐Py‐Tz, and 2‐Py‐Tz, respectively. The Ragone plot (Figure S22, Supporting Information) shows that 2‐Py‐Tz delivered significantly higher specific energies than the other tetrazines.

During the GCD cycling, their specific capacities were gradually diminished (Figure S9a, Supporting Information), which is due to the dissolution of the s‐tetrazine molecules into the electrolyte solution.[ 26 ] Their solubilities in a DOL/DME (1/1 v/v) solvent mixture were measured to be 0.83, 4.59, and 6.09 mM for Ph‐Tz, 4‐Py‐Tz, and 2‐Ph‐Tz, respectively (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Although 2‐Ph‐Tz has the highest solubility, it exhibited more stable cycle performance than 4‐Py‐Tz, likely attributed to the intramolecular locking structure induced by the Li+ coordination. This locking effect would promote a planar molecular structure, lowering the solubility of 2‐Py‐Tz in the reduced states.

To improve the capacity retention, we increased Li salt concentration in the electrolyte solution. The increased Li+ concentration reduces the number of free solvent molecules that can solvate the active materials from the electrodes.[ 4 ] Indeed, by increasing the LiTFSI concentration from 1 to 6 M, the capacity retention of 2‐Py‐Tz electrode was improved from 17 to 42% for 30 cycles (Figure S9c, Supporting Information). However, after prolonged cycling, its capacity decayed, indicating the need for further optimization.

DFT Calculations for the Reduction Processes

To understand the Li+ coordination behavior, we calculated electrostatic potential (ESP) mapping of the s‐tetrazine molecules. The most negative electron‐density part in the ESP mapping corresponds to the Li+ ion coordinate sites.[ 27 , 28 ] In the neutral, radical anion, and dianion states, Ph‐Tz consistently has the most negative surface potential at the four N atoms of the tetrazine core (see red‐colored area in Figure 3a and Figure S11a, Supporting Information), where Li+ ions can coordinate.

DFT calculation results for the s‐tetrazine derivatives. a) ESP mapping for the dianion forms of the s‐tetrazines. The optimized geometries of the dianions coordinated with the two Li+ b) top and c) side view). d) The Li‐ion coordination energies (E coord) for the s‐tetrazine derivatives in the singly and doubly reduced states. E coord is calculated by Gibbs free energy difference between Li+‐coordinated and noncoordinated reduced s‐tetrazines (E coord = G [Li + ]n[Tz n− ] − G Tz n− − nG Li +).

Geometry optimization process for the reduced Ph‐Tz with Li+ ions confirmed the Li+ coordination sites (Table S4, Supporting Information). According to the ESP mapping results, two different positions are most plausible for the Li+ ion coordination upon the electrochemical reductions: i) adjacent to the two N atoms of the tetrazine core in the same horizontal plane and ii) perpendicular to the tetrazine core plane (Figure S13, Supporting Information). These two configurations were calculated for the two‐electron‐transfer processes, respectively, revealing that Li+ coordination in the horizontal direction is more stable (Figure 3b,c). In this orientation, two N atoms of the tetrazine core coordinate with each Li+ ion to form a three‐membered chelate ring.[ 15 ]

For 4‐Py‐Tz, in the neutral state, the most negative electron‐density site is the para‐position of pyridine rings to the tetrazine core. However, it is relocated to the four N atoms of the tetrazine core in the radical anion and dianion states (Figure S11b, Supporting Information). Geometry optimization process verified that the most stable Li+ coordination site is the N atoms of the tetrazine core, identical to the Ph‐Tz case, rather than 4‐pyridine N atoms (Figure S14, Supporting Information).

Interestingly, the reduced 4‐Py‐Tz forms possess significantly delocalized negative charges across the entire molecule for both radical anion and dianion states. Therefore, it is anticipated that the delocalized charge distribution would weaken the Li+ coordination strength, although the Li+ ions interact with N atoms of the tetrazine core during the redox events. Indeed, 4‐Py‐Tz exhibited considerably low Li+ ion coordination energy for the two‐electron reduction compared to Ph‐Tz (Figure 3d and Table S4, Supporting Information).

In contrast, 2‐Py‐Tz has negative charges localized between the ortho‐positioned N atoms in the pyridine rings and the four N atoms in the tetrazine core for all states (Figure S11c, Supporting Information). Thus, the Li+ ion can be strongly coordinated with both the tetrazine core and the 2‐pyridine rings of 2‐Py‐Tz to form an intramolecular locking structure via five‐membered ring. Such a strong chelating structure contributes for 2‐Py‐Tz to form the most stable Li+ ion coordination complex among the s‐tetrazines upon the electrochemical reductions (Figure 3d). This resulted in the significantly elevated redox potentials and rapid electron‐transfer kinetics of 2‐Py‐Tz (vide supra).

Taken together, the DFT calculations reveal that the Li+ coordination significantly influences the optimized geometries of the reduced s‐tetrazine derivatives. As shown in Figure S16, Supporting Information, all s‐tetrazine derivatives have bent conformations in the absence of Li+, whereas Li+ coordination induces planarization in their geometries. Comparison of the calculated reduction potentials with the measured values (Table S5 and S6, Supporting Information) shows that inclusion of Li+ interactions provides more accurate predictions, highlighting the critical role of Li+ in the redox reactions of the s‐tetrazine derivatives.

Redox Mechanism Study

The Li+ ion coordination behavior of the s‐tetrazine electrodes upon the discharge/recharge processes was experimentally evidenced using XPS. Before the analysis, electrode samples were washed with DOL three times. The LiTFSI salt residues in the electrodes were almost completely removed after washing, as confirmed by the atomic ratio of sulfur being less than 1 wt% (Figure S17, Supporting Information).

The XPS Li 1s spectra show that a single peak emerged upon discharging for all tetrazine electrodes (Figure S18, Supporting Information), indicating Li+ ion coordination. We calculated the atomic ratio of Li for Ph‐Tz at various depths of discharge (DODs) (Figure S18, Supporting Information). The Li content in the Ph‐Tz electrode increased progressively upon discharging and was reduced to nearly zero following the recharging process. This clearly demonstrates the reversible Li+ coordination/decoordination during the discharging and charging process, respectively.

Spectral changes in the N 1s region underpin the Li+ ion coordination behavior of Ph‐Tz. The pristine Ph‐Tz electrode showed a single peak at 400 eV, indicating that all four N atoms (i.e, −N=) in the tetrazine core were in an identical chemical environment (Figure 4a). Upon discharge, at 50% DOD, the signal separated into two peaks at 399.4 and 398.5 eV. The peak at lower binding energy (BE) is assigned for the noncoordinate N atoms, which carried increased negative charge with reduced aromaticity.[ 29 ] In contrast, the Li+ coordination reduced the negative charge density of the coordinated N atoms, shifting their peak to higher BE. Upon further discharging to 100% DOD, a single broad peak was observed at 398.8 eV, indicating that the four N atoms were once again in an identical chemical environment, with two Li+ ions coordinated. Then, by recharging, the XPS spectra returned to their original state in a stepwise fashion, confirming reversible redox behavior of Ph‐Tz (Figure S19, Supporting Information).

The XPS spectra of the N 1s BE region for a) Ph‐Tz, b) 4‐Py‐Tz, and c) 2‐Py‐Tz at various DOD. The top, middle, and bottom panels correspond to the spectra in the pristine state, at 50% DOD (half‐discharged), and at 100% DOD (fully discharged), respectively. The inset figures illustrate the proposed molecular structure of the s‐tetrazines with Li+ coordination at the corresponding state.

To further verify the stepwise occurrence of two single‐electron redox, we monitored EPR signals from ex situ Ph‐Tz electrodes at various DODs (Figure S20, Supporting Information). At 50% DOD, a single paramagnetic peak appeared, indicating formation of a radical anion Ph‐Tz •− upon discharging. Subsequently, at 100% DOD, diamagnetic Ph‐Tz 2− was formed, leading to considerable decrease in the EPR signal intensity. Finally, upon the recharging process, the paramagnetic signal recovered its strong intensity at 50% DOD, which virtually disappeared again at the fully recharged state.

In the case of 4‐Py‐Tz electrode, two peaks were observed in the XPS N 1s spectra for N atoms of the tetrazine core and the 4‐pyridine rings at 401.2 and 399.6 eV, respectively, in the pristine state (Figure 4b). At 50% DOD, the tetrazine core N signal split into two peaks at 401 and 400 eV, assigned to Li+ coordinated and noncoordinated N atoms, respectively. These were merged into one peak, corresponding to Li+ coordinated four N atoms, upon further discharging to 100% DOD, similar to the Ph‐Tz electrode. Meanwhile, the peak for 4‐pyridine N atoms at 399.6 eV was remained virtually unchanged throughout the discharging process.

Similar to 4‐Py‐Tz, the pristine 2‐Py‐Tz electrode exhibited two N 1s peaks (Figure 4c).[ 18 ] In sharp contrast, at 50% DOD, a new peak emerged at 399.1 eV, which can be assigned to the Li+ coordinated N atom in a 2‐pyridine ring. Simultaneously, the signal for the noncoordinate N atom in the other 2‐pyridine ring was shifted to lower BE (398.4 eV), due to the increased negative charges, with the reduced intensity. In the meantime, the N signal for the tetrazine core separated into two peaks at 400.8 and 399.2 eV with an intensity ratio of 1:3, corresponding to the Li+ coordinated and noncoordinated N atoms. Upon further discharging to 100% DOD, the signal for the noncoordinate N atom in 2‐pyridine ring completely disappeared, while the intensities of the two Li+ coordinated peaks (i.e., N atoms in tetrazine and 2‐pyridine rings) increased. These changes in the XPS spectra clearly demonstrate the distinctive Li+ coordination behavior of 2‐Py‐Tz compared to the other s‐tetrazines.

Conclusion

In summary, we investigated the electrochemical reversibility of the second‐electron transfer in 1,2,4,5‐tetrazine (s‐tetrazine) molecules by varying the aromatic substituents at the three‐ and six‐positions. As anticipated, replacing phenyl groups with pyridine rings facilitated the electron‐transfer kinetics, attributed to the electron‐withdrawing nature of the pyridine substituents, which stabilized the generated negative charges. Notably, 2‐pyridine substitution, compared to 4‐pyridine, significantly improved the electron‐transfer rate constant and Li+ diffusion coefficient, enabling a reversible second redox reaction of the s‐tetrazine core.

DFT calculations and ex situ XPS analysis revealed that the ortho‐positioned pyridine nitrogen atoms in 2‐Py‐Tz formed stable five‐membered coordination rings with the inserted Li+ ions and tetrazine core nitrogen atoms. This coordination locked the molecular geometry into a coplanar conformation, which significantly stabilized the reduced states of the s‐tetrazine and thereby promoted the reversible second‐electron redox process.

As a result, 2‐Py‐Tz exhibited superior rate performance with significantly elevated redox potentials in the GCD tests compared to the other two s‐tetrazine derivatives. Although 2‐Py‐Tz displayed some capacity fading due to its high solubility, this issue may be mitigated through electrolyte optimization and/or polymerization strategies in future studies.

Given its high specific capacity and multielectron transfer capability, s‐tetrazine represents a promising redox center for high performance organic electrode materials (see Table S7 and Figure S21, Supporting Information for a performance comparison with reported small‐molecule cathode materials of similar molecular weights). We believe this study highlights the critical role of metal‐ion coordination in achieving reversible multielectron redox processes, offering new insights for the development of sustainable organic batteries.
