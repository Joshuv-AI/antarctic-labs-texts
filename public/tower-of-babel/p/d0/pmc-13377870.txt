How urban green spaces influence adolescent mental health in urban fringe areas: A cultural ecosystem services perspective
Authors: Yan Long, Cairui Zhen, Lianghao Cheng, Fan Kang, Yudi Lin, Jin Li, Zixuan Chen, Wei Chen, Xi Liu, Yang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile urban green spaces are known to support psychological restoration, their benefits for adolescents in urban fringe areas and the pathways of cultural ecosystem services (CESs) remain unclear. Using multi-source data and structural equation modeling, the findings reveal that: (1) CESs improve mental health directly and via social interaction; (2) green view index and sky view factor positively affect mental health, while normalized difference vegetation index shows a negative effect; (3) point of interest density in 500-m park buffers exhibits functional differentiation: catering, corporate, and transportation facilities enhance mental health, lifestyle services reduce it, and commercial-residential facilities show no effect; (4) social interaction and neighborhood satisfaction are key mediators; healthcare facilities have the strongest total effect but a direct pathway, with effects varying by type and usage pattern. This study emphasizes that urban fringe planning should prioritize cultural services and social integration over mere green space metrics.Subject areas: health sciences, environmental science, psychology

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines adolescents as individuals aged 10–19 and characterizes their mental health as a positive state that enables them to cope with stress, realize their potential, learn effectively, and contribute to society.1 Urban green space (UGS), comprising semi-natural areas such as parks, trails, and woodlands primarily designed for recreation,2 plays a crucial role in promoting adolescents’ nature exposure, social interaction, and emotional regulation.3,4 During the ongoing expansion of China’s megacities, urban fringe areas face multiple challenges. These areas are transitional zones with intensive land use restructuring and sensitive spatial transformation.5 The challenges include underdeveloped green infrastructure, inadequate management and maintenance, and weak social support networks. Although urban fringe areas often possess more abundant green space resources in terms of land availability compared to central urban districts, their highly dynamic and complex nature leads to notably inadequate accessibility, service quality, and safety of UGS, thereby exacerbating mental health risks among adolescents in these areas.6 Despite this, current UGS planning remains primarily adult-oriented, with the spatial needs and lived experiences of adolescents—who are capable stakeholders—often systematically neglected.7 A review by Gascon found that only 21% of studies examining UGS and mental health analyzed adolescents separately, with most merging them into adult samples, thereby skewing intervention recommendations toward adults.8 Furthermore, existing research has largely emphasized high-density built environments or older populations, particularly the elderly, while significantly under representing urban fringe areas that blend urban and rural characteristics. The multifaceted environmental pressures facing adolescents in these zones may either enhance or diminish the mental health benefits of UGS, highlighting the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms and pathways.

Cultural ecosystem services (CESs) refer to the non-material benefits derived from natural environments and ecosystems, including aesthetic enjoyment, recreational opportunities, place identity, cultural heritage, and social interaction.9 As the primary interface between humans and nature, UGS serve as the core spatial carriers for the provision of CES.10 Unlike other ecosystem services, cultural services are directly perceived and experienced, making them more sensitive to demographic characteristics, cultural backgrounds, and group differences.11 Existing research on UGS and mental health has been developed based on attention restoration theory (ART) and stress reduction theory (SRT). ART emphasizes that the soft fascination of natural environments can restore directed attention fatigue,12 while SRT focuses on the alleviating effect of natural scenes on physiological stress.13 Together, these two theories constitute a universal narrative that nature benefits health, assuming homogeneous benefits across all populations.14 Their shared characteristic lies in focusing on the natural physical attributes of green spaces—such as vegetation coverage and scene complexity—and assuming that these attributes produce uniform psychological restoration functions. However, recent research has pointed out that the aesthetic, recreational, identity, and social opportunities afforded by nature—namely, CES—are precisely the key to explaining the differential psychological benefits experienced by different population groups.15 The CES perspective shifts the analytical focus from what nature does to people toward how people, through cultural and social pathways, perceive, use, and assign meaning to green spaces. This issue is particularly pronounced in urban fringe areas. Adolescents, whose cultural practices, activity preferences, and social needs differ markedly from those of adults, face structural constraints such as insufficient green space facilities and poor accessibility, while also holding expectations of the sociocultural functions of green spaces that are distinctly different from those of both adults and younger children; consequently, their pathways of perceiving and obtaining CES may diverge. For adolescents in urban fringe areas, who confront structural disadvantages, cultural marginalization, and unique social needs, whether green spaces are beneficial depends not only on the objective degree of greening, but more critically on whether these spaces can provide non-material services such as cultural identity, subcultural social spaces, and experiences of autonomy. In other words, the CES perspective effectively complements the universal narrative of ART/SRT: health benefits are not an automatic output of natural attributes but are closely tied to the cultural practices and value ascriptions of specific groups.

Research on the relationship between UGS and mental health has advanced from simple correlation analyses to mechanistic investigations. According to the theoretical framework proposed by Markevych,16 UGS influence mental health through three main pathways: mitigating environmental hazards, facilitating psychological restoration, and promoting individual health by providing opportunities for physical activity and social interaction. Among these, green space exposure—as a passive exposure mechanism—can yield significant restorative benefits even through window views or brief glimpses.17

Within this framework, UGS characteristics are measured through two main approaches: objective metrics—including quantity, size, and vegetation indices such as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)18,19; subjective perceptions, which capture residents’ attitudes, preferences, and restorative experiences through questionnaires.20 With advancements in computer vision, visual indicators derived from street view imagery, such as the green view index (GVI) and sky view factor (SVF), have been validated in studies on elderly health in Europe and North America due to their effectiveness in capturing human-scale green exposure levels.21 However, research in China has remained concentrated on adult or elderly populations,22 leaving the exploration of adolescents in urban fringe areas nearly unaddressed. Furthermore, the health benefits of green spaces are not solely derived from natural elements; the quality and configuration of internal and surrounding service facilities also play a critical role by influencing the usability, safety, and attractiveness of green spaces, thereby modulating their core functions of promoting social interaction and enhancing satisfaction.23

At the mechanistic level, SRT and ART together provide the theoretical foundation for understanding environment-behavior-psychology chain reaction.24,25 Extensive research has established that physical activity, social interaction, and neighborhood satisfaction serve as key mediating variables linking UGS to mental health.26,27 Studies focusing on children and adolescents further reveal that contact with UGS is significantly associated with reduced stress levels and fewer emotional problems, primarily through the promotion of outdoor activities and social interaction.28 As high-quality public social spaces, UGS provide adolescents with informal meeting venues, which help strengthen community cohesion and social support, thereby enhancing their self-identity and psychological well-being.

Despite considerable progress, findings remain inconsistent owing to two key limitations. First, most studies examine single factors in isolation, making it difficult to understand how multiple pathways work together.29 Second, studies focus too much on adults in city centers, while paying little attention to teenagers in urban fringe areas—a vulnerable group facing both psychological risks and limited environmental resources. This narrow focus reduces how well we can apply these findings and develops effective policies.

The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MA) (2005) defines CES as “the non-material benefits people obtain from ecosystems,” encompassing aesthetic appreciation, recreation, cultural heritage, education, and spiritual values.9 CES are characterized by experiential and subjective perceptions that are closely shaped by individuals’ cultural backgrounds and life experiences.30 The cultural meaning of UGS lies in both spatial narratives—such as historic relics, monuments, and traditional plantings—and participatory cultural experiences including festivals, rituals, and art performances.31 When individual cultural values align with these spatial symbols, emotional attachment and psychological restoration are strengthened; conversely, cultural mismatches may diminish these effects.32 The preferences of marginalized groups, such as adolescents, low-income residents, and ethnic minorities—are often overlooked, exacerbating spatial exclusion.33 It is therefore imperative to include these marginalized groups in CES assessments. Traditional CES assessment tools, such as the contingent valuation method (CVM) and social value evaluation system (solves), face challenges in capturing spiritual and religious values, and conventional questionnaires are limited in reflecting adolescents’ authentic emotions.34 With the rise of social media, user-generated content on platforms like Weibo and Instagram provides spontaneous, large-scale perceptual data that can more accurately reflect public attitudes and experiences, offering new opportunities for CES-based evaluations.35 In summary, CES research has made significant progress, yet its application to mental health remains underdeveloped. Future studies should quantify the psychological benefits of cultural elements in UGS and address perceptual differences among marginalized populations.

Urban fringe areas are transitional zones between urban and rural contexts undergoing rapid urbanization and land-use change, characterized by unstable community environments and insufficient public services, which pose significant challenges to adolescent mental health. Uhlhaas et al. note that adolescence is a critical period for the onset of mental disorders, whose effects can persist into adulthood, highlighting the importance of early intervention.36 Compared to central urban areas, adolescents in fringe zones are often excluded from high-quality public spaces, with limited opportunities for physical activity and peer interaction, undermining self-esteem and identity formation.

Adolescents in urban fringe areas are a heterogeneous population, comprising locally born youth, children of migrant workers, and left-behind children. The first group may face cultural conflicts and insufficient educational resources,37 while the latter may experience emotional and behavioral problems due to the absence of parental care.38 A study in Turkey reported that 17.5% of adolescents aged 13–18 scored at or above the clinical threshold on the Youth Depression Inventory.39 The intersection of urban and rural cultural influences further contributes to identity confusion and psychological stress, increasing their vulnerability amid rapid urbanization. However, existing research on UGS and mental health has largely focused on high-density urban areas, leaving a lack of empirical evidence regarding adolescents in fringe zones. It is therefore crucial to investigate whether UGS can mitigate multiple stressors and promote mental health by providing cultural identity cues and spaces for social interaction.

In summary, this study focuses on the adolescent population in China’s urban fringe areas, using the urban fringe area of Wuhan as the empirical scope (Figure 1). Sixteen urban parks in the region were selected as the study subjects, and 558 valid youth questionnaires were collected. Objective indicators (NDVI, point of interest [POI]) and subjective perceptions (GVI, SVF) were integrated into a multi-source data framework. From the perspective of CES, using structural equation modeling (SEM), the study aims to explore the pathways through which UGS affect adolescents’ mental health in urban fringe areas and quantify the differences in the impact of various UGS feature indicators, as well as their potential mediating pathways (Figure 2). The study attempts to answer the following questions.

Results

Visiting preferences of adolescents in urban peripheral youth parks

This study examines park usage characteristics among adolescents in urban fringe areas from a sociodemographic perspective (Table 1). Males account for 64.7% of the sample. This gender imbalance primarily reflects the actual user structure of parks in urban fringe areas, rather than sampling bias (as shown in Table S1). During the survey period, it was found that these parks are dominated by facilities such as basketball courts and badminton areas, with peak usage concentrated in the evening and night time hours, which objectively attracted more male users. The findings revealed two peak usage times for adolescents: during the evening and night (80.5%), and in the afternoon (45.1%). This bimodal pattern is related to after-school free time and is also influenced by parental pick-up and supervision. In terms of companionship, 61.1% of adolescents visit parks with peers, while 46.1% are accompanied by parents. Parents tend to view parks as places for supervision, while adolescents regard them as important social spaces. Regarding transportation, walking (66.5%) and cycling (49.8%) dominate, indicating that most adolescents primarily engage in activities within a 500-m radius of their residential area, highlighting the constraints of their living circle on mobility. Non-local adolescents have an 18% lower walking rate, indicating that certain groups are disadvantaged in terms of spatial access, and consequently, have fewer opportunities for natural contact and psychological restoration.

Service facilities and mental health

Significant positive effects

As shown in Table 3, catering facilities exert a significant positive effect on adolescent mental health in urban fringe areas (β = 0.345, p < 0.001). This finding is supported by multi-dimensional research. First, the catering environment within a 500-m buffer zone around parks significantly enhances adolescent mental health by expanding informal social networks. Second, from a neuro-mechanism perspective, Telzer’s research revealed a specific activation pattern in the brain’s reward system during social dining situations for adolescents.41 Additionally, some studies suggest a negative correlation between the frequency of using catering facilities and stress hormone levels.42 In resource-limited fringe areas, catering venues often function beyond their basic role, serving as social hubs, providing valuable social spaces for adolescents in urban fringe areas. The direct impact of corporate entities on the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas only reached marginal significance (β = 0.079, p = 0.016). However, transportation facilities (β = 0.097, p = 0.004) and healthcare facilities (β = 0.774, p < 0.001) show a positive effect, consistent with findings by Lam in their study on elderly samples in Hong Kong, where high accessibility to transportation and healthcare facilities directly lowers the risk of depression.43

Significant negative effects

Disturbing livelihood service facilities show a significant negative effect (β = −1.178, p < 0.001), indicating that each unit increase in such services is associated with a 1.178-unit increase in psychological stress. These facilities are often small in size, numerous, and densely distributed, making it easy to form negative crowding effects at a micro-scale. The reasons include unpleasant odors, visual disturbances, and reduced feelings of safety due to insufficient nighttime lighting. Kong demonstrated that areas with higher densities of public facilities such as restrooms and sanitation infrastructure exhibit significantly elevated levels of psychological distress reported by residents.44 The underlying mechanisms primarily involve reduced feelings of security stemming from odors, noise, visual disturbances, and inadequate nighttime lighting. Furthermore, life service facilities within parks mainly serve transient users rather than the surrounding permanent population. Adolescents in urban fringe areas are chronically exposed to the negative sights and odors of facilities like public restrooms and garbage stations, making it difficult for them to enjoy the restorative benefits offered by UGS.

No significant effect

The effect of business and residential facilities (β = −0.038, p = 0.31) was not statistically significant, possibly reflecting a balance among multiple competing mechanisms. Based on existing research, the mixed-use characteristics of commercial-residential developments in peripheral areas may yield positive impacts by providing employment opportunities, yet may also generate negative effects due to environmental disorder, resulting in an insignificant net effect. Concurrently, adolescent mental health is more susceptible to proximal factors such as family relationships and peer networks.45 The protective role of natural or public natural spaces on emotional well-being has been found to exhibit weak and inconsistent effects.46 Furthermore, differences in the sensitivity of adolescents of different age groups to the community environment may be an important reason for the overall non-significant effect.47

Mediating effects between UGS and mental health

This study employs the Bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) to construct 95% confidence intervals and test the mediating pathways through which park green space factors affect the psychological health of adolescents in urban fringe areas. As shown in Table 4, CES have a significant indirect effect on mental health through social interaction (β = 0.031, p = 0.021), accounting for 30.7% of the total effect. In terms of facilities, the overall facility number (POI) does not show a direct significant relationship with mental health, but plays a positive mediating role through neighborhood satisfaction (β = 0.013, p = 0.048). Further classification analysis reveals that healthcare-related POI density has the strongest total effect (β = 1.399, p = 0.003), and its indirect effect through social interaction is marginally significant (β = 0.625, p = 0.060), accounting for 44.6% (p = 0.047), indicating that social interaction partially mediates this pathway. In terms of greenery, NDVI has a significant negative total effect on psychological health independent of the mediating variables (β = −0.076, p = 0.038), while physical activity shows no significant mediating effect. In conclusion, social interaction and neighborhood satisfaction are the core mediating variables affecting the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas. Therefore, UGS affects the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas through physical activity is not supported, while UGS affects the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas through social interaction and neighborhood satisfaction are supported.

Discussion

How do CES promote the psychological health of adolescents in urban fringe areas?

This study uses SEM to address the central question of whether CES exert a significant positive effect on adolescent mental health in urban fringe areas. The findings indicate that CES in urban parks positively influences the subjective mental health of adolescents in these areas, with its effect even surpassing that of traditional factors such as social interaction and neighborhood environment, which is consistent with previous research.31 Although existing studies have not fully explored the unique value of CES in relation to the subjective mental health of this group, this study highlights its importance in the context of urban fringe areas. From a theoretical perspective, both ART and SRT predict that any green space with sufficient natural features can universally relieve stress or restore attention. However, compared with the generalized natural restoration mechanisms emphasized by these traditional restoration theories, the CES perspective focuses more on how adolescents, within their specific life experiences and cultural contexts, imbue green spaces with sociocultural attributes and derive mental health benefits from them. The contribution of CES to the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas is significantly larger than the direct effect of physical green space indicators such as NDVI, indicating that for this group the mental health function of green spaces stems more from the symbolic meanings and interactive opportunities they provide as venues for sociocultural practices than from mere natural exposure.

The mental health benefits of CES derive from its dual attributes. The intangible nature of CES enables it to provide aesthetic experiences, cultural identity, and other immaterial effects, offering fundamental psychological restoration through visual aesthetics and ecological experiences. On the other hand, as a space for social and cultural practices, the subjectivity of CES directly influences users’ psychological and cognitive processes by supporting leisure activities, place identity, and social interaction, thereby meeting the psychological and social development needs of adolescents during their growth (Figure 3: in the form of a conceptual schematic diagram, the theoretical mechanism through which CESs influence adolescent mental health; the arrows in the figure indicate only hypothesized directions of influence and do not represent path analysis results from a structural equation model).

First, the physical characteristics of UGS shape the provision of CES, which encompasses aesthetics, recreation, social interaction, and culture. Given that individuals’ aesthetic values, the size of UGS affects people’s perceptions, and these characteristics can influence CES.48 Studies have shown that larger parks can enhance the sense of connection to nature.49 Other attributes, such as species composition and canopy cover, are part of a range of variables that may influence the benefits (CES) derived from UGS.50 Second, UGS users tend to prefer the most familiar and easily accessible areas. Since individuals are considered responders, their perception of CES reflects their intrinsic emotions and highlights their subjective experience of the natural environment, effectively conveying the services they receive and retain from the ecosystem.51 Lastly, there is a complex interaction between the social and environmental factors of UGS, which together shape people’s perceptions and experiences of the urban natural environment. The objective green space metrics widely used may overestimate the actual psychological and social benefits generated.50

Urban parks, as primary providers of CES, derive their cultural service value through people’s perceptions and use. As Buchel and Frantzeskaki noted, these services are not inherent attributes of parks, but arise from the interaction between people and the environment.52 People’s recognition of the value of UGS exhibits distinctive regional characteristics. Research by Burgess in London showed that local residents gained rich, multi-sensory natural experiences through their daily interactions with nearby UGS.53 This emotional connection to UGS often transcends material considerations. Similarly, Dwyer found that urban residents develop a strong emotional attachment to urban trees, which goes beyond simple aesthetic or monetary value, highlighting the importance of the regional and sociocultural dimensions of CES.54 This view was further corroborated by subsequent studies, with Andersson confirming that people often attach unique emotional and cultural meanings to the UGS they are familiar with, meanings that cannot be fully interpreted or measured through physical attributes alone.55 In the planning and management of UGS, more emphasis should be placed on the role of sociocultural context in shaping their perception, use, and value.

Adolescents’ cultural experiences of UGSs are not formed independently but are embedded in the context of family and intergenerational social interactions. Research in developmental psychology and family sociology has shown that parents’ choices significantly influence adolescents’ opportunities for cultural exposure and environmental preferences in leisure consumption and outdoor activities.56 In other words, adults’—especially parents’—perceptions and preferences regarding the cultural values of parks largely shape the actual green space environments that adolescents encounter and perceive. Therefore, although the textual data used to extract CES in this study primarily originate from adult users, the cultural environment characteristics they reflect—including aesthetic appreciation, spiritual fulfillment, and social functions—can serve as valid proxy indicators for the supply of objective cultural environments in which adolescents are situated. This theoretical positioning draws on the research tradition of intergenerational cultural transmission and cultural resources in cultural sociology, treating adults’ cultural preferences as an important window for understanding adolescents’ cultural exposure. Moreover, this intergenerational congruence is particularly applicable to leisure activities involving family co-participation (e.g., park visits, parent-child outings). For cultural experiences that adolescents engage in independently (e.g., solo cycling, gatherings with friends), adult-based CES expressions may be subject to bias. However, given that this study focuses on urban parks as typical family leisure spaces, and preliminary data indicate that parental accompaniment accounts for 46.10% of visits (see Table 4), the mechanism of intergenerational transmission remains dominant.

Therefore, parks with higher CES levels offer greater spiritual, aesthetic, and social value, which may benefit the mental health of adolescents in fringe areas. As emphasized by Hunter50 and Kremer,57 effective green space strategies for promoting mental health need to consider two dimensions: one is the material attributes of the UGS, including vegetation composition, spatial layout, and other objective elements; the second is the sociocultural background of the user group and its local knowledge. This study broadens the understanding of the relationship between UGS and mental health, helping decision-makers and planners obtain more targeted landscape planning or design by comprehensively considering the needs and preferences of adolescents.

Significant differences in the effects of different UGS indicators on the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas

The results reveal significant differences in the effects of different UGS dimensions on adolescent mental health in urban fringe areas. Specifically, NDVI, which represents macro-scale vegetation coverage, shows a significant negative effect, while the GVI, which reflects visual perception, shows a significant positive promoting effect. This finding addresses the core question raised in this study: “do different UGS characteristics have significant differences in their effects on the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas?” It indicates that more attention should be given to enhancing the micro-visual dimensions in urban fringe areas, rather than simply pursuing the increase of macro-scale greenery.

Negative effect of NDVI

A substantial body of research has emphasized the positive effects of UGS on health. The biophilia hypothesis posits that exposure to nature typically yields psychological benefits.58 Similarly, the natural attention hypothesis suggests that individuals tend toward aesthetically pleasing environments, particularly natural settings, thereby enhancing mental well-being. The ART12 and SRT13 further highlight the therapeutic effects of UGS on individual stress and attention capabilities. While there is ample theoretical research supporting the established positive impact of UGS,16,59,60 the “nature contact is beneficial” assumption upon which these theories are based needs to be reconsidered in the specific context of urban fringe areas (Figure 4: to illustrate the potential negative effects of the macro-level greening represented by NDVI, Figure 4 provides a conceptual schematic diagram summarizing the underlying factors, and it does not contain actual parameter estimates).

The negative effect of NDVI points to a complex, potentially nonlinear relationship between natural environments and mental health. Overabundant or poorly managed UGS may heighten feelings of insecurity and psychological discomfort, such as loneliness, fear, and anxiety, which can negatively affect mental health.61 Furthermore, ecological psychology emphasizes that busy UGS may exacerbate feelings of social isolation, negatively impacting mental health.62 This suggests that the presence of visible green vegetation may not always align with its functional effectiveness, indicating that the relationship between UGS and mental health is not a simple linear progression, but may involve a threshold effect.63 A study on children in Australia found that the peak happiness level was related to the range of UGS coverage64 highlighting the importance of considering the quantity and design of UGS to maximize mental health benefits in specific environments and populations. Excessive UGS may lead to feelings of isolation,62 potential pollution risks,65 and sensory overload negatively impact mental health.

In the context of China’s urban fringe, areas with high NDVI values often overlap with under maintained wildland type green spaces, vacant or idle land awaiting construction, poorly accessible shelterbelts, or informal green spaces lacking basic facilities and lighting conditions. Although these areas have high vegetation cover, they commonly suffer from a lack of maintenance, unclear land ownership, and insufficient security facilities. For adolescents, especially female adolescents, such spaces are prone to triggering a lack of perceived safety, discouraging actual visits and active use, limiting opportunities for social interaction and physical activity, and may even exacerbate feelings of isolation and anxiety. This interpretation is consistent with existing research, which suggests that the quality, safety, and management level of green spaces are key determinants of their health effects. Simply pursuing vegetation cover while neglecting design and maintenance may not only fail to deliver the expected health benefits but may even produce unintended negative effects.

Therefore, in urban fringe areas, merely implementing high-density greening as a symbolic gesture may be insufficient to generate meaningful health benefits and could even become a risk factor. This finding suggests that urban fringe green space planning needs to go beyond the simplistic pursuit of increasing greenery quantity and focus more on the quality, accessibility, and safety management of green spaces.

More green does not necessarily mean healthier

One explanation is that NDVI and GVI capture greenery from fundamentally different vantage points: a “bird’s-eye view” for NDVI versus an “eye-level view” for GVI. The green exposure mechanisms reflected by these indices are fundamentally different in terms of psychological pathways. NDVI is typically derived from remote sensing imagery, which can provide a macroscopic reflection of the quantity and status of vegetation cover but struggles to capture the spatial structure, vertical layers, and the actual visual experience of individuals in UGS.66 In contrast, GVI quantifies the proportion of visible greenery from an eye-level perspective, which is closer to the daily perception of residents. Especially in high-density or vertical greening areas, GVI can more accurately reflect the visual level of natural contact67 (Figure 5: the conceptual schematic diagram in Figure 5 contrasts the two ways of perceiving green space—from an eye-level perspective [GVI] and a bird’s-eye perspective [NDVI]—aiming to explain their differences from the perspectives of spatial scale and visual information, rather than presenting a statistical model.).

Another explanation is that GVI, together with SVF, reflects visual openness and spatial transparency. Prior studies have shown that these attributes can significantly alleviate anxiety and promote psychological restoration.68 In contrast, areas with high NDVI may result in increased visual closure and reduced permeability due to excessive vegetation density and homogeneity, potentially inducing feelings of oppression or anxiety.69 NDVI reflects the potential level of greening, but it does not represent its actual usability and perceived quality, often leading to the phenomenon of “greening without effect”—where vegetation exists but fails to translate into health benefits.70 Especially among adolescents in urban fringe areas, the accessibility, cultural fit, and social equity of UGS are more complex. High-quality UGS resources are often restricted by spatial, economic, and social thresholds, making traditional greening indicators, such as NDVI, inadequate for truly reflecting this group’s actual use of UGS and their mental health.

Social interaction and neighborhood satisfaction as important mediators in the impact of UGS on adolescents’ mental health in urban fringe areas

Physical activity, neighborhood satisfaction, and social interaction are widely regarded as key mediators linking UGS to residents’ mental health. Through empirical analysis of adolescents in urban fringe areas, this study confirms that neighborhood satisfaction and social interaction maintain a stable mediating role in this group. However, the mediating pathway of physical activity was found to be insignificant, highlighting that in the specific context of urban fringe areas, factors such as UGS accessibility, facility completeness, and perceived safety limit the physical activity pathway, while neighborhood satisfaction and social interaction pathways emerge as more core mediating factors.

Multiple studies consistently demonstrate that UGS can impact mental health by enhancing residents’ satisfaction with their living environment.26,71 This mechanism aligns with findings from research on the built environment and mental health.31,63 Improved neighborhood satisfaction may result from UGS enhancing community aesthetics, safety, and sense of belonging, all of which collectively promote psychological well-being. A longitudinal study by Ding72 in Australia showed that community participation can significantly predict subsequent mental health levels, with more cohesive communities helping residents cope with stress and thus improving overall mental health. Numerous studies also support the significant mediating effect of social interaction in the relationship between UGS and mental health.26,27 UGS provide places for informal interaction and social opportunities, helping to alleviate daily stress and enhance neighborhood support,73 as well as promoting community trust and cohesion.74 Therefore, UGS indirectly improve adolescents’ mental health in urban fringe areas through social interaction and neighborhood satisfaction.

By contrast, evidence for physical activity as a mediating variable is considerably heterogeneous. Although UGS provide potential spaces for physical activity,75 the connection between UGS and mental health varies across different cultural and geographical contexts. Some studies show that UGS can promote physical activity,73,76 but multiple studies from Europe have found no such association.26,71 This inconsistency may stem from various factors, such as UGS quality, types of facilities, accessibility, and methodological differences in buffer zone settings. Particularly in the Chinese context, adolescents in urban fringe areas face challenges such as heavy academic burdens, fragmented extracurricular time, and parental control due to safety and traffic risks, all of which limit their frequency and manner of using UGS at a distance. Even when entering UGS, adolescents tend to engage more in static, low-intensity activities rather than medium-to-high intensity physical exercises, thereby weakening the potential mental health benefits of UGS through physical activity. Furthermore, the widespread use of electronic devices further encroaches on the time adolescents spend on outdoor physical activities in urban fringe areas. Increasing screen time not only directly reduces opportunities for physical activity but may also alter leisure preferences and behavioral patterns, further diminishing the role of UGS as places for physical activity.

Future directions

First, greater attention should be directed toward the relationship between mental health and cultural elements among vulnerable groups in disadvantaged areas. For example, the perception of cultural symbols, participation in cultural activities, and appreciation of natural landscapes are important interactions between adolescents and cultural landscapes.

Second, future research should not only examine the impact of cultural elements in UGS on adolescent mental health but also extend inquiry to vulnerable groups and the influence of culture-related behaviors on mental health. New technologies and methods can be employed to obtain multi-source data, such as portable biosensors, mobile signaling data, and artificial neural networks (ANNs). For instance, mobile signaling data can be used to analyze the time spent by people in parks, extracting information about duration, time distribution, and social interaction density; future research should develop CES perception measurement tools specifically for adolescents or combine participatory methods such as adolescent-led photo diaries and walking interviews to obtain data that more closely reflect their actual experiences. Additionally, efforts should be made to integrate multiple social media sources (e.g., Xiaohongshu, Bilibili—platforms popular among adolescents) to improve data representativeness.

Third, future research should adopt more representative sampling strategies, such as: (1) multi-channel recruitment combining community, school, online platforms, and clinical settings to cover adolescents with different mobility levels and mental states; (2) using geographic information systems (GISs) to assess individual residential green space exposure and establishing cohorts based on population registration data to reduce entry bias caused by differences in individuals’ active mobility; (3) combining qualitative methods to gain in depth understanding of the actual perceptions, needs, and barriers faced by adolescents who avoid green spaces due to psychological problems, thereby addressing the blind spots of quantitative research.

Finally, combining objective physiological measures with self-reported mental health instruments would yield more comprehensive data, such as using accelerometers, heart rate variability (HRV), and cortisol saliva tests to quantify physical activity intensity and acute physiological stress. In addition, future research should adopt longitudinal follow-up designs or quasi-experimental studies to more accurately clarify the causal direction and dynamic evolution patterns between CES and adolescent mental health.

Limitations of the study

To address the gap in research on CES effects on adolescent mental health, this study selected 16 parks in the fringe area of Wuhan and verified the positive effect of CES on adolescents’ subjective mental health. Nevertheless, the study has certain limitations. First, the classification of CES within leading international frameworks exhibits conceptual ambiguity,9,77 with definitions of dimensions such as “spiritual fulfillment” and “social interaction” lacking specificity and exhibiting overlap across frameworks,78,79,80 which may affect the consistency and comparability of CES identified from social media content. Social media data inherently carry demographic biases, reflecting unequal access to technology and platform usage among different groups.81 As an opportunistic data generation platform,82 Dianping’s attributes and platform accessibility may cause CES indicators to over represent dining-related cultural values while underestimating other dimensions that adolescents may prioritize, such as sports facilities and social spaces.83 Furthermore, CES are fundamentally context dependent and place based,84,85 yet large scale assessments often rely on standardized categories that neglect contextual nuances.86 Such generalization may lose the diversity of emotional attachments that connect people to natural environments. Thus, although this study reconceptualized adult-based CES data as a proxy for “cultural environment supply,” the risk of ecological fallacy cannot be completely eliminated, and a gap remains between public sentiment quantified from social media data and actual public sentiment. Second, this study only selected a youth group in the urban fringe areas for case studies, and differences in urban levels, climate, and social culture have not been explored. Third, in the data collection process of this study, questionnaires were randomly distributed near parks. Although this method is highly feasible in practice, its inherent sampling framework may introduce selection bias, systematically excluding adolescents who, due to poor mental health conditions (e.g., moderate to severe depression, social anxiety, agoraphobia), tend to avoid public spaces and reduce outdoor activities. Consequently, the sample composition of this study may objectively skew toward relatively active, healthier adolescents, potentially leading to an overestimation of the positive effects of UGS. The findings primarily reflect the protective role of UGS for adolescents who are able to access green spaces, but fail to capture the real situation of vulnerable adolescents who are disconnected from green spaces due to psychological problems. This prevents us from determining whether UGS has a similar protective effect on this group, nor can we assess the higher mental health risks and health inequalities they may face due to lack of access to UGS. Fourth, the mediators in this study (physical activity, social interaction, and neighborhood satisfaction) are all self-reported measures, which may be affected by subjective preferences and recall bias. Fifth, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits the certainty of causal inference. Although we constructed a structural equation model (SEM) based on theoretical foundations and tested the path relationships among variables, the cross-sectional data can only reveal statistical associations rather than prove causal relationships. Due to these limitations, we suggest that future research should focus on the following points.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study area

Wuhan (Figure 1), located between 29°58′–31°22′N and 113°41′–115°5′E, is a national central city in the middle Yangtze River basin. Its polycentric urban structure, with the three major urban districts and multi-axis expansion, results in a typical radial expansion of the city’s fringe areas, making it a representative example of rapid urbanization in China. In this study, the boundary of Wuhan’s fringe areas is defined using changes in impervious surface area, landscape fragmentation, and population density. Based on social media data, 16 parks located in the urban fringe areas of Wuhan were selected. The reasons for selecting these parks are as follows: (1) The parks feature diverse facility types, reflecting the functional differences of UGS in urban fringe areas; (2) There is a rich dataset of social media comments available for analysis, supporting a quantitative analysis of urban adolescent usage behaviors; (3) The parks are highly attractive to local adolescents, serving as frequent sites for their daily leisure activities while catering to all age groups; (4) The parks vary in terms of scale, type, and surrounding environment, rather than focusing on homogenous parks. These parks collectively reflect the behavior patterns of adolescents using UGS in urban fringe areas, making them a representative sample for studying the psychological health of this demographic.

Technical approach

To systematically examine the mechanisms through which UGS influence adolescent mental health in urban fringe areas, this study adopts a four-step analytical framework: “spatial delineation - data integration -model construction - effect testing” (Figure 2). First, the boundary of the urban fringe area is scientifically delineated through Geographic Information System (GIS) spatial analysis, and parks frequently used by adolescents in these areas are selected as the study sites based on social media data. Second, multi-source data are integrated, including social media data (CES), remote sensing data (NDVI, Green Space Area), street view data (GVI, SVF), land cover data (facility quantity and density), and survey data (mental health, physical activity, neighborhood satisfaction, social interaction, etc.). Third, a theoretical model is constructed, with UGS characteristics (NDVI, green space area, facility density and quantity, CES) and environmental visual dimensions (GVI, SVF) as independent variables, physical activity, social interaction, and neighborhood satisfaction as mediating variables, and the subjective mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas as the dependent variable. Socio-demographic characteristics are included as control variables in a multiple parallel mediation structural equation model. Finally, based on the model paths and mediation effect results, the impact of UGS factors on the mental health of adolescents in urban fringe areas is analyzed, providing scientific evidence and practical guidance for improving the psychological well-being of adolescents in these areas.

Informed consent

Data names and their sources

Social media data

Social media data were obtained from Dianping, a mainstream local lifestyle platform. Its publicly available user-generated reviews offer real-time, large-scale reflections of public sentiment and value judgments toward locations, making them suitable for exploring CES. The study collected 22,285 anonymous comment texts from 16 parks in Wuhan’s peripheral districts between June 2021 and October 2024. Only the anonymous texts and publication dates were retained for subsequent thematic classification and sentiment analysis.

Street view data

OpenStreetMap (OSM) data were used to obtain the road networ road network of Wuhan’s urban fringe areas, and street view images were collected through the Baidu Maps Static API at intervals of every 100 m. Considering the limitations of automated network crawling in urban fringe zones, the research team conducted manual street view photography during morning and evening peak hours from November to December 2024, capturing a total of 8,986 images. According to Wraga,87 the average eye level of adults is approximately 160 cm, while that of children aged 10–12 is about 110 cm, representing a ratio of 11/16 relative to adults. To simulate the child’s eye-level perspective, the upper 5/16 portion of each image was cropped to adjust the balance between ground and sky areas, thereby providing a reliable visual basis for subsequent analysis.88

Remote sensing data

NDVI was derived from remote sensing imagery to quantify vegetation cover and growth conditions, serving as a proxy for adolescents’ perception of surrounding greenery. The NDVI data originated from Landsat 8 imagery captured in 2022. Processed through Google Earth Engine with cloud removal, interpolation, and S-G smoothing, the final output yielded annual maximum NDVI values at 30-meter resolution.

Land cover data

Parks and their surrounding environments contribute to residents’ mobility and recreational experiences. In this study, land cover data were obtained from the Amap (Gaode) Map JavaScript API, yielding a total of 111,991 POI records from 2022 with a spatial resolution of 30 m. These POIs were categorized into functional types, including shopping, dining, attractions, transportation, finance, education and culture, healthcare, and accommodation services. UGS accessibility was defined by the presence of green areas within a 500 m buffer zone surrounding each child’s place of residence, representing an approximate 10-min walking distance consistent with children’s mobility patterns and prior studies.89 Based on this definition, a 500 m buffer was delineated outside each park boundary to quantify the number of facilities by category and calculate POI density.

Survey data

The questionnaire survey was conducted in two rounds: June–July and September–November 2024. Control variables included gender, educational level, and mode of transportation; mediating variables were physical activity, neighborhood satisfaction, and social interaction; and the dependent variable was adolescents’ self-reported mental health. Prior to the formal survey, a pilot test involving 175 questionnaires was conducted between June and July 2024 to assess the validity and reliability of the instrument and make necessary revisions. The formal survey was carried out from September to November 2024, with questionnaires randomly distributed near parks, proportionally allocated according to park size. A total of 668 questionnaires were distributed, of which 558 were valid, yielding an effective response rate of 83.53%. The remaining 16.47% were excluded due to incomplete or unreliable responses (see Data S1. Research Questionnaire).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Semantic sentiment analysis

Semantic Sentiment Analysis (SSA) employs Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to identify sentiment orientation (positive, negative, or neutral) and its intensity in text. The core of this process lies in understanding the semantics of the text, including context, sarcasm, metaphors, and other complex phenomena. In order to quantify the CES in urban parks, this study employs the K-means clustering algorithm and the TF-IDF model. The cleaned text is tokenized using the third-party Python library, Jieba, retaining only adjectives, verbs, and nouns.

Compared with the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model—which requires fine-tuning of hyperparameters such as α and β and is sensitive to the number of topics—K-means directly measures Euclidean distance on the TF-IDF sparse matrix in a high-dimensional space. It does not require prior knowledge, converges quickly, and is more suitable for clustering large-scale, unlabeled short texts. To ensure the rationality and reliability of the clustering results, this study evaluates the different clustering outcomes using the silhouette coefficient. The optimal number of CES clusters is determined to be 7, as shown in Figures 3A and 3B.

This study employs the Baidu Open Intelligent Platform’s sentiment analysis interface. Comments are first segmented using the Jieba word segmentation tool, generating Chinese word segments. Then, the built-in sentiment lexicon assigns a score from 0 to 1 for each sentence (where 0 represents extreme negativity and 1 represents extreme positivity). The positive and negative values correspond to the public’s evaluation of the park’s CES, either positively or negatively. Finally, the average score of all reviews for each park is taken as the emotional index of the cultural services for that park.

The comment data used in this study are sourced from Dianping, a commonly used tool for urban residents’consumption decisions. The user generated content reflects the distribution of cultural resources and mainstream cultural expressions within specific regions. These adult dominated cultural narratives constitute the objective cultural contexts accessible to adolescents in their daily lives (e.g., family dining choices, community recreational facilities, public cultural events). Accordingly, the CES indicators constructed in this study aim to capture the characteristics of the cultural environment at the regional level, rather than directly measuring adolescents’subjective cultural values. This theoretical positioning draws on research on “cultural resources” and “intergenerational cultural transmission” in cultural sociology,90 treating parents’/adults’ cultural preferences as a meaningful proxy for adolescents’cultural exposure.

Structural equation modeling

SEM is a multivariate method that integrates confirmatory factor analysis and path analysis to examine the direct and indirect effects of multiple variables on a given outcome and the underlying mechanisms. Mediation effects refer to the indirect influence of an independent variable (X) on a dependent variable (Y) through a mediator (M), which can represent a single pathway or the sum of multiple mediating paths.91 The mediation effect is quantified as the product of the standardized regression coefficients along the X → M → Y path (ab), with larger coefficients indicating greater contribution. Nonparametric percentile bootstrap is commonly used for significance testing: by repeatedly resampling with replacement, a confidence interval (CI) for ab is constructed, and if the CI does not include 0, the mediation effect is considered significant.92 SEM has been widely applied in psychology, economics, and urban studies for multiple mediation analyses.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) tests whether a hypothesized factor structure fits the observed data and assesses the contribution of each indicator to its latent factor. The initial model we constructed did not meet the required fit indices, and there was significant multicollinearity among the independent variables. Following the variable selection strategy by Dzhambov,93 after eliminating redundant indicators (as shown in Table S2), we selected NDVI, CES values, SVF from street view data, GVI, and the density of POIs (restaurants, corporate enterprises, transportation facilities, commercial residential, disturbing livelihood service facilities, and healthcare) within the 500-meter buffer zone as independent variables. The “disturbing livelihood service facilities” are defined according to the “life services” category in the “Code for Classification and Coding of Urban Geographic Information Systems” (GB/T 28590-2012), specifically including public toilets, waste transfer stations, recyclable resource collection points, and other necessary urban infrastructure. Although such facilities are essential for residents’ daily lives, their physical environmental characteristics (e.g., odor, noise) may generate negative externalities in specific spaces such as park green spaces. We then reset covariance links and removed insignificant paths in AMOS 28.0, ultimately developing a simplified and robust structural equation model.

Following the model fit criteria proposed by Hu and Bentler,94 we evaluated model fit using chi-square tests, the standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR), the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA), and comparative fit index (CFI). Maximum likelihood estimation was used for parameter estimation.
