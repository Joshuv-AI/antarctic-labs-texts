Barriers and facilitators to implementing discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity: a qualitative study guided by the updated CFIR
Authors: Xing Wu, Xu Yao, Shanshan Dou, Jiahui Lv, Yajuan Feng, Ru Zhang, Ping Yan, Xiuyan Ye, Maidinaimu Nuermaimaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore barriers and facilitators to implementing a discharge preparation service program for older adults with multimorbidity, and to inform improvements in this service.MethodsUsing purposive sampling, we recruited 4 physicians, 14 nurses, 6 patients, and 6 caregivers for semi-structured interviews. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) guided the development of the interview guide and analysis. Inductive-deductive analyses were conducted.ResultsThe findings encompassed 20 constructs across the four CFIR domains (Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, and Individuals), identifying 11 facilitators, 14 barriers, and 2 neutral factors. Key implementation facilitators included a reliable evidence base, favorable environmental conditions and resources, multidisciplinary team establishment, and leadership support. Primary barriers comprised intervention complexity, increased labor costs, incomplete policies and models, lack of financial incentives, and poor compliance.ConclusionIn summary, this study comprehensively analyzed the potential influencing factors for implementing discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity. Providing discharge preparation services for this population requires not only a solid evidence base, but also appropriate staffing, convenient and standardized workflows, enhanced training for service providers, and supportive policy and referral infrastructure. These findings advance our understanding of the multilevel conditions necessary for successful implementation of discharge preparation services in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15061-5.

Introduction

Multimorbidity is defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual [1]. With population aging and increasing life expectancy, its prevalence rises markedly with age [2, 3]. In China, multimorbidity affects 65.14% of older adults [4], and the 30-day readmission rate after hospital discharge among these patients ranges from 20% to 22% [5, 6]. Compared with individuals with a single chronic condition, those with multimorbidity are at greater risk of premature mortality, hospitalization, and prolonged hospital stays [1]. Furthermore, these patients face persistent challenges in activities of daily living, disease management, and complications [7, 8].

Discharge Preparation Service (DPS), a continuum-of-care model originating in the United States, aims to improve patients’ discharge readiness and self-care capacity, ensure continuous and comprehensive post-discharge care, reduce readmission and recurrence rates, shorten hospital stays, and enhance patient satisfaction [9, 10]. Therefore, implementing comprehensive and standardized DPS is particularly important for optimizing health outcomes and disease management among older adults with multimorbidity [11].

To improve health outcomes among older adults with multimorbidity, our research team previously conducted a literature review, synthesized relevant evidence, and developed a discharge preparation service program for this population. The program includes admission screening and assessment, in-hospital planning and intervention, pre-discharge preparation and referral, and post-discharge follow-up and support. However, a substantial gap remains between available evidence and its application in clinical practice. Therefore, systematically identifying implementation barriers using evidence-based concepts and methods, and optimizing the program accordingly, is essential to facilitate its implementation [12].

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) is one of the most widely used determinant frameworks in implementation science. In 2022, Damschroder et al. updated the CFIR based on user feedback, revising its domains and constructs [13, 14]. The definitions of the CFIR domains are presented in Table 1. Understanding target groups’ expectations of clinical interventions and their perceptions of implementation determinants before implementation can inform the selection and adaptation of implementation strategies, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful implementation [15]. Moreover, applying theoretical frameworks in empirical research helps build a comprehensive knowledge base for effective implementation [16]. Therefore, guided by the updated 2022 CFIR, this qualitative study explored key barriers and facilitators to implementation from the perspectives of physicians, nurses, patients, and caregivers. The findings may provide a basis for developing implementation strategies and improving discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity.

Method

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive approach with directed content analysis to explore barriers and facilitators to implementing a discharge preparation service program for older adults with multimorbidity, with the aim of informing the development of sustainable and effective services for this population. A brief, targeted, non-systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Database using search terms including “multimorbidity,” “CFIR,” and “discharge preparation service.” Articles were selected according to their relevance to the study objectives, and the findings informed the specific context of this investigation. As the study was conducted before program implementation, the Implementation Process domain was excluded, and the remaining four CFIR domains (Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, and Individuals) were used to guide the development of the interview guides [18]. Within these domains, constructs most relevant to the pre-implementation assessment of discharge preparation services were selectively applied, consistent with the CFIR developers’ recommendation to select constructs appropriate to the study context [19]. Following team discussions, two initial interview guides were developed based on the CFIR interview guide [19, 20]: one for physicians and nurses, and another for patients and caregivers. The generic question templates for each CFIR construct were adapted to the study context. Pilot interviews were then conducted with one nurse, one patient, and two caregivers. Based on the pilot findings and feedback from clinical experts and research team members, the guides were revised and finalized; see Additional file 1. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [21].

Setting and sample

During the discharge preparation process, physicians and nurses are directly responsible for service delivery, while patients and their caregivers are the service recipients. Therefore, these four stakeholder groups were selected as study participants. Purposive sampling was used to maximize sample variation. Physicians and nurses were recruited with diverse professional ranks, educational levels, and years of clinical experience; patients varied in the number and types of chronic conditions; and caregivers differed in age and caregiving duration. This approach helped ensure a rich and complementary information base.

All participants were recruited from the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between November and December 2025. Patients and caregivers were recruited as two independent groups and were not paired. The included caregivers were not the direct caregivers of the patients in the study sample. This design ensured the independence of perspectives across groups, reduced informational homogeneity arising from familiar relationships, and enriched the diversity of viewpoints.

Sample size was determined according to Hennink et al.’s recommendations for qualitative research, with data saturation as the stopping criterion [22]. Saturation was assessed at the aggregate level across all participant groups rather than separately by group. Data collection ceased when no new information emerged during analysis.

Data collection

All research team members, including the interviewer (X.Y.), received training in qualitative research methodology. The training was delivered face to face by doctoral-prepared nursing faculty from Xinjiang Medical University and covered basic qualitative theory, study design, interview techniques, and data coding. Additional internal guidance was provided by senior researchers, Professor Yan Ping and Associate Professor Wu Xing, both of whom have experience in qualitative health research. Data were collected through one-on-one, face-to-face semi-structured interviews in Mandarin Chinese using an interview guide. Each interview was conducted jointly by two researchers, X.Y. and S.S.D., both master’s students in nursing. X.Y. served as the primary interviewer, while S.S.D. observed and recorded nonverbal cues, including facial expressions and body language, to support interpretation of participants’ perspectives. Interviews were conducted in a relatively quiet office or ward, with no other study participants, patients, or unauthorized personnel present, to minimize distractions. For bedridden patients, interviews were conducted only when safety could be ensured, with patients in a lying or sitting position and the interviewer seated at the bedside. Patients’ caregivers were allowed to be present, but these caregivers were not included as study participants. If the bedside environment was noisy or crowded, the interview was rescheduled to a quieter time. Interviews with patients and caregivers were conducted 1–2 days before discharge, while those with physicians and nurses were arranged during off-duty hours. Each interview lasted approximately 25–30 min. Neither researcher was familiar with the participants nor had worked at the study sites before the study. We acknowledged the assumption, derived from our prior program development work and nursing background, that discharge preparation services are beneficial, and recognized that this presupposition might have influenced our attention to certain data. To reduce individual bias, participants were encouraged to express dissenting views during interviews, and periodic team debriefings were conducted to examine how researchers’ backgrounds and assumptions might have shaped questioning or interpretation. Before each interview, participants were informed of the study purpose, significance, and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. The analysis identified 27 implementation discharge preparation service program was provided in printed form, and the interviewer explained its components and answered questions to ensure participants’ understanding before the interview began. During the interviews, techniques such as repetition, probing, and clarification were used as appropriate, and the need for audio recording was explained. Paper materials, including informed consent forms, were stored in locked cabinets in the Office of the Ethics Committee of the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. Electronic data, including audio recordings and transcripts, were stored on password-protected encrypted devices.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after each interview, two research team members (X.Y. and S.S.D.) transcribed the audio recordings verbatim into Microsoft Word documents and supplemented them with field notes. Any information that could identify participants was removed. Data were organized and managed using NVivo 15.0. To ensure accuracy and completeness, transcripts were translated from Chinese to English and then back-translated into Chinese by two team members (J.H.L. and Y.J.F.). A bilingual researcher (X.W.) carefully reviewed the translations, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by the research team to ensure semantic equivalence. Using a deductive–inductive approach, two researchers (X.Y. and S.S.D.) independently coded the data according to the CFIR Codebook [17], which provides definitions of domains and constructs, coding guidance, and inclusion and exclusion criteria for most constructs. Data were primarily deductively mapped to CFIR constructs. When data did not directly fit existing constructs, the researchers conducted close reading and inductively developed nuanced interpretations, which were then organized under the most relevant CFIR constructs to ensure comprehensive coverage. Although the Implementation Process domain was excluded a priori, the researchers remained attentive to any process-related content spontaneously mentioned by participants. When such content emerged, it was examined according to its substantive meaning and recoded into other applicable CFIR domains. In addition, coded constructs were classified as facilitators, barriers, or neutral factors based on the CFIR evaluation criteria [24]. Detailed rating criteria and final ratings are provided in Additional file 2. Throughout the coding process, the research team held regular meetings to discuss discrepancies until consensus was reached between the two coders (X.Y. and S.S.D.). Finally, all codes were reviewed by the principal investigator, Associate Professor Wu Xing.

The analysis identified 27 implementation determinants of discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity, including 11 facilitators, 14 barriers, and 2 neutral factors, organized within 4 CFIR domains and 20 constructs (Table 3).

Domain one: Innovation

Construct 1 Evidence-Base [Facilitator] Participants generally regarded the program as credible, given its foundation in the evidence summary. This perceived trustworthiness was a key facilitator, as stakeholders were more willing to engage with an intervention backed by rigorous evidence. Physician 1 noted: “We pay attention to certain evidence in everything we do.” Nurse 5 similarly emphasized: “The plan is based on the previous evidence summary, including high-quality guidelines and expert consensus at home and abroad, which makes me feel that the plan is trustworthy.”

Construct 2 Adaptability [Neutral factor] Some respondents emphasized the program’s flexibility, stating that it can be adjusted according to the actual situation. This adaptability was seen as crucial for navigating real-world constraints and individual patient needs. Nurse 4 explained: “About referral services, regular comprehensive evaluation after discharge seems not easy to achieve, appropriate adjustments can be made to facilitate the implementation.”

Construct 3 Trialability [Facilitator] The possibility of piloting discharge preparation services was widely endorsed, with most participants viewing trialability as a strategy to reduce implementation risks. Nurse 2 noted: “It can be tried in advance, and it can be very mature to do this content for more patients in the later period.” This perspective reflects confidence that phased implementation allows for refinement and capacity building. However, not all participants viewed trialability as an unqualified facilitator. Nurse 5’s observation—“feels that it can be gradual, and suddenly it may increase a lot of work”—reveals a conditional concern: even gradual implementation, if poorly planned, could compound workload rather than alleviate it.

Construct 4 Complexity [Barrier] Some respondents thought that the process of carrying out discharge preparation services was overly complicated. However, their accounts revealed different sources of complexity. Nurse 1 attributed complexity to workforce constraints: “We are now in a group system, and there are limitations in the level of nurses. Nurses are responsible for the entire process of patients from admission assessment to discharge follow-up, which is not easy to complete.” In contrast, Nurse 6 emphasized patient-level factors: “The patient is older and has a variety of chronic diseases. It is somewhat complicated to provide personalized discharge preparation services according to the patient’s condition.” Nurse 14 pointed to scheduling rigidity: “Fixed on patients discharged from hospital after seven days, one month, three months of follow-up, if the patient is more, there may be no way to accurately follow-up time (frown).”

Construct 5 Cost [Barrier] The anticipated increase in labor costs emerged as a consistent barrier across participant groups. Nurses highlighted staffing constraints: as Nurse 2 explained, “The ratio of personnel is insufficient, and in this case, if more people are selected to prepare for the discharge preparation service, the difficulty may be greater.” Nurse 12 similarly noted: “Every day everyone is busy with trivial work, which is not enough to support more work to complete related discharge preparation services.” A caregiver’s external observation reinforced this perception. Caregiver 1 noted: “I see they are sometimes busy running, sometimes delayed off work, I think it is not enough manpower, give me the feeling if you add some tasks to him is very difficult.”

Domain two: Outer setting domain

Construct 6 Local Attitudes [Barrier] Limited follow-up opportunities arose from patients’ and caregivers’ distrust of external communication. Nurse 11: “To prevent being deceived, patients and their carers may sometimes refuse to answer follow-up calls.”

Construct 7 Local Conditions (1) Geographic barriers emerged as a practical constraint, particularly for patients traveling from remote areas [Barrier]. Nurse 8 explained: “Some patients come from southern Xinjiang, so they’re in a hurry to catch the train after discharge.” (2) Low institutional trust in community healthcare facilities [Barrier]. Participants expressed low confidence in the professional competence of community healthcare providers, which discouraged patients from seeking care at these institutions. Patient 4 stated, “Facilities like health centers, service stations, and some county-level hospitals—not to mention those that don’t meet tertiary standards—nearly cost me my life on several occasions” (speaking angrily, with an increasingly emphatic tone). Caregivers offered a different vantage point. Caregiver 3 noted, “Community institutions don’t really focus on daily chronic disease management or fall prevention for older adults; at least I’ve never seen such services in our community.” Nurses found that the quality of different elderly care institutions varies greatly, making it difficult for patients and families to judge which institutions are trustworthy. Nurse 1 observed, “Among nursing homes, some have comprehensive medical facilities, while others simply hire staff to feed the elderly—just to keep them from starving.” Finally, physicians identified structural deficiencies in community infrastructure. Physician 2 stated, “Community hospitals may have difficulties in effective referral and lack certain medical and rehabilitation equipment.”

Construct 8 Policies & Laws [Barrier] Some participants identified inadequacies in referral policies and models. At the operational level, nurses highlighted how policy incompleteness creates practical dilemmas. Nurse 10 stated: “We are not in a position to recommend specific hospitals due to conflict-of-interest concerns. Currently, the policy remains incomplete in various aspects, creating certain difficulties in the referral process.” At the patient level, caregivers articulated how policy failures translate into fragmented, even dangerous, care experiences. Caregiver 4: “My father has multiple chronic conditions. When he needs to go to a specialized ward such as endocrinology, the doctors there only focus on endocrine issues and tend to ignore or superficially manage other problems. This elderly man had a severe urinary tract infection—why was he discharged without proper treatment, resulting in ICU admission just five days after returning home? Moreover, every hospital visit requires numerous tests, and when transferring between departments, the next department refuses to accept our patient even though the previous one had already completed all the examinations. (Angrily, and with an increasingly emphatic tone)” At the system level, physicians connected these observable problems to specific policy mechanisms. Physician 2: “Since the Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG) payment system is currently implemented, there are various policy restrictions on treatment.” Physician 1: “The current three-tier referral system is poorly implemented. (with a bitter smile)”.

Construct 9 External Pressure [Facilitator] Participants widely acknowledged that implementing discharge preparation services would confer competitive advantages on departments or hospitals. The advantage was framed in terms of patient satisfaction and professional recognition. Nurse 6 stated: “This will improve patient satisfaction and make them perceive us as more professional, resulting in greater recognition.” Nurse 9 added: “Higher satisfaction also increases patient loyalty, meaning patients become more willing to seek treatment from the same provider, department, or hospital.” Nurse 10 noted: “Discharge preparation services are well-established in developed countries and regions; implementing them would certainly give our department a competitive edge and generate positive social impact.”

Domain three: Inner setting domain

Construct 10 Structural Characteristics (1) Physical Infrastructure [facilitator]. Dedicated infrastructure and conditions for implementing discharge preparation services and comprehensive geriatric assessment were available. Nurse 8 stated: “Currently, our ward has a dedicated comprehensive geriatric assessment room equipped with instruments such as a body composition analyzer.” Patient 5 noted: “The hospital ward has a good environment and can meet my various activity requirements.” (2) Work Infrastructure [barrier]. Some clinical nurses had a vague understanding of the discharge preparation services concept and lacked the tools to implement the plan. Nurse 2 noted: “Many nurses in our hospital and other hospitals have never heard of the term ‘discharge preparation services’.” Nurse 12 added: “I recently joined the geriatrics department; on a scale of 0–10, my understanding of discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity would be only one point. (An awkward smile)” Nurse 13 added: “At present, the department does not have discharge manuals, videos and other materials related to older adults with multimulbidity, and may need to be produced.” Nurse 14 noted: “There is currently a lack of a clear flowchart for discharge readiness services.”

Construct 11 Relational Connections and Communications (1) Multidisciplinary collaboration infrastructure [facilitator]. The hospital had established systems for multidisciplinary consultations, and some departments had formed dedicated multidisciplinary teams. Nurse 1 noted: “The department director has established a multidisciplinary team, and pharmacists and rehabilitation specialists participate in joint ward rounds during morning handovers.” Nurse 5 highlighted supportive team dynamics: “If I encounter difficulties with a task, I can request the head nurse to reassign staff or help mediate the situation.” (2) Fragmented multidisciplinary collaboration [barrier]. Some participants reported challenges in coordinated teamwork. Nurse 4 explained: “Due to factors such as different work locations, it is rare for multidisciplinary team members to convene and discuss a single patient.” Nurse 7 added: “Communication with pharmacists and rehabilitation therapists is limited.”

Construct 12 Culture [Facilitator] Healthcare professionals have patient-centered values. Patient 5 added: “My attending doctor is very kind, and I really enjoy communicating with her.” Caregiver 4 noted: “Besides excellent medical skills, medical ethics and professionalism are also essential. I think the healthcare staff at this hospital excel in this area—I’d travel here from anywhere for treatment.”

Construct 13 Tension for Change [Neutral factor] Participants held divergent views regarding the urgency of implementing the change. Proponents emphasized its necessity: Nurse 6 stated, “If we aim to enhance our service quality and patient satisfaction, I believe it is very much needed,” while Nurse 10 asserted, “I consider discharge preparation services necessary for every patient.” In contrast, Nurse 9 expressed reservations: “It is not particularly urgent. First, we have no prior exposure to these initiatives; second, we are currently engaged in another project.” Nurse 7 noted: “We are also attempting to develop educational videos to provide patient guidance.”

Construct 14 Compatibility (1) Congruence with existing clinical workflows [facilitator]. Some participants perceived alignment between the program and current practices, though this congruence was partial rather than absolute. Physician 4 noted: “Mostly consistent, because we are doing these things too, but not exactly following this pattern.” (2) Perceived misalignment between the programand clinical reality [barrier]. More extensively discussed were various forms of incompatibility, which clustered around distinct temporal and behavioral dimensions. Nurse 1 noted: “Comprehensive geriatric assessment requires considerable time to complete. Even when offered free of charge, patients are reluctant because they perceive it as wasting their time. (In a joking tone)” Nurse 7 explained: “Typically, patients are notified of discharge on the same morning. Many simply want to leave immediately after receiving their discharge documentation and are unwilling to listen to explanations, let alone undergo pre-discharge assessments.” Nurse 11 explained: “Patients are unwilling to return to the hospital specifically for post-discharge comprehensive assessment, nor do we conduct home-based evaluations.” Caregiver 3 noted: “Regular comprehensive assessments after discharge—essentially, patients only return when they themselves feel unwell, correct?”

Construct 15 Incentive Systems (1) Professional development opportunities [facilitator]. Participants identified potential for knowledge acquisition and skill enhancement. Nurse 1 noted: “Systematically reviewing a patient’s condition from start to finish reinforces my own memory and builds clinical experience.” Nurse 11 stated: “In the process of promoting this initiative, I would naturally gain more knowledge in this area.” (2) Absence of material incentives [barrier]. Despite these intrinsic motivators, participants identified a critical gap in tangible rewards. Nurse 6 explained: “Since this generally does not involve chargeable services, it may increase workload without corresponding enhancement in performance-based compensation. (Waving her hand, indicating helplessness)”.

Construct 16 Access to Knowledge & Information [barrier] Some participants expressed unwillingness to engage in training activities. Nurse 1 highlighted practical constraints: “Training will inevitably encroach upon our off-duty time.” Nurse 2 identified procedural uncertainties: “How to train nurses in discharge preparation services and how to assess training competency remain unclear.”

Domain four: Individual domain

Construct 17 Innovation Recipients [barrier] Poor adherence among patients and caregivers was widely reported, though participants attributed this barrier to different underlying causes. Some emphasized patient characteristics: as Caregiver 3 noted, “Older adults, particularly those over 80, are stubborn and confused; they simply disregard our advice and do as they please.” Physician 3 similarly observed, “Patients have their own established behaviors and mindset; even when instructed, they fail to comply or cooperate.” Other accounts pointed to family dynamics rather than patient traits. Physician 1 explained: “Some patients have family conflicts, and caregivers do not want to take care of patients, so no matter how much healthcare professionals train caregivers, it is useless.”

Construct 18 Need [facilitator] Participants expressed a need for discharge preparation services and related knowledge. Patient 1 stated: “We hope to receive follow-up calls after discharge asking about our condition, recovery progress, medications, and so on.” Patient 2 noted: “I hope to receive a call from my attending physician after discharge, as he best understands my condition.” Caregiver 2 stated: “I want to learn more to better take care of the patient.” Nurses identified their own knowledge gaps as drivers of need. Nurse 11 reported: “My own understanding in this area is insufficient, so I believe I should learn more about these aspects.”

Construct 19 Capability (1) Caregivers lack relevant care knowledge [barrier]. Participants identified gaps in family caregivers’ professional knowledge and technical skills. Nurse 1 noted: “Patient readmissions sometimes result from family caregivers’ negligence.” Nurse 10 explained: “Family caregivers lack professional knowledge and caregiving skills; improper patient repositioning results in pressure ulcers.” Nurse 14 noted: “Different caregivers have varying levels of caregiving ability, and the information obtained during follow-up is not necessarily accurate. ” Physician 3 stated: “In China, it is common for adult children to care for elderly parents. When several children take turns caring for a patient, some caregivers are aware of the health education content while others are not.” (2) Some nurses lack solid theoretical knowledge and practical experience [barrier]. Participants acknowledged limitations in their own competencies. Nurse 1 added: “Typically, the department chief or head nurse provides suggestions, as my own knowledge is limited in its ability to help patients.” Nurse 2 stated: “When providing health education to patients, I can offer some information, but there remain areas where my teaching is inadequate.” Nurse 3 observed: “Most nurses in the ward are relatively junior and lack understanding of disease progression and subsequent changes in patient conditions.”

Construct 20 Motivation (1) Leadership support [facilitator]. Participants highlighted institutional backing for discharge preparation services. Nurse 8 stated: “Our department director is quite supportive, and our department is already implementing similar initiatives.” Nurse 10 stated: “We really want to implement discharge preparation services to benefit the patient and reduce their re-hospitalization, rather than doing it for scientific research.” (2) Potential for professional recognition and economic benefits [facilitator]. Participants anticipated both symbolic and tangible rewards. Nurse 4 noted: “If patients are highly satisfied, they might present me with a banner of appreciation.” Nurse 9 noted: “Although readmission rates may decrease, when patients require hospitalization again, they will still choose our hospital. Moreover, other patients may seek care here after hearing about our quality services. With increased patient volume and workload, there may be enhanced economic benefits. (nodding in agreement)”.

Discussion

Formative, or pre-implementation, evaluations are valuable for clarifying how local context may shape the feasibility and sustainability of a new intervention before it is formally introduced into practice [25]. In this study, the updated CFIR provided a structured lens for interpreting the conditions under which a discharge preparation service program for older adults with multimorbidity could be adopted, adapted, and sustained. Consistent with other formative CFIR evaluations, the Implementation Process domain was excluded because the program had not yet been implemented [26]. Rather than representing isolated determinants, these factors highlight that implementation readiness depends on the alignment among intervention design, institutional capacity, external policy environment, and stakeholder preparedness.

From the perspective of the innovation domain, the role of evidence should be understood not only as a scientific basis for intervention adoption, but also as a source of legitimacy for clinical decision-making in a risk-sensitive healthcare environment. This interpretation is consistent with Nibbelink et al. [27], who emphasized that credible, valid, and generalizable evidence is fundamental to translating clinical decisions into practice. However, evidence derived primarily from international contexts may not be directly transferable to local clinical settings. Therefore, before implementation, the program should undergo contextual adaptation to ensure that its components are compatible with local care pathways, patient needs, and resource conditions [28]. At the same time, the perceived complexity of the program highlights the need to balance comprehensiveness with feasibility, as in other CFIR-guided pre-implementation studies [26]. Complex interventions are more likely to be perceived as burdensome when their procedures are not clearly embedded into routine practice [29, 30]. To reduce implementation burden, core intervention components should be prioritized, clinical pathways should be clearly defined, and complex tasks should be divided into manageable modules [29]. Because human resource constraints may further weaken continuity and fidelity, implementation planning should also include competency-based training, rational scheduling, pilot testing, and iterative refinement before wider dissemination [31].

The outer setting findings suggest that successful implementation requires more than institutional willingness. Although discharge preparation services may enhance hospital reputation and respond to growing social demand, these potential advantages can only be realized when external service networks are sufficiently developed. In regions with geographic barriers and limited trust in primary care, post-discharge continuity may be difficult to achieve through hospital-based efforts alone. This concern is consistent with Wang et al. [32], and supports the need for a “hospital–community–family” linkage model. Such a model could combine telemedicine follow-up, community capacity building, public education, and multimodal health education materials, including illustrated and dialect-based resources, to improve accessibility and patient understanding. Policy-level coordination is also essential [33]. Payment and referral policies that are designed to improve efficiency may unintentionally weaken continuity of care if responsibilities across healthcare levels remain unclear. Therefore, clarifying referral accountability and establishing unified electronic referral platforms may help ensure timely information transfer and strengthen coordination across care settings.

Within the inner setting, the central issue is not simply whether the program is valuable, but whether it can be integrated into existing organizational routines. When new services are not aligned with current workflows, healthcare professionals may regard them as additional tasks rather than as part of standard care, a concern also noted by Becky et al. [29]. Targeted communication and education may help staff understand the rationale, expected benefits, and practical procedures of the program, thereby improving acceptance of change. In addition, the absence of billing items and performance-related incentives may limit motivation, especially when staff are already working under high workload pressure. Healthcare institutions should therefore consider incentive mechanisms that recognize both departmental contributions and individual professional effort, such as external training opportunities, performance rewards, or formal recognition of service participation. Multidisciplinary collaboration is another organizational prerequisite. This interpretation is consistent with Li [34] and Ward-Stockham et al. [35], who similarly emphasized that unclear role boundaries and inadequate coordination can weaken team-based care. As suggested by Fu et al. [29], team-based education may help different professionals recognize each other’s expertise and develop mutual trust. Clear role division in assessment, discharge planning, referral, and follow-up, together with structured communication mechanisms and quality control groups, may support more efficient and coordinated implementation.

At the individual level, leadership support should be viewed as a mechanism for transforming discharge preparation services from a project-based activity into an organizational priority. Top-down change requires leaders to provide strategic direction, allocate resources, and create a supportive climate for staff participation, consistent with Mitchell et al. [36]. Leadership engagement may be strengthened by making frontline contributions visible, for example through case presentations, experience-sharing sessions, and recognition of nurses’ professional development during service delivery. For patients and caregivers, implementation strategies should address differences in health literacy, cultural background, and care capacity. Personalized education materials, such as simplified patient versions of discharge preparation flowcharts, dialect-language videos, and illustrated guides, may improve comprehension and adherence. Caregivers should also be regarded as active partners in implementation rather than passive recipients of information. Because caregiver support can improve patient outcomes and strengthen self-care capacity [37], caregiver training should be incorporated into routine discharge preparation. Helping caregivers understand the purpose and value of the service may further enhance their cooperation and participation [38]. For healthcare professionals, capacity building remains essential. Training programs should include not only knowledge delivery, but also evaluation, feedback, and supervision mechanisms to ensure that staff acquire and retain the skills needed to deliver discharge preparation services effectively [39].

To move beyond determinant identification and support practical implementation, future work should map the CFIR-based determinants to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC), a well-established compilation of implementation strategies [40]. The CFIR–ERIC matching tool can be used to identify additional strategies that correspond to the barriers and facilitators identified in this study [41]. These strategies may then be refined through Delphi stakeholder consensus to ensure their relevance, acceptability, and feasibility within the local healthcare system, ultimately supporting the adoption and sustained implementation of discharge preparation services for older adults with multimorbidity.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it employed the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) as its theoretical framework, applying CFIR systematically across data collection, analysis, and interpretation. Second, the simultaneous inclusion of four core stakeholder groups—Physicians, nurses, patients, and caregivers—enhanced the credibility and contextual richness of our findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the perspectives of other stakeholders—including pharmacists, rehabilitation therapists, dietitians, social workers, and community healthcare personnel—were not captured, which may have constrained the diversity and richness of the data. Second, this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in China with a relatively small sample size, thereby limiting generalizability. Third, as a pre-implementation study, we were unable to utilize the complete CFIR structure (i.e., the Implementation Process domain was excluded). Fourth, While team discussions were held regularly to resolve coding discrepancies, we did not maintain individual reflexive journals throughout data collection and analysis. This may have limited our ability to systematically track each researcher’s evolving assumptions, emotional responses, and positional influences over time. Finally, all interviews were conducted in Chinese and translated into English for analysis; despite forward-backward translation and bilingual review, subtle linguistic and cultural nuances may have been attenuated during this process.

Despite these limitations, the findings hold significant implications for future research and clinical practice. We plan to implement the discharge preparation service program for older adults with multimorbidity and intend to use this study as baseline data to compare barriers and facilitators identified during and after implementation, thereby enhancing the sustainability and effectiveness of the program.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of potential facilitators and barriers to implementing a discharge preparation service program for older adults with multimorbidity. Guided by the CFIR framework, perspectives from physicians, nurses, patients, and caregivers were examined across four domains: innovation, individuals, inner setting, and outer setting. These findings clarify the multilevel conditions required for successful implementation and may inform more targeted responses to the identified barriers. Future research should translate these findings into actionable implementation strategies and evaluate their effectiveness in real-world clinical settings.
