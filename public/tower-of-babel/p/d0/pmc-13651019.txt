Postnatal care services utilisation in sub-Saharan Africa countries: a scoping review
Authors: Monsurat Adepeju Lateef, Desmond Kuupiel, Julian David Pillay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPostnatal care is a critical component of maternal and newborn health services. Most maternal and infant deaths occur during this time according to the World Health Organisation, yet its utilisation rates remain suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries. Thus, this scoping review aimed to examine, describe, and identify the gaps in the utilisation of PNC services.MethodA scoping review guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework was conducted. The review study explored the literature published from 2013 to 2024 on PNC services utilisation in sub-Saharan African countries. A search of five electronic databases search engines: PubMed, SCOPUS, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and the WHO African Index Medicus, was undertaken for relevant articles. A backward and forward reference check was done to identify additional relevant literature. Duplicates were removed, two authors independently screened the abstract and full text, and data extraction of the included articles. Results are presented narratively after an exploration of the data was undertaken with descriptive analyses.ResultsA total of 350 potentially eligible articles were screened, and 32 studies were included for data extraction and analysis. From the 32 included studies, the majority were from Ethiopia (n = 21, 65.6%), with most of the studies published between 2019 and 2024 (n = 23, 71.9%); a cross-sectional study design was largely employed (n = 28, 87.5%). The included studies revealed the following themes: Awareness and knowledge of PNC healthcare services, resources in the healthcare system, and urban-rural gaps and disparities.ConclusionThere is low utilisation of postnatal care services in the aforementioned places in this study. There is a need for significant improvements in maternal and child healthcare utilisation by focusing on the areas of greatest need. This can be enhanced through the development of more health facilities close to the community residence to improve accessibility; provision of access to transportation and awareness of the relevance of postnatal care, as a few key strategies. The education of the girl child should be a priority in order to improve PNC services utilisation. Interventions should be targeted at rural areas where there appears to be a significant dearth of services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09631-y.

Background of the study

Postnatal care (PNC) services play a critical role in decreasing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality rates, especially in many African regions [1, 2] It is an essential component of obstetric care that is vital in preventing of complications during and after childbirth [1, 3, 4]. The first twenty-eight days of life are the most vulnerable periods for both mother and child survival, and it lasts for six weeks according to the World Health Organisation (WHO) [5, 6]. The prevalence of maternal and infant deaths is highest in the first month following delivery: almost 50% of maternal deaths in the postnatal phase occur within the first 24 h [7], while 66% happen during the first week [8]. In 2013 alone, there were 2.8 million newborn deaths within the first month of life, with 1 million occurring within the first day [7, 9]. Considerable progress has been made globally to improvement of maternal health, with approximately 72% of women worldwide give birth with the support of skilled personnel [10], which has led to the reduction in maternal mortality ratio from 380 to 210 per 100,000 live births between 2000 and 2013 [10]. Yet, in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), only 48% of women are attended by skilled personnel during childbirth [7], also less than half of women receive a postnatal care visit within 2 days of childbirth [9]. Furthermore, in SSA, maternal and neonatal mortality are major issue, 40% of women are affected with complications after delivery [1, 5, 11].

Though PNC is a crucial aspect of the ongoing health services for mothers and newborns, it significantly helps in reducing deaths and helps avoid lasting health issues, its use and quality are still not at satisfactory levels levels [12, 13]. This reveals that poor awareness of PNC information and knowledge is one of the major reasons that influence underutilization of PNC services amongst mothers in SSA [14, 15].

PNC ensures immediate assessments of the newborns and the mother for danger signs during the postpartum period [2]. This care is to be provided within 24–72 h, 7–14 days, and 6 weeks after birth [1, 2].It is reported that in 2020, every two minutes, obstetric-related complications occurred, leading to maternal death, and 3 million newborn babies died during the first few weeks to months of life due to insufficient provision of PNC [16]. Almost 95% of all maternal deaths occurred in low-and lower-middle-income countries in 2020 [17]. This trend of mortality rates is unacceptably alarming, as most of these deaths can be prevented with the right care by skilled health professionals before, during, and after childbirth to save the lives of women and newborns [17]. The fact that nearly all of the deaths are preventable and occur in low- and middle-income countries could be an indicator of poor healthcare systems. Furthermore, the lack of access to quality healthcare, lack of health education, and socioeconomic status contribute to these deaths [17–19]. To achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target on maternal mortality rate reduction and prevention of neonatal deaths, PNC services are key components and essential to improve reproductive, maternal, and child health [1, 2].

The year 2015 vs. 2016 marked the beginning of the SDGs, which aim at reducing the global preventable maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 deaths per 100,000 live births, and neonatal mortality to 12 per 1,000 live births by 2030 [18, 19]. In most countries in SSA, maternal deaths that occur due to complications after childbirth are increasing despite the global commitment [18]. It is imperative to highlight that one of the major benefits or roles of PNC services is the improvement of the health and well-being of mothers and newborns. This contributes towards SDGs 3.1 and 2; The aim is to meet a target of reducing maternal and neonatal deaths by 2030 [18]. The aforementioned is borne out of the need to curb how many mothers, babies, and children die from preventable obstetric complications, particularly in SSA countries [20].

Research evidence indicated that despite the progress in improving maternal, neonatal, and child health, there are still high disproportions of PNC services in low-and middle-income countries [21]. For example, a study conducted in Ethiopia indicated that only 34% of mothers received PNC within 48 h [22]. Ghana also indicated 25.4% of PNC within 48 h, and only 8.0% out of the 25.4% of mothers completed PNC for their children [23]. All pregnant women need access to maternal healthcare and quality PNC services. Although the maternal mortality ratio per 100,000 live births has improved by 218 deaths in 2020 worldwide. While neonatal mortality rate (NMR) improved by 19.5 deaths in 2020, from 36.8 deaths per 1,000 live births to 17.3 deaths per 1, 000 live births. There is still a high number of maternal deaths in some areas of the world. This highlights the gap that exists in different regions. In 2020, low-income countries had 430 per 100,000 live births versus 13 per 100,000 live births in high-income countries.

It is reported that women experience obstetrical complications that are life-threatening [5], and the trend in the utilisation of PNC services shows only one-fourth of women received all the services provided after birth for newborns [22, 24]. Yet, PNC is essential for mothers and their babies when talking about health and well-being; it helps to identify any health problems that may have arisen during antenatal care to ensure that both (the mother and child) receive the necessary healthcare and support needed after delivery. These services are underutilized. For instance, in Congo, 93% of pregnant women receive antenatal care (ANC) and skilled birth attendance but only 35% of mothers and newborns obtain PNC services [25]. In Kenya, out of 90% of pregnant mothers who received ANC services, only 47% use PNC services after delivery [26]. It is cogent to state that this underutilization leads to missed opportunities like diagnosis, quality care and treatment. Furthermore, causing disability and death, which negatively impact the mother, the newborns, and the nation.

The high drop in the PNC level of maternal and child healthcare, despite the importance of the services, prompted the investigation of this study. Yet there are limited available studies on the utilisation of PNC services [27], particularly in SSA countries. This study aimed to identify existing evidence on the utilisation of PNC services, where the gaps are, and to use the knowledge as a basis for recommendation for policy practice.

This study will contribute to the existing body of evidence on PNC services in relation to individual, community, and health system factors, thereby offering a more detailed comprehension of both the receipt of care by women and the degree to which such care aligns with the WHO recommended standards. In addition, the study is crucial for guiding policy interventions tailored to specific contexts, enhancing health systems, and ultimately advancing maternal and newborn health outcomes throughout SSA region. Thus, the purpose of this review was to examine, describe, and identify the gaps in the utilisation of PNC services through the evidence of a scoping review.

Methods

The importance of clarity, consistency, and transparency throughout all stages of a scoping review process is critically significant to ascertain that the findings are rigorous, comprehensive, and trustworthy [28]. To achieve this, this study utilized the methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley, which was further refined by Levac and colleagues [28, 29]. The framework facilitated a thorough examination of the literature regarding the use of PNC services in sub-Saharan Africa, allowing us to identify research gaps and systematically derive significant conclusions from the existing evidence base. The following steps were considered “identification of the research question, identification of relevant studies, selecting studies process, data charting, compiling, summarising, and presenting findings.” [29, 30].

Identifying relevant studies

To identify the relevant studies, eligibility criteria were used which included publication date (from January 2013 to January 2024), language of publication (English), and geographical location (Africa). Both quantitative and qualitative studies were considered. The time frame— 2013 to 2024, was selected based on the policy practice of the 2013 recommendations on maternal and newborn care for a positive postnatal experience by the WHO [32]. In addition, the 10 years period was also used to search for recent studies to capture the trends and developments regarding postnatal care in SSA countries. The following databases were searched: Medline, SCOPUS, and Web of Science. Other databases were the WHO African Index and Google Scholar. The search terms were ‘Postnatal care’, ‘child health, maternal health, utilisation, and Africa. Mesh term and Boolean were employed and adjusted to each database. Adjustments were made to the syntax to align with the specific requirements of each database (Supplementary file 1).

Study selection process

The developed Google form was created based on informed established inclusion and exclusion criteria using population, concept and context according to [31]. The Arksey and O’Malley established framework which was further developed by Levac, Colquhoun, and O’Brien [28, 29] guided in mapping evidence from empirical studies, including quantitative, qualitative, mixed method, and research thesis. The tool was pilot tested by two independent reviewers (MA and DK) using 3 study titles and abstracts before it was applied for the study to ensure accuracy. A search for duplicates was subsequently performed using the “Find Duplicate” feature in the EndNote reference manager to identify and remove all duplicates. All full-text articles from titles and abstracts screening that met our inclusion criteria were obtained. Thereafter, screened by MA & DK independently using the eligibility criteria characteristics as outlined by Levac and Colquhoun [28], which emphasize that inclusion and exclusion criteria must be well-defined and tailored to the specific research questions. Table 1. presents the eligibility criteria of the study. However, discrepancies between the two reviewers (MA & DK) were resolved using a third reviewer (JD). In addition, the PRISMA flow diagram was used to report (Fig. 1), the study selection process. This was done to keep records and ensure transparency and accountability [33].

Quality appraisal

The ‘Mixed Method Quality Appraisal Tool (MMAT)’ Version 2018 [30] was used to assess the included studies’ quality, reliability, and bias [34] This tool helped evaluate the suitability of the study’s aim, the relevance of the study design, the methods used for participant recruitment, the techniques for data collection, the approaches to data analysis, and the presentation of results. Scores were assigned as follows: 50% (low), 51–75% (average), and 76–100% (high). Two independent reviewers conducted the appraisal, and a third reviewer resolved all disputes.

Charting the data

Five articles (17.5%) from the eligible studies were pilot tested using an Excel spreadsheet to ensure effective data extraction. The pilot test aimed to identify potential data deficiencies during the extraction process and improve the Excel spreadsheet to ensure accurate data capture. Information such as author (s), year of publication, country, aim/objective, study design, study setting, and size, and the key study findings, were extracted from the included studies. Both inductive and deductive approaches were employed [35].

Compiling, summarising, and presenting findings

A hybrid approach was adopted for data extraction and analysis, combining inductive and deductive strategies [35]. This involved an in-depth analysis of the extracted information to identify patterns, themes, and trends related to the use of PNC services in Sub-Saharan Africa. The data were systematically compiled and analysed in a narrative format to enhance understanding. Findings were presented using descriptive analysis and narrative synthesis, ensuring a comprehensive representation. This detailed review of PNC service utilisation in Sub-Saharan Africa followed the standards outlined in the PRISMA-ScR checklist [36].

Our review included all the studies from sub-Saharan Africa. A total of 350 articles were identified during the search process. Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, data were finally extracted from 32 articles (Fig. 1). Out of the 32 included studies, the majority of the studies were conducted in Ethiopia (n = 21, 65.6%) and most of the studies were conducted and published between 2019 and 2024 (n = 23, 71.9%). Findings

Awareness and knowledge of PNC healthcare services

Several studies have been carried out that explore PNC service utilisation, as well as its outcomes. This review found that a range of factors, such as awareness of PNC, information, and knowledge, influences PNC service utilisation. (Table 2). Abota and Atenafu; Manote & Gebremedhin and Ngowi et al., studies in Ethiopia and Tanzania respectively reported that poor information and poor awareness about the availability of PNC schedule were the main reasons for not utilising and following PNC services [37, 49, 51]. The Olajubu et al. study in Nigeria revealed that providing information to mothers followed by reminder messages to them is an impetus to utilise PNC services [54]. The Angore et al. and Olajubu et al. studies in Ethiopia and Nigeria respectively indicated that maternal level of knowledge about PNC services is important and significantly associated with the rate of utilisation of PNC services [40, 54]. Berhe et al.; Ngowi et al. and Zeleke et al. studies in Ethiopia and Tanzania reported on lack of awareness of the need for PNC among women before childbirth [42, 51, 65], Zeleke et al. further identified traditional beliefs, religious practices, and waiting time as a barrier to PNC service utilisation [65].

Woldasemayat et al.; Tesfahun et al. and Beyene et al. studies conducted in Ethiopia respectively reported that awareness of the benefit of PNC services among the majority of the women prior to childbirth is linked to utilisation of PNC services [43, 58, 62]. Worku et al. study in Ethiopia investigated the effect of individuals in the utilisation of PNC. Their study found awareness about PNC services and the perception of the individuals are closely related to PNC service utilisation. Low awareness about the need for PNC among mothers was reported by [57, 63] to be a challenge to PNC utilisation services. Ibrahim et al. and Mohammed et al. studies in Ethiopia also established that promoting antenatal care and skilled delivery in rural areas will help mothers learn about PNC, improve awareness of PNC service and increase utilisation services [47, 52]., while quality antenatal care visits were reported by Abel et al.; Towongo et al.; and Awoke et al. in their studies respectively conducted in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda and Ethiopia [38, 41, 59] as influencing factors to PNC scaling-up. Knowledge of postnatal care services was reported to have a positive impact on postnatal care service utilisation in a study conducted by Gebrehiwot et al. in Ethiopia [45]. A study conducted in Tanzania by Konje et al. reported that mothers are not being scheduled for postnatal care by healthcare providers after childbirth which negatively affects PNC services uptake [68].

Maternal educational level was found to be positively associated with PNC service utilisation according to Berhe et al.; Sacks et al.; studies Wudineh et al. and Handebo et al. findings in Ethiopia, Zambia and Uganda respectively [42, 46, 64, 66]. Low educational level was identified to be associated with poor PNC service utilisation [60]. Sserwanja et al. study reported on mothers’ educational level [56]. Lack of formal education (61%) and socioeconomic variables impacted negatively on the use of PNC [55]. The rate of utilisation of PNC services was significantly associated with educational status [54]. The findings show that the higher the socio-demographic variable, the more the utilisation of PNC services is closely linked to certainty. The poor utilisation of PNC services was found to be associated with inadequate awareness, knowledge, illiteracy, low maternal educational level, PNC schedule, ANC visits and understanding of obstetric complications.

Resources in the healthcare system

The findings from the study conducted in Ethiopia highlight significant barriers and challenges faced by mothers in accessing and receiving PNC services. Their study reported the poor healthcare system and financial empowerment for maternal health fee services were statistically associated with improved PNC service uptake [39, 54, 63], transportation system [65] was also identified as barriers to PNC visits. Tessema et al. in a study conducted in the Central and Eastern Region of Africa and Yimer et al. reported accessing health services is a huge problem and determining factor for mothers to use PNC in SSA. Their findings further revealed that due to place of residence, access to health facilities is a big challenge to mothers [60, 67], which contributes to low PNC utilisation services. Somefun & Ibisomi; Zeleke et al. and Sacks et al. studies in Nigeria, Ethiopia, Uganda and Zambia respectively reported on proximity to the health facilities as significantly associated with low-to-non-utilisation of PNC services [41, 55, 57, 63, 65, 66]. Long distance to a health facility and lack of service were identified in a study conducted by Tesfahun et al. in Ethiopia as the most frequent reasons for not utilising PNC services [58]. Obstetric-related factors were identified by Handebo et al. to be significant determinants for utilising PNC services [46]. A study conducted in Tanzania by Konje et al. reported a shortage of staff, poor infrastructure and shortages of medical equipment supplies leading to an overwhelming workload, which thwarts the delivery of PNC services [48]. The study highlighted the negative impact of limited resources on PNC services [48]. The review shows the low uptake of PNC services. The factors identified as influencing the low uptake of PNC services were availability and accessibility of resources, distance to healthcare facilities, finance as well as poor roads and poor transportation system. These factors can be improved with targeted interventions from the government to improve the utilisation of PNC services.

Urban-rural gaps disparities

Several studies highlighted the significance and impact of rural-urban disparities. Tessema et al.; Yimer et al.; and Tiruneh et al. reported that place of residence was found to be associated with low PNC service utilisation [60–62, 69]. Low levels of utilisation of PNC among women from rural areas were also identified by Mekonnen et al. in Ethiopia [50]. The study conducted by Ibrahim et al. reported that only four out of ten mothers at least utilised PNC service once. Their findings established the need to improve focus antenatal care (FANC) for rural communities to help mothers learn about postnatal care and improve utilisation services [47]. Urbanisation was reported to be associated with higher odds of PNC service utilisation [42]. The study further revealed that the majority of the mothers who did not receive PNC (n = 1110, 73.3%), due to awareness, mode of transportation and many of the participants were from rural communities [42]. Dankwah et al. study conducted in Ghana reported that the odds of using PNC services among mothers who resided in a community with a higher poverty level was 0.60 (95%CI:0.44–0.81) and this was significantly associated with the non-utilisation of PNC services [44]. Their study further highlighted the importance of communities’ resources in relation to the non-use of PNC services [44]. The review highlighted that the provision and utilisation of effective PNC services are hindered by limited resource allocation in many rural areas.

Discussion

This scoping review examines the utilisation of postnatal care services in SSA. The study established that the PNC is crucial for positive experience of maternal and newborns for better health outcomes and reducing mortality rates, due to the fact that both are vulnerable to developing problems [70, 71]. It is revealed from this study, that lack of awareness of PNC information and knowledge is one of the major reasons that influence underutilization of PNC services amongst mothers in SSA region. A good information dissemination process and reminder messages to mothers [49]; can make all the difference and help improve the utilization of PNC [54]. Although it is also noted that traditional beliefs, cultural practices and waiting time are also contributing factors as regards underutilization of PNC [65]. Adequate awareness, knowledge and understanding about PNC services may lead to effective service utilisation. This could have a positive impact on the mothers.

This review demonstrates that there is need for promoting ANC and skilled delivery in rural areas as it will help increase knowledge about PNC services and also increase utilization of service [47, 52]. In addition, health promotion should be taken to their doorsteps—homes, community, health facilities, radio, and television to improve awareness and pregnancy outcomes. Policymakers should be inform and empower on the need to improve health systems and increase access to PNC services.

Our findings corroborate with the studies conducted in Tanzania and the Republic of Congo by Konje et la. and Abel et al. [38, 48]. Their findings reported limited health education by healthcare workers on PNC to pregnant women during ANC visits could have contributed to the low utilisation of PNC services. From the perspective of healthcare providers, only 38% provide education on PNC to women during ANC visits [38], while 62% of them provide no education, and 33% provide information on the danger signs after childbirth. Limited information on postnatal danger signs with rural pregnant women in Burkina Faso, Uganda and Tanzania was identified [72]. Similarly, poor knowledge influences the low utilisation of PNC services contributing to the poor utilisation and increasing mortality rates among women and newborn babies [48, 73–75]. Healthcare providers should therefore provide sufficient information on the importance of PNC for pregnant women during antenatal care visits. Constantly recapped PNC benefits and create substantial awareness strategies that could shift PNC utilisation in a direction leading to contribute towards achieving SDGs 3.1.2. In-service training for healthcare providers is also needed.

Education of the girl child as policy priority will also go a long way in reducing obstetrics mortality rate as it has been discovered that lack of formal education plays crucial role as regards PNC utilization. Maternal educational level was found to be positively associated with PNC service utilisation [42, 46, 66]. Low educational level was identified to be associated with poor PNC service utilisation [60]. Skilled healthcare professionals are advised to create awareness for PNC during ANC visit and also try to educate pregnant women about the necessity and advantages of PNC. This has been observed to improve the utilization of PNC in certain place [45].

Identical to our findings is Sabetghadam et al. study that reported that maternal education was significantly associated with good health outcomes during and after pregnancy [76]. Women with high literacy levels demonstrated better behaviour towards maternal and neonatal health services [77]. Nyondo-Mipando et al. study in Malawi also revealed that one of the things that facilitates understanding the relevance of utilising PNC services is the maternal level of education [78]. The density of service utilisation should be well understood in the region—health system and community context are evolving over time. However, care should be taken to minimise the risks of potential deterioration of service utilisation, urban-rural inequalities, healthcare accessibility and poor resources. Poor attitude of the healthcare providers which could be due to work overload leading to maltreatment of mothers being shouted at or ignored or being roughly examined when they come to the health facilities for PNC were all barriers to mothers. Governments, policymakers, and other stakeholders should improve the health system, recruit more healthcare workers and ensure female education is priority—thereby increasing maternal literacy and reduction in mortality rates.

From the findings of this study, the underutilization service can be largely traced to certain factors like urban-rural gap disparities, individual, socioeconomic, and physical environment [79]. A lot of women in rural areas do not have adequate access to health facilities due to lack of basic infrastructure that hinders transportation, lack of awareness and poverty level [42]. These are issues that SDGs should primarily target in order to improve the utilization of PNC services. Findings from Li et al. and Meng et al. respectively are consistent with our findings on urban-rural disparity [80, 81].

Other studies indicated the health systems are weak in rural areas leading to high health disparities [82, 83]. Studies revealed that the highest inequality occurred in rural settings [84, 85] – rural populations are faced with many challenges such as overcrowding in healthcare facilities due to limited healthcare facilities, transportation challenges, inadequacy of emergency medical services and limited drug supply along with limited manpower resources [84]. Chen et al. and Coombs et al. studies identified unfair health resource allocation in rural areas as another major factor affecting rural populations [86, 87].

Gaps in the resources in healthcare system were identified in this study. A dearth of utilisation of PNC was found in this scoping review. Long distance from place of residence to health facility, inadequate infrastructure, shortage of manpower and lack of equipment in many sub-Saharan Africa, were identified barriers in the healthcare system. These factors undermine the utilisation of PNC services contributing to the increasing mortality rate in sub-Saharan Africa. Similar studies conducted by Amsalu, Talie (87), Dzinamarira, Moyo (88) and Nyondo-Mipando, Chirwa (77) agreed with our findings that walking a long distance to the health facility is of great concern among mothers who reside in the rural community [78, 88, 89]. It gets worse when there is nobody to cater for the transportation to the health facilities. Then, the women are forced not to use healthcare facilities even when they are aware of the relevance. Previous studies reported on the significant impact of not being able to access healthcare services in relation to maternal mortality rate [90–92]. Similarly, Nyondo-Mipando et al. and Abdullah et al. reported on poor transportation and bad roads as barriers to healthcare services in the majority of the community settings [78, 93]. The effects of climate change cannot be undermined. Another study indicated inaccessibility to public health facilities care services may cause a decline in the utilisation of services [94, 95]. Hence, availability and accessibility of resources in the healthcare system is one of the challenges the government and other key stakeholders may want to address to improve PNC utilisation. It is recommended that the government build more health infrastructures not far from the place of residence of the community to expand and improve access to PNC services. Address the issue of inadequacy of medical equipments by providing medical equipment, recruit more nurses, midwives, and doctors to improve the quality of services provided to postnatal women.

In line with the findings from this study, SSA countries is unique with its own problems such as war, economic hardship. These have devastating consequences for women and newborns. Without access to proper healthcare, women and newborns are at an increased risk of complications and death. Therefore, gaps in postnatal care have a significant impact on vital health indicators, such as maternal and neonatal mortality. The health system has been severely affected by the political instability and economic hardship in many parts of SSA region. The outbreak of COVID pandemic 2020 then worsen the issue. All these factors have a catastrophic effect on the health of mothers and newborns. There is need for more future research on PNC in SSA.

Strengths and limitations of the study

An important strength of this review is that it covers a wide range of literature on PNC services utilisation in sub-Saharan Africa. Mapping this research evidence would serve as a valuable resource for researchers, policymakers, and health professionals. Having three reviewers assess the same data, makes the result of the review more reliable and accurate since it allows the reviewers to independently evaluate every study and identify any discrepancies or errors that may have been overlooked by one. A key contribution of this study is its ability to identify key themes based on the trends in literature, and important gaps in the existing research, which can be used to inform government policies and future studies. Despite the strength of this study, the work has potential limitations. The fact that all the studies are published in English may not reflect the diversity of the research population. Also, the majority of the studies in this area were from the Demographic Health Survey and all of them were cross-sectional design. In addition, the 65.6% of the included studies were conducted in Ethiopia, therefore, the findings can not be generalised to other setting. Further study on PNC services in SSA regions where the burden of maternal mortality rate is highest is required to fill the research gaps.

Implication for policy and practice

The low utilisation of postnatal care is of great concern due to access to healthcare and socioeconomic factors contributing to the mortality rate. Mapping this research evidence would serve as a valuable resource for researchers, policymakers, and health professionals. There is a policy intervention. Policy makers to strengthening health systems by establishing more health facilities, particularly preparedness, and reprioritisation of maternal needs of women and children at the grass root and the national level, to improve accessibility and acceptability of service. To train and recruited more healthcare worker to improve the quality of postnatal care services to the community particularly the rural areas. Targeted health awareness campaign programs for women about the benefits and adoption of quality service delivery of postnatal care, as well as health education to correct some cultural beliefs against the use of postnatal care. Women empowerment on financial protection and ensuring female children have formal education are key strategies that can be implemented as health interventions.

Conclusion

Postnatal care utilisation services is low from this study. There is a need for health awareness strategic intervention programs, and policymakers to make more allocation resources in the rural setting, increase and improve healthcare facilities can significantly improve the promotion of utilisation of postnatal care services by focusing on the areas of greatest need as identified from this study. Additionally, further studies on PNC services should be conducted in countries where maternal and neonatal mortality is not a burden.
