Impact of dual antiplatelet therapy duration after percutaneous coronary intervention on outcomes in acute coronary syndrome patients with and without chronic kidney disease: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Kai-Wei Chang, Pei-Lun Liao, Jing-Yang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) reduces thrombotic risk in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) but may increase bleeding, especially in chronic kidney disease (CKD). This study evaluated the impact of different DAPT durations on cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes in ACS patients with and without CKD. Using the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (2001–2018), patients ≥20 years with ACS who underwent PCI and received clopidogrel plus aspirin for ≥1 month were analyzed. Primary outcomes were cardiovascular events and bleeding events. Subgroup analysis was conducted by CKD status, which was defined using claims-based International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9/ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Among 4,800 patients with ACS identified (median age: 62 years; 77% male), there were no significant differences in cardiovascular events across DAPT duration groups. No associations between DAPT duration and cardiovascular outcomes were identified in patients with or without CKD. However, DAPT duration ≥7 months was associated with a higher incidence of other major bleeding in patients with CKD (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 29.7, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.80–491.05, p = 0.018). In patients undergoing PCI for ACS, no clear association was observed between longer DAPT duration and reduced cardiovascular events. In prespecified subgroup analyses, prolonged DAPT was associated with a higher incidence of bleeding events among patients with claims-defined CKD; however, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory given the observational design and low event rates. These results highlight the importance of individualized decision-making regarding DAPT duration, particularly in patients with increased bleeding susceptibility.Keywords: Reinfarction, acute coronary syndrome, percutaneous coronary intervention, antiplatelet therapy, Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD)

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) encompasses a spectrum of clinical conditions, including unstable angina, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), all of which are characterized by acute myocardial ischemia and infarction [1]. ACS represents a major global health burden, arising from coronary artery disease (CAD), which affects over 300 million people worldwide [2,3]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the standard treatment for ACS, effectively restoring coronary perfusion and reducing both short- and long-term adverse outcomes [4]. Despite the effectiveness of PCI, post-PCI thrombotic complications remain a major concern, necessitating optimal antiplatelet therapy to prevent the formation of thrombi [5].

Antiplatelet treatment can consist of single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) [6–8]. DAPT consists of aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor and is recommended for ACS patients undergoing PCI to minimize the risk of thrombotic events [6–9]. Despite its benefits, prolonged DAPT could increase bleeding risk, particularly in high-risk populations [10]. The optimal duration of DAPT remains a critical question, as shorter durations may reduce bleeding but increase thrombotic risk, while extended durations may provide greater ischemic protection at the cost of higher bleeding complications. Current guidelines recommend tailoring DAPT duration based on individual risk factors, but evidence supporting specific durations, particularly in high-risk groups, remains limited. For example, the 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend DAPT for 12 months with a potent P2Y12 inhibitor for patients with ACS, but they also suggest considering shorter durations in patients at high bleeding risk [11].

One particularly high-risk subset of ACS patients is those with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [12]. CKD affects approximately 10–15% of the global population and is increasingly recognized as a key comorbidity in patients with ACS [13]. CKD is known to influence both the pathophysiology of CAD and the pharmacokinetics of antiplatelet medications, complicating treatments and increasing the risk of adverse outcomes [14].

Given the limited data on the optimal duration of DAPT in ACS patients, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of different DAPT durations on clinical outcomes in patients who underwent PCI. Additionally, we assessed whether the effects of DAPT duration differed between patients with and without CKD.

Methods

Data source and ethics consideration

This population-based, retrospective cohort study extracted patient data from the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), which was a closed network database established in Taiwan in 1995 and now covers over 99% of the population (approximately 23 million people) [15]. The portal of the link is https://www.apre.mohw.gov.tw. The NHIRD was established by the National Health Research Institute (NHRI) and contains claims data and comprehensive patient and medical treatment information, including demographic characteristics, prescription drugs, medication usage, and diagnostic procedures [16]. Thus, it can be used to study the disease burden and health care process of the entire Taiwanese population.

To fulfill the requirements according to the Declaration of Helsinki, the study protocol for this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (IRB approval number: CS1-23189). Since this research used secondary data from a de-identified public insurance database and there was no direct patient involvement, the IRB waived the requirement for signed informed consent.

Study population

Patients ≥20 years old admitted for ACS and treated with PCI between January 2001 and December 2018 were identified in the NHIRD and were eligible for inclusion in the analysis. Patients with missing information on key baseline variables, including age, sex, or comorbidity data, were excluded. In addition, patients with prior coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery, those who underwent both PCI and CABG during the index hospitalization, and those who died due to ACS during hospitalization were also excluded. In addition, patients who were not treated with DAPT (i.e., clopidogrel + aspirin), used DAPT for <1 month after PCI, and used other antithrombotic agents than DAPT immediately after PCI were also excluded. Patients were then grouped by DAPT duration (1–3, 4–6, ≥7 months) for analysis. DAPT consisted of aspirin plus clopidogrel, as other P2Y12 inhibitors were not widely reimbursed in Taiwan. Cutoffs were chosen to reflect early discontinuation (≤3 months), intermediate duration (4–6 months), and prolonged therapy (>6 months), which corresponds to key clinical decision points in post-PCI management.

Formal interaction testing was not performed; instead, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted for exploratory assessment of potential effect heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were stratified by the presence or absence of CKD to evaluate whether the associations between DAPT duration and clinical outcomes differed according to renal function status. CKD was identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9/ICD-10) diagnostic codes recorded before or at the index hospitalization. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, CKD is defined by abnormalities of kidney structure or function persisting for more than 3 months; however, because laboratory data such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and albuminuria were not available in the NHIRD, CKD severity and KDIGO staging could not be determined. Therefore, the subgroup analyses based on CKD status should be interpreted as exploratory.

Clinical diagnoses and procedural information, including PCI and other cardiovascular procedures, were identified using ICD-9/ICD-10 codes as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Medication exposure was determined using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system codes recorded in outpatient and inpatient prescription claims (Supplementary Table S2).

Major outcomes and covariates

The primary outcomes were overall cardiovascular events and overall bleeding events. Cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes were ascertained using claims-based diagnostic and procedure codes recorded in the NHIRD, including ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Overall cardiovascular events were defined as readmission between 30 days and 1 year after PCI due to acute myocardial infarction (AMI), ischemic stroke (IS), or transient ischemic attack (TIA), or receipt of PCI, CABG, or fibrinolytic therapy during follow-up. This composite endpoint was designed to capture a broad spectrum of clinically relevant cardiovascular deterioration after PCI in a claims-based real-world dataset, including recurrent ischemic events and subsequent cardiovascular interventions. Because administrative claims data may not fully capture angiographic findings, lesion progression, or physician-assessed ischemic burden, procedure-based outcomes were included to identify clinically significant events requiring further cardiovascular treatment. Overall bleeding events were defined as a composite of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, and other major bleeding events. ICH, GI bleeding, and other major bleeding were also evaluated separately to provide a more detailed assessment of bleeding patterns.

Covariates evaluated in this study were selected a priori based on prior literature, clinical knowledge, and data availability in the NHIRD and included demographic characteristics, ACS subtype, comorbidities, and concomitant medication use considered clinically relevant to ischemic or bleeding outcomes after PCI. These were age, sex, comorbidities, and type of ACS. ACSs were separated into NSTEMI, STEMI, and unstable angina. Comorbidities studied were atrial fibrillation (AF), diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, systemic embolism, peripheral vascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, CKD, congestive heart failure, anemia, cerebrovascular disease (CVD), and any malignancy. Medication use before and after PCI was also evaluated. Preoperative antiplatelet drug use was categorized as no or irregular use, any single antiplatelet drug, and double antiplatelet or multiple antiplatelet drugs. After PCI, treatment with vitamin K-based oral anticoagulants, antihypertensive drugs, antiarrhythmic drugs, or switching clopidogrel or aspirin was also analyzed. The ICD codes used to identify these conditions are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared with the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and compared with the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were performed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for estimating the associations of DAPT durations and outcomes. Multivariable models were adjusted for covariates with a p-value of <0.05 in univariate analyses based on various outcomes. All p values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered to represent statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient selection

A flow diagram illustrating the study population selection process is presented in Figure 1. A total of 17,719 patients ≥20 years old who were diagnosed with ACS and underwent PCI between January 1, 2001 and December 31, 2018 were identified in the NHIRD. A total of 132 patients who underwent both PCI and CABG, 43 patients who died during hospitalization, 436 patients with a history of CABG, 3,036 patients who received DAPT for < 1 month, and 1,066 patients who discontinued DAPT without transitioning to SAPT were excluded. Ultimately, 4,800 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Patient characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 62.0 years, and 77% were male. Of AMI cases, 49% were categorized as STEMI, and the majority of patients transitioned to clopidogrel (81%). Patients with a longer duration of DAPT had higher frequencies of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker (ACEI/ARB) (66–72%), calcium channel blocker (CCB) (20–29%), diuretics (17–29%), type I and III antiarrhythmic agents (4.3–7.3%), and type IV antiarrhythmic agents (6.2–10.7%) use. Patients with a DAPT duration of 1–3 months had higher numerically crude rates of overall cardiovascular events (6.2% vs. 4.9%–6.0%), while those with a DAPT duration of ≥7 months experienced the highest percentage of overall bleeding events (2.8% vs. 1.7%–2.1%) (Table 1).

The associations between DAPT durations and outcomes are summarized in Table 2. After adjusting for relevant confounders in the multivariable analyses, there were no statistically significant differences in overall cardiovascular events between DAPT duration groups. Discussion

To address ongoing uncertainty regarding the optimal duration of DAPT after PCI in routine clinical practice, we examined the associations between DAPT duration and 1-year cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes in a nationwide cohort of patients with ACS. Notably, the DAPT duration categories were defined a priori to reflect real-world prescribing patterns and clinically relevant decision points after PCI, corresponding to early discontinuation (≤3 months), intermediate duration (4–6 months), and prolonged therapy (>6 months), rather than guideline-mandated thresholds. A key finding of this study is that no clear association was observed between DAPT duration (1–3, 4–6, or ≥7 months) and the overall rate of major cardiovascular events within 1 year after PCI among patients with ACS. Shorter DAPT duration (1–3 months) was not associated with a higher incidence of cardiovascular events compared with longer courses, although these findings should be interpreted in the context of the observational study design. In prespecified subgroup analyses, no significant differences in cardiovascular outcomes were observed across DAPT duration categories regardless of CKD status. In contrast, prolonged DAPT (≥7 months) was associated with a higher incidence of other major bleeding events among patients with claims-defined CKD; however, this finding was based on a small number of events and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. This observation is directionally consistent with prior evidence suggesting that extended antiplatelet exposure may increase bleeding susceptibility in patients with impaired renal function. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of individualized clinical decision-making when selecting DAPT duration, particularly in patients at increased bleeding risk.

Current international guidelines generally recommend around 12 months of DAPT after PCI for patients with ACS, with a suggestion to shorten or extend DAPT based on individual patient risk of bleeding or thrombosis. The 2016 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines and the 2017 ESC both recommend treatment with DAPT for 12 months in patients with ACS treated with PCI and advise considering a shorter duration for patients at high bleeding risk and an extended duration of treatment for patients at low bleeding risk and high ischemic risk [17,18]. These recommendations were largely driven by earlier trials demonstrating that a longer duration of DAPT was associated with benefits with respect to ischemia; however, the prolonged duration was associated with a slight increase in all-cause mortality. More recently, antiplatelet management after PCI has evolved toward tailored strategies, including DAPT de-escalation and personalized treatment approaches based on patient-specific thrombotic and bleeding risks. This issue is particularly relevant in patients with CKD, who simultaneously exhibit elevated ischemic and hemorrhagic susceptibility. In this context, a recent sub-analysis from the CORALYS registry suggested that P2Y12 inhibitor selection may influence ischemic and bleeding outcomes in patients with advanced CKD undergoing PCI [19], further highlighting the complexity of antiplatelet decision-making in high-risk populations. Our findings align with the bleeding concerns noted in previous trials but diverge on the necessity of prolonged therapy for ischemic protection. A meta-analysis by Palmerini et al. [20] demonstrated that DAPT beyond 1 year after drug-eluting stent implantation reduced myocardial infarction and stent thrombosis but was also associated with increased non-cardiovascular mortality. Similarly, the STOPDAPT-2 ACS trial showed that 1–2 months of DAPT followed by clopidogrel monotherapy failed to demonstrate non-inferiority compared with 12 months of DAPT in patients with ACS, with a numerical increase in cardiovascular events despite a reduction in bleeding events [21]. These observations further support the evolving concept of individualized antiplatelet strategies based on patient-specific ischemic and bleeding risk profiles. Studies performed since 2017 have given somewhat diverse results with respect to the benefits and risks of prolonged DAPT. While some randomized trials suggested that prolonged DAPT may further reduce ischemic events in selected patients without high bleeding risk [22], other studies and meta-analyses demonstrated that shorter DAPT strategies were associated with lower bleeding risk without a significant increase in ischemic complications [23,24]. In particular, recent evidence supporting abbreviated DAPT followed by P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy has contributed to the evolving concept of tailored antiplatelet therapy based on individual ischemic and bleeding risk profiles.

The results of our study found that a shorter duration of DAPT was not inferior to longer durations and even appeared protective in some respects. It is likely that the time-dependent nature of ischemic risk after PCI plays a role. The risk of stent-related thrombosis and recurrent ACS is highest in the initial weeks to months after PCI, when patients are almost universally receiving DAPT [25,26]. In contemporary practice, with improvements in stent technology, residual ischemic risk after ACS may increasingly arise from progression of non-culprit coronary lesions or systemic atherothrombotic vulnerability, including cerebrovascular and peripheral arterial beds. Therefore, the potential benefit of prolonged DAPT may not be interpreted solely in terms of preventing stent thrombosis, but rather within the broader balance between reducing systemic ischemic risk and increasing bleeding risk. Beyond the early period, especially after 3–6 months, the incremental benefit of continuing 2 antiplatelet agents diminishes as the coronary stent neointimal healing stabilizes. Thus, a short DAPT course may already have reduced risk in the ‘peak risk’ interval, and extending DAPT further yields little additional benefit. However, cumulative exposure to DAPT could increase bleeding risk, which can counteract any marginal benefit with respect to ischemic [20,27]. Bleeding complications not only cause morbidity directly but can also disrupt antiplatelet treatment continuity (e.g., due to required cessation for bleeding management), potentially negating the protective effects of DAPT.

Notably, our analysis of patients with CKD showed that patients with CKD who were treated with DAPT for ≥ 7 months had a significantly higher rate of major bleeding events, a finding that is generally consistent with those of other studies of patients with CKD. Patients with CKD are at high risk of bleeding after PCI; however, studies of patients who are at high risk of bleeding after PCI suggest that 1 month of DAPT is not inferior to the continuation of therapy for at least two additional months with respect to net adverse clinical events (NACE) and major adverse cardiac or cerebral events, and a shorter duration of treatment is associated with a lower incidence of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding [28,29]. Although not specifically focused on patients undergoing PCI, a Cochrane Database Systematic Review published in 2022 studied the effects of antiplatelet drugs in patients with CKD [14]. After analysis, the authors concluded that antiplatelet drugs probably reduce myocardial infarction and increase major bleeding in patients with CKD but do not appear to reduce all-cause and cardiovascular death among people with CKD. An earlier meta-analysis of patients with CKD treated with PCI and drug-eluting stents concluded that a short duration of DAPT (≤6 months) was not inferior to DAPT for 12 months with respect to a composite primary endpoint of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, or stent thrombosis (definite or probable) [30]. Notably, our real-world evidence underscores the clinical relevance of the association of DAPT duration and adverse events in patients with CKD and highlights the need for careful monitoring and individualized treatments for these patients.

It is relevant to postulate the potential mechanisms of the results seen in patients with CKD. Platelet function and drug metabolism are altered in patients with CKD, which simultaneously creates a prothrombotic and pro-hemorrhagic state [31]. Uremia can cause platelet dysfunction that paradoxically raises bleeding tendency despite high thrombotic risk [31]. In addition, antiplatelet drug pharmacokinetics may be affected by the active metabolites of some P2Y12 inhibitors (e.g., prasugrel) and the antiplatelet effects of ticagrelor can be increased in patients with reduced renal clearance [32]. As such, a standard DAPT treatment might have stronger or more prolonged effects in patients with CKD, elevating bleeding risk without a commensurate increase in anti-ischemic efficacy [33].

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study have important implications for individualizing antiplatelet therapy in patients post-PCI. Routine 12-month DAPT may not be optimal for every patient, particularly those with high bleeding risk, such as patients with CKD. Clinicians should carefully weigh an individual patient’s ischemic risk (e.g., diffuse disease, prior MI, diabetes) against their bleeding risk (e.g., older age, CKD, prior hemorrhage) when determining DAPT duration. For a substantial subset of ACS patients, especially those resembling our study population with higher bleeding propensity, a shorter DAPT course followed by continuation of a single antiplatelet drug (usually aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor) might achieve the best balance. Our study underscores the need for a personalized DAPT strategy, and by applying an individualized approach, shortening DAPT duration in patients with increased bleeding risk and potentially prolonging it in those at extreme ischemic risk, outcomes can be optimized by improving safety without sacrificing efficacy.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of the study lies in its use of a large, population-based dataset, the Taiwanese NHIRD, which includes comprehensive, real-world clinical data from a broad cohort of ACS patients. The large sample size and inclusion of both patients with and without CKD enhance the generalizability of the findings and provide valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of different antiplatelet regimens in this high-risk population.

However, this study has several limitations. As a retrospective observational analysis, it is subject to confounding and cannot establish causality. Although multivariable logistic regression was used to adjust for measured covariates, DAPT duration was not randomized, and confounding by indication may persist; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

In addition, DAPT duration was defined as a post-baseline exposure based on observed prescription patterns after PCI; thus, immortal time bias and reverse causation cannot be fully excluded, as patients classified into longer-duration groups must have survived and remained event-free long enough to receive prolonged therapy. Conversely, patients who experienced early adverse events, such as bleeding or death, were more likely to discontinue therapy prematurely and thus be categorized into shorter-duration groups. These factors may introduce systematic differences between groups and potentially bias the observed associations, thereby limiting causal interpretation. Outcomes were also evaluated within a fixed follow-up framework using logistic regression, without accounting for event timing, competing risks, or recurrent cardiovascular and bleeding events. In addition, only the first occurrence of each outcome was analyzed, and recurrent cardiovascular or bleeding events were not modeled, which may underestimate the total event burden, particularly in high-risk populations such as patients with CKD. Although time-to-event or cumulative incidence approaches may provide additional insight, the current analysis was designed to assess overall event occurrence within a clinically relevant follow-up period. Moreover, ischemic and bleeding outcomes were analyzed separately, and a formal integrated measure of net clinical benefit, such as NACEs, was not performed.

The use of administrative claims data may also introduce misclassification or incomplete capture of clinical factors such as medication adherence. Cardiovascular and bleeding outcomes were ascertained using claims-based diagnostic and procedure codes; therefore, event misclassification or incomplete capture of events is possible. In particular, events that did not result in coded medical encounters or hospitalizations may not have been captured, which may partly explain the relatively low event rates observed in this study. Because the NHIRD does not include laboratory data such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albuminuria, or proteinuria, CKD severity, KDIGO staging, and renal phenotypes associated with differential ischemic and bleeding risks could not be evaluated. This limitation may reduce the granularity of CKD subgroup analyses and should be considered when interpreting renal-specific findings. Furthermore, information on stent type (bare-metal versus drug-eluting) was unavailable, and bleeding risk scores such as HAS-BLED or PRECISE-DAPT could not be calculated due to missing laboratory and clinical parameters; therefore, residual confounding related to baseline bleeding susceptibility may remain. The composite cardiovascular endpoint incorporated both ischemic events and procedural outcomes (revascularization or fibrinolytic therapy) to comprehensively capture real-world clinical deterioration and repeat interventions following PCI. However, procedural outcomes may also reflect physician decision-making, institutional practice patterns, and coding behavior rather than true clinical events, which may introduce heterogeneity in outcome interpretation. In addition, because multiple outcomes and DAPT duration comparisons were evaluated, the possibility of chance findings due to multiple comparisons cannot be excluded. No formal multiple-comparison adjustment was performed; therefore, statistically significant subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory. Finally, certain subgroup analyses, particularly among patients with CKD, were based on a limited number of events, resulting in wide CIs and imprecise estimates; these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

In this Taiwanese national cohort of patients with ACS undergoing PCI, no clear association was observed between longer DAPT duration and reduced cardiovascular events within 1 year. In prespecified subgroup analyses, prolonged DAPT was associated with a higher incidence of bleeding events among patients with claims-defined CKD; however, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory given the low event rates and observational design. These findings provide additional real-world evidence regarding DAPT duration in patients undergoing PCI and may contribute to the ongoing discussion surrounding antiplatelet treatment strategies in patients with varying bleeding risk profiles. Further prospective and randomized studies are warranted to clarify the optimal duration of antiplatelet therapy in high-risk populations.
