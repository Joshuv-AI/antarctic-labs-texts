Assessing basic Emergency Obstetric and Neonatal Care (BEmONC) service provision in community health centers: a mixed-methods study from Jember, Indonesia
Authors: Maria Cellina Wijaya, Courtney M Yuen, Hannah N Gilbert, Budi Prasetyo, Sandeep Nanwani, Alfian Nurfaizi, Byron J Good
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal and newborn mortality remains a persistent global health issue, and most of these deaths are preventable through timely provision of Emergency Obstetric and Neonatal Care (EmONC). Despite the integration of EmONC into health systems, many low and middle-income countries struggle with suboptimal quality and readiness of services. This study aimed to assess the implementation of EmONC in community health centers (Puskesmas) in Jember, Indonesia, focusing on facility readiness and availability of the seven Basic EmONC (BEmONC) signal functions and the factors influencing service provision.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods study was conducted in 50 Puskesmas health facilities providing delivery services in Jember from January 1 to July 31, 2023. Quantitative data were collected using facility surveys adapted from the Rapid EmONC Assessment Tool. Qualitative data were gathered through semi-structured interviews with stakeholders (n = 12), patients (n = 10), and focus group discussions with midwives (n = 2). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize trends, and t-test or Mann-Whitney U test to compare between groups. Qualitative data were analyzed using inductive content analysis.ResultsNone of the Puskesmas met standards for fully functional BEmONC facilities. Among the seven BEmONC signal functions, parenteral uterotonics were most commonly provided (88% of facilities), while assisted vaginal delivery was not provided by any facility (0%). Surveyed facilities had a median of 70% (interquartile range 63–77%) of the 160 essential EmONC equipment and medications available. Key gaps were identified in essential medicines, equipment, provider training, and monitoring systems. Qualitative findings revealed barriers at systemic and facility levels, including insufficient provider training, inconsistent resource availability, and inadequate monitoring. Facilitators included growing community trust, existing infrastructure, and political commitment to maternal health.ConclusionEmONC implementation in Jember faces critical barriers undermining service readiness and delivery, with notable gaps in service provision and availability. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive service delivery redesign approach, focusing on centralizing and strengthening EmONC services in strategically selected facilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14794-7.Keywords: EmONC, Maternal health, Newborn health, Health systems, Jember, Indonesia

Background

Reducing maternal and newborn mortality remains a critical global health challenge, despite being prioritized in both the Millennium Development Goals and the subsequent Sustainable Development Goal 3, Target 3.1, which aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to less than 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030. Progress has been insufficient, with only 9 out of 95 high-mortality countries achieving the Millenium Development Goals target of a two-thirds reduction in maternal mortality by 2015 [1]. From 2000 to 2020, the global MMR declined by 34%, representing an average annual reduction of 2.1%, which is not sufficient to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals target by 2030. As of 2020, an estimated 287,000 women still died due to maternal causes annually, equivalent to 800 deaths every day [2].

Most maternal deaths result from complications during pregnancy or childbirth, with hemorrhage, sepsis, unsafe abortion, and obstructed labor accounting for over two-thirds of global maternal mortality [3]. It is estimated that 15% of women will develop life-threatening complications during pregnancy and childbirth, many of which cannot be predicted or prevented [4, 5]. However, most complications can be successfully treated through the timely provision of quality emergency obstetric and neonatal care (EmONC) [6].

EmONC encompasses a set of lifesaving services that are essential for managing obstetric complications and preventing maternal and neonatal deaths [7]. The World Health Organization outlined two levels: Basic EmONC (BEmONC) and Comprehensive EmONC (CEmONC) [6]. BEmONC facilities provide seven “signal functions” which are key medical interventions used to treat direct obstetric complications that cause most maternal deaths. These signal functions include: administration of parenteral antibiotics, administration of parenteral anticonvulsants, administration of parenteral uterotonics, manual removal of placenta, removal of retained products, assisted vaginal delivery (AVD), and basic neonatal resuscitation [6]. CEmONC facilities, in addition to providing BEmONC signal functions, should also be able to perform cesarean sections and provide blood transfusions [6]. Evidence shows that quality EmONC can avert the majority of maternal deaths [8]. Basic EmONC alone can avert 40% of intrapartum-related newborn and maternal deaths [9].

Subsequently, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have integrated EmONC provision into their health services. Despite efforts to implement EmONC, significant challenges persist, with a widening gap in mortality rates between high-income and low-income settings. In 2020, the MMR in low-income countries was 430 per 100,000 live births, compared to just 12 per 100,000 in high-income countries. This gap is particularly concerning given that 95% of global maternal deaths occur in low and low-middle-income countries, where many countries lack the health system capacity to provide quality services [2]. Many LMICs struggle with inadequate facility readiness and suboptimal quality of services [10–17]. Therefore, the focus should be shifted from merely increasing access to improving the quality of maternal health services. Provision of quality health services can save over eight million lives each year in LMICs [18].

Indonesia, a middle-income country, serves as an example of the challenges faced in providing quality EmONC services. Despite having high rates of skilled birth attendance (91%) and facility-based deliveries (74%), Indonesia faces a persistently high MMR of 186 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2020 [19, 20]. This paradox suggests that widespread access to midwifery services has not translated into commensurate reductions in maternal mortality. Studies have shown that health service access and characteristics account for 23% of the difference in MMR between high and low MMR provinces in Indonesia, with factors such as the density of doctors and distance to the nearest hospital playing significant roles [21].

The two leading causes of maternal deaths in Indonesia are hemorrhage and hypertensive disorder in pregnancy (pre-eclampsia and eclampsia), complications that are treatable with effective EmONC [22]. In response to these challenges, the Ministry of Health has integrated Basic EmONC into its primary healthcare system through a strategy known as PONED [23]. This approach designates certain community health centers in Indonesia, known as Puskesmas, as PONED and are required to provide 24/7 services and perform all seven BEmONC signal functions [24]. For more complex cases, Puskesmas PONED can refer patients to Comprehensive EmONC facilities, known as PONEK hospitals. However, the implementation of the PONED program has remained suboptimal due to multiple factors, including insufficient quality and quantity of human resources [25, 26], inadequate facility inputs [27, 28], fragmented referral pathways [29], variable adherence to clinical standards [30], and weak oversight or policy support [26]. While these issues have been documented in both peer-reviewed and gray literature in the past, few recent studies examine how BEmONC operates in community health centers, especially in high-mortality districts such as Jember, and integrate both quantitative and qualitative data to provide a comprehensive view.

Therefore, we conducted a study assessing the implementation of BEmONC in Puskesmas health facilities in Jember, a regency district in East Java. In 2022, Jember recorded the highest number of maternal and neonatal deaths in the province. Given the complexity of EmONC implementation, we employed a mixed-methods approach to capture both measurable indicators and contextual factors. We used quantitative surveys to evaluate facility readiness and availability of the seven BEmONC signal functions, and qualitative interviews and focus group discussions to explore factors influencing service provision. By integrating these complementary data sources, our research yields a more holistic understanding on strengthening EmONC services in resource-limited settings and contributes to broader global efforts to reduce maternal mortality.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Jember, East Java, Indonesia, a district with a population of 2.6 million in 2023. Jember’s healthcare system includes 50 Puskesmas, of which 25 are designated as BEmONC facilities (Puskesmas PONED), and 6 hospitals designated as CEmONC facilities (PONEK hospitals).

Study design

We used a convergent mixed-methods approach, collecting quantitative and qualitative data concurrently (Fig. 1). The quantitative component aimed to describe facility readiness and availability of BEmONC signal functions, while the qualitative component aimed to explore factors influencing service provision, focusing on barriers and facilitators. We first analyzed each dataset independently, then integrated the quantitative and qualitative findings using a joint display.

Summary of convergent mixed-methods design

Data collection and sampling

Quantitative

We surveyed all 50 Puskesmas in Jember offering delivery services, representing the total population of community health centers in the district. Quantitative data were collected using a survey instrument adapted from the Rapid EmONC Assessment Tool developed by the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and the Averting Maternal Death and Disability program at Columbia University [31]. We adapted the tool to the Indonesian context based on the Ministry of Health’s guidelines for EmONC implementation in Puskesmas [24] (see Additional file 1). The survey assessed facility readiness by measuring the availability of 160 key items (infrastructure, equipment, medications, human resources) and captured data on deliveries, obstetric and neonatal complications from January to July 2023. We recorded whether each facility provided each of the seven BEmONC signal functions at least once during the reference period (yes/no), with reasons for non-provision if applicable. The survey was completed by a designated representative from each Puskesmas, typically the head of the facility or the coordinator for maternal and child health services.

Qualitative

We purposively sampled stakeholders, providers, and patients from eight Puskesmas PONED facilities. The facilities were identified in consultation with the Jember Department of Health, whose officials served as key informants. The facilities were chosen to capture variation in PONED program implementation across Jember, with consideration of delivery volume, level of active management of obstetric cases, and facility willingness to participate. This sampling strategy was designed to capture diverse perspectives and ensure representativeness.

We conducted 22 semi-structured interviews (12 stakeholders, 10 patients) and 2 focus group discussions (FGDs) with providers. Stakeholders included individuals who worked at the Jember Department of Health or held managerial roles in Puskesmas PONED for more than 1 year. Two stakeholder interviews were conducted at the Jember Department of Health with officials directly responsible for the PONED program (the Head of the Health Services Division and the Head of the Primary Care Sub-Division). Across the eight Puskesmas, stakeholder interviews were conducted with 1–2 participants per facility, consisting of Heads of Puskesmas, PONED coordinators, and/or maternity ward coordinators.

Providers consisted of midwives who have more than 1 year of experience in Puskesmas PONED and were involved in EmONC provision. Each facility sent 1–2 providers to the FGDs, some of whom had also participated in the stakeholder interviews.

Patients were postpartum women who delivered within the past month, and experienced obstetric or neonatal complications, accessed PONED services, and attended antenatal or postnatal care at the Puskesmas. Patient interviews were conducted at the four higher-capacity facilities (1–4 patients per facility), where the volume of eligible cases permitted recruitment. Participants unwilling to take part, employees not involved in PONED, or patients without documented obstetric/neonatal emergencies were excluded.

Interview and FGD guides were developed specifically for this study for each participant group by the primary author (MCW) and a co-author (HG) (see Additional files 2, 3, and 4). The guides were developed in English, translated to Bahasa Indonesia, then back translated to English to ensure consistency. The interview guides were subsequently pilot tested with two participants from each group by the primary author (MCW). The guides explored perceptions of the EmONC program’s impact, implementation barriers and facilitators, and recommendations for improvement. Patient interviews focused on care experiences, service satisfaction, and suggestions for enhancement.

All interviews and FGDs were conducted by the primary author (MCW), a female physician and public health researcher with training in qualitative methods. The interviewer had no prior relationship with the participants. Participants were informed that the study was part of a graduate research project aimed at improving EmONC services. Sessions were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia in private rooms at the health facilities or Department of Health offices, lasting 60–75 min each. They were audio-recorded and supplemented with field notes. A research assistant provided support for note-taking. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English by a professional translator. The primary author (MCW) and another author (AN) checked translations for accuracy and consistency.

Data analysis

Quantitative

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the availability of EmONC infrastructure, equipment, medications, and human resources, delivery trends, signal function provision, and complication management. For each facility, we calculated the percentage of items available overall and within each category defined by the Rapid EmONC Assessment Tool. We described availability of services in Jember by presenting the median and interquartile ranges of these percentages across health facilities. As PONED facilities would be expected to be better equipped to handle obstetric emergencies and handle more obstetric and neonatal complications than regular Puskesmas (referred to as non-PONED facilities), we assessed the difference between the two statistically using t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Excel and R.

Qualitative

Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive content analysis approach [32]. Four interview and FGD transcripts were initially open-coded by the primary author to develop a preliminary codebook. This codebook was then refined and piloted to assess comprehensiveness and finalize the coding structure. The final codebook was applied to the entire dataset using Dedoose software (version 9.2, SocioCultural Research Consultants, LLC, Los Angeles, CA) by the primary author.

Two researchers (MCW and HG) independently reviewed the coded data using an inductive approach to identify patterns and construct a draft set of themes. Where the two researchers diverged in their interpretation, discrepancies were resolved through team discussion until consensus was reached. The draft themes were then reviewed and discussed by the research team to confirm their labels, definitions, and illustrative quotes. Through an iterative process of discussion and refinement, a final set of six themes was developed to capture the key findings from the qualitative data.

To enhance the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings, we employed several established strategies. Credibility was supported through triangulation of perspectives across our three participant groups (stakeholders, providers, and patients) and through member checking of preliminary findings with a subset of stakeholder participants. Dependability and confirmability were supported through peer debriefing and weekly team review meetings throughout coding and analysis. Data saturation was determined when no new themes emerged from the analysis of additional transcripts.

We examined the extent to which qualitative themes either corroborated or provided additional context for the gaps in service provision and readiness identified by our quantitative survey, and conversely, we explored instances where the quantitative findings helped to explain the challenges described in the qualitative data. We subsequently synthesized these results in a joint display to highlight points of convergence and divergence across data sources.

Results

Quantitative results

Of the 50 Puskesmas health facilities in Jember, representatives from 48 (96%) filled in the survey. Of the responding facilities, 25 were PONED health facilities, and 23 were non-PONED health facilities.

Availability of supporting items and staff for EmONC

None of the 48 surveyed facilities had complete availability of all 160 essential equipment and medications for EmONC (Table S1). At the category level, complete availability was most frequent for laboratory tests (46 facilities, 95.8%) and delivery and examination equipment (32 facilities, 66.7%), and least frequent for emergency resuscitation equipment (1 facility, 2.1%) and infusion and injectable medicines (no facility, 0.0%). To characterize the extent of partial availability, we examined the proportion of items available per facility (Table 1). Health facilities had a median of 70% (interquartile range [IQR] 63–77%) of the 160 essential equipment and medicines available.

Facilities had a median of 62% (IQR 57–65%) of the 26 essential infusion and injectables and 75% (IQR 63–78%) of the 8 essential oral medications. Injectable uterotonics like oxytocin were available in 46 (96%) of facilities, and methylergometrine injections in 42 (88%). In terms of parenteral anticonvulsants, magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) was available at all facilities (n = 48, 100%). Parenteral antibiotics showed lower availability, with procaine penicillin injections at 1 (2%) facility, ampicillin injections at 5 (10%), gentamicin injections at 6 (13%), and metronidazole injections at 15 (31%). Misoprostol tablets were available at only 12 (25%) of facilities.

Facilities had a median of 69% (IQR 55–85%) of the 40 essential surgical instruments available. Only 9 (19%) facilities reported having Manual Vacuum Aspirator kits and vacuum extractors. Facilities had a median of 52% (IQR 44%-65%) of the 23 emergency resuscitation equipment items available, which was the category with lowest availability. Neonatal resuscitation kits were available in only 37 (77%) of facilities. PONED facilities were better equipped than Non-PONED facilities in terms of emergency resuscitation equipment availability (p = 0.01), but this was the only category of equipment in which there was a statistically significant difference between PONED and Non-PONED facilities. In terms of human resources, all facilities (n = 48, 100%) were staffed with doctors, nurses, midwives, and lab technicians. The median staffing level per facility was 2 doctors (IQR 2–2), 17 midwives (IQR 15–20), 16 nurses (IQR 12–18), and 1 lab technician (IQR 1–2). Furthermore, all the facilities (n = 48, 100%) reported possessing essential infrastructure, including clean water, electricity, laboratory facilities, and ambulances.

Deliveries and complications

The median number of vaginal deliveries reported by these facilities over the seven-month period was 56 (IQR 38–86). PONED facilities had a higher median of 75 vaginal deliveries (IQR 50–111) as opposed to the non-PONED facilities, which reported a median of 45 (IQR 30–59). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.053).

The median number of obstetric complication cases received among all facilities was 34 (IQR 22–85), and the median for total neonatal complications was 7 (IQR 2–11) (Table 2). Facilities in the PONED group saw a higher median number of obstetric complications (55 cases), compared to the non-PONED group (32 cases), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.25). In general, obstetric complications tended to be referred rather than being treated in the facility. Conversely, neonatal complications tended to be treated rather than referred.

Provision of EmONC signal functions

Facilities from both the PONED and Non-PONED groups reported providing all of the basic EmONC signal functions, except for assisted deliveries using vacuum or forceps (Fig. 2). The median number of signal functions performed among all facilities was 5 (IQR 3–5). PONED facilities reported performing a median of 5 (IQR 4–6) functions while Non-PONED facilities reported performing a median of 4 (IQR 3–5) functions, a difference that was statistically significant (p = 0.02). None of the facilities reported performing deliveries with vacuum or forceps. The EmONC signal function most commonly performed was the administration of parenteral uterotonics, with 42 (88%) facilities reporting its provision.

Qualitative results

Characteristics of the qualitative study participants are summarized in supplementary Table S2. Provider and stakeholder participants were experienced practitioners (median age 44 years, median work experience 20 years), nearly all female, and drawn from three cadres: health administrators at the Department of Health, physicians serving as Heads of Puskesmas, and midwives in PONED, maternity ward, and maternal and maternal and child health program coordination roles.

Barriers

Our qualitative findings identified several barriers that impede the effective implementation of PONED in Puskesmas facilities and consequently hinder the provision of quality EmONC services. These barriers can be categorized into three main themes: (1) decentralization of financial management, (2) gaps in facility readiness, and (3) complex conditions affecting providers’ decision-making criteria. Sub-themes identified under each theme are bolded in the elaborations where applicable.

Decentralization of financial management

The transition to a new financial management system mandated by local government has unintentionally hindered effective EmONC provision. This shift towards autonomous financial management marks a significant departure from previous dependence on the Department of Health. During this transition, facilities are hesitant to initiate independent budgeting or procurement without further guidance.

Regarding equipment, even before becoming a PONED facility, we were provided with incubators, infant warmers, and ultrasounds. But this year, there’s a shortage of consumables due to the transition to a BLUD [an independent financial management system] system in 2023. It’s a transitional period, so the procurement regulations are not yet clear, which makes us hesitant to purchase.

– Stakeholder #5, Puskesmas PONED Representative

This decentralization has introduced delays in procurement processes. Puskesmas staff feel unprepared for independent procurement, lacking trained officials and familiarity with new methods. Consequently, facilities face critical drug and equipment shortages. The Department of Health’s budget reduction exacerbates the issue, preventing them from supplying Puskesmas as before.

Previously, the Department of Health provided; they [health centers] just submitted their plans to the local Department of Health. If approved, they were given drugs, like Fe [iron] blood boosters. But now, what they usually ask the Department of Health for, they have to procure on their own. This means that planning should be at the health center. They also can’t purchase something that isn’t written in the plan because it would be a legal violation when sampled for accuracy testing. That’s the challenge we face now.

– Stakeholder #1, Department of Health Representative

Gaps in facility readiness

Providers and stakeholders described several connected gaps in the readiness of Puskesmas to deliver EmONC services. Healthcare providers expressed an urgent desire for updated EmONC training, pointing to insufficient provider training as a key concern. Although the quantitative results did not identify “lack of training” as a direct barrier, interviews revealed a clear need for refresher courses and mentoring, particularly for low-frequency, high-risk procedures. Staff turnover has exacerbated the issue, with trained personnel leaving and their replacements lacking the necessary training, and this gap directly impacts providers’ confidence and service capability to the extent that some are willing to self-fund their training.

We face human resource challenges. We need PONED training, but no one is trained. Ideally, the training should be provided from higher authorities. We’re unsure of the exact challenges. From our end, we’ve tried to independently participate in training, but support from above is also essential.

– FGD #2, Participant 5, Midwife

Closely tied to these training gaps is inconsistent resource availability at the facility level. Health facilities experience inconsistent availability of equipment which undermines the provision of effective EmONC services, and the challenge is exacerbated by inadequate maintenance services for broken equipment. There are also instances where the available equipment is not used, primarily due to a lack of personnel trained to operate it.

I think we are still lacking in medical equipment, like infant warmers which often have problems. Consulting for repairs is difficult and expensive. They should be checked every six months, and if not repairable, they should be replaced promptly. Dopplers are expensive, costing about 4 million each. We sent them for repair, waited 3 days, but they came back still broken and irreparable due to high costs. We had to buy smaller, more affordable ones.

– Stakeholder #7, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Furthermore, the inconsistent availability of essential medicines at Puskesmas, notably antibiotics, threatens EmONC service delivery. While emergency medicines are typically stocked, the shortfall in non-emergency drugs can still impact the overall quality of care.

Compounding these training and resource concerns is an inadequate monitoring and evaluation system. The absence of a robust and cohesive monitoring and evaluation framework specifically for PONED services has emerged as a challenge, since current practices remain insufficient and hinder stakeholders’ ability to respond effectively to healthcare delivery needs.

I recently conducted an evaluation, but only regarding the availability of resources. […] However, concerning PONED’s performance, how many childbirths they’ve aided, the cases they’ve managed, and whether the patient was referred or followed up at home by midwives, that we haven’t done yet, but it’s on our agenda.

– Stakeholder #1, Department of Health Representative

Complex conditions affecting providers’ decision-making criteria

Providers reported several factors that complicated the clinical decision-making required to implement the national BEmONC guidelines. A service authorization discrepancy exists between the nationally mandated services for Puskesmas PONED and local government restrictions, often stemming from past obstetrical complications resulting in deaths at Puskesmas. In addition, providers report varying standards for determining when to treat or refer a patient, resulting in inconsistencies in treatment cutoff. Despite well-established guidelines, the practical application of these protocols is influenced by various considerations, which ultimately comes down to the provider’s decision and may lead to inconsistent care practices.

The rule is to handle cases with scores of 2 to 6 [referring to a pregnancy risk assessment scoring system used in all facilities in Jember], as we’re a PONED. But if it’s above 6, it’s risky. We refer to cases with scores of 6 that show signs of worsening or those with scores of 12 and above. We’re more cautious with high scores, especially with PE [pre-eclampsia, a pregnancy complication characterized by high blood pressure], which is not to be taken lightly. Higher scores also indicate potential high-risk patients. But if it’s progressing well, even with a score of 10, we assist. Cases like Grande MultiPara [a woman who has given birth to five or more children] with scores of only 2 to 4 are manageable, but we remain cautious about potential complications.

– Stakeholder #11, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Conflicting guidance between national and local governments, the weight of past adverse events at Puskesmas, and the responsibility placed on individual providers for clinical decision-making have together created a risk-averse culture. Healthcare providers report that their fear of being blamed for adverse outcomes has produced a cautious environment, significantly impacting their decision-making processes. This culture often leads providers to prefer referring patients to higher-level facilities rather than treating them in-house, even when such referrals might result in detrimental outcomes for the patient due to delays in care.

In the past, there was an incident – just one – but it set a precedent. There was a case of OD [oxytocin drip; a uterotonic drug used to induce or speed up labor] and high blood pressure where the patient died in the hospital. Since then, there’s been reluctance to handle OD cases at community health centers, even if they are PONED-equipped. […] After that one incident, it was assumed that certain procedures couldn’t be done at all. […] We’ve cared for low birth weight babies before, consulting with a pediatrician, but sometimes, we also fear the responsibility. We had an incident where an incubator malfunctioned unexpectedly. We try to provide the best care, but if we’re unsure, we prefer to refer.

– FGD #1, Respondent 3, Midwife

Facilitators

The analysis of qualitative data revealed several key facilitators that contribute to the successful implementation of PONED services in Puskesmas facilities. These facilitators include (1) the community’s growing trust in Puskesmas, (2) existing systems and infrastructure, and (3) political commitment to maternal health. Sub-themes identified under each theme are bolded in the elaborations where applicable.

Growing trust in puskesmas services

Patients described how community perceptions of Puskesmas have shifted over time, with overcoming distrust through patient-provider relationships emerging as a key dynamic. Previously, patients tended to distrust care at government-funded Puskesmas, associating free services with poor quality. Strong patient-provider relationships have been crucial in overcoming this skepticism and facilitating PONED implementation. Trust often develops during antenatal care, where consistent, quality interactions with midwives help patients overcome apprehensions about birthing at Puskesmas. Consequently, these relationships establish Puskesmas as a trusted source for maternal services, enhancing adherence to medical advice.

These relationships also have a broader community effect, with positive experiences driving demand: improved services at Puskesmas have positively shifted community perceptions, making nearby community health centers more attractive for childbirth to patients compared to distant hospitals. As more patients have positive experiences delivering at Puskesmas, they share their stories with others in the community, and this ripple effect has led to an increased demand for giving birth at these facilities.

We were scared about the community health center, especially with insurance, fearing the free service might be unsatisfactory. But I find it good. I had my ultrasound here, lab checks here. The midwife, A, and D [names of midwives] are stationed at my place. I feel it’s good, continuous, and connected. All the midwives here are nice. […] My parents even said it’s nice to give birth at the community health center. All four of my parent’s children, including me, were delivered by a midwife [referring to delivery assisted by private practice midwife at patient’s home] because they were afraid of unsatisfactory services. […] my mother’s four children were all delivered by a midwife. This was my first at the community health center. My mother said, if she knew the service was this good, she would’ve come here.

– Patient #2

Existing systems and infrastructure

Providers and stakeholders pointed to existing systems and infrastructure as a foundation that supports BEmONC service delivery, beginning with enhanced facility and transportation resources. The infrastructure in Puskesmas facilities, including ambulances and updated building facades, has been identified as a foundational support for the program, and the recent refurbishments and increased availability of transportation resources are conducive to better service implementation, although they come with their own operational challenges.

We have 8 ambulances. […] 7 are from the villages, and one is from the community health center. Each village has its own ambulance. Our coverage area is quite large, so we need many ambulances, but this increases operational costs like maintenance and fuel.

– Stakeholder #5, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Working alongside this physical infrastructure is a digital referral system that has been recognized as a crucial component of PONED, since the presence of an integrated and effective electronic system ensures that patients receive timely and coordinated care across different healthcare levels and optimizes the service delivery pathway.

Based on experience, we refer to Subandi hospital. There’s also SISRUTE [a nationally integrated electronic referral system] now. […] Many people monitor SISRUTE, eliminating issues of non-acceptance for referral cases.

– Stakeholder #7, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Despite SISRUTE’s benefits, referral effectiveness is often limited by receiving facilities’ capacity. Even when providers follow standard operating procedures and refer to designated hospitals, these facilities may lack resources to accommodate patients.

When referring a low birth weight baby below 2 kg, following SOPs [standard operating procedures], we might refer to a specific hospital in Patrang [name of district]. However, if that hospital is full and suggests another, it puts the patient at risk, especially if the condition worsens at the Puskesmas. This creates a dilemma between following procedures and ensuring patient safety.

– FGD #1, Midwife

These structural systems are further reinforced by intersectoral collaborations, which strengthen BEmONC service provision in Puskesmas by helping to mitigate patient or family resistance to timely referrals, a significant barrier to efficient BEmONC service delivery especially in emergency cases. By leveraging these partnerships, Puskesmas can better address referral challenges and maintain PONED system integrity.

If someone refuses [to be referred], we bring in the midwife, the community health worker, and even involve cross-sectoral entities like village heads to manage the situation.

– Stakeholder #10, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Political commitment to maternal health

The success of EmONC service provision in Puskesmas has been significantly bolstered by the strong political commitment demonstrated by local leaders. Health system leaders have taken a proactive stance in prioritizing resources and shaping policies maternal mortality reduction efforts.

The support from the regent [equivalent of a mayor] is a significant strength. If he’s supportive, everything flows smoothly. At the end of the day, leadership plays a crucial role. Our regent is enthusiastic, which is evident from the declining maternal and infant mortality rates.

– Stakeholder #1, Department of Health Representative

A notable example of political commitment is the local government’s financing scheme providing free maternal care and transportation. This initiative addresses gaps in the national social health insurance (Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional) by covering transportation costs, thereby improving access to Puskesmas delivery services, especially for vulnerable populations.

Usually, the obstacle is the cost of attendance during the delivery. Delivery costs are covered by the JPK [the local government financing scheme for maternal care] program, including transportation and everything else, all free

– Stakeholder #5, Puskesmas PONED Representative

Discussion

This study revealed critical gaps in BEmONC readiness, as well as systemic barriers to service delivery in Jember. None of the designated BEmONC facilities in Jember met the UNFPA standard for fully functional EmONC facilities [31]. This was primarily due to the non-provision of AVD because of local restrictions, although provision of other functions was also lacking. This finding is consistent with evaluations from other LMICs, which have documented discrepancies between the number of designated EmONC facilities and their actual functionality [10–17]. Interpreted through the WHO Health Systems Building Blocks framework [33], the barriers we identified in Jember span five of the six building blocks: financing (decentralization of financial management), workforce (insufficient provider training), medical products and equipment (inconsistent resource availability), information systems (inadequate monitoring and evaluation), and governance and service delivery (complex conditions affecting providers’ decision-making). This pattern indicates that the gaps in EmONC provision cut across interrelated parts of the health system. At the same time, community trust, political will, and existing physical and digital infrastructure support EmONC implementation.

As in Jember, AVD is often the least provided signal function in other low and middle-income settings [12–14, 17, 34]. This reflects a global trend of declining use of AVD especially in LMICs, largely due to the method’s perceived complications and associated morbidity [35]. However, it is crucial to understand that AVD, when performed correctly, can be a lifesaving intervention in cases of complications during the second stage of labor or in emergencies where access to emergency caesarean section is lacking [36]. Previous researchers have identified several potential drivers for the underuse of AVD in LMICs, including lack of functioning equipment, insufficient staff training in selecting appropriate candidates for AVD or performing the procedure, suboptimal supervision and support, and fear of litigation in case of adverse outcomes [36]. These patterns align closely with the broader theme of our qualitative findings in Jember. The non-existence of AVD in Jember represents a significant missed opportunity in EmONC provision, pointing to a need for targeted training and awareness programs for health providers, potentially leading to a reintroduction of AVD.

Although none of the surveyed facilities reported lack of training as a reason for not providing a specific signal function (Table 3), our qualitative data revealed a more nuanced picture: providers feel inadequately prepared to manage emergencies without ongoing professional development. In other words, while they may technically know how to perform the procedures, they do not feel confident or comfortable managing these emergencies without regular refresher training. This situation is further exacerbated by the low monthly delivery volumes observed even in Puskesmas designated as BEmONC, which provide fewer opportunity to practice and reinforce these skills, thereby contributing to a risk-averse culture in handling obstetrics emergency. Studies have shown that low delivery volume is consistently associated with poor quality of care in basic maternal health services, including some BEmONC signal functions [37], while providers working in higher-level facilities with greater exposure to complicated cases demonstrate higher EmONC knowledge scores compared to those in lower-level facilities [38]. Additionally, limited opportunities to manage obstetric emergencies may lead to a gradual erosion of skills [39], whereas frequent performance of these procedures is linked to higher levels of confidence [40].

The situation in Jember highlights the inadequacy of Indonesia’s current EmONC implementation model, particularly the PONED program’s approach of designating facilities as BEmONC without ensuring their functionality. To address these challenges, we propose adopting a service delivery redesign (SDR) approach, as suggested by the 2018 Lancet Global Health Commission on High-Quality Health Systems [18]. This approach involves concentrating both obstetric and neonatal services and improvement efforts in fewer facilities to improve health system efficiency [41]. Our recommendation are thus: first, conduct a nationwide mapping exercise to identify and prioritize “highly functional” BEmONC facilities (Puskesmas PONED). These would be facilities with higher delivery volumes, strategic locations, or those receiving a higher number of referrals. These prioritized facilities should then receive targeted resource allocation and quality improvement initiatives. Simultaneously, existing non-functional Puskesmas PONED should undergo a thorough re-evaluation to determine whether these facilities should be downgraded to regular Puskesmas status or receive targeted interventions to meet BEmONC standards, taking into account the constraints of limited resources. Second, enhance providers’ capacity through targeted training, particularly in BEmONC facilities, to manage certain complications and reduce referrals to higher-level facilities. This could involve in-service training methods such as low-dose, high-frequency sessions to maintain competence in managing less common obstetric emergencies [42], and potentially reintroducing AVD for providers [35]. Third, creating demand by collaborating with private practice midwives, who assist a significant proportion of deliveries in Indonesia [43], to encourage patients to deliver in these facilities [44]. This would improve delivery volumes in BEmONC facilities and enhance provider skills through increased case exposure.

Although various models of SDR have been discussed for over a decade in Indonesia, widespread adoption remains limited. Several models have been implemented in other low income settings [45, 46]. One such model in Tanzania of decentralizing CEmONC by upgrading primary care facilities showed promise in improving maternal health outcomes [47]. Implementing such a redesign will require substantial government commitment, adequate resource allocation, and active community engagement to ensure its success and sustainability, while avoiding the pitfall of creating yet another well-intentioned but ineffective policy that fails to address root issues.

Our study’s primary contribution lies in examining BEmONC services in Jember, a high-mortality district that has received limited attention in previous research, combining both quantitative and qualitative data to capture system-wide and on-the-ground realities. In doing so, we offer current insights that build on past research while pinpointing actionable strategies such as SDR and targeted provider training that can strengthen BEmONC services in similar contexts. Nonetheless, our reliance on self-reported data and the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences or observe trends over time. Future research should adopt longitudinal approaches to evaluate the impact and sustainability of policy interventions on EmONC provision and maternal health outcomes in Indonesia. Additionally, implementation research on targeted training programs and resource allocation strategies could provide valuable insights for policy and practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals significant challenges in the implementation of EmONC services in Jember, Indonesia, reflecting broader issues within the country’s maternal health system. The absence of fully functional BEmONC facilities, compounded by low delivery volumes and insufficient provider training, undermines efforts to reduce maternal mortality. To address these challenges, we propose a comprehensive service delivery redesign approach, focusing on centralizing and strengthening EmONC services in strategically selected facilities. This approach, while requiring substantial commitment and paradigm shift in healthcare delivery, has the potential to significantly improve the quality and accessibility of maternal care within resource constraints. The success of this approach could serve as a model for other regions facing similar challenges in EmONC provision.
