Nanomedicine strategies for overcoming the blood–brain barrier in central nervous system drug delivery: mechanisms, advances, and challenges
Authors: Mukul Pratap Singh, Ananda Kumar Chettupalli, Sarad Pawar Naik Bukke, Bharath Kumar Mamilla Mugaiahgari, Pavani Uppu, Priyanshu Pathak, Bayapa Reddy Narapureddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral nervous system (CNS) disorders remain one of the most significant challenges in medicine given the restrictive properties of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which substantially reduces the delivery of most therapeutics to penetrate the brain. Although there has been considerable progress in the generation of support neuroactive substances, approximately 98% of possible CNS medicines never get to effective milligram proofs in the brain, mostly due to exclusion from BBB. This research offers an in-depth and analytical assessment of approaches utilizing nanomedicine aimed at addressing the limitations of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and improving the delivery of drugs to the brain. It thoroughly explores the structural and functional properties of the BBB, identifies the major obstacles hindering molecular movement, and investigates the cellular and molecular processes that regulate the movement of nanoparticles. Significant focus is given to various nanocarrier platforms, encompassing lipid-based systems (such as liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers), polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, dendrimers, nanogels, nanoemulsions, exosomes, carbon nanotubes, and metallic nanoparticles. These platforms are particularly noted for their important functions in improving drug stability, bioavailability, controlled release, and precision targeting within the central nervous system. Furthermore, recent developments in innovative technologies, including nanofiber scaffolds and multidimensional (3D-6D) printing methods for neurological uses, are also discussed. The paper also analyses translational hurdles, safety issues, and toxicity factors that persistently obstruct the clinical application of nanomedicine-based CNS therapeutics. This paper highlights the revolutionary potential of nanotechnology in brain drug delivery and offers insights into future strategies for the rational design of safe and effective nanocarriers to meet unmet demands in CNS disease therapy.Keywords: Blood–brain barrier (BBB), Nanomedicine, Liposome, Solid lipid nanoparticle, Carbon nanotube, Metallic nanoparticle

Introduction

Neurological diseases are rising around the turn of the third millennium, owing to extended aging, with a significant societal effect due to their frequency and/or high morbidity and death. Neurological diseases are a principal reason for the mortality of 12% of all global mortalities [1]. In 2016, neurological disorders accounted for approximately 276 million disability adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide and represented the second most common cause of mortality, with nearly 9 million fatalities [2].

The crude prevalence rate ranged from 967 to 4070 people per 100,000, with an average of 2394 people per 100,000. It is estimated that 30 million individuals in India suffer from neurological diseases out of 1.27 billion [3].

The brain is one of the most intricate and vital organs of the human body and depends on a continuous supply of nutrients and gases to maintain normal physiological function. Because of its vital physiological importance, the brain is preserved by various protective systems, including the cranial bones, meninges, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) [4]. Although numerous candidate therapeutics have been evaluated for potential application in treating neurological disorders, their clinical effectiveness remains limited due to multiple challenges. One of the major obstacles in CNS therapy is the efficient delivery of drugs, nucleic acids, amino acids, imaging agents, proteins, and other biomolecules across the protective brain barriers, particularly the BBB along with the blood–CSF interface [5]. As a result, rather than inventing a novel treatment, the most challenging element of treating brain diseases is getting drugs across the barriers [6].

Over 98 percent of all prospective medicines emerging from drug development pipelines are predicted to be incapable of crossing the BBB enough to achieve therapeutic drug concentration. By eliminating these obstacles, a variety of neurological diseases can be successfully treated [7]. Although traditional approaches like Intra-Cerebro-Ventricular (ICV) injection, BBB interruption, implant usage, and the practice of prodrugs have had specific achievements in disabling these barriers, researchers are constantly looking for new ones only one-time suitable replacements for improved transport of therapeutic agents into brain [8].

The recent advances in nanomedicine have significantly expanded strategies for delivering therapeutics across the blood–brain barrier (BBB).

The potential of a variety of nanocarrier systems, such as lipid-based nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, exosomes, and inorganic nanomaterials, to improve drug stability, increase targeting accuracy, and allow controlled release of therapeutic agents within the central nervous system has been thoroughly investigated. Particle size, surface charge, and ligand-mediated functionalization are important physicochemical factors that regulate nanoparticle transport across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and impact treatment outcomes in neurological illnesses, according to emerging data.

In addition, improvements in surface modification techniques, stimuli-responsive nanocarrier systems, and receptor-mediated targeting have greatly enhanced brain-specific drug delivery while lowering off-target toxicity [X–X]. When taken as a whole, these advancements highlight the increasing significance of nanocarrier-based delivery systems as a key paradigm in contemporary neuropharmacology, with ongoing development fueled by new advances in nanotechnology.

Barriers to drug transport to the brain

Maintaining the homeostatic equilibrium of the human brain is vital because it plays a major role in controlling and coordinating physiological processes. Specialized barrier mechanisms carefully control the exchange of vital nutrients, oxygen, and therapeutic substances between the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and the systemic circulation [9]. The blood–brain barrier (BBB), the arachnoid barrier, which divides CSF in the subarachnoid space from the dura mater, the CSF–brain barrier that exists during early development, the pia–glia limitans, which creates a boundary between CSF and extracellular fluid, and the blood–CSF barrier, which is made up of the choroid plexus' epithelial cells, are some of the important barrier interfaces [10].

BBB

The BBB is a group of highly specialized cells exhibiting distinct barrier properties to medication delivery into the brain [11, 12]. It functions as a protective porter, protecting the brain from dangerous chemicals in the bloodstream while also providing the nutrition it requires to function properly. It possesses a multicellular structure that is exceptionally complex and well-organized, with successive layers of astrocytes and pericytes that end-feet through the tightly sealed Brain capillary endothelial cells (BCECs) constitute the cellular basis of BBB and basal lamina. Alongside vascular elements, neurons, microglia, and other cell populations form integral components of the neurovascular unit [12].

The brain's endothelial cells vary markedly from those found elsewhere in the body, owing to constant intercellular tight junctions (TJs), hydrophilic molecules, paracellular diffusion, high metabolism due to the existence of multiple mitochondria [13]. A combination of numerous transmembrane proteins and cytoplasmic auxiliary proteins forms the TJ between neighbouring BCECs. Certain components maintain direct connections with the actin cytoskeleton, allowing rapid and direct communication between neighbouring cells [14, 15].

A molecule must be hydrophobic/lipophilic and must have a 400 Da to 500 Da molecular weight to traverse the BBB passively [16]. However, most tiny molecular medicines do not meet these two criteria, making them impossible to penetrate the BBB. 7 New pathways and prospects for better medication transport across the BBB have opened as the thoughtfulness of molecular and cellular biology of BBB has improved. Various enzymes, receptors, and transportation mechanisms have been identified in BCECs that oversee molecule permeation; for example, receptor-mediated transcytosis transports protein and peptides [17].

Challenges in drug transport across the blood–brain barrier

Approximately 1.5 billion people worldwide suffer from central nervous system (CNS) illnesses, which represent a significant global health burden. The strict regulatory characteristics of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) hinder the successful management of many disorders, despite significant advancements in therapeutic development [18]. Through tight junctions, enzymatic activity, and specialized transport mechanisms, the BBB acts as a highly selective biological interface that prevents the majority of therapeutic drugs from entering the brain [19]. Drug transport to brain tissues is severely hampered by these defense systems, despite the fact that they are essential for maintaining CNS homeostasis [20]. Overcoming BBB limitations and the role of nanoparticles

The blood–brain barrier's (BBB) strict protective characteristics make it difficult to deliver therapeutic medicines to the brain. The need for invasive surgical procedures limits the practical applicability of conventional site-specific delivery methods, such as convection-enhanced delivery, catheter-based administration, and biodegradable implant systems, which allow for localized and sustained drug release [22]. On the other hand, intranasal administration has become a viable non-invasive method that provides a direct access to the central nervous system (CNS) through the trigeminal and olfactory nerves. However, problems with repeatability and dose variability still pose difficulties (Fig. 2) [23, 24].

Comparing nanomedicine and conventional methods for BBB drug administration

The olfactory pathway and the trigeminal nerve pathway are the two main ways that drugs administered through the nasal cavity can enter the brain. Although the trigeminal pathway facilitates distribution to deeper brain regions, such as the brainstem and spinal cord, the olfactory route enables direct transfer of therapeutic substances from the nasal epithelium to the olfactory bulb. When combined, these channels provide a quick and straightforward way to transport medications to the central nervous system (CNS), partially getting over the blood–brain barrier's (BBB) limitations [23–25].

Intranasal drug delivery has been extensively investigated using nanocarrier-based delivery platforms, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, dendrimers, and lipid-based nanoparticles. Improved drug stability, longer nasal cavity residence time, and more effective transport across the nasal epithelium are all facilitated by their nanoscale size, surface functionalization potential, and capacity to increase mucosal permeability [26–28].

Intranasal infusion allows for faster access to the brain while limiting peripheral side effects and lowering systemic exposure when compared to intravenous administration. Furthermore, it has definite advantages over oral delivery, where medications frequently undergo considerable first-pass digestion and have restricted blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. Despite these advantages, building intranasal nanocarrier systems for CNS drug administration requires careful consideration of a number of parameters, including as mucociliary clearance, limited dosage volume, and variability in nasal absorption [29–31].

Molecular alteration, prodrug design (e.g., levodopa in Parkinson's disease), reversible BBB disruption via osmotic agents like mannitol, and physical methods like focused ultrasound are alternative approaches to improving blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. These methods may increase drug delivery to the brain, but they also carry the danger of temporarily compromising the integrity of the blood–brain barrier [25]. In this regard, technologies based on nanocarriers have shown promise in circumventing BBB-related constraints [22]. Improved drug stability, targeted delivery, and effective BBB translocation are made possible by their adjustable size, customizable surface properties, and functionalization capabilities without appreciably impairing barrier function. As a result, nanocarriers offer a flexible and extremely promising platform for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and safety of CNS medication delivery [26].

Pathophysiological alterations of the BBB in CNS disorders

Drug delivery and therapeutic results are impacted by a number of central nervous system (CNS) illnesses that significantly change the blood–brain barrier's (BBB) integrity and functional characteristics. Tight junction protein disruption, reduced glucose transporter-1 (GLUT1) expression, and poor amyloid-β clearance are all associated with BBB failure in Alzheimer's disease.

BBB permeability can be altered by neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are linked to Parkinson's disease. The BBB becomes very diverse and partially impaired in glioblastoma, which leads to unequal medication distribution and difficulties with targeted administration. Similarly, ischemic stroke causes a brief breakdown of the blood–brain barrier, which may improve drug absorption but also raises the risk of cerebral edema and neurotoxicity. In order to maximize therapeutic efficacy, these disease-specific changes emphasize the necessity of context-dependent nanocarrier design that is adapted to the pathophysiological condition of the BBB.

Nanoparticle-based strategies for brain drug delivery

To improve targeted drug delivery to the brain, a number of approaches are being actively researched, such as pharmacological changes, blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption techniques, and delivery systems based on nanocarriers or nanoparticles [22]. Many professionals worldwide are interested in the use of nanoparticles (NPs), a cutting-edge and complex drug delivery technique [27].

The Latin word for dwarf is the source of the prefix "nano." The design, synthesis, characterization, and use of materials systems and devices at the nanoscale are all part of nanotechnology [28]. The renowned physicist Richard Feynman first presented the fundamental idea of nanotechnology in 1959, and he went on to describe its possible uses in medicine in 1961 [29]. In 1995, the first effective medication delivery across the BBB happened. The drug used was dalargin, a peptide with antinociceptive activity that cannot penetrate the BBB on its own; it was delivered intravenously in the form of polysorbate-coated nanoparticles [30].

These advances have resulted in the establishment of a novel research area termed nanomedicine, which applies molecular-level knowledge and techniques to preserve and enhance human health [31]. Although the accepted definition of nanoparticles limits nano to particles comprising at least one dimension within the 1–100 nm scale range, nanocarrier systems are commonly reported with diameters extending up to 1000 nm [32, 33]. The markedly different physicochemical and biological properties of nanoparticles relative to their bulk forms provide the basis for their expanding use in biological and medical fields [34]. The use of nanoparticles offers multiple benefits for brain-targeted medication delivery.

The produced particles are robust and readily freeze-dried, and procedures are used to produce the basic, cheap, and reasonably straightforward to scale up. Protect integrated medicines against enzymatic, chemical degradation and reduce some active medications' adverse effects. Drug loading capacity is strong, and drug integration occurs without a chemical reaction, retaining its activity. Pharmaceuticals that are both hydrophilic and hydrophobic can be used. Various approaches can administer it, like nasal, oral, sublingual, parenteral, etc.

By masking the physicochemical properties of the integrated medicines, bioavailability, and effectiveness of the NPs transferal across the BBB are increased (Fig. 3). NP permits long-term, controlled medication discharge following injection at the desired location, lasting from days–weeks. Varying matrix composition can readily adjust particle breakdown and controlled drug release properties. Modifying the surface, chemical characteristics, adding specifically targeted ligands, or utilizing magnetic guiding of the NPs can all be used to achieve cell-specific or organ-specific drug delivery. Nanoparticles, owing to their extensive surface area relative to volume, enable multivalent display of ligands and receptors, which markedly improves binding affinity. Surface-coated and drug-loaded NPs with PEG mainly prevents phagocytes or reticuloendothelial system (RES) cells from clearing them quickly [35, 36].

Translocation of nanocarriers across the BBB

Nanomedicine carrier systems

Several nanocarrier systems have been effectively studied for better brain medication delivery, including lipid-based NPs, polymer-based NPs, nano emulsions, exosomes, carbon nanotubes, and metallic nanoparticles (Fig. 4). Currently, therapeutic strategies for many brain disorders are largely limited to symptomatic management, as most pharmacological agents are unable to effectively traverse the BBB [37]. Progress in nanotechnology-driven drug delivery platforms has shown significant potential to enhance drug penetration and bioavailability within the CNS [38]. Consequently, several nanocarrier-based platforms have received regulatory approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and are now employed in marketed drug products [39].

Nanocarriers system

Comparative analysis of nanocarriers for CNS drug delivery

The physicochemical properties, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, targeting ability, and safety profiles of nanocarriers intended for central nervous system (CNS) medication administration vary greatly, making systematic comparison necessary for well-informed treatment selection. Excellent biocompatibility and the ability to contain both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs are features of lipid-based nanocarriers, such as liposomes and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs); nonetheless, they may be constrained by physical instability and quick systemic clearance [40, 41].

Although polymer-associated toxicity and degradation behavior remain significant issues, polymeric nanoparticles offer benefits such as regulated drug release, structural flexibility, and ease of surface functionalization [42]. Although inorganic nanocarriers, such as carbon nanotubes and metallic nanoparticles, have a large surface area and an excellent ability to load drugs, their use is frequently limited by possible problems with long-term biocompatibility and neurotoxicity [43, 44].

Because of their innate biocompatibility, minimal immunogenicity, and capacity to cross biological barriers, biological nanocarriers—exosomes in particular—have become extremely attractive delivery methods. However, issues with large-scale production, structural variability, and repeatability impede their clinical application [45].

Comparative studies show that important characteristics of nanoparticles, including as surface charge, ligand-mediated functionalization, and particle size (usually 10–100 nm), are crucial in influencing BBB transit efficiency, biodistribution patterns, and overall therapeutic effectiveness [40–42].

Lipid-based nanocarriers

Liposome nanocarriers

Liposomal nanocarriers have been the important often utilized nanocarriers in therapeutic settings. When some lipids are deferred in aqueous media, they produce nano or microparticulate colloidal carriers that develop quickly and instinctively [46, 47]. An internal watery core and an adjacent phospholipids bilayer membrane are the two main components of these particles. Around the liquid inner core, they might have a unilamellar or multilamellar. Glycerophospholipids, shingo-phospholipids, and sterols are similar architectural components of liposomes [48].

Liposomal nanocarriers show inherent capability of incorporating and delivering both water-soluble and lipid-soluble medicines simultaneously. The hydrophilic medications contain an internal water core. In contrast, the hydrophobic drugs favour the encircling bilayer membrane's hydrophobic environment [49]. The binding of lipoproteins to their receptors generates complexes that mediate the transport of liposome-based delivery systems through the BBB [50].

This one-of-a-kind characteristic, along with ease of production, less toxicity, non-immunogenicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, makes them a particularly appealing carrier system for drug delivery into CNS [51]. Liposomes in circulation are efficiently taken up by cells of the phagocyte-based clearance system, reducing their effectiveness as drug carriers. By reducing liposome size to the nanometer range and applying hydrophilic polymer coatings like PEG, their retention in the bloodstream is improved, resulting in Sterically Stabilized Liposomes (SSLs) [52]. Targeting SSLs to the CNS necessitates significant surface engineering, such as attaching monoclonal antibodies or ligands capable of binding to natural receptors displayed on BCECs [53]. Liposomes linked to anti-transferrin receptor antibodies have been reported to successfully penetrate the BBB in earlier studies [54]. PEG-coated liposomes with Abs against a glial fibrillary acid protein on their surface attach preferentially to astrocytes in the CNS. Liposomes containing the apoE4 peptide as a signal sequence were likewise found to be occupied up by BCECs in vitro [55, 56].

As adaptable nanocarrier systems that can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic therapeutic molecules while improving medication stability and targeted delivery to the brain across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), liposomes have garnered a lot of attention. Their phospholipid bilayer structure, which is very similar to biological membranes, facilitates better BBB permeability and encourages effective cellular interaction. However, issues including the mononuclear phagocyte system's quick clearance and possible instability during storage restrict their clinical use.

Solid lipid nanoparticles

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) are a relatively new development in lipid-based nanocarrier systems, consisting of lipid molecules that stay solid at ambient and physiological temperatures. Their biocompatible and biodegradable nature allows for diverse applications. A phospholipid monolayer in solid lipid NPs coats a core matrix of solid hydrophobic material wherein the medication of interest can be hydrolyzed or disseminated [57]. The core of solid lipid NPs consists of various solid lipids, including triglycerides, monoglycerides, diglycerides, steroids, fatty acids, and waxes. The suitability of SLNs as brain-targeted drug carriers arises from their small particle size (40–200 nm), robust solidity, dual drug-loading ability, ligand conjugation for reduced cytotoxicity, targeted delivery potential, and moderately negative surface charge [58].

Despite their potential as a carrier system, these NPs have several drawbacks, including a poor drug-loading capability and the probability of drug ejection throughout storage or burst release after injection. When just solid lipids are employed, these issues are caused mainly by developing a flawless crystalline structure [59]. With enhanced targeted brain medication delivery, SLNs have been produced with various medicines such as resveratrol, apolipoprotein E, donepezil, and others [60]. Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) have been widely investigated as promising carriers for central nervous system (CNS) drug delivery due to their biocompatibility, ability to provide controlled drug release, and capacity to enhance drug stability. However, some constraints, such as poor drug loading capacity and drug ejection caused by lipid crystallization, may impair their therapeutic efficacy. Recent studies highlight the importance of optimizing the lipid matrix composition to improve encapsulation efficiency and formulation stability in SLN-based systems [61].

Nanostructured lipid-based nanocarriers for drug delivery

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), which are a next-generation development of solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), were created to address issues such as limited drug loading capacity and premature drug ejection. Unlike SLNs, which typically consist of a single solid lipid, NLCs are formulated using a combination of solid and liquid lipids. This lipid blend produces a less ordered crystalline matrix with structural imperfections, which enhances drug encapsulation efficiency and minimizes drug leakage [62].

In this system, solid lipids remain in a solid state at both ambient and physiological temperatures, whereas liquid lipids are fluid at room temperature, contributing to the improved structural flexibility of NLC formulations. Modifying the lipid matrix composition can produce various drug-release patterns [63]. Owing to their novelty in the field of drug delivery, nanostructured lipid carriers are the focus of ongoing research aimed at leveraging their exceptional flexibility. NLCs have been used to manufacture a variety of medicines. Nasiri and colleagues developed and evaluated NLCs for enhanced brain-targeted delivery of Indinavir, using a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) screening approach coupled with comprehensive optimization techniques. The optimized nanoparticles were then surface-functionalized with human transferrin to improve targeted drug delivery. According to in vitro studies, the Weibull model most accurately represented the IDV release from the nanoparticles, reflecting hybrid diffusion–erosion kinetics. The strategic use of excipients in conjunction with NLCs has shown potential for improving the brain-targeted delivery of Atazanavir (ATZ). Khan and his colleagues used the quality-by-design method to construct ATZ-loaded NLC, which was then optimized with the Box-Behnken design. The findings indicated that enhanced bioavailability and brain-targeted delivery by NLC may help to improve atazanavir's therapeutic potential [64].

A sophisticated family of lipid-based delivery devices called nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) was created to overcome the drawbacks of solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs). NLCs increase drug loading capacity although decreasing drug leakage during storage by combining both solid and liquid lipids. The recent research has demonstrated their potential to increase medication bioavailability and enable brain-targeted delivery, particularly in the treatment of neurodegenerative illnesses. Nonetheless, issues with formulation complexity and long-term stability continue to prevent their wider clinical implementation.

Cationic liposomes

The cationic liposome is a potentially feasible option for conveying therapeutic drugs into the brain. This is a group of positively charged lipid molecules [65]. Bola amphiphile nanovesicles, consisting of hydrophilic termini flanking a hydrophobic chain, stabilize the membrane of drug-loaded cationic liposomes and can penetrate the BBB for targeted and regulated medication delivery. Bola amphiphile nano-vesicles can penetrate the BBB, allowing for regulated medication delivery to specific locations. Transfection agents can also be made from lipoplexes made from cationic liposomes and DNA solutions. Adsorption-mediated endocytosis enables cationic liposomes to traverse the BBB and then internalize in endosomes of endothelial cells. Physical changes in endothelial cells might be achieved by using lipoplexes to transfect the cells. Some nanoparticle drug carriers may be able to traverse the BBB more efficiently as a result of these physical modifications [66].

Lipoplexes are complexes generated through electrostatic interactions between cationic lipids and nucleic acids. They are typically formed by the direct mixing of cationic liposomes with DNA-containing solutions, resulting in stable lipids–nucleic acid assemblies. The binding of cationic liposomes to DNA occurs through electrostatic interactions with its negatively charged phosphate moieties, with the resulting complexes protected from the surrounding environment. The cationic liposomes employed in this study are generally tiny before being added to DNA; nevertheless, the complexes produced with DNA have diameters covering a size range from 200 nm to 2 μm [67].

Several studies have shown that the electrostatic binding of cationic liposomes to negatively charged cell membranes promotes adsorptive-mediated endocytosis, rendering cationic nanocarriers superior to neutral or anionic liposomes for CNS drug delivery [68]. To enhance brain-targeted delivery of andrographolide, Piazzini et al. engineered cationic liposomes through surface coating with Tween 80, either as a single modifier or combined with didecyl dimethyl ammonium bromide. Both liposomes were found to be acceptable for parenteral administration based on homogeneity and zeta potential. With preservation as suspension or freeze-dried products for a month, the system demonstrated outstanding stability. The best cryoprotectant agent was glucose. The generated cationic liposomes improved the Permeability of Andrographolide in Artificial Membranes (PAMPA) and hCMEC/D3 cells transport experiments [69].

Despite their enhanced cellular uptake and ability to facilitate adsorption-mediated transcytosis across the blood–brain barrier, cationic liposomes also present certain limitations. The positive surface charge may cause cytotoxicity, membrane disruption, and rapid clearance due to interactions with negatively charged biological components. In addition, serum protein adsorption and potential immunogenic responses may reduce circulation time and targeting efficiency. Therefore, strategies such as PEGylation or surface modification are often employed to improve stability and biocompatibility of cationic liposomal formulations [65–68].

Polymer-based nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles

Therapeutic medicines can be liquefied, trapped, adsorbed, captured, or conjugated to the matrix in polymeric NPs that comprise nano capsules and nanospheres produced from natural or synthetic polymers. Polymeric nanoparticles have been prepared using materials such as chitosan, gelatin, polyglycolides (PGA), poly (n-butyl cyanoacrylate) (PBCA), polylactides (PLAs), poly (lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) copolymers, poly (alkyl cyanoacrylates) (PACA), as well as certain proteins and polysaccharides. The desired features for polymeric nanoparticles in cerebrovascular drug administration include minimal toxicity, stable drug encapsulation, biodegradability, and surface functional groups that allow controlled drug release [70] (Table 1). Microspheres were the first particle devices utilized for direct medication supply to the brain. These systems are being used to provide many treatment options comprising imatinib, mitoxantrone, cyclosporine, paclitaxel, phenytoin, and the growth factor of the nerves [81, 82].

Emerging advances in nanomedicine for brain disorder management

Polymeric nanoparticles have been widely investigated as drug delivery platforms due to their ability to provide controlled drug release, enhanced stability, and surface functionalization for targeted delivery. The recent research has shown how well these systems work to transfer neuroprotective and chemotherapeutic drugs across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), especially when treating glioblastoma and other illnesses of the central nervous system. When choosing polymeric nanocarriers for therapeutic purposes, critical factors such as polymer toxicity, degradation kinetics, and production complexity must be carefully considered.

Polymeric micelles

Amphiphilic block copolymers self-assemble in aqueous settings to generate polymeric micelles, which are colloidal nanocarriers with a core–shell structure. The hydrophobic segments of the copolymers form the inner core, while the hydrophilic segments, which are commonly made of polyethylene glycol (PEG), form the outer shell. Because of this structural configuration, hydrophobic medications can be effectively encapsulated within the core, and the hydrophilic corona improves water stability and extends systemic circulation.

The two primary drivers for the dispersion of drug-loaded polymer micelles are their surface and size characteristics. Particle size is smaller than 50 nm in diameter for polymeric micelles, making these ideal medicinal carriers as they evade prompt clearance by MPS/RES cells [82]. Target deliveries of medications mediated by these NPs are most frequently delayed by the premature discharge of medicinal products from carriers before reaching specific goals. The potential for the drug supply of these NPs can be increased by combining specific ligands (Abs or proteins) to their surface [83].

Micelles produced with Dapoxetine increased absorption and effectiveness across the BBB, according to Abourehab and colleagues. It enhanced brain delivery to improve patients' convenience and compliance [84]. Ritonavir polymer–drug conjugates that form micelles and allow for controlled drug release were developed by Koziolová and colleagues for tumor treatment. Following the release of the hydrophobic drug, a decrease in polymer hydrophobicity caused the micelles to disintegrate under mildly acidic conditions, reflecting the environment within tumor tissues or cells [85].

Polymeric micelles are especially useful for improving the solubility and bioavailability of medications that are poorly soluble in water. Their core–shell architecture allows for the encapsulation of hydrophobic substances within the micellar core, while the hydrophilic shell enhances stability in biological settings. The recent advances in stimuli-responsive polymeric micelles have accelerated targeted drug delivery and allowed for more accurate controlled release patterns. However, micellar instability under physiological dilution settings is still a serious restriction that must be carefully considered during formulation design.

Dendrimers

Polymer-based nanoparticles known as dendrimers have garnered a lot of attention as potential drug delivery vehicles. The term dendrimer comes from the Greek word dendra, which means "tree," referring to their extremely branching, tree-like design. These nanostructures are made from both natural and synthetic monomers, such as amino acids, nucleotides, and monosaccharides, with poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) being one of the most well-studied dendrimer types [86, 87]. Dendrimers show great promise for drug delivery because to their internal chambers, regulated size, high degree of branching, and simplicity of surface modification. Their homogeneous size distribution and the availability of several functional terminal groups allow for receptor-specific interactions, which facilitates targeted therapeutic administration.

Kannan et al. found that systemic administration of poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers resulted in preferential accumulation in astrocytes and activated microglial cells in a neonatal rabbit model of cerebral palsy, indicating their potential for clinical use in neuroinflammatory disorders [88]. Furthermore, Albertazzi et al. demonstrated that surface modification of PAMAM dendrimers has a considerable influence on their diffusion behavior, underscoring the relevance of surface engineering in optimizing biological performance [88, 89].

Dendrimers have a high drug-loading capacity and a well-defined design, but they have some drawbacks that could prevent them from being used in therapeutic settings. Because of their potent electrostatic interactions with biological membranes, cationic dendrimers in particular can cause hemolytic and cytotoxic consequences. Large-scale manufacturing may also be constrained by the intricate synthesis process and comparatively expensive production costs. Therefore, in dendrimer-based drug delivery systems, surface modification techniques like PEGylation or ligand conjugation are frequently used to increase biocompatibility and lower toxicity [86–89].

Polymeric nanogels

Nanogel-based delivery platforms offer significant potential for next-generation drug delivery applications. Nanogels are ionic and non-ionic polymeric chains that form a cross-linked polymeric network. Chemically cross-linked or physically self-assembled nanogels can contain hydrophilic or hydrophobic medicines. Charged biomolecules such as proteins, polynucleotides, oligonucleotides, and low molecular weight drugs can associate with oppositely charged ionic chains within the polymeric nanogel network [90]

Glucose-functionalized polymeric nanocarriers, designed to associate with glucose transporter-1 (GLUT-1) expressed on brain capillary endothelial cells, have been reported to possess an average particle size of about 45 nm. A suitable glucose-functionalized ligand density enabled efficient and stable association of ASOs in nanocarriers with a particle size of about 45 nm. In several brain regions, including the cerebral cortex and hippocampal regions, the enhanced nanocarrier clumps in brain tissue approximately 1 h after intravenous injection and suppresses a target long non-coding RNA [91]. Vinogradov and colleagues recently employed this method to transport oligonucleotides through the BBB. In vivo evaluations revealed improved oligonucleotide delivery to the brain accompanied by decreased sequestration in reticuloendothelial tissues. Transcytosis through BCECs appears to be involved in nanogel-mediated drug delivery methods. It has been demonstrated that surface modification of nanogels with targeted polypeptides, like insulin or transferrin, greatly improves their therapeutic efficacy [92].

Polymeric nanogels have a number of drawbacks that could prevent their clinical application, despite their encouraging qualities. These obstacles include complicated synthesis processes, inconsistent drug release patterns, and limitations related to long-term stability and large-scale manufacture. To obtain optimal CNS drug delivery, formulation parameters must be carefully optimized since interactions between the polymeric network and biological surroundings might impact drug-loading efficiency and release kinetics [90–93].

Micro/nanoemulsions

Emulsifying agents, such as surfactants and co-surfactants, allow two otherwise immiscible liquids to be incorporated into a single homogeneous phase, creating thermodynamically stable and isotropic systems. The size and shape of the dispersed phase are the main characteristics that set nanoemulsions apart from ordinary emulsions. Typically, nanoemulsions have droplet sizes between 20 and 200 nm. These formulations are often made by mixing aqueous phases with biocompatible oils, such as fatty acids and triglycerides, and surface-active substances. Notably, bioactive components found in oils high in omega-3 fatty acids may help transport across the blood–brain barrier's (BBB) tight junctions [93].

Using a high-pressure homogenization method, Harun et al. created cefuroxime-loaded nanoemulsions and then assessed their physicochemical characteristics. The modified formulation demonstrated improved in vivo pharmacokinetic performance and met parenteral administration requirements, indicating its potential to boost cefuroxime's brain-targeted distribution [94].

Micro- and nanoemulsion systems have a number of drawbacks that could prevent them from being used in clinical settings, despite their benefits in increasing drug solubility and bioavailability. These include potential irritation or toxicity from high surfactant concentrations, problems with thermodynamic and kinetic instability during extended storage, and limited drug-loading capacity for specific therapeutic medicines. Moreover, a major obstacle to successful clinical translation is maintaining formulation stability and repeatability during large-scale production [93–96].

Drug-loaded nano emulsion

The chemical and biological characteristics of inorganic nanomaterials with sizes between 10 and 1000 nm have drawn increasing attention in recent years. Because of their distinct physicochemical properties, drug delivery methods based on nanoparticles are thought to be extremely promising [95]. These systems allow for the inclusion of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic therapeutic agents and offer high drug-loading capacity, enhanced stability, regulated and sustained release profiles, and targeted delivery to certain tissues or cell populations. When taken as a whole, these benefits aid in addressing numerous drawbacks of traditional medication delivery methods [96, 97]. Diffusion, erosion, carrier degradation, and externally driven stimuli are some of the ways that drugs can be released from such systems.

Ceramic nanoparticles and protein-based carriers are commonly used in targeted delivery applications. The ease of surface functionalization and the biocompatibility of the engineered material are important considerations when choosing a nanoparticle manufacturing approach [98]. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have become one of the most effective and adaptable inorganic nanocarriers for drug delivery. Specifically, AuNPs loaded with doxorubicin have shown improved drug accumulation and therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of cancer, in part by circumventing pathways of multidrug resistance (MDR) [99].

Although nanoemulsion systems have many advantages, they also have certain disadvantages.

These include reduced drug-loading capacity for some hydrophilic medicines, possible toxicity linked to high surfactant concentrations, and physical instability like droplet coalescence and Ostwald ripening. Furthermore, clinical translation still faces difficulties in sustaining long-term stability and repeatability throughout large-scale production [95–98].

Exosomes

Exosomes have gotten the greatest attention because of their capacity to mediate the intercellular transfer of bioactive cargo to both neighboring and distant cells, therefore controlling the normal physiological functions and disease-related responses of target cells. Exosomes have extensive promise to be turned into a practical, accurate, and safe drug delivery system because of their natural and unique signalling mechanism [100]. Exosomes are naturally occurring extracellular nanovesicles released by many cell types, characterized by lipid bilayer membranes containing surface proteins that facilitate efficient membrane interactions. Exosomes, naturally occurring extracellular vesicles that transport tiny nucleic acids and proteins between cells, have shown great potential for delivering exogenous medicines or biological therapies, particularly to the brain [101].

Because of their biocompatibility, exosomes offer numerous benefits over manufactured NPs, particularly intrinsic non-immunogenicity, and long circulation durations. However, surface-functionalization and synthetic exosome-inspired nanovesicles have been reported to exhibit promising therapeutic efficacy [102]. Drugs carried through an exosomal vector are generally more effective and have fewer side effects. Exosomes' natural drug-delivery capabilities can be enhanced and used for a very appealing therapeutic strategy [103].

Alvarez-Erviti et al. utilized engineered exosomes displaying lysosome-associated membrane protein 2b (Lamp2b) and functionalized with a neuron-targeting rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptide to enable siRNA delivery to the mouse brain. They additionally assessed circulating levels of interleukins, interferon-γ–induced protein, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-α (IFN-α) to compare the in vivo immunological responses elicited by siRNA-RVG exosomes and siRNA-RVG-9R. In comparison to siRNA-RVG-9R, they observed no significant alterations in the cytokines [104]. In contrast to i.v. Administration of normal dopamine, dopamine-loaded exosomes generated from mouse blood was utilized to transport medicines through the BBB with low toxicity [105].

Exosomes containing Huntingtin mRNA-targeting hsiRNAs were effectively absorbed by cortical neurons, resulting in Huntingtin mRNA and protein silence. Infusing hsiRNA-loaded exosomes unilaterally into the mouse striatum, instead of hsiRNAs alone, caused oligonucleotide dispersion and achieved statistically significant bilateral suppression of Huntingtin mRNA levels by up to 35%. These findings suggest that hsiRNA-loaded exosomes, owing to their widespread brain delivery and efficacy, may contribute to the rapid advancement of therapeutic strategies for Huntington’s disease and related neurodegenerative disorders [106].

Exosomes have recently emerged as highly promising nanocarriers for CNS drug delivery due to their intrinsic biocompatibility and ability to cross biological barriers. Engineered exosomes have demonstrated effective delivery of therapeutic molecules across the BBB, particularly for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. However, challenges including large-scale production, purification, and heterogeneity of exosome populations remain major barriers to clinical translation.

Carbon nanotubes

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) consist of large, sp2 hybridized carbon atoms stacked in a hexagonal configuration. CNTs bring different qualities that make them be among the finest drug delivery vehicles for the brain, including robust electrochemical characteristics, good absorption into cell membranes, maximum load potential, pH-dependent emptying, greater diameter, and ease of customization [107].

In recent years, CNT has been intensively explored as a medication transporter to the brain. Functionalized CNTs can be utilized as nanoparticles for medicines with low permeability across the BBB and for diagnosing and treating brain diseases. Electric arc release and thermal decomposition by using vaporization of graphite or chemical vapor deposition both might have been used to create CNT [108]. CNTs are categorized as a primary wall or multi-wall based on the number of graphene layers arranged concentrically. CNTs exhibit diameters spanning ~ 0.4–100 nm, determined by their layered structure. CNT may be chemically modified in both covalent and noncovalent ways [109]. Liu et al. reported the covalent conjugation of paclitaxel (PTX) to branched polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains on single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs) through a cleavable ester linkage, resulting in a water-dispersible SWNT–PTX nanoconjugate. In a 4T1 mouse model of breast cancer, the SWNT–PTX conjugate demonstrated superior tumor growth inhibition compared with clinically used Taxol, which was attributed to prolonged systemic circulation and an approximately tenfold increase in tumor paclitaxel accumulation following SWNT-mediated delivery, likely resulting from an enhanced vascular permeability and prolonged retention effect [110].

Ren et al. reported the design of angiopep-2–conjugated, PEGylated oxidized MWCNTs aimed at improving therapeutic delivery to brain gliomas. They found strong CNT absorption and buildup in the targeted region, as well as high loading ability. Angiopep-2 attaches to LDLR specifically and increases cellular internalization. CNT encapsulated with doxorubicin was shown to have more substantial anti-glioma effects than doxorubicin alone [111]. In a separate study, Angiopep-2 was attached to morphologically diverse functionalized multi-walled carbon nanotubes, yielding wide (w-MWCNT-ANG) and thick (t-MWCNT-ANG) nanoconstructs. At 24 h after injection, w-MWNT-ANG exhibited increased enhanced accumulation in glioma-bearing brain tissue compared with normal brain tissue. t-MWNTs-ANG, on the other hand, accumulated more in the brain than w-MWNTs. The capacity of f-MWNTs to accumulate in the brain, along with ANG's enhanced brain targeting, suggests that f-MWNT-ANG might be used to deliver active therapies for brain glioma treatment in the future [112].

CNTs have gained significant attention as an effective and versatile nanocarrier system for CNS-targeted drug delivery, offering new possibilities in neuroscience research and therapeutic development. Therapeutic drugs can be delivered to the central nervous system (CNS) effectively due to the unique physicochemical characteristics of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), such as their enormous surface area, excellent biocompatibility, and capacity to cross biological barriers. There is growing evidence that CNT-based delivery systems can improve therapeutic outcomes in neurodegenerative illnesses including Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, decrease off-target toxicity, and increase medication bioavailability.

In addition, CNTs' structural adaptability enables accurate surface functionalization and tailored drug loading, making it easier to respond to particular therapeutic requirements. With enormous implications for neuropharmacology and clinical applications, CNTs provide a viable platform for the development of highly focused and successful neurotherapeutic treatments by overcoming the primary obstacle of drug transport across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [113].

Because of their large surface area and capacity to cross biological membranes, functionalized carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have demonstrated significant promise for targeted drug delivery in brain malignancies and neurodegenerative illnesses. However, its clinical translation is still restricted by issues with toxicity and long-term biocompatibility.

Metallic nanoparticles

Metal-based nanoparticles are widely used in both the medicinal and technical domains and have long piqued scientific interest. Their significant promise in nanotechnology has prompted a great deal of research, especially when it comes to brain-targeted medicine delivery. Because of their advantageous biocompatibility and adaptable physicochemical characteristics, metals including gold, silver, and platinum are widely used. Because of their large surface area, inherent antibacterial action, and structural and biochemical selectivity, these nanoparticles are widely used. In addition, they are easily functionalized with a range of organic compounds, allowing for covalent conjugation with medicinal drugs, ligands, and antibodies. Numerous applications, including as gene therapy, biomolecule separation, and targeted medication administration, are supported by this flexibility [114].

Silver ions produced from silver nanoparticles can interact with the negatively charged bacterial cell membrane, increasing membrane permeability and allowing foreign chemicals to enter the intracellular milieu [115]. Transcytosis is one of the main ways whereby metallic nanoparticles get through the blood–brain barrier (BBB) in the context of brain administration. Additionally, peptide-mediated functionalization of nanoparticles improves transport across the blood–brain barrier by increasing their permeability to the central nervous system (CNS) [116]. The significance of receptor-mediated targeting techniques has been highlighted by studies showing that gold nanoparticles functionalized with transferrin receptor–targeted peptides showed dramatically increased delivery efficiency to brain endothelial cells [117].

Metallic nanoparticles have a number of drawbacks that must be taken into account for CNS medication delivery, despite their encouraging qualities. Potential cytotoxicity and oxidative stress are key concerns, especially for silver and other reactive metal nanoparticles that, if improperly surface-modified, could harm biological components. Furthermore, metallic nanoparticles can build up in tissues and frequently show poor biodegradability, which raises questions about systemic toxicity and long-term safety. Non-specific biodistribution presents another difficulty since it might cause nanoparticles to build up in organs like the liver and spleen, which lowers the effectiveness of brain targeting. Therefore, careful design strategies including surface functionalization, size optimization, and biocompatible coatings are required to improve their safety profile and targeting capability for CNS drug delivery [115–118].

Recent advances in therapeutic targeting of brain disorders

Nanofiber scaffolds

Self-assembling peptide nanofibers have gained significant attention as biomaterial scaffolds for promoting CNS repair, particularly in optic nerve and spinal cord injuries. Because these nanofibers are composed of simple L-amino acids, they are extremely biocompatible structures that are created when peptides self-assemble under physiological conditions [102].

The Arg–Ala–Asp–Ala (RADA) peptide is one well-researched system. Critical neuronal behaviors, such as synapse formation in brain neurons and increased neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells, have been demonstrated to be supported in vitro by nanofiber scaffolds made from RADA peptides [129]. In a hamster optic tract transection model, experimental in vivo studies showed that RADA-based scaffolds enable axonal regeneration across lesion locations, suggesting the optic nerve retains intrinsic regenerative capacity after injury [130]. Nevertheless, there was no discernible functional improvement as a result of this regeneration. The authors speculate that the observed limitation may be due to either areas of neuronal injury possibly caused by Mn2+ contrast chemicals or the absence of intact ganglion cell axons at the lesion site [131].

Notwithstanding these drawbacks, further development of nanofiber scaffold technologies has great potential for brain repair, especially when combined with cellular or pharmacological cues that promote axonal regeneration [132]. In relation to spinal cord injury (SCI), aligned (longitudinally structured) nanofiber scaffolds have also been investigated. Implanting a crosslinked gelatin–dextran sulfate nanofiber scaffold seeded with human embryonic spinal cord–derived cells led to a notable functional recovery in rat SCI models, including complete transection [133].

In addition, the research has demonstrated that, in comparison to non-seeded structures, self-assembling peptide nanofiber scaffolds in conjunction with neural stem cells (NSCs) increase axonal innervation [134]. The significance of supporting cell inclusion in scaffold-based regeneration techniques was highlighted by the observation that scaffolds containing Schwann cells showed even higher degrees of neuronal integration [135]. All things considered, these results highlight the therapeutic potential of peptide- and polymer-based nanofiber scaffolds as novel methods for encouraging regeneration in the damaged central nervous system (CNS), especially when combined with neural or glial cells [136].

3D printing technologies

The creation of intricate and extremely accurate biological structures has been made possible by the combination of sophisticated 3D printing technology with adaptable biomaterials. These developments are especially pertinent to illnesses of the central nervous system (CNS), such as brain tumors, neurodegenerative diseases, cerebrovascular abnormalities, and traumatic injuries [133].

3D-printed nerve guiding conduits have been designed to aid with nerve regeneration and repair in the peripheral nervous system (PNS). In addition to preventing nerve compression and minimizing scar formation, these designed scaffolds can serve as structural bridges to promote regeneration across massive nerve deficits [134]. When taken as a whole, these developments highlight how versatile additive printing is in neural tissue engineering.

The creation of 3D-printed scaffolds for regulated medication distribution is a noteworthy development. Levodopa-loaded scaffolds were created in one investigation, and during a 14-day period, the drug release followed a Fickian diffusion profile. MTT tests, fluorescence microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to verify the biocompatibility of these structures. Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hAD-MSCs) showed high survivability on the scaffold surface [135].

These results imply that 3D-printed scaffolds based on PLA/chitosan (PLA/CS) offer a potential platform for brain tissue engineering and could be an effective way to distribute levodopa for the treatment of Parkinson's disease [136].

4D printing

The capabilities of traditional 3D printing have been greatly expanded by developments in materials science and additive manufacturing. As a result, 4D printing has emerged, in which manufactured structures undergo controlled, time-dependent transformations in response to external stimuli, with time being considered the fourth dimension. Research has increasingly concentrated on nanocomposite systems, in which nanoparticles (NPs) are integrated into host matrices to improve functional performance, due to present constraints in nanoscale production [137].

Methods like programmed structural design, which allows for the exact spatial organization of heterogeneous materials (inks) across layers and promotes self-morphing behavior, can be used to create 4D printing [138]. Since Schwann cells secrete neurotrophic factors that promote peripheral nerve regeneration, bioprinting these cells within hydrogels like alginate has shown promising results in the field of neural engineering [139]. Additionally, the functional capabilities of 4D printing systems have been further improved by the addition of nanoparticles to bioinks, which modulate characteristics like shear-thinning behavior and introduce stimuli responsiveness [140].

The promise of 4D printing in medical applications, especially in tissue regeneration and repair, is becoming more widely acknowledged. Although scaffolds with intricate geometries can be created using 3D printing, the dynamic character of natural tissue regeneration cannot be entirely replicated. On the other hand, 4D-printed structures have the ability to react to environmental stimuli and cellular traction pressures, which may greatly improve regenerative results by affecting cell shape, alignment, density, and intercellular communication [141].

5D and 6D printing technologies

5D and 6D printing technologies extend conventional 3D printing by integrating additional functional capabilities into the manufacturing process; although still in early development, they have the potential to revolutionize the fabrication of complex biological architectures.

Five-dimensional (5D) printing refers to a multi-axis additive manufacturing technique in which the print head and the printing platform move along five different axes, allowing curved-layer deposition instead of the traditional planar layer-by-layer approach used in 3D printing. This enables the fabrication of structures with improved mechanical strength, structural integrity, and complex geometries, which may be beneficial for biomedical scaffold design and tissue engineering applications [142, 143].

Unlike 4D printing, which incorporates time-dependent shape transformation through stimuli-responsive materials, 5D printing primarily focuses on improving structural design through advanced multi-axis fabrication rather than dynamic material responsiveness. In biomedical engineering, this approach may improve the fabrication of complex scaffolds and implants that better mimic natural tissue architecture [143].

While 4D and 5D printing have gained traction in biomaterials research, six-dimensional (6D) printing remains largely conceptual and is generally described as the integration of multi-axis printing capabilities with smart, stimuli-responsive materials and additional design parameters such as topology and hierarchical microstructures. In theory, 6D printing could combine the structural advantages of 5D multi-axis fabrication with the adaptive properties of 4D stimuli-responsive materials, enabling the development of advanced biomedical constructs capable of dynamic functional responses to environmental stimuli [144].

Challenges of using NPs in CNS delivery

Despite the great potential of nanoparticles (NPs) to enhance the transport of medications across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), there are still considerable obstacles that prevent their clinical use in CNS settings. Physical and chemical instability, biological safety, production constraints, and regulatory hurdles are all part of these problems.

Particle aggregation and physical instability

In addition to improving drug loading and surface functionalisation, nanoparticles' high surface-to-volume ratio strengthens strong interparticle interactions, such as van der Waals forces. Aggregation could occur as a result of these interactions whether the substance is being prepared, stored, or exposed to bodily fluids. When nanoparticles aggregate, it changes their size distribution, surface charge, biodistribution profile, and colloidal stability. Reduced therapeutic efficacy and potential toxicity may result from larger aggregates' inability to pass the blood–brain barrier (BBB), rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), or obstruction of capillary microcirculation. Nanoparticle compositions are difficult to physically manipulate, reproduce, and scale due to aggregation.

Uncontrolled burst release and drug leakage

The risk of uncontrolled or premature medicine release is a major drawback of CNS delivery systems based on nanoparticles. An injection of poorly optimised NP matrices can cause the encapsulated medications to release quickly, leading to transiently raised local drug concentrations that could cause neurotoxicity or systemic side effects. There may be less medicine reaching the brain and less therapeutic efficacy if drugs leak out of the bloodstream too soon. One of the biggest challenges in pharmaceutical formulation is getting the release kinetics of strong neuroactive chemicals exactly regulated.

High production costs and scalability issues

Developing drug delivery systems based on nanoparticles is usually a complex multi-step process that calls for specialised equipment, strict environmental standards, and costly raw materials. Problems with scaling up and maintaining batch-to-batch consistency may arise when using methods like microfluidics, solvent evaporation, nanoprecipitation, or high-pressure homogenisation. The commercial viability and widespread clinical application of NP-based CNS medicines are hindered by these restrictions, which increase production costs. Manufacturing costs are also driven up by the need for sterile production and stringent quality control.

Toxicity and biocompatibility concerns

Despite the use of biocompatible materials in the construction of many nanoparticle systems, there are still significant safety concerns regarding their interactions with biological systems, particularly those located within the central nervous system (CNS). During nanoparticle manufacturing, stabilisers and surfactants like polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) can be used excessively or persistently, which might lead to cytotoxic, immunogenic, or inflammatory effects. Neuronal signalling and the integrity of the blood–brain barrier can be compromised by mild inflammatory reactions in the brain. In addition, nothing is known about the long-term fate of nanoparticles, which includes biodegradation, accumulation, and removal from brain tissue. There is cause for concern about the potential long-term neurotoxicity of certain inorganic or non-biodegradable nanoparticles that might persist in neural tissues.

Immune recognition and rapid clearance

Plasma proteins often opsonise systemically administered nanoparticles, allowing reticuloendothelial macrophages to quickly remove them. This process drastically cuts down on circulation time and limits the amount of nanoparticles that can cross the blood–brain barrier. Repetitive treatment may trigger immunological responses or speed clearance owing to the formation of anti-PEG antibodies, even if surface changes like PEGylation can somewhat limit immune recognition.

Limited targeting specificity and off-target effects

Achieving precise targeting of nanoparticles to specific brain regions or cell types poses a considerable challenge. The indiscriminate dissemination of nanoparticles may lead to unintentional accumulation in peripheral organs, such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, hence increasing the risk of systemic toxicity. In addition, competing with endogenous ligands, receptor saturation, and individual differences in receptor expression may hinder receptor-mediated targeting techniques.

Regulatory and translational barriers

The transfer of nanoparticle-based CNS therapies from lab research to clinical use is hampered by significant regulatory obstacles. Standardized frameworks for evaluating the safety, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and long-term toxicity of nanomaterials are still desperately needed. Furthermore, regulatory agencies require comprehensive preclinical and clinical data to accurately determine the risk–benefit profile of nanomedicines, which often leads to extended development timelines and increased costs [40].

Future perspectives

The BBB acts as a major pharmacological barrier in CNS therapy, and consequently, brain-targeted drug delivery strategies have gained increasing attention as a means to circumvent its restrictive properties. Because of its stringent selectivity, the BBB significantly reduces drug access to target areas of the brain, thereby restricting effective therapy. Despite encouraging evidence from preclinical models and clinical trials, the clinical application of many nanoformulations faces obstacles, including insufficient knowledge of their ultimate toxicological profile and issues such as nanoparticle aggregation and altered bioavailability. Toxicological evaluations of brain-targeted nanoformulations, both with and without incorporated drugs, focusing on their mechanisms of action and pharmacokinetic profiles.

Nanomedicine's therapeutic potential will be determined by a rational approach and future nanomaterial design established on a thorough knowledge of biological processes. The oral, mucosal/sublingual, transbuccal, and nasal routes should be explored for nanocarrier-mediated delivery of therapeutics to the CNS. Major challenge in translating nano-formulations from bench to bedside is toxicity, which must be carefully addressed.

It is crucial to look into how nanocarriers behave and work in comparison to bulk materials before going on sale. Therefore, in order to overcome these obstacles and guarantee the effective use of nanoformulations, fundamental research must be carried out as soon as possible.

Even while nanomedicine-based CNS medication delivery has made great strides, there are still a number of important unexplored areas. Increased permeability, decreased transporter efficiency, and persistent neuroinflammation are among the BBB alterations linked to aging that may have a major impact on nanoparticle transport and therapeutic response (Halwani, 2022). Furthermore, little study has been done on uncommon neurological conditions such lysosomal storage diseases, Huntington's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, where targeted nanodelivery methods may provide significant therapeutic advantages. Future research should also emphasize the development of personalized nanomedicine approaches, where nanocarrier design is tailored according to patient-specific pathological and genetic profiles to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

The number of people who die due to neurological or neurodegenerative illnesses is comparable to the number of people who die in a war, with significant socioeconomic consequences and expenses. The existence of BBB, which is impassable by most currently available and potentially valuable medicines, makes the treatment of such illnesses difficult. As a result, both the academic and pharmaceutical communities have a significant challenge in discovering and developing new drug delivery methods to manage such illnesses. Recent progress in nanotechnology provides viable strategies to overcome delivery-related obstacles, supporting the effective treatment of multiple CNS disorders. Consequently, nanomedicine has emerged as a potential and innovative approach for managing functional CNS diseases. The continuous discovery of novel therapeutic targets is driving further innovations in nanotechnology-mediated brain-selective drug delivery strategies. Additional research is required to better understand how various nanoparticles facilitate drug delivery into CNS. In contrast, the considerable work necessary for moving from preclinical studies to clinical implementation is worthwhile despite the increased costs.
