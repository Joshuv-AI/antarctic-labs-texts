Community based cohort studies in China: critical insights for shaping the future of population and public health
Authors: Hongmei Zeng, Xiangfeng Lu, Fangchao Liu, Xifeng Wu, Simin Liu, Dongfeng Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dongfeng Gu and colleagues review community based cohort studies in China, finding strategies to improve the quality and scale of these studies in China and beyond

Community based cohort studies collect data from free living individuals residing in specific communities over a designated timeframe. Many longitudinal community based cohort studies were initiated with extensive follow-up to discover unknown “risk factors” for incident diseases. Global examples are the landmark Framingham Heart Study and the Rotterdam Study.1 2 Although the scope and design of these studies vary according to research goals and available resources, overcoming challenges such as cost, sample collection, and follow-up duration is critical. When carefully designed, large scale, community based cohort studies have been shown to be instrumental in uncovering risk factors and informing public health policies worldwide.

Conducting community based cohort studies provides a unique landscape for the rigorous investigation of risk factors and prevention of diseases. Given the heterogeneity of risk factors and disease patterns by socioeconomic status, population specific research findings from different countries are needed for formulation of health policy around the world. Since the 1980s, researchers from developing countries such as China, the Philippines, and Iran have established several community based cohorts, which provided an important source for monitoring long term trends of risk factors and diseases and generated valuable insights to guide preventive strategies.3 4 5 6 7 8 China’s rapid economic development brings tremendous rural-urban migration and diverse sociocultural characteristics, which exacerbate challenges to recruitment and follow-up in maintaining these cohorts.

In this article, part of a BMJ Collection focusing on large scale cohort studies in China and around the world, we review the lessons learnt from community based cohort studies in China, aiming to highlight how these studies are essential for answering local contextual questions, examining social factors, and capturing information that cannot be obtained from hospital based, disease based, or registry based studies. We emphasise strategies to enhance the quality and scale of future studies in China. As one of the largest developing countries, China offers valuable insights and lessons that can inform strategies and practices in other nations. The findings and methodological development will remain highly relevant for informing similar work in developing countries experiencing rapid urbanisation.

Distinct advantages of community based cohort studies compared with other cohort studies

Community based cohort studies can access diverse study populations and a wide range of health related data. Combined with longitudinal design, these features may enable multipurpose studies, improve cost effectiveness, and facilitate valuable interdisciplinary collaboration (box 1).

Diverse population representativeness

Community based cohort studies typically include participants across a wide spectrum of health status and diseases, providing a broader representation than disease based cohort studies.1 Through proper sampling, certain community based cohort studies in China show a high degree of representativeness. For example, by using multistage random cluster weighted sampling, the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) has tracked changes in nutrition, prevalence of risk factors for cardiometabolic disease, and health status across diverse socioeconomic groups both spatially and temporally (box 2). The CHNS identified shifting dietary and activity patterns that contributed to a much steeper rise in obesity burden among the low income population in China. With the large sample size, biosamples, and open data sharing, the CHNS allows for diverse analyses in public health, medical research, economics, and health services. Disease or registry specific cohorts (for example, studies from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program) primarily recruit patients with a particular condition and track their progression and the effectiveness of treatment.9

The Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) is a large, nationally representative survey of older adults in China.3 As one of the largest cohorts of the oldest older people and centenarians, the CLHLS shows that later birth cohorts have a higher prevalence of disabilities, such as limitations in activities of daily living, cognitive impairment, and other health challenges, despite socioeconomic progress, a phenomenon often described as a “cost of success.” These findings underscore the intricate interplay between modernisation, chronic disease, and ageing. Community studies such as CLHLS can be used retrospectively to examine extreme heat events and mortality, illustrating the survey’s broader applicability to interdisciplinary research.4 The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) comprises adults (primarily ≥45), covers a broad age range, and emphasises health, retirement, and care needs . This study captured a rapid demographic transition, marked by the retirement of the “second baby boomers” (born 1962-75) since 2022 and declining family support. It showed a rise in multimorbidity caused by non-communicable diseases and disparities by gender, rural-urban residence, and socioeconomic status, and it identified key underlying risk factors such as health literacy, social engagement, and access to care.5

Multidimensional and multimodal data collection

Community based cohort studies are well positioned to capture a broad spectrum of health determinants, including socioeconomic, environmental, and behavioural risk factors (for example, dietary patterns, physical activity, and sleep habits), which are often overlooked or not adequately covered in hospital, disease, or registry based studies.10 Community based cohort studies are also valuable for assessing factors not measured by routine medical records, such as frailty and subjective quality of life. If feasible, the systematic collection of biosamples in some large scale, community based cohort studies facilitates the identification of a wide range of biomarkers and supports monitoring of the trajectories of disease incidence and progression. Given the diverse data collected by community based cohort studies, they serve as invaluable resources to evaluate the multiple causes and complex patterns of comorbidity and multimorbidity, as exemplified by a CHARLS study that found a multimorbidity prevalence of 42.4% among participants ≥50 years.5

Community based cohort studies enable the investigation of risk factors and the early detection of subclinical changes. For example, using four repeated lipid measurements from the Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China (China-PAR) cohort, Li and colleagues identified critical age windows (before age 40 years for men and 40-49 years for women) during which lipid concentrations deteriorated at a notably faster rate than at other ages. These findings suggest that interventions to improve lipid concentrations could be implemented within these critical age windows in China.11

Unique contributions of Chinese community based cohort studies in shaping public health

Expanding cohort diversity as an essential global health resource

Currently, high quality cohort resources are predominantly located in developed countries and focus on “western” populations, as evidenced by many systematic reviews or meta-analyses.12 This gap significantly hinders our global understanding of disease risk factors and public health interventions targeting East Asian populations. With more than 1.4 billion people, China is not only one of the most populous nations but also a multiethnic society with diverse cultural norms, dietary customs, environmental exposures, and health risks. The rise in China’s urbanisation rate from 19% to 64.5%, alongside growth in gross domestic product from $191bn to $17.8tn from 1980 to 2023, has led to significant shifts in the environment (for example, air pollution), lifestyles (for example, diet), and disease epidemiology. The share of adults aged ≥65 in China doubled from 7% to 14% in just two decades; in high income countries, the same shift took nearly 50 years. Population ageing, due to increases in longevity and reductions in birth rate, brings key challenges in supporting older people, such as the rising prevalence of chronic diseases and heightened social and economic burdens. Community based cohort studies such as the CHNS and CHARLS had the unique opportunity to capture enormous heterogeneity and dynamic change of social demographics, risk factors, and disease patterns both spatially and temporally in the country, which yielded several findings that could not be extrapolated from elsewhere in the world. They are national and global resources that allow the outside research community to analyse the collected data and to originate novel ancillary studies. They may also contribute to global health initiatives. For example, the China-PAR project quantitatively investigated the long term effects of PM2.5 exposure on cardiovascular health, which contributed to the World Health Organization’s updated air quality guidelines in 2021. A study from the CHNS found a higher proportion of the Chinese population with a body mass index <25 compared with non-Hispanic white people and a steeper association between body mass index and prevalence of hypertension, supporting the notion of ethnicity specific body mass index cut-offs in clinical settings.13 With the distinctive genetic architecture of the Chinese population, the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) assessed the causal relevance of alcohol consumption for cardiovascular disease and stroke, which has global implications.14

Producing region specific findings that can better inform local interventions

Large scale, community based cohort studies in China could generate region specific findings that are more feasible for local interventions. One such initiative is the China-PAR project, which has identified unique genetic backgrounds of cardiovascular disease in China, including novel genetic variants, through a genome-wide association study.15 As a result of ethnic differences in risk factors for and prevalence of cardiovascular disease, risk prediction tools developed from the Framingham Heart Study were not suitable for the Chinese population, which resulted in an overestimation of the risk of cardiovascular disease in the Chinese population by more than 50%. On the basis of previous research findings of lifestyle, environmental, metabolic, and genetic factors of cardiovascular disease, China-PAR has constructed a more accurate risk prediction model tailored for the Chinese population by incorporating four additional variables (waist circumference, geographical region, urbanisation, and family history of cardiovascular disease), which has been validated by other studies.16 Notably, waist circumference, rather than body mass index, was included in the model, suggesting that central obesity deserves more attention among Chinese populations. These insights have promoted a more individualised approach to primary prevention of and timely intervention for cardiovascular disease in the Chinese population, which may, in turn, contribute to improved life expectancy and healthy life expectancy.

Support for resource allocation and policy implications

Another unique contribution of community based cohort studies in China is their ability to capture extensive heterogeneity and temporal-spatial variations in one of the world’s most rapidly changing environments. A broader representation of community based cohort studies, encompassing diverse age groups, socioeconomic status, and geographical regions, allows for high level evidence that significantly affects public health practices and informs resource allocation within China. For example, findings from the CHNS have supported the implementation of national nutrition policies, including the enactment of a national fortification law to enrich soy sauce and flour with iron,17 as well as substantial dietary restrictions on salt intake.18 Adopting the cost effectiveness evidence derived from the China-PAR project, the Chinese government has revised reimbursement policies for treatment of hypertension in China.19

Experiences from established community based cohort studies in China

Through strategic planning, China’s top down governance with efficient bottom level implementation has the capacity to mobilise resources and achieve large scale goals. Strong support from central and local government, particularly in the context of the national 2030 Healthy China strategy, has been crucial in the development and sustainability of large scale, community based cohort studies. Maintaining collaborative relationships with local leaders, community representatives, and community health workers is also key to success.20 Community health workers act as a bridge between community members and health services. The provision of in-house training and performance based incentives for community health workers such as the village doctors in China, who serve as long term and reliable assistants for epidemiologists, has been shown to enhance adherence to community cohort maintenance.21 By training community health workers and using multiple outreach strategies, including social media (local television stations, radio, WeChat), transportation stipends, flexible participation schedules, and follow-up visits, an annual liver cancer screening cohort achieved unprecedented participation rates.22 As a result of close collaborations with local governments and clinical centres,23 some regions have also established field offices for community based cohort studies, facilitating cohort maintenance. To maintain participants’ engagement and motivation, need based benefits, such as complementary health consultations and health checks, have been found to enhance participants’ compliance.

Limitations of community based cohort studies versus other study designs

Community based cohort studies are criticised for high costs, resource intensity, inefficient resource use, and difficulties in maintenance and follow-up. In particular, maintaining sample representativeness in community based cohort studies may face challenges in practice, such as lower response rates and higher attrition, especially among populations with lower socioeconomic status. Rural-to-urban migration in China has posed significant logistical challenges. Mistrust further exacerbates attrition rates. In general, cohort studies are an inefficient way to study uncommon exposures with long lag times. Case-cohort or registry based cohort studies may be better suited for investigating rare or less common diseases.

The value of representativeness, or do we truly need it?

The importance of representativeness depends on the research question. The traditional inclination towards representativeness is more relevant for direct projection or estimation of the actual distribution of exposure and outcomes in the population.22 For aetiological research, population representativeness is non-essential when the community based cohort studies are sufficiently large and capture adequate heterogeneity of exposure and disease patterns.24 For example, the hazard ratios for smoking and diseases generated from the CKB cohorts were largely generalisable to the Chinese population.25 Under certain circumstances, statistical techniques such as stratified, fine grained analyses and inverse probability weighting can help to generate unbiased and generalisable results, even with non-random samples. When selecting the baseline population for community based cohort studies, researchers must consider the distinctiveness of the exposure factors and the feasibility of maintaining a longlasting study.

Strategies to enhance community based cohort studies in China

Community based cohort studies are still relevant in a new data rich age. Behavioural, social, and environmental exposure patterns continue to evolve. Data sharing and global partnerships are crucial for maximising the impact of research and optimising data utilisation.26 The China Cohort Consortium is an academic initiative aimed at fostering collaboration in China. Conducting data linkage must comply with legal regulations and ethical frameworks. Particular challenges emerge when complex content documentation intersects with limited health literacy, creating barriers to obtaining consent from participants, especially in rural populations and older people. In such a context, neighbourhood committees and village doctors may serve as mediators by delivering culturally adapted explanations during the consent process to enhance transparency and trust. Modern technologies such as Blockchain and Federated Learning facilitate secure multiethnic analysis. An urgent need exists to enhance the efficiency and trustworthiness of cross border collaborations while ensuring data protection and privacy.26 Coordinated action through a centralised expert commission is crucial. Application of novel technologies such as multi-omics detection, artificial intelligence (AI), and big data analytics brings new research opportunities. This integration enables more accurate risk assessments and a better understanding of disease triggers and mechanisms. Kalia and colleagues applied high resolution mass spectrometry based metabolomics in a community based cohort, shedding light on how PM2.5 exposure alters metabolic outcomes in older adults.27

Coordinated action to ensure sustainable development of community based cohort studies

Strong governmental support, public-private funding mechanisms, and tackling challenges in long term follow-up and maintenance are crucial for the sustainable development of community based cohort studies. Time to build enduring trust and long term goodwill between community participants and researchers may help to mitigate the challenges of recruiting people, especially those who are vulnerable.

The establishment of a central expert advisory commission would be a transformative step towards ensuring that community based cohort studies in China achieve their full potential. This commission would provide strategic, systematic, and integrated guidance for the development and governance of community based cohort studies. It could oversee the initiation and management of large scale cohorts and consortiums, aligning their ethical design and objectives with national priorities and providing ongoing funding support. Such a centralised structure ensures coherence in planning, avoids duplication of effort, and enhances the scientific and policy impact of these studies. Without such a structured and unified approach, a risk exists that fragmented efforts will prioritise short term projects or funding objectives over the broader goals of sustainability and scientific excellence. This fragmentation not only diminishes the impact of cohort studies but also leads to inefficient resource use and missed opportunities to tackle urgent public health challenges.

Leveraging scalable technologies to revolutionise community based cohort studies

Technological transition provides new opportunities for researchers and communities, with information technology and biotechnology allowing for precision measurements and evolving real time data collection. Analytical capacities using AI can identify emerging risk factors, track disease trajectories, predict disease dynamics, and inform precision medicine. Within community based cohort studies, the application of wearable devices and digital health information technology has opened new frontiers for collecting multimodal health data and macro-environmental data. These technologies enable a more comprehensive assessment of individuals’ health status, lifestyle behaviours, and environmental exposures, thereby advancing our understanding of the multifactorial causes of diseases. The technological shift not only enhances the precision of research but also supports more personalised health management and early detection of potential health risks. Researchers used a web based telemedicine platform to conduct standardised management for hypertension in 66 community health centres in China, significantly improving the blood pressure control rate and lowering blood pressure levels.28 Enhancing data through multisource linkage without the need for continuous active contact with the population can reduce costs while ensuring more accurate and comprehensive information.

Conclusions

Community based cohort studies in China have evolved into invaluable global resources, providing unparalleled data crucial to inform preventive strategies and policy making. Strategic government support, engagement of communities and participants, and multidisciplinary collaborations are essential for their success. These cohorts also form the foundation for cross regional collaborative studies, which can provide new insights into disease epidemiology.

Key messages

Community based cohort studies offer advantages such as a wide range of health status and diseases, the ability to understand the trajectories and multiple factors that influence health and longevity, and valuable insights for health that traditional hospital, disease, or registry based studies cannot provide, thereby leading to more effective evidence based preventive measures and public health policies
