The relationship between sport competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation levels among professional athletes in Turkey
Authors: Mesut Bulut, Metin Yildiz, Mustafa Kizilkoca, Gürkan Tokgöz, Buğra Can
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRumination is a cognitive process characterized by the development of repetitive and difficult-to-control thoughts regarding negative experiences. The aim of this study is to examine the cross-sectional association between sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation levels among professional athletes actively competing in team sports in Turkey, and to investigate whether these levels differ according to gender, age, sport discipline and sports experience.MethodA total of 657 professional athletes (289 women, 368 men) with a mean age of 21.88 ± 5.53 years participated in the study. Data collection tools included a demographic information form, the “Sports Competition Rumination Scale,” and the “Sport-Specific Achievement Motivation Scale.” Data analysis involved the Independent Samples t-test for pairwise comparisons, ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, and the Tukey post-hoc test. Relationships between the scales were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis, while the statistical association between rumination and achievement motivation was evaluated using regression analysis.FindingsIt was determined that levels of sports competition rumination showed significant differences according to the variables of gender, age, sport discipline, and years of participation in sports. Significant differences in sport-specific achievement motivation levels were also identified based on age and sport discipline. Correlation analysis results indicated a positive and low-level significant relationship between sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation (r = 0.105; p < 0.01). According to the results of the regression analysis, it was determined that sports competition rumination shows a low-level and positive association with achievement motivation (R² = 0.011; p < 0.01).ConclusionThe research findings reveal a low-level yet significant positive relationship between sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation. This result suggests that athletes’ post-competition cognitive processes may have a limited but significant relationship with their success orientations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01899-z.Keywords: Achievement Motivation, Soccer Players, Performance, Professional Athletes, Rumination

Introduction

Competitive athletes are characterized by their capacity for deliberate and sustained performance under highly stressful conditions, which are inherent to the demanding nature of athletic competitions and the immediate impact of their actions. Managing these high-pressure scenarios effectively necessitates an exceptional degree of both immediate and long-term self-regulatory skills. Consequently, elite performance is consistently associated with the utilization of proactive, problem-focused, and resilient coping [1]. Within the realm of competitive sports, these cognitive interferences heavily dictate psychological outcomes; mainly, emotion-focused and meaning-seeking ruminative tendencies may actively hinder problem-solving-oriented coping and instead promote emotion-focused coping strategies, thereby negatively affecting overall athletic performance [2]. In a sports context, rumination manifests as repetitive thought cycles regarding errors, poor performance, and missed opportunities, and can negatively impact athletes’ psychological readiness. The literature reports that ruminative thoughts reduce cognitive flexibility and lower the level of cognitive efficiency in performance-demanding tasks [3]. In their study, Özcan et al. emphasize the critical role of training load, recovery, and individualized support in maintaining the psychological responses and motivation of young athletes. Training-induced fatigue and adaptation stress can only be overcome through proper load management and structured recovery strategies. Indeed, the relationship between the competition perceptions and sport-specific achievement motivation levels of professional athletes in Turkey also shows that sustainable achievement motivation and high performance are only possible through the integration of these balanced load management and psychological support processes [4]. Additionally, it is noted that ruminative thoughts divert athletes’ attention away from the current performance task, thereby increasing the likelihood of making errors [5]. However, the effects of rumination are not limited to cognitive and performance outcomes. It is noted that repetitive negative thoughts can weaken an individual’s self-efficacy perception and disrupt goal-directed behaviors [6]. It is believed that this situation may also exert significant effects on athletes’ motivational processes. Indeed, studies conducted with athletes have shown that there are significant relationships between ruminative thoughts and competitive motivation, and that this relationship may indirectly affect athletes’ psychological adaptation [7]. Furthermore, research in the context of goal orientation reveals that athletes who fail to achieve their goals exhibit higher levels of rumination [8].

Athletic success is a multidimensional structure shaped by the interaction of physical capacity as well as cognitive and psychological processes. Studies in the field of sport psychology indicate that psychological variables such as attention control, emotion regulation, and motivation are significant determinants of athletes’ psychological well-being and success orientations [9, 10]. In this context, achievement motivation is considered a key psychological construct associated with an individual’s desire to achieve goals, level of effort, and tendency to improve their goal-directed outcomes [11]. It is particularly noted that achievement motivation is one of the fundamental determinants of psychological sustainability in competitive sports environments. Research indicates that athletes with high achievement motivation demonstrate greater effort, cope more effectively with challenges, and achieve higher levels of psychological resilience [12, 13]. However, it is noted that athletes’ motivation levels may be influenced not only by internal factors but also by the cognitive processes they experience. In particular, it is suggested that ruminative thoughts may influence athletes’ motivation and coping processes through emotion regulation and awareness mechanisms [14]. Similarly, it is reported that athletes’ cognitive responses to failure and feedback are associated with ruminative thoughts, and this situation exerts decisive effects on anxiety and motivation [15].

A review of the current literature reveals that, within the sports context, rumination is primarily associated with anxiety, stress, and various psychological variables; however, the relationship between post-competition rumination and achievement motivation in professional team athletes has been addressed in only a limited number of studies. There is a need for a more comprehensive examination of the effects of post-competition cognitive processes on motivational structures, particularly among professional athletes. In this regard, determining the post-competition rumination processes and levels of achievement motivation among professional athletes is considered important. It is believed that such studies will not only contribute to the development of athletes’ cognitive awareness and mental preparation processes but will also provide important insights for coaches, sports psychologists, and related academic research.

This study aims to examine the relationship between sports competition rumination and levels of achievement motivation among professional athletes actively competing in team sports. Additionally, the study aims to determine the linear relationship between rumination and achievement motivation, while also investigating whether athletes’ levels of rumination and achievement motivation differ according to variables such as gender, age, sport discipline and sports experience. Furthermore, it is hypothesized that there is a significant relationship between sports competition rumination and achievement motivation, with rumination accounting for a limited portion of the variance.

Materials and methods

Research model

In this study, the correlational survey model, one of the quantitative research methods, was applied. The correlational survey model is an approach used to determine the existence of a relationship between two or more variables.

Study population and sample

The study population consists of approximately 6,700 licensed athletes competing in professional leagues in the sports of soccer, handball, basketball, and volleyball in Turkey. The population size was calculated based on official data from the relevant federations and the number of teams in the professional leagues. The research sample was formed using convenience sampling from 657 professional athletes actively participating in these sports who were reached via online data collection tools (Google Forms). The inclusion criteria for the participants required being actively licensed and competing in the respective professional leagues (ranging from the Super League to the 3rd Division) during the study period, being aged 13 or older, and participating voluntarily.

To determine the statistical adequacy of the sample size, a prior power analysis was conducted using the G*Power (v3.1.9.7) program; the effect size was set at r = 0.20 (moderate), the significance level at α = 0.05, and the test power (1-β) at 0.95. As a result of the analysis, the required minimum sample size was calculated as n = 430; it was confirmed that the current sample of 657 participants more than met the targeted statistical power and that the study possesses high representativeness.

Data collection tools

The Personal Information Form, along with the Sports Competition Rumination and Sport-Specific Achievement motivation scales, were used to collect data. During the data collection process, which was conducted on a voluntary basis, participants were reached via both paper forms (face-to-face) and digital surveys (Google Forms) broadly during the active competitive season, rather than immediately following a specific match.

Personal information form

A personal information form prepared by the researcher, containing questions regarding gender, age, sport discipline, and years of participation, was used in the study to identify participants’ personal information.

Sports Competition Rumination Scale (SMRÖ)

To determine participants’ levels of sports competition rumination, the “Sports Competition Rumination Scale” was used. This scale was developed by Michel-Kröhler, Krys, and Berti [16] and adapted into Turkish by Karafil and Pehlivan [17]. The “Sports Competition Rumination Scale” consists of 6 items and has a unifactorial structure. The items on the scale are presented as a “5-point Likert-type scale.” The Likert options are ranked from 1 to 5 as follows: “Strongly Disagree (1),” “Somewhat Disagree (2),” “Disagree (3),” “Somewhat Agree (4),” and “Strongly Agree (5),” and scoring is based on this scale. The total score on the scale ranges from a minimum of 5 to a maximum of 30 points. The Cronbach’s Alpha (Cronbach-) internal consistency coefficient for the scale was calculated as 0.87. In this study, the Cronbach’s Alpha (Cronbach-) internal consistency coefficient for the collected data was found to be α = 0.85.

Sport-Specific Achievement Motivation Scale (SSAMS)

The “Sport-Specific Achievement Motivation Scale,” developed by Willis [18] and adapted into Turkish by Tiryaki and Gödelek [19], was administered to the participants in this study. The “Sport-Specific Achievement Motivation Scale” consists of 40 items and 3 subdimensions. It is structured using a 5-point Likert-type rating scale. The 12 items in the scale fall under “Power-Oriented Motivation,” 17 items under “Approach Motivation,” and 11 items under “Avoidance Motivation.” Accordingly, high scores on the scale’s “Power Display” and “Approach to Success” dimensions indicate that the athlete has high motivation in those specific areas, while low scores indicate the opposite. A high score on the “Avoidance of Failure” dimension indicates that the athlete experiences greater fear of failure and stress. The scale is applicable to athletes in both individual and team sports, across all disciplines and at every level of competition. In the reliability study of the scale conducted by Tiryaki and Gödelek [19], Cronbach’s Alpha (Cronbach-) values were found to be 0.81 for the power-display motivation, 0.82 for the approach-to-success dimension, and 0.80 for the avoidance-of-failure dimension; and the scale’s overall internal consistency coefficient was determined to be 0.86. In this study, however, the Cronbach’s Alpha (Cronbach-) internal consistency coefficient for the collected data was found to be α = 0.85.

Data analysis

Prior to the statistical analysis, the dataset was screened for missing data and outliers; since the online survey items were set as mandatory fields and paper forms were cross-checked upon collection, no questionnaires were excluded due to missing responses, and all 657 participant responses were included in the final analysis. The SPSS 26.0 program was used for the statistical analysis of the data obtained in the study. The Independent Samples t-test was applied for the data analysis of two-group variables, while the One-Way ANOVA test was applied for the statistical analysis of multi-group variables. Tukey’s test results were examined to determine differences within groups. Before conducting these parametric comparison tests, the homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test (p > 0.05). In cases where the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated, appropriate adjusted degrees of freedom via Welch’s correction were utilized and reported. The Pearson correlation test was used to examine the relationship between the results of the two scales, and linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the shared variance between the variables. In the study, the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Findings

In this study conducted with professional athletes actively competing in various sports, the relationship between participants’ levels of sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation was identified. The data were analyzed in terms of independent variables, and the findings obtained in line with the research questions are presented under the relevant headings.

More than half (56.0%) of the professional athletes participating in the study were male; those aged 13–20 were more numerous than athletes in other age groups (333); and there was a high participation of 258 athletes in the soccer category, there was a higher concentration of professional athletes playing in the 2nd Division, and the number of athletes who had been involved in sports for 6–10 years was higher (249); when looking at the number of years they had continued in professional sports, those with 0–5 years of experience (317) were again more numerous than those with other durations of professional athletic careers (Table 1).

Upon examining the Tukey test results, it was found that the mean scale scores of participants aged 21–30 (21.05) were significantly lower than those of the 13–20 age group (22.24) and the 31 and older group (22.72) (p < 0.05), demonstrating a small effect size (ηₚ² = 0.013). In the analysis of the avoidance of failure subscale, it was found that athletes aged 13–20 (45.72) scored significantly higher than those aged 21–30 (43.76) and those aged 31 and older (44.35), yielding a small-to-medium effect size (ηₚ² = 0.049). In the analysis of the total score on the Sport-Specific Achievement motivation Scale, it was found that athletes aged 13–20 (156.81) had significantly higher scores than those aged 21–30 (153.39), with a small effect size (ηₚ² = 0.018) (Table 4).

When the comprehensive correlation matrix spanning all subdimensions was examined, sports competition rumination demonstrated distinct relational patterns with achievement motivation structures. Specifically, competition rumination showed a non-significant, negligible negative correlation with Power-Oriented Motivation (r = -0.037, p > 0.05). Conversely, statistically significant, low-level positive linear associations were observed between competition rumination and both the Approach Motivation (r = 0.147, p < 0.01) and the motivation to avoid failure (r = 0.138, p < 0.01). Finally, a statistically significant but practically limited low-level positive relationship was confirmed between the overall scores of sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation(r = 0.105, p < 0.01). These findings structurally align with the study’s correlational framework, demonstrating that higher ruminative tendencies are minimally linked to elevated task-approach and evaluation-anxiety structures without establishing any directional or causal inference (Table 7).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between professional athletes’ levels of competition rumination and their sport-specific achievement motivation, and also investigated whether these variables differed according to demographic characteristics. The findings were interpreted in comparison with the sports psychology literature, and recommendations were presented for practitioners.

The mean competition rumination scores and the total scores and subscales of sport-specific achievement motivation—namely, the drive to demonstrate power, the drive to approach success, and the drive to avoid failure—were above average. This suggests that professional athletes tend to exhibit high levels of both rumination and achievement motivation. The literature indicates that elite sports environments increase cognitive load due to constant evaluation and performance pressure [5]. Therefore, high levels of rumination can be explained by intensive performance evaluation processes. Similarly, high achievement motivation can be viewed as a natural consequence of athletes’ competitive nature [12, 21].

Sports competition rumination shows significant differences by gender. Female athletes’ rumination scores (22.63) are higher than those of male athletes (21.28). This suggests that female athletes may experience more repetitive thoughts during the performance process. Additionally, it has been noted in the literature that performance pressure can impair attention control, which may consequently lead to a decrease in overall performance outcomes [5]. It is also emphasized that female athletes may experience social evaluation pressure more intensely [22]. However, no significant difference was found between genders regarding the subscales and total scores of sport-specific achievement motivation (p > 0.05). This suggests that motivation may possess a structure that is more goal- and task-oriented [12, 13, 21]. In the literature, this phenomenon is explained by elite athletes’ high self-regulation skills; professional athletes have learned to focus their energy on long-term goals and technical tasks rather than being influenced by external stimuli or temporary failures during competition. In this context, achievement motivation is considered to enhance cognitive focus by enabling the athlete to perceive competition stress not as a threat but as a potentially step to be overcome.

It is observed that rumination differs significantly by age. There is a particular difference between the 21–30 age group and other age groups. Young athletes’ lack of experience and older athletes’ career concerns may influence rumination. Additionally, the subscales of sport-specific achievement motivation and the total score also show significant differences by age (p < 0.05). It is suggested that young athletes may be more prone to task-irrelevant thoughts. It is emphasized that rumination is associated with performance pressure and evaluation processes [22, 23]. Therefore, it is suggested that age differences may actually be related to the stage of the athletic career and perceived pressure [24, 25]. Regarding motivation, it is stated that goal orientations change and become more balanced with age [12, 13, 21]. The basis for motivation becoming more balanced with age and experience lies in the cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation skills athletes develop as they advance through the career ladder. It is believed that young athletes tend to have a motivation structure focused on external validation and immediate success (ego-oriented), but as experience increases, this structure gives way to a more stable internal motivation and task-oriented focus. This cognitive maturation is conceptualized to assist professional athletes in utilizing ruminative thoughts following failure not as an obstacle, but potentially as a feedback mechanism that could support future performance outcomes.

Ruminative thinking regarding sports competitions shows significant differences by sport (p < 0.05). In particular, higher levels of rumination are observed among soccer and basketball athletes. Similarly, sport-specific achievement motivation also varies by sport. It has been noted that motivation levels tend to be higher in sports with high levels of competition [12, 13]. The higher social evaluation pressure in team sports may also function as a potential factor contributing to increased rumination [20, 26].

There are significant differences between athletes’ years of participation in sports and the subdimensions of rumination and motivation (p < 0.05). In particular, differences were observed between athletes with 6–10 years and those with 11–15 years of experience. It has been reported that as experience increases, cognitive control develops and rumination changes [27, 28]. Regarding motivation, it is noted that as experience increases, intrinsic motivation strengthens and the tendency to avoid failure decreases [29, 30]. However, no significant difference was found in terms of overall motivation scores (p > 0.05). This indicates that motivation is a multidimensional construct [31]. The critical importance of psychological skills for athletic development presents a significant opportunity to develop these skills during the early stages of a career. Thus, athletes may be able to utilize these skills more effectively once they reach career maturity [32].

A significant relationship was observed between sports competition rumination and sport-specific achievement motivation. The literature suggests that low rumination tends to be associated with more successful sports careers [6]. Additionally, the literature suggests that rumination may influence post-performance behaviors and cognitive appraisals [27]. It is also emphasized that ruminative thinking may indirectly affect motivation by negatively impacting attention resources [23, 24, 25]. Pepe et al. [33], in their study, found a significant positive relationship between kinesiophobia and rumination. Roy et al. [34] noted in their study that there is a relationship between success in a sports career and a low rumination style. They reported a relationship between a successful sports career and a low reflective rumination style. This supports the conceptual notion that rumination may serve as a key mechanism in the relationship between mindfulness and sport-specific coping skills [14].

Ruminative thinking has a positive but very low-level shared variance with achievement motivation (R²=0.011). This result indicates that ruminative thinking does not strongly explain motivation directly. The literature also suggests that rumination primarily exhibits indirect associations on various athletic dimensions [22]. It is noted that motivation arises from the interaction of multiple factors and cannot be conceptualized through a single variable alone [21]. Given this limited direct variance, competition rumination should be evaluated as a minor cognitive covariate rather than a primary driving force behind athletes’ achievement motivation.

Conclusion

The research findings reveal that professional athletes’ competition rumination levels vary widely across demographic and sports background variables (gender, age, sport discipline and experience), whereas their achievement motivation is shaped more specifically within the context of age, sport discipline. The study found a low-level positive linear relationship between competition rumination and achievement motivation; this suggests that athletes’ post-competition mental processes share a limited statistical association with their success orientations.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the fact that the study group was limited to professional athletes competing in team sports may restrict the generalizability of the findings to individual sports. Second, the data being collected via a questionnaire and based on athletes’ self-reports may introduce a “social desirability” effect. Finally, the use of a correlational survey model in this study reflects the current state of affairs rather than providing a definitive explanation of the cause-and-effect relationship between variables. A limitation of the current study is that the variance explained by sports competition rumination on achievement motivation was relatively low (R2 = 0.011). Although this direct path is statistically significant, future studies should employ multiple regression models or structural equation modeling (SEM) by integrating sociodemographic and athletic covariates—such as gender, age, sport discipline, and years of experience—to capture a more comprehensive portion of the variance. For future studies, it is recommended to use longitudinal designs and qualitative interview techniques to examine the effects of rumination on athletic outcomes and cognitive processes in greater depth.

Recommendations based on research findings

In light of the research findings, it is recommended that coaches support athletes through routine evaluations to guide athletes’ post-competition rumination processes into constructive strategic analysis, and that informal guidance structures be encouraged in which experienced athletes guide younger athletes. Coaches personalizing their feedback methods according to athletes’ demographic sensitivities, such as age and gender, will enhance the effectiveness of the process. Future studies designed with longitudinal designs incorporating qualitative interviews and indicators of athletic development to deepen current findings and better explain the unexplained variance will provide significant contributions to the academic literature.
