Integrative multi-omics analysis identifies a circadian rhythm-associated gene signature for prognosis and therapeutic stratification in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yiheng Lu, Yi Dong, Cheng Sun, Fan Yang, Shuyan Xiao, Yining Liu, Xiao Han, Qiaowei Liu, Yi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectivesCircadian rhythm disruption is increasingly implicated in tumor progression and therapy resistance. However, its prognostic value and impact on the tumor immune microenvironment in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain unclear. This study aimed to develop a robust circadian rhythm-based gene signature to improve risk stratification and inform personalized therapeutic strategies in LUAD.MethodsWe integrated multi-omics data from over 900 LUAD patients across TCGA and GEO databases. A circadian rhythm-related gene prognostic signature (CRGPS) was constructed from candidate genes using ten machine learning algorithms and validated externally. The tumor immune microenvironment, mutation landscape, and therapy response were analyzed using bioinformatics algorithms. Single-cell RNA sequencing data were utilized to explore gene expression at cellular resolution.ResultsA 10-gene CRGPS was developed, which effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly divergent overall survival in both training and validation cohorts. Unsupervised clustering based on these genes revealed two molecular subtypes (C1 and C2) with distinct characteristics. The two subgroups exhibited significant differences in terms of the tumor immune microenvironment, clinical prognosis, and therapeutic sensitivity. Single-cell analysis localized key signature genes to endothelial and epithelial cells and revealed enhanced endothelial–endothelial communication.ConclusionsThe CRGPS serves as a robust prognostic framework for risk stratification and provides hypothesis-generating insights into therapeutic vulnerabilities based on in silico predictions of immune profiles and drug sensitivity, pending prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05279-4.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounts for over 40% of all lung cancer cases and is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the 5-year survival rate for LUAD patients remains unsatisfactory, particularly for those with advanced disease [3, 4]. This is largely attributed to the high degree of tumor heterogeneity and the complex tumor microenvironment (TME), which contribute to variable treatment responses and underscore the urgent need for reliable prognostic biomarkers to guide individualized therapeutic strategies. Since the development of immune checkpoint inhibitors, immunotherapy has become one of the major treatment options for lung cancer. However, although significant therapeutic efficacy has been observed in some patients, a considerable proportion of patients do not respond to treatment or eventually develop resistance, which greatly limits the broad clinical application of immunotherapy [5]. Therefore, identifying reliable biomarkers to predict patient prognosis as well as response to immunotherapy is critical for developing personalized treatment strategies.

The circadian rhythm is an evolutionarily conserved intrinsic timekeeping system that coordinates various physiological and behavioral processes over a roughly 24-hour cycle [6]. At the molecular level, this rhythm is controlled by a core transcriptional-translational feedback loop involving clock genes such as CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, and CRY, which regulate the expression of a vast array of downstream clock-controlled genes (CCGs) [7]. These circadian rhythm-related genes (CRGs) play pivotal roles in fundamental biological processes including cell cycle progression, DNA damage repair, metabolism, immune response, and apoptosis [8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that disruption of the circadian rhythm is closely associated with the initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance of various malignancies [9–11]. In lung cancer, aberrant expression of core clock genes has been shown to influence cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and sensitivity to immunotherapy, underscoring the potential importance of the circadian clock in LUAD pathogenesis [12, 13].

Previous studies have begun to explore the prognostic value of circadian rhythm-related genes in lung adenocarcinoma and their preliminary links to the immune microenvironment [14, 15], yet the evidence remains fragmentary, with a comprehensive multi-omics analysis still lacking. Recent advances in computational oncology have highlighted the value of integrative machine learning frameworks with interpretable modeling in refining prognostic precision [16]. Concurrently, emerging studies have applied similar computational paradigms to dissect tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) heterogeneity in lung adenocarcinoma, revealing distinct molecular subtypes rooted in specific biological processes—such as disulfidptosis [17]—and linking driver mutations to immune states through machine learning classification [18], thereby illuminating strategies to overcome immune resistance [19]. The integration of single-cell RNA sequencing with computational modeling has further emerged as a powerful approach to resolve cellular heterogeneity and spatial microenvironment dynamics [20]. Leveraging these methodological advances, this study aims to develop a prognostic model using machine learning methods and validate a Circadian-Rhythm-related Gene Prognostic Signature (CRGPS). We then used this signature to (i) stratify patients for survival, (ii) map the tumor immune microenvironment and mutation landscapes, and (iii) predict immunotherapy/chemotherapy response, providing a readily translatable framework for circadian-based precision oncology.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Figure 1 presents the research flowchart. The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data used in this study were sourced from the publicly available dataset GSE189357 [21], which includes primary tumor tissues from 9 LUAD patients (3 cases each of AIS, MIA, and IAC). These matched samples enabled clear analysis of cell types and gene expression patterns in LUAD. Raw sequencing files were downloaded from the database provided in the original publication. RNA-seq data, mutation data, and corresponding clinical information for LUAD were downloaded from public databases. For bulk transcriptomic profiling, we systematically retrieved and harmonized raw count matrices, paired clinical annotations, and somatic-mutation catalogs for TCGA-LUAD patients through the TCGAbiolinks R package [22]. The TCGA-LUAD cohort (N = 503 samples with prognostic information) served as the training cohort. The GSE72094 dataset [23](N = 398 samples with prognostic information) and GSE30219(n = 85 samples with prognostic information) from the GEO database were used as independent external validation cohorts. Clinical data included overall survival (OS) time, survival status, age, gender, TNM stage, and tumor grade. Circadian rhythm genes were obtained from the Circadian Gene Database (CGDB, http://cgdb.biocuckoo.org ) [24] (accessed on 3 July 2025), resulting in 1,677 genes for subsequent analysis (see Supplementary Table S2).

Key prognostic gene screening and model construction

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The R package WGCNA [25] was used to perform WGCNA on the transcriptomic data of the TCGA-LUAD set to identify gene modules highly correlated with clinical phenotypes. The top 50% of genes by variance were used for network construction. The soft thresholding power was set to fulfill scale-free R2 > 0.9. Hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting were used to identify co-expression modules, with a minimum module size of 100 genes. Modules whose eigengenes exhibited a Pearson correlation |Cor| > 0.3 and P-value < 0.05 were merged.

Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis

Differential expression analysis of genes between LUAD tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues was performed using the R package limma [26]. Genes with |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) q-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Candidate gene set acquisition

Genes simultaneously present in the WGCNA module most associated with the trait, the differentially expressed gene list, and the circadian rhythm-related genes were retained for downstream analysis.

Prognostic signature construction and machine learning integration

To establish a robust prognostic signature, we implemented a systematic machine-learning framework evaluating ten distinct algorithms encompassing penalized regression (LASSO [27], Ridge, Elastic Net [28]), tree-based ensembles (Random Survival Forest [29], Gradient Boosting [30]), and survival-specific learners (CoxBoost [31], survival-SVM [32], SuperPC [33], StepCox, plsRcox [34]). These algorithms were combinatorially paired to generate 117 candidate prognostic models. Model selection followed a dual-criterion strategy prioritizing: (i) maximal average C-index across TCGA training cohort, GSE72094, and GSE30219 cohorts, and (ii) optimal parsimony (minimal gene number among top performers). The LASSO-RSF ensemble emerged as the final model, achieving superior cross-platform generalizability (average C-index = 0.683) with only 10 genes, outperforming the conventional single-step LASSO-Cox approach (17 genes, C-index = 0.66). This multi-algorithm aggregation strategy ensures robust predictive accuracy while minimizing overfitting risk across independent patient populations.

Risk score model evaluation and validation

Patients in both TCGA and GEO cohorts were divided into high-risk (HRG) and low-risk (LRG) groups based on the median risk score.

Survival and ROC analysis

Kaplan–Meier (K-M) survival curves and the log-rank test were used to compare OS between HRG and LRG. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the predictive accuracy of the risk score for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, calculating the area under the curve (AUC).

Independent prognostic analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate whether the risk score was an independent prognostic factor compared to other clinical variables (e.g., age, gender, stage).

Nomogram construction

The R package rms [35] was used to construct a nomogram integrating the risk score and other independent clinicopathological features to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probability. Calibration curves and the C-index were calculated to evaluate the predictive performance and calibration of the nomogram.

Consensus clustering and functional mechanism analysis

Consensus clustering

Based on the expression of the signature genes, molecular subtypes were identified with the ConsensusClusterPlus R package (version 1.66.0) using k-means clustering (km) and Pearson distance; 80% of samples were resampled across 10 repetitions. The optimal cluster number (k) was determined from the cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot and average cluster-consensus assessment.

Functional enrichment analysis

The R package clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1) [36] was used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the 10 signature genes and differentially expressed genes between groups. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using Hallmark gene sets were performed to explore pathway activity differences between subtypes.

Somatic mutation analysis

Tumor Mutation Burden (TMB) data were downloaded from the cBioPortal database. The R package maftools (version 2.18.0) [37] was used to process and visualize somatic mutation data (MAF format) from TCGA-LUAD, comparing tumor mutation burden (TMB) and mutation profiles between subtypes.

Tumor immune microenvironment and therapy response analysis

Immune cell infiltration

The CIBERSORT algorithm [38] was used to deconvolute bulk RNA-seq data and estimate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types. The ESTIMATE algorithm [39] was used to calculate the immune score, stromal score, ESTIMATE score, and tumor purity for each sample.

Immunotherapy response prediction

The TIDE score [40] was calculated using the tidepy Python package ( https://github.com/jingxinfu/TIDEpy ) to assess potential immune escape. Immunophenoscore (IPS) data for LUAD were obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) ( https://tcia.at/ ) [41]to evaluate the potential response to CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitors. Additionally, expression levels of HLA molecules, immunogenic cell death (ICD) genes, and immune-checkpoint-related genes (ICGs) were compared between the two subtypes to further characterize immunotherapy-relevant features.

Drug sensitivity prediction

The R package oncoPredict (version 1.2) [42], based on the GDSC2 database, was used to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for 198 FDA-approved drugs for each patient, comparing drug sensitivity between subtypes.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Data preprocessing and cell annotation

A public LUAD scRNA-seq dataset (GSE189357) was downloaded from GEO. Standard preprocessing was performed using the R package Seurat (version 5.3.0) [43], including quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction (PCA), graph-based clustering (resolution = 0.6), and UMAP visualization. Specifically, we calculated quality metrics including:

The number of detected genes per cell (nFeature_RNA).

The total UMI count per cell (nCount_RNA).

Cell complexity (log10GenesPerUMI = log10(nFeature_RNA)/log10(nCount_RNA)).

Mitochondrial gene ratio (percentage of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes).

We applied the following filtering criteria to exclude low-quality cells:

Cells with nCount_RNA < 200 or > 5000.

Cells with nFeature_RNA < 200.

Cells with log10GenesPerUMI < 0.9.

Cells with mitochondrial gene ratio > 0.2.

Additionally, we filtered out lowly expressed genes that were detected in fewer than 100 cells and applied the scDblFinder algorithm [44] to remove doublets for downstream processing. Following quality control, the data underwent dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA), with post-filtering quality metrics confirming normalized gene counts and mitochondrial ratios within acceptable ranges (Fig S10). Cell clustering was performed using a graph-based algorithm on the top 30 principal components at a resolution of 0.6. This parameter was selected based on maximal silhouette score (0.155) indicating optimal cluster separation, coupled with satisfactory modularity (> 0.9) ensuring robust community structure, as validated by Clustree analysis.( Fig. S11). The selection of this resolution parameter was based on a systematic multi-resolution analysis evaluating clustering quality across a range of parameters (0.2 to 2.0). Resolution 0.6 was chosen as it achieved the optimal balance between technical clustering metrics and biological interpretability. Specifically, this resolution yielded the highest silhouette score (0.155), indicating superior intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation, while producing 26 distinct clusters that corresponded well with established cell type markers. The modularity score at this resolution (0.906) further confirmed robust community structure within the cellular similarity network. Importantly, this parameter setting enabled the discrimination of finely resolved cell populations, including distinct immune subsets (T cells, B cells, NK cells, Macrophages, Monocytes, and Neutrophils) as well as stromal components (Epithelial cells, Endothelial cells, and Tissue stem cells), while avoiding over-clustering artifacts observed at higher resolutions. Cell types were annotated by projecting the clusters onto the Human Primary Cell Atlas (HPCA) reference database using the R package SingleR (version 2.4.1), complemented by manual inspection of classical marker gene expression.

Key gene expression visualization

The expression of the 10 key genes was visualized on UMAP plots to observe their distribution across cell subpopulations.

Cell–cell communication analysis

The R package CellChat [45] was used to infer ligand–receptor interaction networks between cell subpopulations and compare communication patterns and signaling pathway activity between groups.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) validation

We sought to validate the protein-level expression of the most critical genes in our CRGPS model by obtaining IHC images from The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org) [46] .The validation focused on the CRGPS genes for which IHC data were available, comparing their staining in normal lung versus LUAD tissue.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics-related analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3) [47]. Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used based on data normality. Survival differences were assessed by the log-rank test. Spearman’s correlation was used to assess the associations between continuous variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was used as the criterion for significant difference.

Results

Identification of circadian rhythm-related prognostic candidate genes

We performed comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to systematically screen circadian rhythm genes (CRGs) associated with LUAD prognosis (Fig. 2). We obtained 1,677 CRGs from the CGDB database. WGCNA performed on the TCGA-LUAD set identified co-expression modules associated with clinical features. The soft threshold power was set to 5 (Fig. 2a-b), resulting in 14 co-expression modules (Fig. 2c). The MEgreen module showed the strongest absolute correlation with LUAD phenotype (Fig. 2d). The significant correlation between gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) within this module (r = 0.56, p < 1e− 200; Fig. 2b) indicated strong disease relevance. To construct a stringent circadian-relevant candidate set, we intersected the 1,677 CRGs from CRDB with the WGCNA-derived DEGs and further retained only genes detected in GEO validation cohorts. This four-way overlap yielded 136 robust genes (Fig. S1) that are circadian-related, differential-expressed in tumor tissue, and consistently present across both TCGA and GEO datasets, which were then carried forward for machine-learning feature selection.

Construction and validation of the risk score model

Ten machine learning algorithms were integrated to construct a robust prognostic model from the 136 candidate genes. Among 117 generated models, LASSO-RSF model demonstrated the best predictive performance (average C-index = 0.683; Fig. 3d) and was selected as the final model. It contained 10 genes constituting the CRGPS. Variable importance analysis from the RSF model confirmed GRIA1 and CAV2 as the top contributors to prognostic prediction (Fig. S2a). High expression of CAV2 was associated with poorer prognosis, while other 9 genes acted protectively.

Patients in the TCGA cohort were divided into HRG and LRG groups based on the median risk score. K-M analysis showed that HRG patients had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared to LRG patients (p < 0.001; Fig. 3a). The risk score distribution plot showed increasing mortality with higher risk scores (Fig. 3e). Time-dependent ROC analysis confirmed the model’s excellent predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (AUC = 0.808, 0.8, and 0.863, respectively; Fig. 3f). Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses identified the risk score as an independent prognostic factor (multivariate HR = 2.57, 95% CI 2.19–3.02, p < 0.001; Fig. 4a-d).

These findings were successfully validated in the external GSE30219 and GSE72094 cohorts, where HRG and LRG also showed significant survival differences (p < 0.01; Fig. 3b), and the risk score maintained good predictive ability across platforms, with GSE30219 showing strong 1-year discrimination (AUC = 0.880) and GSE72094 demonstrating robust 5-year prediction (AUC = 0.716) (Fig. S2b-c).

A comprehensive nomogram integrating the risk score and clinical features (age, stage) was constructed to facilitate clinical translation (Fig. 4e). Calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities (Fig. 4f). Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the nomogram provided greater clinical net benefit compared to individual clinical features (Fig. 4g).

We examined the expression of CRGPS genes using the HPA database, focusing on immunohistochemistry images of protein expression in LUAD samples compared to normal lung tissue (Fig. S3a). Among these genes, all exhibited protein expression levels in normal tissue that were greater than or equal to those observed in LUAD tissue. This finding is consistent with the transcriptional trends identified in the TCGA-LUAD dataset (Fig. S3b).

Consensus clustering based on CRGPS reveals distinct molecular subtypes and functional characteristics

Unsupervised consensus clustering applied to the 10-gene CRGPS profile robustly identified two distinct molecular subtypes across both the TCGA-LUAD cohort (C1, n = 279; C2, n = 262) and the independent GSE72094 validation cohort, with optimal clustering stability observed at k = 2 in both datasets (Fig. 5a-b; Fig. S4a-d). Consensus clustering matrices showed sharp stabilization at k = 2 in both TCGA-LUAD and GSE72094 cohorts, with cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves plateauing at k = 2 and minimal delta area change beyond this point (Fig. S4a-d), confirming the robustness of the two-subtype classification across independent datasets.

Three-dimensional PCA visualization confirmed clear separation between the C1 and C2 subtypes in both cohorts (Fig. 5c-d), underscoring the distinct transcriptomic landscapes of these subgroups. The clinical relevance of this classification was substantiated by a significant survival difference, wherein C2 patients exhibited a markedly more favorable prognosis than C1 patients (p < 0.001; Fig. 5f). Biologically, the 10-gene CRGPS exhibited distinct expression patterns between subtypes, with all signature genes showing significant differential expression between C1 and C2 (p < 0.01; Fig. 5e). This transcriptional divergence underscores the biological coherence of the molecular classification and suggests that the two subtypes represent distinct states of circadian dysregulation in LUAD.

We analyzed the circadian rhythm of the TCGA-LUAD cohort with the TimeTeller package [48] and observed that the inferred phases differ by nearly 1 h across all comparisons (C1 vs. C2, HRGvs LRG, Fig. S5a-b), whereas the overall prediction uncertainty remains > 2 h, suggesting that the phase estimates are relatively close and the differences may not be substantial given the uncertainty.

Somatic mutation analysis within the TCGA-LUAD dataset revealed notable differences between molecular subtypes (Fig. 6). The predominant mutation types in LUAD included missense mutations, nonsense mutations, frameshift deletions, splice site mutations, frameshift insertions, and in-frame deletions, with missense mutations being the most frequently observed. CRGPS in LUAD patients were primarily characterized by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), accompanied by substantial deletion (DEL) events. Among single nucleotide variants (SNVs), C > A transitions represented the most common type, followed by C > T and C > G.

With the exception of TNNC1, all remaining CRGPS exhibited somatic mutations in TCGA-LUAD. The mutation prevalence differed significantly between subtypes: among 308 C1 samples, 31 (10.06%) carried mutations in these genes, while C2 showed substantially lower mutation frequency with 20 of 280 samples (7.14%) affected. Notably, KCNA5 demonstrated the highest mutation rate among CRGPSs, accounting for 4% of mutated samples in C1 and 3% in C2 (Fig. 6c, d).

The mutation landscape across TCGA-LUAD samples was visualized using stacked bar plots (Fig. 6e, f). The C1 subtype exhibited significantly elevated tumor mutation burden (TMB) relative to C2 (median: 4.15 vs. 2.23 mutations/Mb, p < 0.001; Fig. S6), suggesting distinct mutational processes between circadian-driven molecular subtypes.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the 10 CRGs demonstrated significant enrichment in the following terms: regulation of membrane potential, negative regulation of multicellular organism growth, diet induced thermogenesis, and negative regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration (biological process, BP); neuronal dense core vesicle, dense core granule, caveola, and early endosome (cellular component, CC); adenylate cyclase binding, scaffold protein binding, PDZ domain binding, and adrenergic receptor binding (molecular function, MF); as well as cAMP signaling pathway and calcium signaling pathway (KEGG) (Fig. S7a, b; network visualization in Fig. S7a, c,e).

GSVA using Hallmark gene sets confirmed significant differences in pathway activities between the two subtypes (Fig. 7b; S4 Table). GSEA further indicated that the C1 subtype was enriched in pathways such as Melatonin Metabolism and Effects, Tcra Pathway, and Major Receptors Targeted By Epinephrine and Norepinephrine, while the C2 subtype was enriched in Unwinding of DNA, PLK1 Pathway, and RNA Polymerase I Transcription (Fig. S7d, f).

Association of molecular subtypes with immune microenvironment and therapy response

CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE analyses revealed significant heterogeneity in the tumor immune microenvironment between the molecular subtypes. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we evaluated the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types and assessed their associations with C1 and C2 in TCGA-LUAD. A stacked bar plot illustrated the relative proportions of these immune cells across all samples. Comparative analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) in the abundance of multiple immune cell types between C1 and C2 (Fig. 7a-b).

Specifically, C1 was characterized by significantly higher proportions of activated memory CD4 T cells and M0 macrophages. In contrast, C2 exhibited increased infiltration of resting dendritic cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, and activated NK cells. Furthermore, significant correlations were observed between CRGPS expression levels and the infiltration abundance of multiple immune cell types in TCGA-LUAD (Fig. 7c).

The C2 subtype exhibited significantly higher immune score, stromal score, and ESTIMATE score, along with lower tumor purity, compared to C1 (Fig. 8a–d), indicating a more robust immune infiltration.

This distinct immune profile led to divergent predicted treatment outcomes. The C2 subtype exhibited significantly higher immunophenoscore (IPS; Fig. 8e) and lower TIDE scores (Fig. 8f), indicating a greater likelihood of response to anti-CTLA-4/PD-1 immunotherapy. In contrast, C1 appeared more resistant to such treatment. Notably, most human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes were significantly upregulated in C1, suggesting enhanced antigen-presentation capacity. Furthermore, immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) and immunogenic cell death (ICD) modulators showed distinctly different expression patterns between the two subtypes (p < 0.05; Fig. 8g, Fig. S8). Together, these findings imply that although C1 tumors are proficient in antigen presentation (high HLA expression), the subsequent anti-tumor immune response is effectively suppressed. The tumor microenvironment in C1 is likely characterized by T-cell exclusion, forming an immune-privileged niche that renders checkpoint inhibitors less effective despite the presence of targetable antigens.

Drug sensitivity analysis revealed subtype-specific pharmacological vulnerabilities (Fig S9): C1 exhibited significantly lower IC50 values for conventional chemotherapeutics including paclitaxel and cisplatin, while C2 showed enhanced sensitivity to targeted agents such as axitinib and osimertinib. These divergent drug response profiles suggest potential for subtype-tailored therapeutic strategies.

Expression and functional exploration of key genes at single-cell resolution

Analysis of the public scRNA-seq dataset GSE189357, after quality control, normalization, and clustering, identified 10 major cell types (Fig. 9b). The CRGPS genes were not uniformly expressed but were specifically enriched in endothelial cells and Epithelial cells (Fig. 9a, d, e). Epithelial cells emitted the strongest signals (Fig. 9c); and Neutrophil cells received more signals than other cells. Additionally, endothelial–endothelial communication was highly active (Fig. 9d), with PECAM1-PECAM1 homophilic interactions representing the predominant signaling axis (Fig. S12), suggesting that circadian rhythm disruption may potentiate vascular crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study successfully constructed and validated a robust 10-gene circadian rhythm-related prognostic signature (CRGPS) for LUAD by integrating multi-omics data and machine learning algorithms. The CRGPS risk score proved to be an effective and independent prognostic factor that efficiently stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct survival outcomes in both training and external validation cohorts. Furthermore, consensus clustering based on these 10 genes revealed two molecular subtypes (C1/C2) with different clinical outcomes, immune landscapes, pathway activities, mutation profiles, and drug sensitivities. scRNA-seq analysis provided finer insights, localizing key gene expression primarily to endothelial cells and epithelial cells and suggesting that intercellular communication, particularly endothelial–endothelial crosstalk, may play a role in the functional impact of this signature.

Biological significance of genes in CRGPS

At the biological level, the ten genes comprising the CRGPS are experimentally validated circadian genes [24] that coordinately regulate ion homeostasis, membrane dynamics, and neuroendocrine signaling through their rhythmic expression in human tissues. This signature encompasses genes governing electrochemical signaling—including potassium channels (KCNK3, KCNA5) and glutamate receptors (GRIA1) that modulate cellular excitability; membrane microdomain organization and tumor-stromal interaction—involving caveolar proteins (CAV2), carbonic anhydrases (CA4), and chemokine-like factors (CMTM5); adrenergic neuroendocrine signaling—transduced through β-adrenergic receptors (ADRB1, ADRB2) that relay sympathetic nervous system inputs; and cellular proliferation and calcium-dependent regulation—mediated by phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP4) and troponin C1 (TNNC1). In normal physiology, the rhythmic activities of these genes synchronize membrane electrochemical gradients, metabolic pH regulation, neuro-immune communication, and cell-cycle control to maintain tissue homeostasis. In LUAD, the coordinate disruption of these rhythmic patterns—manifested as aberrant baseline expression in our signature—reflects an impairment of circadian coordination across electrochemical, metabolic, and neuro-immune compartments. Several genes in the CRGPS have established roles in cancer. Exhausted CD8⁺ T cells highly express ADRB1, and sympathetic signaling inhibits T cell function and promotes exhaustion through this receptor; blocking ADRB1 with β-blockers can restore T cell activity in malignant melanoma and pancreatic cancer, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity [49]. CAV2 is associated with pro-tumorigenic responses in multiple cancers; in pancreatic cancer, it suppresses endocytosis and promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by modulating the MiR-4723/Wnt7A signaling axis, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [50]. KCNK3 is significantly downregulated even in stage I lung adenocarcinoma, with its overexpression shown to inhibit tumor cell glycolysis, glucose uptake, and proliferation through activation of the AMPK pathway and upregulation of TXNIP, ultimately exerting tumor-suppressive effects [51].

PEBP4 (phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein 4) is a member of the PEBP family, which exerts complex effects during the malignant progression of lung cancer. Mechanistic investigations reveal that PEBP4 promotes the proliferation and invasion of non-small cell lung cancer cells through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling axis [52]. Furthermore, miR-15b promotes cisplatin resistance and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in lung adenocarcinoma cells by targeting and inhibiting PEBP4, suggesting the regulatory role of the PEBP4/miR-15b axis in chemotherapy resistance [53].ADRB2 encodes the β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR), a member of the G protein-coupled receptor superfamily. In small cell lung cancer (SCLC), ADRB2 mediates the interaction between cancer cells and sympathetic nerve fibers: genetic knockout or pharmacological inhibition of ADRB2 significantly suppresses tumor growth and prolongs mouse survival [54]. TNNC1 (troponin C1, slow skeletal and cardiac type) functions as a novel tumor suppressor in lung adenocarcinoma. Studies have shown that TNNC1 is significantly downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma tissues, with its low expression closely associated with KRAS signaling pathway activation and increased patient mortality [55]. Kim et al. revealed that ectopic TNNC1 expression inhibits KRAS G12D-mediated anchorage-independent growth and suppresses colony formation while inducing DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis across various lung adenocarcinoma cell lines [55]. RNA interference-mediated TNNC1 knockdown significantly enhances the invasive capacity of A549 cells, further confirming its role in regulating tumor invasion.

The diverse functions of these genes underscore the pleiotropic mechanisms through which circadian disruption may influence LUAD progression.

Model and clinical implications

Our analysis provided an in-depth characterization of the immune context associated with CRGPS. The C1 and C2 molecular subtypes exhibit distinct immunological phenotypes that may inform differential therapeutic strategies. C1 tumors display an ‘immune-excluded’ profile characterized by high mutation burden and robust HLA expression yet predicted resistance to immunotherapy (elevated TIDE scores, reduced IPS), suggesting intact antigen presentation machinery but potentially impaired effector T-cell infiltration; computationally predicted drug sensitivity patterns suggest that C1 patients may represent candidates for cytotoxic chemotherapy agents such as paclitaxel and cisplatin based on lower predicted IC50 values, though this requires experimental validation before clinical application. Conversely, C2 tumors demonstrate an ‘immune-permissive’ phenotype with lower TMB yet favorable predicted immunogenicity metrics (high IPS, low TIDE), coupled with enhanced sensitivity to targeted agents such as axitinib and osimertinib, indicating a microenvironment potentially conducive to T-cell-mediated killing, which positions this subgroup as a candidate for anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 therapy in silico. This binary stratification may offer complementary discriminatory value alongside conventional single biomarkers (PD-L1 or TMB alone) by hypothetically distinguishing tumors that appear immunogenic but are predicted to be excluded (C1) from those predicted to be responsive (C2), providing preliminary clues for circadian-informed treatment allocation: C2 as a candidate for immunotherapy-priority strategies with C1 potentially warranting chemotherapy-priority strategies. These findings provide framework toward circadian-informed individualized therapy, offering a hypothesis-generating basis that requires experimental validation of the predicted drug sensitivities before clinical translation.

Mechanistic insights

Integrated analysis of bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data provides mechanistic clues regarding how circadian rhythm disruption influences LUAD progression through specific cellular compartments. Our scRNA-seq analysis identified that CRGPS genes exhibit specific enrichment in endothelial cells and epithelial cells (Fig. 9a), with CAV2 predominantly expressed in endothelial cells and PEBP4 in epithelial subsets (Fig. 9d-e). This compartment-specific distribution suggests distinct mechanistic contributions: endothelial CAV2 may modulate vascular permeability and immune cell trafficking (consistent with the enhanced endothelial-endothelial communication via PECAM1-PECAM1 interactions observed in C1 tumors), while epithelial PEBP4 likely influences tumor-intrinsic proliferation and EMT programs. Such cell-type-specific effects, undetectable by bulk transcriptomics, offer a hypothesis-generating explanation for how circadian dysregulation differentially impacts stromal versus parenchymal compartments, potentially contributing to the immune-excluded phenotype observed in C1 tumors. While these observations provide cellular-resolution localization of signature genes, they remain descriptive; functional validation—such as circadian reporter assays, synchronized cell culture models, or perturbation studies in these specific cell types—is required to determine whether circadian dysregulation differentially impacts tumor parenchyma and stromal components. Nevertheless, this distinct cellular distribution raises the hypothesis that circadian rhythm disruption may contribute to LUAD progression through compartment-specific mechanisms, providing a foundation for future experimental investigation.

Analytical considerations for circadian rhythms in retrospective LUAD studies

Circadian rhythms govern the expression of over a quarter of the human genome across tissues. In this context, the lung ranks among the tissues with a higher proportion of rhythmic transcripts [56], underscoring the potential significance of circadian regulation in pulmonary physiology and, by extension, in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). A major obstacle in studying this relationship retrospectively is the lack of sample collection time metadata in most transcriptomic datasets, which introduces an unmeasured confounding variable. To address this, we employed a machine learning-based approach to infer circadian time and mitigate its potential bias. While studies have confirmed that circadian rhythms can be quantified from single timepoint samples of normal lung tissue, these same analyses reveal that lung adenocarcinoma tissues exhibit markedly dampened circadian amplitude compared to their normal counterparts [57]. This inherent dysregulation of the core clock in tumors necessarily constrains the performance of any in silico phase inference algorithm. This specific methodological consideration for circadian rhythm analysis constitutes one of several limitations of our study, which we comprehensively address in the following section.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has certain limitations. It is a retrospective analysis based on public databases, and although extensive bioinformatics validation was performed, prospective clinical cohorts and experimental validation are needed to confirm the clinical utility of the CRGPS. Specifically, computational predictions of immunotherapy response (TIDE/IPS scores) and chemotherapy sensitivity (GDSC2) require validation against real-world treatment outcomes before they can inform clinical decision-making. While the physiological functions of key signature genes such as KCNA5 and ADRB1 have been well-characterized in normal contexts, their specific roles and mechanistic contributions to LUAD pathogenesis remain unclear and require direct experimental validation using in vitro and in vivo models.

Future work should focus on the experimental validation of the biological mechanisms and translational potential of this signature, potentially integrating spatial transcriptomics to further elucidate the spatial context of these circadian genes in the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, as noted in the previous section, prospective studies with rigorously documented sample timing are essential to overcome the fundamental challenge of circadian confounding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this integrated multi-omics and machine learning study developed and validated a robust 10-gene circadian rhythm-related prognostic signature (CRGPS) for LUAD. The CRGPS effectively predicts patient survival and delineates distinct immune and molecular subtypes with differential predicted sensitivities to immunotherapy and chemotherapy based on computational modeling. These in silico predictions require experimental and prospective clinical validation before therapeutic application, but lay the groundwork for future circadian-based precision oncology research.
