MRI analysis of androgen insensitivity syndrome
Authors: Yijuan Xiang, Peng Wen, Rui Hu, Guanghui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAndrogen insensitivity syndrome (AIS) is a rare disorder of sex development, and accurate preoperative imaging assessment is crucial for clinical decision-making. This study aimed to describe the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of AIS, correlate imaging findings with surgical and pathological results, and evaluate the preoperative value of MRI in AIS.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the clinical data and preoperative MRI images of 12 patients with androgen insensitivity syndrome diagnosed through chromosomal karyotyping and confirmed by surgical exploration at our hospital between December 2018 and April 2026; gonadal pathological results were available for most gonads.ResultsAll patients were definitively diagnosed with AIS based on chromosomal karyotyping, laboratory examinations, as well as surgical exploration, with pathological confirmation obtained for most gonads, including 11 cases of complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) and 1 case of partial androgen insensitivity syndrome (PAIS). MRI revealed a blind-ending vagina and dysplastic gonads located in the pelvis or inguinal region in all patients. The gonads showed isointense signal on T1-weighted images (T1WI), slightly hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images (T2WI), hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted images (DWI), and hypointensity on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps. Mild to moderate enhancement was observed on contrast-enhanced images. MRI achieved accurate localization of all dysplastic gonads confirmed by surgical exploration in this cohort (21/21). MRI demonstrated the absence of the uterus and ovarian structures in the pelvic cavity of patients with CAIS. However, MRI in the patient with PAIS revealed a short penis and a strip-like abnormal signal behind the bladder, which was confirmed intraoperatively as tissue resembling the uterus. Only one patient showed severe right perigonadal hydrocele on MRI, with an underlying compressed nodule that exhibited isointense signal on T1WI, slightly hyperintensity on T2WI, hypointensity on DWI, hyperintensity on ADC maps, and rapid marked enhancement. Histopathological examination ultimately confirmed a Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor (SLCT).ConclusionsMRI is a reliable preoperative imaging modality for the localization of dysplastic gonads in AIS, with non-contrast sequences generally being sufficient. Contrast-enhanced imaging may offer complementary information for identifying potential Müllerian remnants or concurrent lesions in selected cases.

Introduction

Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (AIS) was first identified by John Morris from Yale University in 1953. He analyzed the clinical features of 82 female patients with testes, a condition then known as testicular feminization syndrome [1]. Although this term was widely used in early publications, the condition is now known as androgen insensitivity syndrome. AIS is an X-linked recessive genetic disorder caused by mutations in the androgen receptor (AR) gene located in the Xq11–12 region, resulting in dysfunctional AR and a complete or partial loss of androgen responsiveness [2]. Patients with AIS have a chromosomal karyotype of 46,XY, which represents the most common type of disorder of sex development (DSD) [3]. The Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) released by the testes prevents the development of the uterus and proximal vagina due to the presence of the Y chromosome. However, the distal part of the vagina is preserved because it is not under AMH control [4]. Studies on AIS are limited because of its low prevalence. Imaging plays an important role in evaluating dysplastic gonads and Müllerian remnants in AIS. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the MRI features of AIS, which may aid clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (Approval number: Kuai 2025025). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

The inclusion criteria were the following: definitive diagnosis of AIS confirmed by chromosomal karyotyping (46,XY or variant) combined with surgical exploration; complete preoperative pelvic MRI examination performed at our institution within 1 month before surgery; availability of complete clinical, laboratory, surgical and pathological records. The exclusion criteria were the following: MRI images with severe motion artifacts or poor quality affecting image interpretation; completely missing surgical exploration. All eligible patients with AIS who met the above criteria were consecutively enrolled during the study period (December 2018 to April 2026) without selective exclusion.

Data were obtained from patients with AIS admitted to our institution between December 2018 and April 2026. The diagnosis of AIS was established based on chromosomal karyotyping, laboratory examinations, and surgical exploration. A total of 12 patients with AIS aged 12–51 years (mean age, 22.8 ± 13.4 years) were included in this study. The primary clinical presentations included primary amenorrhea (75.0%), pelvic mass (16.7%), and dysplastic gonads (8.3%). Moreover, 6 patients (50.0%) had a history of inguinal hernia surgery during childhood. Regarding family history, 2 patients had affected relatives: the sister of one patient had a 46,XY karyotype, while another patient had both a sister with a 46,XY karyotype and a father with a 46,X, inv(Y) karyotype. Possible but unconfirmed family histories were reported in another 4 families. No parental consanguinity was identified. Physically, all patients exhibited breast development consistent with Tanner stages B2-4, absent or sparse axillary hair, and pubic hair corresponding to Tanner stages P1-2. Gynecological examination of the external genitalia revealed a typical female vulva in 11 patients. One patient presented with ambiguous genitalia and severe perineal hypospadias, characterized by an infantile male phenotype with a short penis, absence of the urethral meatus at the glans penis, a shallow vaginal-like opening located inferior to the penis, and urinary outflow through this opening. Additionally, all patients had a blind-ending vagina with a depth ranging from 1 to 8 cm, and pelvic examinations indicated the absence of the uterus. General clinical data are presented in Table 1.

Scanning methods

The patients were asked to lie on the examination table using an abdominal array coil. All pelvic MRI examinations were performed on a Siemens 1.5-T scanner using the following sequences: axial T1WI with the following parameters: TR = 600 ms, TE = 9.2 ms, FOV = 360 mm, slice thickness = 5 mm; axial T2WI_Tirm with the following parameters: TR = 3500 ms, TE = 39 ms, FOV = 360 mm, slice thickness = 5 mm; sagittal T2WI_fs with the following parameters: TR = 4500 ms, TE = 77 ms, FOV = 230 mm, slice thickness = 4 mm; coronal T2WI_fs with the following parameters: TR = 5000 ms, TE = 88 ms, FOV = 230 mm, slice thickness = 4 mm. The parameters for diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) were TR = 6000 ms, TE = 60 ms, FOV = 360 mm, and slice thickness = 5 mm. Contrast-enhanced axial T1_VIBE_dixon imaging was performed with the following parameters: TR = 6.88 ms, TE = 2.38 ms, FOV = 360 mm, and slice thickness = 2 mm. Furthermore, contrast-enhanced sagittal T1_VIBE_dixon imaging was performed using the following parameters: TR = 6.66 ms, TE = 2.39 ms, FOV = 320 mm, and slice thickness = 2.3 mm. During contrast-enhanced MRI (CE-MRI), 0.2 mL/kg of Gd-DTPA-BMA was administered through a dorsal hand vein using a high-pressure injector at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/s.

Image analysis

The preoperative pelvic MRI images of all patients were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists blinded to the surgical and pathological results. The following features were assessed: (1) presence or absence of the uterus, ovaries, and vagina; (2) location, size, and signal characteristics of dysplastic gonads; (3) presence of complications including perigonadal hydrocele and gonadal tumors. All suspected lesions were correlated with intraoperative surgical findings and histopathological results, where available, which together served as the gold standard.

Results

Laboratory examinations

Cytogenetic analysis revealed that 11 patients had a chromosomal karyotype of 46,XY, and one had a chromosomal karyotype of 46,X, inv(Y). Furthermore, serum sex hormone assay showed that testosterone levels were markedly elevated compared with those in healthy females, with a range of 4.83 to 48.21 nmol/L and a mean ± standard deviation of 19.22 ± 11.06 nmol/L. AR gene analysis was not performed in this cohort.

Surgical and pathological findings

All patients underwent resection of dysplastic gonads. Eleven patients were diagnosed with CAIS and one with PAIS according to the combined clinical, surgical and histopathological findings. Among the 12 patients, 21 dysplastic gonads were identified at surgical exploration. Twenty dysplastic gonads were confirmed histopathologically to contain testicular tissue or underdeveloped seminiferous tubules, while the remaining gonad was diagnosed based on characteristic macroscopic features observed during surgery, together with a definitive clinical diagnosis of AIS. Unilateral gonadal tissue was not identified during surgical exploration in 3 patients, and histopathological analysis of the corresponding surgical specimens revealed only fibrous stromal vascular tissue. Furthermore, no normal uterine or ovarian structures were observed intraoperatively in any patient. Moreover, dysplastic fallopian tube tissue was identified in 5 patients. The patient with PAIS presented with severe hypospadias, and pelvic exploration revealed a uterus-like Müllerian remnant. A right perigonadal hydrocele and a conventional SLCT arising from the right dysplastic gonad were observed in one patient.

Imaging findings

MRI demonstrated dysplastic gonads located in the pelvis or inguinal region in all patients, which were isointense on T1WI, slightly hyperintense on T2WI, hyperintense on DWI and hypointense on ADC maps. Furthermore, mild to moderate enhancement was observed after administration of Gd-DTPA. MRI correctly localized all 21 dysplastic gonads, in concordance with surgical findings. The blind-ending vagina was hypointense on sagittal contrast-enhanced images in all patients (Fig. 1). The uterus and ovarian structures in patients with CAIS were absent in the pelvic cavity. The patient with PAIS had a small penis and a strip-like abnormal signal behind the bladder, which was hypointense on T1WI, slightly hyperintense on T2WI, and showed marked enhancement on contrast-enhanced imaging. This finding was confirmed intraoperatively as uterus-like tissue (Fig. 2). One patient had a coexisting right perigonadal hydrocele (hypointense on T1WI, and hyperintense on T2WI, without contrast enhancement) and an underlying compressed SLCT. This tumor exhibited isointense signal on T1WI, slight hyperintensity on T2WI, hypointensity on DWI, hyperintensity on ADC maps, and rapid marked enhancement (Fig. 3). No other tumorous lesions were detected on preoperative MRI in the remaining 11 patients, which were consistent with postoperative pathological findings of benign dysplastic testicular tissue. The imaging characteristics and corresponding pathological findings for each patient are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

Individuals with AIS have normal female external genitalia but agenesis of Müllerian duct derivatives, characterized by the absence of the uterus and proximal vagina and the presence of intra-abdominal or inguinal testes. AIS is a relatively rare condition, with an incidence of 4.1 per 100,000 live-born females [5]. Furthermore, its incidence is approximately 0.8%-2.4% among phenotypic females with inguinal hernias [6]. Inguinal hernias are uncommon in girls, with a reported incidence of approximately 1%-4% in the pediatric population and male-to-female ratio of 10:1 [7]. In this study, approximately 50% of patients with AIS had a history of inguinal hernia during childhood. A previous study showed that approximately 57% of patients with CAIS have inguinal hernia, with some of them being diagnosed during infancy or childhood [8]. Therefore, AIS should be considered in girls presenting with inguinal hernia.

Based on the degree of androgen insensitivity, AIS can be categorized into CAIS, PAIS, and mild AIS (MAIS) [9]. Most patients with CAIS have female external genitalia, with the absence of the uterus and bilateral ovarian tissue. The dysplastic gonads correspond to dysplastic testes. Furthermore, almost all patients have a blind-ending vagina, with lengths ranging from 2.5 to 8.0 cm. Most patients with PAIS present with varying degrees of ambiguous genitalia and different degrees of feminization, including shorter blind-ending vaginas, micropenis, and hypospadias. Patients with MAIS typically present with gynecomastia or infertility during adolescence [10]. In this study, 11 patients with CAIS exhibited normal female external genitalia, while the patient with PAIS showed infantile male external genitalia with a small penis and severe hypospadias. The vaginal length ranged from 3 to 8 cm in patients with CAIS, while it was approximately 1 cm in the patient with PAIS, which is consistent with previous findings [10]. Sagittal contrast-enhanced sequences contribute to the clear delineation of vaginal structures.

Ultrasonography is the first-line modality for the initial evaluation of Müllerian structures and gonadal localization, with excellent sensitivity for inguinal canal gonads. MRI is more sensitive than ultrasonography for detecting intra-abdominal and pelvic gonads due to its superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar capability. Computed tomography (CT) is generally not recommended for routine gonadal assessment because of ionizing radiation exposure and limited soft tissue differentiation [11–12]. However, MRI manifestations of AIS have not been extensively investigated, and most published articles are isolated case reports. In this study, MRI findings in patients with CAIS demonstrated the absence of the uterus and ovarian structures, as well as the presence of dysplastic gonads in the pelvic or inguinal region. Furthermore, the patient with PAIS showed dysplastic gonads in the left inguinal region and a long strip-shaped penile cavernous body structure. A strip-like abnormal signal located between the bladder and rectum exhibited ill-defined margins on non-contrast T1WI and T2WI and appeared isointense on T1WI and slightly hyperintense on T2WI. After contrast administration, this lesion showed well-defined margins and marked enhancement and was intraoperatively confirmed as uterus-like tissue. These findings suggest that non-contrast sequences may fail to delineate subtle Müllerian remnants because of their similar signal intensity to surrounding soft tissues, whereas CE-MRI may facilitate their visualization and identification. However, the conclusion regarding the diagnostic value of CE-MRI in detecting Müllerian remnants is based on only one single case in our cohort and thus cannot be generalized to all patients with AIS. Larger prospective cohorts are required to further determine whether routine contrast-enhanced imaging is necessary for the evaluation of Müllerian remnants in clinical practice. Previous studies have shown that some patients with AIS may have incompletely regressed Müllerian structures in the pelvis, which might be associated with rare genetic mutations [13]. Therefore, residual Müllerian structures should not be overlooked during the diagnosis of AIS.

Early reports [14], which included only 7 cases, described all AIS gonads as being located in the inguinal region, while subsequent studies have demonstrated a more varied distribution, with approximately 50%-70% of dysplastic gonads located in the abdominal cavity, 20% in the inguinal region, and 10%-30% distributed in both locations, with rare involvement of the retroperitoneum [15]. In our cohort of 12 Chinese patients with AIS, 50.0% (6/12) of dysplastic gonads were located in the pelvic cavity, 25.0% (3/12) in the inguinal region, and 25.0% (3/12) in both regions. These discrepancies in gonadal distribution patterns across studies are most likely attributable to the limited sample sizes of earlier studies and referral bias. Our cohort, with 12 patients and complete surgical-pathological correlation, provides additional evidence regarding the distribution pattern of dysplastic gonads in Chinese patients with AIS. Furthermore, dysplastic gonads located in the pelvis or inguinal region were characterized on MRI by isointensity on T1WI, slight hyperintensity on T2WI, hyperintensity on DWI and mild-to-moderate enhancement. MRI correctly localized all 21 dysplastic gonads, in concordance with surgical findings. MRI also showed no evidence of dysplastic gonadal tissue in the corresponding anatomical regions in the 3 patients in whom unilateral gonadal tissue was not identified intraoperatively, consistent with surgical findings. These results support the value of MRI as a reliable preoperative imaging modality for the localization of dysplastic gonads in patients with AIS.

Moreover, MRI may detect complications such as perigonadal hydrocele and gonadal tumors. The risk of gonadal tumors increases with age, and approximately 1.6% of patients develop malignant tumors [16, 17]. Since prepubertal risk of malignancy is only approximately 0.8%, prophylactic gonadectomy is often deferred until completion of puberty, making long-term clinical surveillance necessary. Nakhal et al. reported that MRI is useful for detecting testicular abnormalities, including paratesticular cysts and Sertoli cell adenomas [18]. In our cohort, multiparametric MRI in a 51-year-old patient with CAIS revealed severe perigonadal hydrocele adjacent to the right dysplastic gonad. Histopathological examination confirmed a benign SLCT, a rare gonadal neoplasm in AIS, which exhibited isointense signal on T1WI, slight hyperintensity on T2WI, hypointensity on DWI, hyperintensity on ADC maps, and rapid marked enhancement. Previous reports on SLCT associated with AIS have mainly described non-contrast MRI features [18], while the present study further characterized the CE-MRI appearance of this rare benign neoplasm. In contrast, malignant seminomas, the most common tumor in patients with AIS, typically show restricted diffusion (DWI hyperintensity and ADC hypointensity) and heterogeneous enhancement [19–20], which differ from the imaging characteristics observed in our case. However, these findings require confirmation in larger series because of the rarity of this condition and the single-case nature of our observation. Additionally, a recent case report on rare seminoma complicating CAIS highlighted the role of MRI in evaluating therapeutic efficacy and monitoring tumor progression [15].

Beyond gonadal assessment and tumor detection, MRI plays an important role in differentiating AIS from other causes of primary amenorrhea, particularly Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome and Swyer syndrome (46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis), through the direct visualization of gonadal characteristics. In MRKH syndrome, despite the presence of uterine and upper vaginal agenesis, bilateral ovaries are preserved, which represents the principal imaging feature distinguishing this condition from AIS [21]. In contrast, Swyer syndrome is characterized by bilateral streak gonads and preserved Müllerian structures, and the absence of testicular-like gonads represents the key MRI feature differentiating it from AIS [22].

In the era of delayed prophylactic gonadectomy, MRI plays an increasingly important role in both preoperative planning and long-term surveillance of dysplastic gonads. The imaging strategy described in this study may serve as a useful non-invasive approach for the detection and preliminary differential diagnosis of gonadal lesions, thereby facilitating clinical decision-making and supporting individualized patient management.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include the small sample size inherent to rare disease research, the retrospective design, the lack of a control group, and the absence of AR genotyping. No pre-study sample size calculation or statistical power analysis was performed, and external validation was not feasible because of the limited number of AIS cases available at our single center. Inherent selection bias related to single-center referral patterns may exist. However, this risk was minimized by the consecutive enrollment of all eligible patients during the study period without selective exclusion. Furthermore, the cohort did not include any cases of invasive gonadal malignancy, precluding direct evaluation of the diagnostic performance of MRI for the early detection of malignant gonadal lesions. Therefore, further studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

Conclusion

This study represents one of the largest single-center imaging cohorts of patients with AIS in China with matched surgical and pathological data and systematically characterizes the MRI features of AIS. Based on the present findings, non-contrast T1WI, T2WI, and DWI are generally sufficient for gonadal localization and basic anatomical assessment. CE-MRI may provide additional information in selected cases with equivocal non-contrast findings or suspected Müllerian remnants or gonadal lesions. These findings provide a useful basis for the optimization of preoperative MRI protocols and personalized management of patients with AIS.
