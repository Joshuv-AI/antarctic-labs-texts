The Pathogenesis‐Related Protein 4b Induced by Rice Stripe Virus Enhances Host's Antiviral Defence
Authors: Binhao Gao, Long Ma, Huiyuan Zhang, Sinan Chen, Qiao Wang, Mingjun Li, Ling Qing, Gentu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRice stripe virus (RSV) causes rice stripe disease, which poses a serious threat to rice production. Pathogenesis‐related (PR) proteins are well known for their ability to enhance plant resistance against pathogen infection; however, reports on the involvement of PR proteins in antiviral defence remain limited. In this study, we found that the expression of PR4b was significantly upregulated in RSV‐infected plants. To investigate the functional relationship between RSV infection and PR4b expression, we generated rice lines differentially expressing OsPR4b. Phenotypic and molecular analyses showed that, compared with wild‐type plants, OsPR4b‐overexpressing plants exhibited enhanced resistance to RSV, characterized by milder viral symptoms and reduced viral accumulation. In contrast, knockdown of OsPR4b compromised the host's resistance to RSV, resulting in more severe symptoms and increased viral accumulation. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that knockdown of OsPR4b activated pathways related to RNA metabolism, oxidative stress, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and glyoxylate metabolism. These results imply that OsPR4b may confer antiviral resistance through multiple mechanisms: regulating RNA metabolism to indirectly modulate viral replication complex activity, and influencing cell wall construction and metabolic homeostasis to indirectly affect intercellular viral movement and replication. Collectively, these findings identify PR4b as a novel target for plant antiviral breeding.Keywords: gene expression, pathogenesis‐related protein 4b (PR4b), rice stripe virus, transcriptome, viral symptoms

Rice ( Oryza sativa ) is one of the most important grain crops in the world, and its yield and quality are critical for ensuring food security worldwide. Rice stripe disease, caused by rice stripe virus (RSV), is a devastating viral disease that results in significant yield losses in rice production. In major rice‐producing regions of Asia, the incidence of RSV can reach up to 80%, leading to 30%–40% yield reduction (Wei et al. 2009). RSV infection induces distinct symptoms in rice, including greenish striping, twisting and arcuate drooping of heart leaves; in severe cases, leaf necrosis occurs. The severity of developmental impairment is inversely correlated with the timing of infection: early‐stage RSV infection leads to severe growth retardation, disrupting normal growth processes and ultimately causing plant death (Wei et al. 2009), while late‐stage infection typically results in milder growth retardation. RSV is a single‐stranded, negative‐sense, RNA virus belonging to the genus Tenuivirus, and it is transmitted by the small brown planthopper ( Laodelphax striatellus ) (Kil and Kim 2023). The RSV genome consists of four RNAs (RNA1–RNA4). RNA1 encodes an RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), while the other three segments (RNA2–RNA4) have ambisense polarity, each containing two non‐overlapping open reading frames (ORFs) on opposite strands separated by a noncoding intergenic region (IR). These ORFs encode NS2 (P2), NSvc2 (glycoprotein, PC2), NS3 (RNA silencing repressor, P3), NSvc3 (coat protein, CP), NS4 (disease‐specific protein, SP) and NSvc4 (movement protein, MP) (Wang et al. 2022). Despite its high infectivity and severe impact on Asian rice production, no rice varieties with significant genetic resistance to RSV have been identified to date.

Pathogenesis‐related (PR) proteins are plant‐encoded proteins that are induced in response to infection by various pathogens, including viruses, bacteria and fungi. They can also be induced by treatment with chemicals that mimic pathogen infection or induce similar stress responses (Van Loon 1985; Lamb et al. 1989; Linthorst and Van Loon 1991). Based on amino acid sequence similarity, enzymatic activity and other biological properties, PR proteins have been classified into 17 families (PR1 to PR17) (Liu and Ekramoddoullah 2006). Different PR families exhibit diverse biological functions in plant defence. For example, the PR1 protein in wheat confers tolerance to multiple abiotic stresses and resistance to Botrytis cinerea and Alternaria solani (Zribi et al. 2024). OsPR1a, OsPR1b and OsPR10a in rice are involved in resistance against Xanthomonas campestris (Son et al. 2024); and the type I protease inhibitor (a member of the PR6 family) from Nicotiana benthamiana impairs potato virus X (PVX) infection by targeting and degrading the viral suppressor of RNA silencing (VSR) P25 (Shen et al. 2025).

PR4 proteins, also known as endochitinases, are divided into two classes (class I and class II) based on the presence or absence of a cysteine‐rich chitin‐binding domain (hevein domain) at the N‐terminus; both classes contain a conserved BARWIN domain at the C‐terminus (Neuhaus et al. 1996). The BARWIN domain, named after the barley seed protein BARWIN, contains six cysteine residues that form three disulphide bonds and mediate sugar‐binding activity (Ludvigsen and Poulsen 1992). The first identified PR4 genes, win1 and win2, are tandemly arranged on the potato genome and encode two highly homologous wound‐inducible proteins (Stanford et al. 1989). Accumulating evidence indicates that PR4 genes play important roles in plant defence responses. In wheat, PR4 genes are induced by systemic acquired resistance (SAR) activators, including Fusarium infection and wounding (Bertini et al. 2003), and they exhibit antifungal activity by inhibiting the growth of certain pathogenic fungi and possess ribonuclease activity (Caporale et al. 2004). In rice, PR4 gene expression is induced by Fusarium oxysporum infection, jasmonic acid (JA) treatment and transcriptional regulators, and it confers significant resistance to F. oxysporum (Zhu et al. 2006).

Accumulating studies have demonstrated that PR4 family proteins also function as key antiviral regulators in diverse plant–virus interaction systems. In Arabidopsis, PR4 is significantly induced by turnip crinkle virus (TCV) and suppresses viral replication through ribonuclease (RNase) activity (Dong 2008). In pepper, CaPR4 is strongly activated by tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV), and enhances host resistance by triggering hypersensitive response (HR) and degrading viral nucleic acids (Park et al. 2004). In tobacco and tomato, PR4 genes are also induced by PVX and TMV to participate in host antiviral signalling pathways (García‐Garrido et al. 2010; Elvira et al. 2008). These reports clearly indicate that PR4 proteins play conserved and critical roles in plant antiviral immunity. However, the function of PR4 proteins in rice antiviral defence remains largely unknown. In particular, whether OsPR4b is involved in regulating RSV infection has not been systematically reported.

In this study, we cloned the PR4b gene, analysed its expression patterns in rice and Nicotiana benthamiana upon RSV infection, and generated PR4b differentially expressing‐rice plants through genetic transformation. Our results demonstrate that PR4b actively participates in the host's defence response against RSV infection, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms of PR protein‐mediated antiviral defence in plants.

PR genes are typically induced in response to pathogen infection. To investigate the expression pattern of PR4b in rice upon RSV infection, reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed. At 14 days post‐inoculation (dpi), early disease symptoms, including greenish‐yellow‐white spots or short streaks, appeared on the heart leaves of RSV‐infected rice plants compared with uninfected control plants. These symptoms subsequently developed into yellowish‐green interveinal stripes with persistent green areas between the veins, and young leaves exhibited severe green curling and wilting (Figure 1a). Successful RSV infection was further verified by western blot analysis using an anti‐RSV CP antibody (Figure 1b). Quantitative analysis showed that the expression level of OsPR4b in RSV‐infected rice plants was 2.26‐fold higher than that in uninfected control plants (Figure 1c), indicating that RSV infection significantly induces OsPR4b expression in rice. In addition, western blot analysis demonstrated that OsPR4b protein accumulation was significantly increased in RSV‐infected rice (Figure 1d), confirming that RSV induces OsPR4b expression at both mRNA and protein levels (materials and methods are provided in Appendix S1).

To characterize PR4b function in Nicotiana benthamiana, we identified NbPR4b, a putative OsPR4b homologue, which shares 61.6% amino acid identity with OsPR4b via NCBI BLAST. A 444‐bp coding sequence (CDS) of NbPR4b was amplified from N. benthamiana cDNA using gene‐specific primers (Table S1) and cloned into a T‐vector (Figure S1a,b). To determine whether NbPR4b expression is induced by RSV infection, we observed that RSV‐infected N. benthamiana plants developed small white spots and chlorosis (Figure S2a). RT‐qPCR analysis showed that the expression level of NbPR4b in RSV‐infected N. benthamiana plants was 4.4‐fold higher than that in mock‐treated control plants, showing a significant difference (Figure S2b).

We generated OsPR4b‐overexpressing (OE) and OsPR4b‐knockdown (ospr4bkd) transgenic rice plants (Wimi Biotechnology (Hainan) Co. Ltd.). After 30 days of growth, RNAi (knockdown) plants exhibited significantly enhanced growth vigour compared with wild‐type (WT) control plants, while OE plants showed growth retardation (Figure 2a). Quantitative analysis of plant height using GraphPad Prism software (v. 8.2.1) revealed that OsPR4b plants were significantly taller than both WT and OE plants (Figure 2b). Western blot analysis using an anti‐PR4b antibody confirmed the differential expression levels of PR4b in transgenic lines: OE plants showed markedly higher PR4b accumulation compared with WT plants, while OsPR4b lines displayed effective silencing of PR4b (Figure 2c).

(b) Histograms showing rice height (Student's t test, **p < 0.01). (c) The stability of OsPR4b expression in transgenic rice by western blot analysis.

To evaluate the antiviral resistance of transgenic rice plants, OsPR4b‐overexpressing, knockdown and WT rice plants were exposed to viruliferous small brown planthoppers in controlled insect chambers for virus transmission, and symptom development was monitored daily. At the onset of viral symptom manifestation (typically 10–14 days post‐inoculation [dpi]), representative leaves were sampled for phenotypic recording. Quantitative symptom scoring showed that OsPR4b‐OE plants exhibited milder viral symptoms compared with WT plants, while ospr4bkd lines displayed more severe symptoms (Figure 3a). RT‐qPCR analysis of RSV CP transcript levels showed a strong correlation with phenotypic severity: OE plants accumulated 93% less viral RNA than WT plants, while ospr4b lines showed a 110‐fold increase in viral RNA accumulation (Figure 3b). Western blot analysis using an RSV‐CP antibody at 30 dpi further confirmed these results, showing significantly reduced RSV CP accumulation in OE plants and markedly increased RSV CP levels in ospr4b‐suppressed mutants compared with WT controls (Figure 3c). These data demonstrate a clear inverse correlation between OsPR4b expression level and RSV accumulation. In addition, RT‐qPCR analysis showed that OsPR4b expression was significantly increased in overexpression lines and markedly reduced in knockdown lines compared with WT plants (Figure 3d), demonstrating a negative correlation between OsPR4b transcript levels and RSV accumulation.

To define the potential pathways and subcellular sites contributing to PR4b‐mediated RSV resistance, we examined the subcellular localization of PR4b. A PCV/GFP‐PR4b fusion construct was generated and transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. Confocal microscopy observation at 48 h post‐infiltration (hpi) revealed that PR4b localized to both the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and the plasma membrane (Figure S3a,b). These findings suggest that PR4b may exert antiviral activity through two mechanisms: (i) indirectly modulating viral replication complex activity to affect viral genomic RNA synthesis, and (ii) indirectly influencing intercellular viral movement by affecting the function or microenvironment of plasmodesmata.

Comparative transcriptome analysis of ospr4bkd13, ospr4bkd5 and WT plants revealed that the number of unique genes was 307 in WT, 210 in ospr4bkd5 and 426 in ospr4bkd13. The higher number of unique genes in ospr4bkd13 indicates more pronounced ‘specific transcriptional changes’ in this line (Figure 4a). Under the screening criteria of p adj ≤ 0.05 and log2fold‐change ≥ |1|, a total of 628 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the ospr4bkd13 versus WT comparison, including 473 upregulated and 155 downregulated genes. In the ospr4bkd5 versus WT comparison, 464 DEGs were identified, including 332 upregulated and 132 downregulated genes (Figure 4b). The number of upregulated DEGs was significantly higher than that of downregulated genes in both knockdown lines, suggesting that knockdown of OsPR4b may trigger the expression of more ‘compensatory’ antiviral genes. The total number of DEGs in ospr4bkd5 was lower than that in ospr4bkd13, and the ratio of upregulated to downregulated genes was similar, further supporting the notion that ospr4bkd13 may have a higher degree of gene silencing.

Transcriptome analysis revealed that both ospr4bkd13 and ospr4bkd5 exhibited differential expression patterns in specific transcription factor families following RSV infection. While sharing common features, the two lines also displayed distinct characteristics, primarily attributed to varying degrees of OsPR4b silencing. In terms of commonality, both lines upregulated transcription factors from the NAC, MYB and ERF families (e.g., Os01g0326100, Os08g0170700, Os05g0442400, Os11g0214400 and Os03g0235000) (Table S2). This represents a shared compensatory defence response activated by the host upon OsPR4b silencing, which may compensate for defence deficiencies by activating downstream disease resistance genes, reinforcing cell walls and coordinating hormone signalling pathways. The specificity was reflected in the downregulated gene families. ospr4bkd13, with higher silencing efficiency, primarily downregulated WRKY family members critical for antiviral defence (e.g., Os01g0656400 and Os11g0677101), leading to significantly higher RSV accumulation. In contrast, ospr4bkd5 mainly downregulated MADS‐box family genes associated with growth and development (e.g., Os03g0375200 and Os12g0551900), resulting in a milder perturbation of core antiviral pathways. This differential pattern further confirms that OsPR4b participates in antiviral defence by multidimensionally regulating transcription factor networks, and the extent of silencing directly influences the host's defence strategy and efficiency.

GO functional enrichment analysis showed that compared with WT plants, ospr4bkd13 and ospr4bkd5 lines were significantly enriched in biological processes (BP) such as RNA metabolic process, DNA modification and stress response. This suggests that OsPR4b may function by regulating RNA metabolism to disrupt the assembly of the RSV replication complex. In cellular components (CC), ER‐ and plasma membrane‐related terms were weakly enriched, which may be indirectly associated with the regulatory roles of PR4b with the interaction between RSV MP and plasmodesmata, thereby affecting viral intercellular movement through indirect regulatory pathways. In molecular function (MF), oxidoreductase activity, peroxidase activity and cellulose synthase activity were significantly enriched, indicating that knockdown of OsPR4b activates oxidative stress pathways and physical barrier construction as compensatory defence responses (Figure S4a). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathways (Figure S4b). The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway may affect cell wall strength and viral protease activity, supporting both the ‘disruption of RC assembly’ and ‘blockage of viral movement’ functions of PR4b. The glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathway may reduce antiviral efficiency by interfering with energy metabolism and reactive oxygen species (ROS) balance. Collectively, these results illustrate the potential pathways through which PR4b confers resistance against RSV. Consistent with the above transcriptomic differences, western blot analysis confirmed that OsPR4b protein levels were obviously lower in ospr4bkd13 than in ospr4bkd5 (Figure 4c), further verifying the higher silencing efficiency of ospr4bkd13.

To verify transcriptome data validity, we subjected several key DEGs to RT‐qPCR validation. As shown in Figure S5b, the expression patterns of these representative DEGs were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results, confirming the reliability of our transcriptome analysis. RT‐qPCR detection in non‐RSV‐inoculated WT and ospr4bkd lines (Figure S5a) revealed that the expression trends of these key genes were consistent with those observed in the transcriptome, demonstrating that the transcriptional changes were caused by OsPR4b knockdown rather than differences in viral load.

To test for potential physical interactions between OsPR4b and viral proteins, a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay was conducted. Results showed that OsPR4b exhibited no direct interaction with RSV MP (Figure S6a) or any of the five fragments of RSV RdRp (Figure S6b). Combined with the subcellular localization of OsPR4b at the ER and plasma membrane, these results indicate that OsPR4b may indirectly affect viral replication and intercellular movement through regulatory pathways rather than direct physical binding to viral proteins.

Plants are constantly challenged by various pathogens, including viruses, bacteria and fungi, and viral infections pose a significant threat to global crop productivity. During plant–virus interactions, host defence mechanisms are activated, including the induction of PR proteins, which play multifaceted roles in restricting pathogen proliferation and spread (Van Loon et al. 2006). For example, in soybean infected with soybean mosaic virus (SMV), PR1 specifically accumulates in necrotic leaf tissues, promoting localized cell death to limit viral spread (Zhao et al. 2023). Although PR proteins are recognized as key components of antiviral defence, the molecular mechanisms underlying their antiviral activity remain poorly characterized. Our study demonstrates that PR4b expression is rapidly induced in both rice ( Oryza sativa ) and Nicotiana benthamiana following RSV infection, with 2.3‐fold and 4.4‐fold upregulation at 7–14 dpi, respectively (Figure 1b, Figure S2b). This pronounced induction suggests that PR4b actively contributes to the host's antiviral response, consistent with the established roles of PR proteins in plant defence (Zhao et al. 2023; Rabie et al. 2024).

Plants employ multiple defence mechanisms to combat pathogen attacks. The first line of defence includes physical barriers such as lignin‐rich cuticles and cell walls, which prevent the invasion of most microorganisms (Chassot et al. 2007). However, these structural defences are often insufficient against highly adapted pathogens. To counteract such threats, plants activate induced defence responses, including the expression of PR proteins, which play a pivotal role in antiviral defence (Van Loon et al. 1994, 2006; Van Loon and Van Strien 1999). PR proteins are well‐documented for their broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity. For instance, MdPR4 in apple ( Malus domestica ) exhibits direct antifungal activity by inhibiting fungal mycelial growth (Bai et al. 2013), in cucumber ( Cucumis sativus ), PR1, PR2, PR4 and PR5 are upregulated upon fungal infection, enhancing resistance (Wang et al. 2024); and grape ( Vitis vinifera ) PR4 confers defence against powdery mildew (Dai et al. 2016). PR4 proteins contain a conserved BARWIN domain and are involved in both biotic and abiotic stress responses. For example, rice OsPR4 is induced by drought, salinity, cold and rice blast fungus (Magnaporthe oryzae) (Wang et al. 2011). However, the role of PR4 proteins in antiviral defence remains poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrated that OsPR4b positively regulates resistance against RSV. We generated OsPR4b‐overexpressing (OE) and OsPR4b‐knockdown transgenic rice lines and confirmed the differential expression levels of PR4b at the protein level (Figure 2c). Following RSV inoculation via the small brown planthopper, OE plants exhibited significantly reduced viral accumulation, whereas knockdown lines showed enhanced susceptibility to RSV (Figure 3a–d). These results clearly establish a negative correlation between OsPR4b expression level and RSV accumulation.

Subcellular localization assays revealed that PR4b localizes to the ER and plasma membrane (Figure S3), suggesting that PR4b‐mediated antiviral immunity likely interferes with viral replication and cell‐to‐cell movement. Upon knockdown of OsPR4b, DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes such as RNA metabolism, cellular components related to the ER/plasma membrane, and molecular functions including oxidoreductase activity (Figure S4a). This indicates that PR4b may indirectly modulate RSV replication complex activity by regulating RNA metabolism and, through its subcellular localization, influence the microenvironment or function of plasmodesmata to indirectly affect intercellular viral movement protein and plasmodesmata, thereby affecting viral intercellular movement through indirect regulatory pathways. Furthermore, the enrichment of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (Figure S4b) suggests that PR4b may also contribute to antiviral defence by enhancing physical barriers such as cell wall fortification.

PR proteins not only exhibit direct antimicrobial activity but also act as regulatory hubs, broadly influencing the expression networks of host endogenous genes to coordinate multilayered defence responses. Although PR proteins are generally considered downstream defence effectors, our transcriptomic results revealed that OsPR4b knockdown significantly altered upstream defence pathways, including RNA metabolism, oxidative stress, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and glyoxylate metabolism, indicating that PR4b is not merely a terminal downstream, but may contribute to the broader immune network activated during RSV infection. Numerous studies have reported that various PR proteins participate in activating signalling pathways mediated by JA, salicylic acid (SA) and ethylene (ET) (Flors et al. 2008; Rayapuram and Baldwin 2007). In particular, members of the PR1, PR2 and PR5 families often serve as markers for SAR (Liu et al. 2019), and their upregulation is typically accompanied by the coordinated induction of a suite of defence‐related genes. In this study, we found that knockdown of OsPR4b perturbed endogenous gene expression (Table S2). Silencing of OsPR4b triggered a common upregulation of defence‐associated transcription factors from the NAC, MYB and ERF families, reflecting a host compensatory response. However, varying silencing efficiencies led to specific transcriptional reprogramming: the high‐efficiency silencing line specifically downregulated WRKY family members, which are central to antiviral defence, whereas the low‐efficiency silencing line primarily affected MADS‐box family genes associated with growth and development. These results reveal that OsPR4b functions not only as a defence executor but also as a key regulatory node within the defence transcriptional network. Its expression level finely tunes the landscape of defence programmes and determines antiviral efficacy, thereby deepening our understanding of the functional mechanisms of PR proteins.

In summary, our study not only confirms that PR4b is specifically induced upon RSV infection and directly contributes to the host's antiviral defence but also preliminarily reveals that PR4b may exert its antiviral function through multiple pathways. These include acting at the ER and plasma membrane to coordinately regulate RNA metabolism, cell wall construction and indirectly influence viral movement and replication processes. Although the precise molecular details of PR4b‐mediated RSV inhibition require further investigation, our findings establish PR4b as a critical component of the host antiviral defence system, providing a novel potential target for crop antiviral breeding.
