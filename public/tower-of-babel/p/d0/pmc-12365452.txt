Evaluating Cost Disparities and Utilization of Shoulder Arthroscopy: A National Analysis of Racial, Ethnic, and Socioeconomic Determinants
Authors: Timothy A. Reiad, Peter V. Dinh, David Bruni, Cameron Thomson, Brett D. Owens, Stephen E. Marcaccio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Shoulder arthroscopy is increasingly common for treating various shoulder pathologies, but racial, ethnic, and geographic disparities in its use persist, especially as more procedures move to ambulatory surgery centers. Identifying and addressing these disparities is crucial for ensuring equitable orthopaedic care in the United States.Hypothesis/Purpose:It was hypothesized that racial and ethnic disparities exist in shoulder arthroscopy utilization. The purpose of this study was to assess these disparities and their contributing factors in greater detail.Study Design:Descriptive epidemiology study.Methods:This study used the 2019 National Ambulatory Surgery Sample (NASS) to evaluate utilization patterns for shoulder arthroscopy in the United States. Age-standardized utilization rates were calculated for racial and ethnic subgroups. Multivariate Poisson regression models were used to assess disparities, incorporating patient demographics, socioeconomic factors, and facility characteristics as covariates. Differences in total charges were also analyzed.Results:An estimated 340,892 shoulder arthroscopy surgeries were performed nationwide in 2019, with a mean charge of $32,910. The patient cohort was 57.34% male and 80.26% White, and 78.4% of the patients were older than 45 years. The most common indications for shoulder arthroscopy in this patient cohort were rotator cuff injury (59.96%), labral tear (8.17%), and impingement syndrome (8.32%). There were notable disparities in shoulder arthroscopy by race and ethnicity. White patients had higher utilization rates for shoulder arthroscopy (138.7/100,000) compared with Black (73.0), Hispanic (50.7), and Asian/Pacific Islander (36.5) patients, with an adjusted rate ratio (aRR) of 1.29. White patients were charged less on average for shoulder arthroscopy procedures ($32,183) compared with non-White patients ($35,474). Disparities persisted across all indications, with White patients showing higher utilization rates for rotator cuff repairs (aRR = 1.17) and shoulder instability (aRR = 1.22) and consistently lower charges ($34,438 vs $37,175 for rotator cuff repair; $32,568 vs $35,732 for instability). Geographic disparities were observed, with mean charges of $28,657 in the Northeast and Midwest and $36,309 in the South and West. Black (15.38%), Hispanic (13.15%), and Asian/Pacific Islander (11.24%) patients had higher Medicaid rates than White patients (7.3%). Black (42.9%) and Hispanic (28.97%) patients were more likely to live in low-income areas compared with White patients (20.52%).Conclusion:There are well-defined disparities in the use of orthopaedic surgery among different patient populations in the United States. Differences in utilization rates, costs incurred, and patient-reported outcomes of shoulder arthroscopy mirror those of hip and knee arthroscopy. These trends continue to worsen and may reflect the changing landscape of healthcare delivery. These trends must be closely analyzed to help define their effect on the disparities experienced by disadvantaged populations in the United States.

Racial disparities exist across many areas of medicine and surgery, affecting a variety of minority groups. Racial and ethnic minorities in the United States, including Black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander patients, as well as other socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, face numerous healthcare disparities. These groups have reduced access to preventive care, higher rates of emergency resource utilization, and a greater likelihood of being uninsured or underinsured, and they report lower levels of trust in the healthcare system.9,14,27 After orthopaedic surgery, non-White patients experience more complications, worse outcomes, greater lengths of stay, and more post–acute care facility discharges compared with White patients.1,21,33,40 To combat healthcare disparities, in 2011 the US Department of Health and Human Services launched a comprehensive action plan providing federal funding aimed at improving insurance coverage, quality of care, workforce diversity, population health, and data collection. 39 Several orthopaedic-specific programs and initiatives were implemented to address healthcare inequalities, including the Musculoskeletal Healthcare Disparities Symposium and the Movement Is Life campaign.20,29-32

Despite the emergence of these programs, however, disparities persist across orthopaedics. Several recent analyses of hip and knee arthroscopy and arthroplasty utilization in the United States found that White patients were significantly more likely to undergo surgery, and non-White patients experienced more perioperative complications, greater lengths of stay, and more post–acute care facility discharges.3,4 A more recent analysis of utilization rates of total shoulder arthroplasty found that such racial disparities not only persist but are worsening over time.6,12 Unfortunately, this is compounded by many orthopaedic surgeons being unaware that these disparities exist. 2

Shoulder arthroscopy is an increasingly used approach for the treatment of a multitude of pathologies, including instability, rotator cuff disease, subacromial impingement, stiffness, and osteoarthritis. In a shift from open surgery, the rate of arthroscopic shoulder surgery nearly doubled throughout the early 2010s, and its use and indications continue to increase.15,36 Additionally, the majority of arthroscopic shoulder surgery is transitioning to being performed at ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs) with planned same-day discharge as opposed to inpatient centers with planned postoperative admission. 34 The effect of these shifts in shoulder arthroscopy practices on healthcare equality is unknown. This study examines the patterns of arthroscopic shoulder surgery utilization in the United States during 2019 in order to identify and characterize potential disparities among minority populations. We hypothesized that despite the emergence of healthcare equality initiatives, disparities in the utilization of shoulder arthroscopy between White and non-White patients have persisted.

Methods

This study used the Nationwide Ambulatory Surgery Sample (NASS) as its data source. NASS is part of a suite of databases and software tools created for the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). 16 NASS is the only comprehensive, all-payer database for ambulatory surgeries in the United States, providing national estimates for outpatient surgeries performed in hospital-owned facilities. Data on individual outpatient surgery encounters were gathered from NASS and analyzed for this study. The HCUP databases compile encounter-level healthcare data through a collaboration among state data organizations, hospital associations, and private data organizations, collectively known as HCUP Data Partners. 17

Variables

The primary predictor variables were (1) race (White, Black, or Asian or Pacific Islander); (2) ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic), as self-reported by patients in the NASS dataset; and (3) the census region of the surgical center where the procedure was performed (Northeast, Midwest, South, West). The main outcome of interest was the utilization of shoulder arthroscopy, identified by Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 29805, 29806, 29807, 29819, 29820, 29821, 29822, 29823, 29824, 29825, 29826, 29827, and 29828 (Table 1). All encounters that included any one of these codes were included in the study. International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes were used to identify the specific indications for shoulder arthroscopy procedures. Drawing from Amen et al, 3 we used demographic characteristics including age, sex, urban-rural classification (according to the National Center for Health Statistics), median household income quartile (based on patient zip code), insurance status, and presence of medical comorbidities. Facility characteristics, such as type (ASC vs hospital-based), bed size (small, medium, large), ownership (public, not-for-profit, for-profit), and teaching status, were also collected. Total charges (in 2019 US dollars) were analyzed. Total billed charges approximate surgery costs but typically do not reflect the actual expenses. Total charges billed vary by insurer; however, they are useful for identifying trends and potential cost disparities and determinants, despite their limitations. 23 Recent HCUP studies have shown that total charges are a reliable estimate of surgical costs.5,8,10,19,22,23,24,41

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Stata Statistical Software Version 18.0 (StataCorp). Given that the NASS dataset is a stratified cluster sample, weighted sampling techniques were applied to generate national estimates and account for the design of the database. All reported estimates and summary statistics are survey-adjusted to reflect the complex sampling design of the NASS, and may therefore differ from values calculated using raw sample sizes or simple subgroup averages. To calculate age-standardized utilization rates for shoulder arthroscopy, NASS estimates were adjusted based on US Census population estimates for each racial and ethnic group. 38 Differences in baseline characteristics between groups were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables. For continuous variables, Student t test was used for 2-group comparisons, and analysis of variance was used for comparisons across >2 groups. Poisson regression was used to model age-standardized utilization rates and adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) for shoulder arthroscopy. The aRRs were used to compare the relative rates of shoulder arthroscopy between different groups while adjusting for confounding variables, including sex, race and ethnicity, census region, and medical comorbidities. This study also used a multiple regression model to assess factors influencing the cost of care using all variables that were significant under univariate analysis. Beta coefficients in the multiple linear regression were used to measure the effect of each factor on the overall cost of shoulder arthroscopy. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

An estimated 340,892 shoulder arthroscopy surgeries were performed nationwide in 2019, with a mean charge of $32,910. The patient population was 57.34% male and 80.26% White, and 78.4% of the patients were older than 45 years. The demographic characteristics of patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopy varied by race and ethnicity. Black, Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander patients were more likely to have Medicaid compared with White patients, with respective rates of 15.3%, 13.1%, and 11.2% versus 7.3% for White patients (P < .001) (Table 2). Black and Hispanic patients were also more likely to live in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, with 42.9% and 28.97%, respectively, residing in the lowest income quartile, compared with 20.52% of White patients (P < .001).

Geographical distributions also varied by race and ethnicity. A higher proportion of Black and Hispanic patients were located in the South (52.3% and 31.9%, respectively) versus 35.38% of White patients (P < .001), whereas a higher proportion of Asian or Pacific Islander and Hispanic patients underwent arthroscopy in the West (57.1% and 41.5%, respectively) compared with 18.2% of White patients and 8.8% of Black patients (P < .001) (Figure 1). White patients were more likely to be treated in smaller facilities (30.0% vs 18.37%; P < .001) and nonteaching facilities (38.2% vs 26.67%; P < .001) compared with non-White patients.

Utilization Rates and Charges

At the national level, shoulder arthroscopy utilization rates were significantly higher among White patients compared with Black, Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander patients (White, 138.7/100,000; Black, 73.0/100,000; Hispanic, 50.7/100,000; Asian or Pacific Islander, 36.5/100,000). The aRR for White versus non-White patients across the study period was 1.29 (95% CI, 1.26-1.33; P < .001).

Significant geographic variations in utilization rates were observed, with racial and ethnic disparities being most pronounced in the Northeast (White vs non-White aRR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.51-1.73]; P < .001) and South (White vs non-White aRR, 1.4 [95% CI, 1.28-1.42]; P < .001) (Table 3).

Geographic disparities in charges were also present, with patients in the Northeast and Midwest being charged significantly less than those in the South and West (Table 5). Patients in the Northeast and Midwest were charged a mean of $28,657 (95% CI: $28,559-$28,757), whereas those in the South and West were charged $36,309 (95% CI: $36,193-$36,426). Disparities across insurance types were also observed. Private insurance showed significant cost savings compared with nonprivate insurance (eg, Medicaid, Medicare, self-pay). Patients with private insurance were charged a mean of $32,326 (95% CI: $32,215-$32,437), whereas patients with other insurance types were charged $33,631 (95% CI: $33,512-$33,750; P < .001). Significant differences existed in the specific arthroscopic procedure distribution across insurance types. Most notably, compared with patients who had public insurance (Medicare and Medicaid), patients with private insurance were more likely to undergo shoulder arthroscopy for capsulorrhaphy (private 11.81% vs public 5.06%; P < .001) and superior labrum anterior-posterior (SLAP) lesion repair (private 7.50% vs public 3.76%; P < .001). Conversely, compared with those with private insurance, patients with public insurance were more likely to undergo shoulder arthroscopy for rotator cuff repair (public 48.38 vs private 40.37; P < .001) and synovectomy (CPT 29820, 29821; public 1.19% vs private 0.96%; P = .001).

Disparities by Indication

The most frequent indications for shoulder arthroscopy in our sample included rotator cuff injury (204,399; 59.96%), impingement syndrome of the shoulder (28,362; 8.32%), labral tear (27,851; 8.17%), primary osteoarthritis of the shoulder (12,681; 3.72%), and shoulder instability (9,852; 2.89%). Other diagnoses included adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), tendinitis, bursitis, other shoulder sprain, and other general shoulder-related indications (Table 6).

Racial and ethnic differences in utilization rates and charges for shoulder arthroscopy were observed across specific indications for surgery, including rotator cuff repair and shoulder instability. Non-White patients had lower utilization rates, most pronounced in patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopy for rotator cuff injuries (White vs non-White aRR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.13-1.21]; P < .001) and shoulder instability (White vs non-White aRR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.07-1.39]; P = .004). Racial disparities in total charges were observed across all indications. For rotator cuff injuries, non-White patients had a mean charge of $37,175 (95% CI, $36,925-$37,426) compared with White patients at $34,438 (95% CI, $34,320-$34,558; P < .001). For shoulder instability, non-White patients had a mean charge of $35,732 (95% CI, $35,002-$36,463) compared with White patients at $32,568 (95% CI, $32,130-$33,006; P < .001). For impingement syndrome, non-White patients had a mean charge of $29,107 (95% CI, $28,541-$29,673) compared with white patients at $25,784 (95% CI, $25,526-$26,041; P < .001).

Discussion

This nationwide database study evaluating differences in the utilization of shoulder arthroscopy revealed significant racial and ethnic disparities. Shoulder arthroscopy encompasses a range of procedures addressing various shoulder pathologies, including rotator cuff disease, shoulder instability, and arthritis-related procedures. 18 Socioeconomic differences are evident, as a higher proportion of Black and Hispanic patients reside in lower income areas and rely on Medicaid insurance, which likely affects their access to specialized care and ability to pursue surgical interventions.21,28,37 These disparities in access to care are observed across multiple orthopaedic procedures, such as total knee arthroplasty (TKA), total hip arthroplasty (THA), and shoulder arthroplasty, and limit access to specialized care.4,35 The combination of lower socioeconomic status and reduced access to specialized care is a consistent driver of inequity in orthopaedic surgery. 13 As the indications for shoulder arthroscopy continue to expand and procedures shift to ASCs, it is becoming increasingly important to identify and address barriers to care among different patient populations.

This nationwide database study has highlighted significant racial and ethnic disparities in surgical intervention rates. Specifically, White patients were found to be 1.9 times more likely than Black patients, 2.7 times more likely than Hispanic patients, and 3.8 times more likely than Asian or Pacific Islander patients to undergo shoulder arthroscopy after adjustment for age. These disparities persisted across different clinical indications, including rotator cuff injuries and shoulder instability, although the magnitude varied somewhat by specific pathology. For rotator cuff injuries and shoulder instability, non-White patients had notably lower utilization rates compared with White patients. However, the gap in utilization rates for rotator cuff repairs was slightly smaller than for shoulder instability procedures. This suggests that although racial disparities are present across indications, their extent may differ based on the specific shoulder pathology and patient preferences. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating racial and ethnic disparities in other orthopaedic subspecialties.4,11

The geographic variation in shoulder arthroscopy utilization patterns was particularly pronounced in the Northeast and South, indicating that regional factors play a significant role in access to care. This trend was consistent across various shoulder procedures, suggesting a systemic pattern of disparities. These findings align with previous studies indicating that disparities in orthopaedic procedures are often exacerbated by geographic location.25,26 Factors contributing to this variability may include differences in healthcare infrastructure, availability of specialized orthopaedic services, and regional socioeconomic conditions. For instance, areas with a higher concentration of healthcare facilities may provide greater access to surgical interventions, whereas regions with fewer resources may experience significant barriers. 25

Differences in total charges for shoulder arthroscopy highlight ongoing issues related to healthcare equity. Non-White patients faced higher charges for shoulder arthroscopy regardless of the underlying condition. This observation suggests that financial barriers may disproportionately affect minority populations, potentially affecting their decisions to pursue necessary surgical care. The mean cost of shoulder arthroscopy was $32,910, with non-White patients facing mean charges of $35,474, whereas White patients had mean charges of $32,183. These differences in healthcare costs may present additional challenges for non-White patients who are often already experiencing socioeconomic disadvantages.

The observed differences in procedure distribution across insurance types may contribute to these cost disparities. Patients with private insurance were more likely to undergo shoulder arthroscopy for capsulorrhaphy and SLAP lesion repair, whereas patients with public insurance were more likely to undergo rotator cuff repair and synovectomy. Variations in the types of procedures performed among different insurance groups could reflect differences in patient demographics, injury patterns, or treatment preferences. For instance, the complexity and associated costs of certain procedures might vary significantly between insurance groups, potentially influencing overall charges. Understanding these nuances is crucial for addressing the financial barriers that disproportionately affect minority populations.

The characteristics of the facilities where patients receive treatment may also contribute to these disparities; the present study found that White patients were more likely to be treated in smaller, nonteaching facilities. As previously mentioned, there is a nationwide trend moving sports medicine procedures, including shoulder arthroscopy, to be performed in ASCs. 37 This trend may further affect racial minorities, as ASCs often function through different care and insurance networks than neighboring hospital systems. ASCs may not provide the same level of financial resources or support as hospital-based settings, potentially limiting access for patients from disadvantaged backgrounds. Research indicates that disparities in surgical care can be exacerbated when procedures are performed in ASCs, particularly for minority populations who may face additional barriers related to transportation, insurance coverage, and overall access to care. 37 Addressing these disparities requires a comprehensive understanding of both socioeconomic factors and the implications of facility type on patient access to surgical interventions.

Disparities in shoulder arthroscopy utilization share similarities with those observed in TKA, THA, and shoulder arthroplasty, but there are also notable differences. Like TKA, THA, and shoulder arthroplasty, shoulder arthroscopy demonstrates significantly lower utilization rates among Black, Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander patients compared with White patients.3,7 For example, White patients were nearly twice as likely to undergo TKA or THA as Black patients, and similar trends are evident in shoulder arthroscopy.3,7 However, unlike TKA or THA, which are often performed for advanced arthritis with clear indications, shoulder arthroscopy encompasses a broader range of procedures (eg, rotator cuff repair, instability management) with varying levels of urgency. This variability may exacerbate disparities due to differences in healthcare-seeking behavior or referral patterns among racial groups.

As shoulder arthroscopy techniques continue to evolve and indications continue to expand, it is essential to consider whether disparities in utilization are primarily driven by insurance coverage or whether other factors, such as racial bias, also play a significant role. Although these findings highlight significant disparities in access and charges for shoulder arthroscopy, it is important to note that this study design does not establish direct causality between race and healthcare outcomes. The observed disparities likely stem from a complex interplay of factors, many of which were not directly measured in our analysis. These may include variations in referral patterns from primary care to orthopaedic specialists, regional differences in healthcare availability and quality, and disparities in insurance coverage and type. The question arises: If insurance coverage were equalized, as might be the case in a nationalized healthcare system, would the rates of shoulder arthroscopy utilization among different racial and ethnic groups converge? Trends in the management of physician groups and ASCs, such as private equity acquisition, and negotiations with different payers, may preclude certain populations from accessing specialized orthopaedic surgical care. Although we cannot definitively answer this question at this stage, it is vital to explore this topic in future research. Addressing these disparities will require early interventions by orthopaedic surgeons and policymakers to ensure optimal and equitable care for all patients. Proposed strategies may include targeted outreach programs, improved cultural competency training for healthcare providers, and policy measures aimed at addressing systemic barriers to care. Further investigation into the interplay between insurance status and other sociocultural factors will be crucial in understanding and ultimately reducing the disparities observed in shoulder arthroscopy utilization.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to national database analyses. The analysis was restricted to the factors included in the NASS database. Although we were able to study the effect of specific patient characteristics, such as demographics and comorbidities, the database does not allow for an evaluation of specific surgical characteristics, such as the use of more expensive equipment or advanced surgical techniques. Coding biases such as misclassification of procedures or comorbidities may skew results. Moreover, the NASS database only provides total charges, as opposed to the actual cost of care. However, analysis of charges as a proxy for cost has proven to be an acceptable approach in recent orthopaedic publications.5,8,22,23,41 As with any retrospective database study, inconsistent coding can lead to misclassification of patient characteristics, procedures, comorbidities, and other clinical endpoints in the database. Additionally, NASS lacks detailed clinical information, such as the severity of shoulder pathology, specific surgical indications, and preoperative, perioperative, and/or postoperative complications, all of which can influence utilization rates and outcomes. These factors likely affect both access to surgery and its outcomes, limiting our ability to fully capture and understand the drivers of disparities.

The database also does not capture information on patient preferences or health literacy, which may play a role in decision-making regarding shoulder arthroscopy. Furthermore, the study's cross-sectional nature prevents us from analyzing trends over time or establishing causal relationships between the observed disparities and potential contributing factors.

Last, although the NASS database is extensive, it may not capture all shoulder arthroscopy procedures performed in the United States, particularly those done in nonparticipating facilities or in inpatient settings. This could potentially affect the generalizability of our findings to the entire US population.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant racial and ethnic disparities in the utilization of shoulder arthroscopy across the United States, with lower rates observed among Black, Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander patients compared with White patients. These disparities were most pronounced in the Northeast and South, indicating that utilization varies by region. Additionally, differences in total charges for the procedure further exacerbate these disparities, with non-White patients facing higher costs on average.

These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted efforts to enhance access to necessary arthroscopic procedures and ensure equitable sports medicine care for all patients. Future research should focus on characterizing the long-term outcomes associated with these disparities and identifying their root causes. This will be essential for developing effective strategies aimed at reducing inequality in shoulder arthroscopy care. As the field of sports medicine continues to advance, it is imperative that these advancements are accessible to all patient populations, ensuring that no group is left behind in receiving optimal care. Addressing these disparities will not only improve individual patient outcomes but also contribute to a more equitable healthcare system overall.
