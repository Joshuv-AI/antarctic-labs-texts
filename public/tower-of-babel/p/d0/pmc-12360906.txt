Association of Serum Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor-A With Pubertal Stage by Sex and Its Relationship With Precocious Puberty in Girls
Authors: Hyo-Kyoung Nam, Young Jun Rhie, Kee-Hyoung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A) is essential for normal growth plate morphogenesis, including blood vessel invasion and cartilage remodeling. We assessed serum VEGF-A levels according to pubertal stage by sex and investigated their relationship with precocious puberty.MethodsOur study included 180 children aged 6–15 years (153 children with normal puberty [92 boys and 61 girls] and 27 girls with precocious puberty). Clinical and biochemical characteristics according to prepubertal stages were compared in boys and girls, respectively. Serum VEGF-A and leptin levels were performed across Tanner stages.ResultsMean VEGF-A levels were similar among boys and girls with normal puberty (433.7 ± 326.1 pg/mL vs. 483.9 ± 390.5 pg/mL). There was no significant difference in the VEGF-A levels between prepubertal and pubertal boys. VEGF-A levels were significantly higher in pubertal girls than in prepubertal girls (595.9 ± 405.4 pg/mL vs. 264.2 ± 138.0 pg/mL, P < 0.001) and were positively correlated with bone age advancement, Tanner stage, and estradiol level. There was no association between the VEGF-A levels and the body mass index standard deviation scores in either sex. VEGF-A levels were significantly higher in girls with precocious puberty than in prepubertal girls of the same age.ConclusionOur study showed sex-related differences in VEGF-A levels according to the pubertal stage. Therefore, VEGF-A may be involved in the progression of precocious puberty.Keywords: Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor-A; Body Mass Index; Puberty, Precocious; Growth Plate; Girls; Boys

INTRODUCTION

Girls are now starting puberty at a younger age, and there is evidence that the increasing prevalence of childhood obesity over the same period is a major factor associated with this.1 Several studies have demonstrated that obesity leads to early puberty, particularly in girls,2,3 but the relationship between obesity and the timing of pubertal onset remains controversial. Several hypotheses involving factors such as insulin resistance, sex hormone-binding globulin, estrogen, and leptin have been proposed to elucidate the mechanisms by which obesity affects pubertal progression.4

Adipocyte differentiation is linked to angiogenesis. Fat tissue is the source of many cytokines, such as interleukin-6, which is responsible for the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-A. Thus, VEGF accounts for most angiogenic activity in adipose tissue.5,6 VEGF is present in six different protein forms (A–F) and is involved in angiogenesis, chondrocyte differentiation, and survival. Although a previous study showed no correlation between VEGF-A levels and obesity,7 several studies have demonstrated that higher serum VEGF-A levels are associated with a higher body mass index (BMI).8 Few studies have investigated the association of VEGF-A levels with obesity and puberty in children.

As obesity affects the onset of early puberty differently in boys and girls, we hypothesized that VEGF-A may be involved in this process. Therefore, we aimed to assess serum VEGF-A levels according to the pubertal stage by sex and to investigate their relationship with precocious puberty.

METHODS

Subjects

A total of 180 children (153 children with normal puberty [92 boys and 61 girls] and 27 girls with precocious puberty) aged 6–15 years who visited the Pediatric Endocrinology Clinic of the Korea University Hospital were enrolled in this study. Children with normal puberty who visited the hospital for a health checkup and had no symptoms of the disease were recruited as volunteers. Central precocious puberty (CPP) was diagnosed based on the appearance of breast budding before the age of 8 years and bone age advancement, accompanied by a peak luteinizing hormone (LH) level ≥ 5 IU/L.9,10,11 Children showing signs of Tanner stage I were included in the prepuberty group, while children in Tanner stages II–V, appropriate with their chronological age, comprised the puberty group. Children under the age of 9 years who arrived at the hospital for the evaluation of early pubertal signs and whose peak LH level was < 5 IU/L were considered to have normal puberty.

Methods

Data on age, sex, height, weight, BMI (kg/m2), bone age, and laboratory profiles of each child were collected. Height, weight, and BMI were expressed as standard deviation scores (SDSs) for Korean children of the same chronological age and sex. Tanner stages were classified as stage I, II–III, and IV–V and were used to assess the sexual maturation rate.

Serum basal LH and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels were measured using an immunofluorimetric assay kit (Diasource Immunoassay, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium). Serum estradiol levels were measured using a radioimmunoassay kit (Siemens, Frimley, Camberley, UK). Testosterone levels were measured using a radioimmunoassay kit (Cisbio Bioassays, Codolet, France).

For the VEGF-A assay, blood samples were centrifuged (2,330 × g for 7 minutes) within 30 minutes, and serum was stored at –70°C until analysis. Serum VEGF-A levels were measured using a PromoKine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were less than 6.2% and 4.3%, respectively, and the lower limit of detection was 7.9 pg/mL. Serum leptin levels were measured using a PromoKine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (PromoCell). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were less than 10.0% and 12.0%, respectively, and the lower limit of detection was 2.0 pg/mL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). The independent t-test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare the data between the two groups. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and repeated-measures ANOVA were performed to compare the clinical and biochemical parameters among the groups. The χ2 test was used to assess the frequency of the Tanner stage. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the relationship between the VEGF levels and other factors. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Hospital (IRB number: ED11227). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from the children and their parents.

RESULTS

Clinical and biochemical characteristics of the children in the study group

Among the 180 children aged 6–15 years enrolled in this study, 153 had normal puberty (92 boys and 61 girls) and 27 girls had CPP. The mean chronological and bone ages were 11.3 ± 2.1 and 11.3 ± 2.7 years, and 9.5 ± 2.3 and 9.8 ± 1.7 years in boys and girls, respectively. Their mean height SDS, weight SDS, and BMI SDS were 1.2 ± 1.3, 2.3 ± 1.9, 1.2 ± 1.2, and 0.9 ± 1.2, 1.5 ± 1.8, 0.8 ± 1.4 in boys and girls, respectively (Table 1).

Clinical and biochemical characteristics of boys and girls in prepuberty and puberty stages

The mean testosterone, LH, and FSH levels of the prepubertal boys were lower (0.4 ± 0.6 ng/mL vs. 3.6 ± 3.0 ng/mL, P < 0.001; 0.7 ± 0.4 mIU/mL vs. 2.9 ± 1.3 mIU/mL, P < 0.001; and 2.2 ± 2.1 mIU/mL vs. 4.0 ± 2.0 mIU/mL, P = 0.029, respectively) than those of the pubertal boys. However, there was a similarity in the VEGF-A and leptin levels between these two groups (433.7 ± 326.1 pg/mL vs. 483.9 ± 390.5 pg/mL, P = 0.505, and 11.7 ± 7.5 ng/mL vs. 9.6 ± 6.9 ng/mL, P = 0.466, respectively) (Table 2).

In girls, the chronological and bone ages of the prepubertal girls were lower (8.5 ± 1.4 years vs. 11.5 ± 2.5 years, P < 0.001 and 8.9 ± 1.3 years vs. 11.6 ± 2.2 years, P < 0.001, respectively) than those of the pubertal girls. Bone age advancement was lesser in the prepubertal girls (2.2 ± 11.1 months vs. 12.7 ± 17.9 months, P = 0.001) than that of the pubertal girls. The height SDS, weight SDS, and BMI SDS of the prepubertal girls were similar to those of the pubertal girls (Table 2).

The mean estradiol, LH, and FSH levels of the prepubertal girls were lower (3.1 ± 1.9 pg/mL vs. 13.7 ± 12.3 pg/mL, P < 0.001; 0.5 ± 0.5 mIU/mL vs. 2.3 ± 2.1 mIU/mL, P < 0.001; and 2.1 ± 1.1 mIU/mL vs. 3.8 ± 2.9 mIU/mL, P = 0.007, respectively) than those of the pubertal girls. The VEGF-A level of the prepubertal girls was lower than those of the pubertal girls (264.2 ± 138.0 pg/mL vs. 595.9 ± 405.4 pg/mL, P < 0.001), while the leptin level was similar in these two groups (9.1 ± 6.1 ng/mL vs. 12.8 ± 7.0 ng/mL, P = 0.088, respectively) (Table 2).

In girls with precocious puberty, the VEGF-A level was significantly higher than that of prepubertal girls (264.2 ± 138.0 pg/mL vs. 484.0 ± 322.6 pg/mL, P < 0.001), despite the same chronological age using Tukey’s multiple comparison test using one-way ANOVA.

Linear regression analysis of factors associated with VEGF-A levels in boys and girls

The VEGF-A level was significantly increased corresponding to the Tanner stage in the girls (264.2 ± 138.0 pg/mL in stage I, 534.8 ± 347.6 pg/mL in stage II–III, and 561.6 ± 434.4 pg/mL in stage IV–V, P < 0.001); however, this was not the case among the boys (433.7 ± 326.1 pg/mL in stage I, 507.3 ± 403.0 pg/mL in stage II–III, and 407.6 ± 353.3 pg/mL in stage IV–V, P = 0.582). The levels of leptin also increased with the Tanner stage in girls (9.1 ± 6.1 ng/mL in stage I, 9.4 ± 4.7 ng/mL in stage II–III, and 11.8 ± 8.1 ng/mL in stage IV–V), whereas they decreased in late pubertal boys (11.8 ± 7.5 ng/mL in stage I, 12.2 ± 6.6 ng/mL in stage II–III, and 4.3 ± 4.1 ng/mL in stage IV–V). Univariate analysis using simple linear regression was performed to determine the correlation between the VEGF-A levels and other factors. VEGF-A levels were positively correlated with bone age advancement (r = 0.391, P = 0.002), Tanner stage (r = 0.326, P = 0.002), and estradiol levels (r = 0.299, P = 0.021) but not with BMI SDS in either sex (Table 3). Multivariate analysis using simple linear regression adjusted for BMI, SDS, and Tanner stage showed that VEGF-A levels were positively correlated with female sex (r = −2.894, P = 0.005) and bone age advancement (r = 3.188, P = 0.002).

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study evaluated the differences in pubertal progression between boys and girls by examining serum VEGF-A levels in children. Our findings showed that the mean VEGF-A levels in boys and girls were similar. Serum VEGF-A levels were higher in pubertal than in prepubertal girls, whereas they were similar in prepubertal and pubertal boys. Girls with CPP had higher serum VEGF-A levels than prepubertal girls of the same age. Serum VEGF-A levels were positively correlated with female sex and bone age advancement, irrespective of the BMI SDS.

Precocious puberty is approximately ten times more common in girls than in boys.12 There are also sex-related differences in pubertal timing and proportion of adipose tissue. Excess adiposity during childhood may advance puberty in girls and delay puberty in boys.13 Fatness is reportedly related to early sexual development rather than to early puberty, which causes an increase in body fat.14 However, the underlying mechanism remains unknown.

Obesity is related to the expansion of adipose tissue, and VEGF is associated with the pathogenesis of obesity. Serum VEGF-A levels have been shown to be higher in both adults and murine obesity models.15 After body weight reduction, such as after a multidisciplinary approach or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, VEGF levels decrease.15,16 In a mouse study, Nishimura et al.17 observed that adipocyte differentiation is related to angiogenesis, which requires VEGF. Direct measurement of fat mass is an ideal manner to assess its influence on pubertal onset; however, this is challenging to perform, and BMI is thus considered a suitable surrogate for the measurement of body fat.18 Our findings demonstrated no relationship between BMI SDS and VEGF-A levels in either sex.

VEGF-A is expressed on the growth plate and is believed to be important in the regulation of longitudinal bone growth.19 An increase in pubertal bone mineral density is associated with BMI and VEGF concentrations in boys.20 Emons et al.21 have reported that VEGF was expressed in pubertal growth plates and that VEGF protein levels increased with pubertal progression in female rats and humans. VEGF expression is also upregulated by estrogen, which is increased in adipose tissue and is essential for the progression of puberty and the regulation of growth plate maturation.

In our study, we investigated the factors associated with sex-related differences in pubertal progression. We found a significant difference in the change in VEGF-A levels according to the Tanner stage between boys and girls. VEGF-A levels in girls increased with pubertal progression and were higher in girls with precocious puberty than in prepubertal girls of the same chronological age. In girls, serum VEGF-A levels correlated with estradiol levels and bone age advancement, irrespective of BMI SDS.

Leptin, initially known as an appetite and energy regulator, has been suggested to be involved in pubertal progression. Administration of leptin completely reversed pubertal delay in leptin-deficient mice, whereas feeding only partially reversed this delay.22 However, the role of leptin in secreting gonadotropin-releasing hormone remains debatable.23,24 It has been proposed that leptin plays a permissive role rather than an initiator of pubertal progression. Human studies have shown that leptin levels in boys increase transiently and then decrease to one-third of the levels observed in girls with delayed puberty.17 In this study, we found that leptin levels increased according to the Tanner stage in girls (from 9.1 ± 6.1 ng/mL in stage I to 11.8 ± 8.1 ng/mL in stage IV–V), whereas it fell in late puberty stages in boys (from 11.8 ± 7.5 ng/mL in stage I to 4.3 ± 4.1 ng/mL in stage IV–V). However, there were no significant changes in leptin levels corresponding to the Tanner stage in either sex. Although previous studies have demonstrated the potentiating action of leptin on VEGF-A expression,25,26 our study did not reflect this finding.

Similar to our study, a few other studies have examined VEGF-A levels in children. However, we further analyzed serum VEGF-A levels according to the Tanner stage and their association with precocious puberty in girls. Nevertheless, our study has some limitations. As this was a cross-sectional study, it was difficult to determine whether the elevation in VEGF-A levels was a consequence of early puberty or had a permissive effect. Nonetheless, our study demonstrated that VEGF-A levels differed depending on sex and that higher VEGF-A levels were associated with CPP in girls. However, further studies are required to investigate the effects of VEGF-A on pubertal bone maturation.

In conclusion, we showed that VEGF-A levels are associated with sex. Furthermore, this study is the first to detect a link between serum VEGF-A levels and bone maturation in children. The role of VEGF-A in puberty progression remains unclear, and further large-scale longitudinal studies involving boys with CPP are required.
