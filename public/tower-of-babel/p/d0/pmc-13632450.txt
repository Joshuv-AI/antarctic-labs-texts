Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis revealed the potential role of hesperetin in wheat resistance to leaf rust
Authors: Yaqiong Zhao, Weiwei Gong, Ruimin Wang, Dingming Zhang, Fuxin He, Yingpeng Wu, Minjie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNatural plant metabolites, such as flavonoids and lignin, exhibited safe and environmentally friendly roles in defending against pathogen attack. However, their functional roles in the interaction between wheat and Puccinia triticina (Pt) remained poorly characterized.ResultsIn this study, we integrated transcriptomic and quasi-targeted metabolomic analyses to systematically characterize differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and metabolites associated with the compatible and incompatible interactions between wheat and Pt. In the wheat-Pt interaction system, a total of 1,242 metabolites were identified, predominantly classified into amino acids and derivatives, flavonoids, and related compound classes. In the flavonoid and lignin biosynthesis-related pathways—namely, Flavonoid biosynthesis and Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis—significant differences were observed in both the accumulation levels of key metabolites and the expression of genes associated with their biosynthetic pathways. Notably, in the incompatible interaction, the expression of TaCAD, a gene implicated in syringyl lignin (S-lignin) biosynthesis, was significantly up-regulated. Gene regulatory network (GRN) analysis further suggested that transcription factors (TFs) including ERF, LBD, BBR-BPC, and C2H2 may play critical regulatory roles in mediating wheat resistance to leaf rust. Moreover, the key flavonoid hesperetin was markedly enriched in the incompatible combination (TcLr24_120 h vs. TcLr24_0 h). Exogenous application of hesperetin significantly inhibited urediniospores germination in vitro. More importantly, it significantly reduced disease severity of wheat leaf rust in vivo.ConclusionsThese findings indicated that hesperetin may serve as a potential defense compound in wheat during the early stages of leaf rust infection, providing crucial insights for developing novel natural products and a theoretical foundation for genetic improvement in disease-resistant wheat breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13149-0.Keywords: Puccinia triticina, Metabolites, Transcriptome, Hesperetin, Resistance

Background

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the world’s major staple crops. Wheat leaf rust, caused by Puccinia triticina (Pt), is an obligate, macrocyclic fungus that primarily infects wheat leaves, leading to disease development and epidemics [1, 2]. Wheat leaf rust is widespread in wheat-growing regions globally and is one of the most important diseases affecting the safe and stable production of wheat, with severe infections causing yield losses exceeding 50% [3, 4]. Currently, the management of wheat leaf rust primarily relies on the development of resistant cultivars and the application of chemical fungicides. However, the continuous evolution and frequent emergence of new physiological races of Pt often results in the breakdown of genetic resistance in wheat varieties [5]. At the same time, chemical control is constrained by several challenges, including a narrow spectrum of effective fungicides, the development of pathogen resistance, and the widespread misuse of pesticides, all of which undermined its long-term sustainability [6]. In this context, the development of efficient, safe and environmentally friendly natural products for disease management holds significant promise for green plant protection [7]. Such alternatives not only reduce dependence on synthetic chemicals but also helps mitigate the risk of fungicide resistance in pathogen populations.

Plant metabolites, comprising primary and secondary metabolites, represent an extremely diverse group of compounds. Over 200,000 plant metabolites have been identified to date, highlighting their vast chemical variety [8]. These metabolites play crucial roles in enabling plants to adapt to adverse environmental conditions [9] and in defending against plant disease [10]. Primary metabolites are produced during fundamental plant physiological processes such as respiration, photosynthesis, and cell growth and reproduction, and include compounds like sugars and amino acids. In contrast, secondary metabolites are derived from the transformation of primary metabolites and encompass diverse chemical classes such as terpenoids, phenolics, nitrogen-containing compounds, flavonoids, and lignins [11–14]. Flavonoids are a major class of phenolic secondary metabolites that play crucial roles in plant defense responses against pathogens [15–18]. Their biosynthesis is dependent on the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, which provides key precursor molecules. Accumulation of flavonoids contributed to cell wall thickening and enhanced both physical and chemical resistance in plants against invading pathogen [19].In addition, flavonoids can directly inhibit fungal spore germination and hyphal growth [16, 20]. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, naringenin has been found to activate NPR1 through the MAPK and salicylic acid (SA) pathways, thereby enhancing resistance against Pseudomonas syringae [21]. Studies have shown that during infection by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), incompatible wheat cultivars accumulated multiple flavonoids, such as luteolin, apigenin, and naringenin. Among them, luteolin can significantly inhibit the spore germination and hyphal infection of Pst [22]. Furthermore, sissotrin has been shown to exert significant inhibitory effects against Magnaporthe oryzae and Rhizoctonia solani [23]. Isoflavonoids also strongly inhibited the mycelial growth of Fusarium oxysporum [24] and Colletotrichum lupini [25]. Hesperetin, a naturally occurring dihydroflavonoid, is widely distributed in various plant species. Related research has demonstrated that hesperetin can inhibit mycelial growth in F. oxysporum [26]. Collectively, these findings highlighted the promising potential of flavonoids for application in plant disease management.

A comprehensive understanding of plant—pathogen interactions can be achieved by analyzing and effectively integrating the multidimensional qualitative and quantitative relationships between the host and its associated microbial components [27]. Cao et al. employed phenotypic characterization, transcriptome sequencing and metabolomic profiling to identify key genes and metabolics in maize inbred lines ZC17 and CH72 following infection with F. proliferatum [28]. They found that the phenylalanine metabolic pathway played a critical role in disease resistance. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of the maize near-isogenic lines (NILs) H95 and H95: Rp1-D following Puccinia sorghi infection revealed significant up-regulation of genes and metabolites involved in phenylpropanoid, flavonoid and terpenoid biosynthesis pathways [29]. Pushpa et al. compared the metabolomes of resistant and susceptible potato cultivars, demonstrating that hydroxycinnamic acid amides (HCAAs) were strongly induced and may be served as potential biomarkers for resistance to late blight [30]. Integration of metabolomic and transcriptomic data from wheat infected with the crown rot revealed pronounced changes in flavonoids and phenolic acid compounds, and identified two antifungal compounds—benzoxazolin-2-one (BOA) and 6-methoxy-benzoxazolin-2-one (MBOA)—as potent inhibitors of fungal growth [31]. Liu et al. characterized the changes in specific metabolites and genes during the resistance of wheat cultivar Shuiyuan 11 to Pst pathotype CYR23, and identified homogentisic acid as a potential natural antifungal compound [32]. Jiang et al. used transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to elucidate the beneficial role of melatonin in wheat resistance to Pst and functionally validated TaASMT3 as a key gene in melatonin biosynthesis through virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) [33]. Collectively, multi-omics integration approaches have emerged as powerful strategies for deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying plant disease resistance and pathogen virulence, deepening our understanding of plant—pathogen interactions and providing a theoretical and technical foundation for developing novel disease resistance strategies.

In this study, integrated transcriptomic and quasi-targeted metabolomic approaches were utilized to systematically analyze DEGs and metabolites in the compatible and incompatible interactions between the wheat NILs Thatcher (Tc) and TcLr24 (Thatcher: Agent, carrying Lr24 gene) and Pt race THTT. By focusing on the analysis of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway and integrating GRN analysis, this study elucidated the interactions between genes involved in key metabolite biosynthesis and potential TFs. Aiming to identify crucial genes and metabolites associated with disease resistance, uncover natural products with potential for disease control, and provide a theoretical foundation for research on wheat resistance mechanisms and breeding programs.

Methods

Plant materials and pathogen inoculation assay

To avoid the influence of varying genetic backgrounds on the experiment, this study utilized two wheat NILs, Tc and TcLr24, as experimental materials. These NILs were pre-soaked for germination and then sown in pots approximately 7 cm in diameter, containing around 15 seeds each. Post-sowing, these pots were placed in a growth chamber under controlled conditions—maintaining a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle at a temperature of 18 ± 2℃. Seedlings with fully expanded first leaves were inoculated with a spore suspension prepared by mixing 3 mg of Pt urediniospores (physiological race THTT) with 10 mL of a 0.1% Tween-80 solution. The suspension was uniformly sprayed onto the seedling leaves. Following inoculation, the seedlings were kept in darkness at 18 ± 2℃ with high humidity for 24 h to facilitate infection, after which they were returned to the growth chamber. At predetermined intervals post-inoculation, three seedlings from each of the Tc and TcLr24 lines were randomly selected for sampling as part of one biological replicate, with this process repeated three times per treatment condition. Non-inoculated plants served as controls. For metabolomic analysis, samples were collected at 0, 24, and 120 h post-inoculation (hpi); for transcriptomic analysis, samples were taken at 0, 12, 24, 48, and 120 hpi. All collected samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃ until further processing. Sequencing was subsequently carried out by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the 30 samples collected in Sect. 2.1 for transcriptome sequencing. First-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using oligo (dT) primers and the EZ-First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Biological Industries, Israel). The integrity of the extracted RNA was assessed using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit to ensure all samples met the required RNA integrity number (RIN) threshold for library construction. Libraries were subsequently sequenced using a high-throughput sequencing platform. Raw image data were processed using the CASAVA base-calling pipeline to generate sequencing reads, which were output in FASTQ format for downstream bioinformatics analysis.

Transcriptome data analysis

To ensure data quality and reliability for subsequent analysis, raw sequencing data were filtered to obtain high-quality clean data. The filtering criteria included: removing reads containing adapter sequences, discarding reads with more than 50% unknown bases (represented by N), and filtering low-quality reads (i.e., reads where bases with Qphred score ≤ 20 account for more than 50% of the entire read length). Following data filtering, the quality of the resulting clean data was further assessed by evaluating key metrics, including Q20 and Q30 scores, as well as GC content. Subsequently, the high-quality clean reads were aligned to the wheat reference genome (Triticum_aestivum IWGSC_CS_RefSeq_v2.1, GenBank accession: GCF_018294505.1; genome and annotation files available at: https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/018/294/505/GCF_018294505.1_IWGSC_CS_RefSeq_v2.1/). Gene expression levels were quantified using the featureCounts tool from the Subread software package [34] and were reported as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). FPKM represented the number of sequencing fragments mapped to a gene per kilobase of transcript length, normalized per million mapped reads, and served as a standardized metric for assessing gene expression abundance. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (version 1.20.0) [35] to identify gene expression changes between different treatment groups. DEGs were defined as those with a statistically significant adjusted P-value < 0.05 and log2|(fold change)|≥1. All analyses were performed following established bio-informatics best practices to ensure the accuracy, reliability and reproducibility of the results.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Weigh 100 mg of plant tissue sample previously ground in liquid nitrogen and transfer it into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. 500 µL of 80% (v/v) methanol aqueous solution was added, followed by vigorous vortex mixing and a 5 min incubation on ice. The sample was then centrifuge at 15,000 × g and 4℃ for 20 min. An aliquot of the supernatant was diluted with mass spectrometry-grade water to achieve a final methanol concentration of 53%. The diluted sample was then centrifuged at 15,000 ×g and 4℃ for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the final extract for analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using an ExionLC™ AD ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (SCIEX) coupled with a QTRAP® 6500 + triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX). Samples were injected into an Xselect HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.5 μm). The mobile phases comprised solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), with a flow rate set at 0.4 mL/min. A 20-minute linear gradient elution program was utilized [36], with the following gradient profile: hold at 2% B for 2 min; ramp from 2% to 100% B over 15.0 min; maintain at 100% B until 17.0 min; decrease from 100% to 2% B by 17.1 min; and hold at 2% B until 20 min.

Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The ion source parameters for positive mode were optimized as follows: Curtain Gas at 35 psi, Collision Gas at “Medium”, IonSpray Voltage at + 5000 V, source temperature at 550 ℃, and Ion Source Gas 1 and Gas 2 both set to 60. For negative mode, the parameters were identical except for the IonSpray Voltage, which was set to − 4500 V. All data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to ensure high sensitivity and selectivity.

Qualitative analysis of the experimental samples was conducted using the MRM mode, based on the Novogene in-house database (novoDB). Mass spectrometry raw data were processed using SCIEX OS software (version1.4) for chromatographic peak integration and correction, ensuring accurate peak identification. The peak area values for each metabolite were subsequently extracted and used for further qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Functional annotation and classification of the identified metabolites were performed by integrating data from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), Human Metabilome Database (HMDB) (https://hmdb.ca/metabolites), and LIPIDMAPS (http://www.lipidmaps.org/) databases. This included assignment to metabolic pathways, chemical taxonomy and biological function, providing a foundation for downstream pathway analysis and the interpretation of biological significance. For multivariate statistical analysis, raw data were normalized and transformed using the metabolomics data processing pipeline metaX [37]. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were then applied to assess sample clustering trends and derive variable importance in projection (VIP) scores for each metabolite. In univariate analysis, a two-tailed t-test was applied to assess the statistical significance (P-value) of each metabolite between the two groups, and the fold change (FC) was calculated. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using the following default criteria: VIP > 1, P < 0.05 and Log2|(FC)|≥1.

Gene regulatory network analysis

Identified wheat TFs were annotated using the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB). Potential target genes were predicted via the Find Individual Motif Occurrences (FIMO) tools, leveraging known conserved DNA-binding motifs for each TF. Regulatory associations were inferred by integrating TF-target gene co-expression patterns with predictions of cis-regulatory elements in the promoters of key genes involved in metabolite biosynthesis. Finally, transcription factor-target gene interactions were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.0) to construct GRNs, thereby elucidating potential regulatory mechanisms underlying wheat’s response to leaf rust disease.

Antifungal activity assay of Hesperetin against Pt

Hesperetin powder (CAS: 520-33-2) was dissolved in absolute ethanol to prepare a 50 mmol/L stock solution, which was stored at room temperature in the dark for subsequent use. Determination of spore germination rate: the stock solution was serially diluted with 0.05% water agar solution to obtain treatment solutions with final concentrations of 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25 and 0.5 mmol/L. Urediniospores of Pt were evenly dispersed onto the surface of each solution and incubated in the dark at 20 ± 2℃ for approximately 12 h. Spore germination rates were determined and calculated according to the method described by Zhao et al. [38].

The 50 mmol/L hesperetin stock solution was serially diluted with ddH₂O to obtain working concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 mmol/L. Each dilution was dispensed into 10 mL centrifuge tubes and stored for subsequent use. A spore suspension (1.0 mg/mL) in fluorinated solution was inoculated onto leaves of the wheat cultivar Mingxian 169. Once distinct chlorotic lesions developed (approximately 4 days post-inoculation), leaf segments 6–8 cm in length were excised and immersed in the corresponding hesperetin solutions for 30 min. Sterile water treatment served as the control and five leaf segments were used per treatment. Following treatment, the leaf segments were removed, gently air-dried, and transferred to 0.5% water agar plates supplemented with 0.06 mg/mL 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA), ensuring the adaxial side remained uppermost. The plates were incubated in a controlled growth chamber (Sect. 2.1). Once disease symptoms had fully developed in the control group, digital images were captured for each treatment. The pixel areas of disease lesions (pustules) and the total leaf segment area were quantified using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019. Lesion coverage (%) was calculated as (lesion pixel area / total leaf segment pixel area)×100% and used to assess the inhibitory effect of hesperetin on disease progression. Wheat plants were treated with 10 mL hesperetin solution at a concentration of 1 mmol/L. Samples were collected at 3, 6, 9, 12 and 24 h to analyze the relative expression level of Pathogenesis-related (PR) genes. The control group was treated with water, with three biological replicates for each treatment. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and their sequences were listed in Table S1.

Results

Metabolomics data analysis

The wheat NILs Tc and TcLr24 were inoculated with the Pt (physiological race THTT) to establish compatible and incompatible interactions, respectively. Following inoculation, TcLr24 displayed a clear hypersensitive response (HR), characterized by typical necrotic lesions, confirming an incompatible interaction (Fig. 1A). To elucidate the underlying metabolic responses, leaf samples were collected from both interaction types at 0, 24 and 120 hpi for metabolomic analysis. Metabolite identification was conducted using Novogene’s in-house database, leading to the annotation of 1,242 metabolites. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess sample reproducibility. The three biological replicates of each treatment group clustered closely on PC1 (22.5%) and PC2 (16.2%), and mixed quality control (QC) samples presented good experimental repeatability. These results demonstrated high reproducibility and reliability of the data (Fig. 1-C). Additionally, PCA revealed obvious distribution differences between Tc and TcLr24 samples at the same time point, suggesting varied metabolic levels in different wheat varieties after leaf rust infection.

Metabolite profiling of compatible and incompatible interactions in wheat following Pt infection. Note: (A) Phenotypic responses of wheat to Pt infection. TcLr24 displayed a typical HR post-inoculation, characteristic of an incompatible interaction. (B) Classification of DAMs identified in compatible and incompatible interactions. (C) PCA of samples from different treatments, with distinct colors and symbols representing each treatment group

Metabolite classification and annotation were presented in Fig. 1B. The identified metabolites were predominantly assigned to the following categories: amino acids and derivatives, flavonoids, carbohydrates and its derivatives, nucleotide and its derivatives. DAMs were further identified between groups based on the predefined selection criteria (Table S2). In the TcLr24_24 h vs. TcLr24_0 h, 214 DAMs were identified, with 186 up-regulated and 28 down-regulated. In the TcLr24_120 h vs. TcLr24_0 h, 223 DAMs were detected, comprising 195 up-regulated and 28 down-regulated metabolites. In the Tc_24 h vs. Tc_0 h, 260 DAMs were identified (149 up-regulated, 111 down-regulated), and in the Tc_120 h vs. Tc_0 h, 303 DAMs were found (158 up-regulated, 145 down-regulated). These results indicated that both wheat genotypes underwent significant metabolic reprogramming following inoculation with the Pt. Moreover, the number of DAMs increased progressively over time, reflecting a dynamic and sustained metabolic response.

Transcriptome data analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying wheat resistance to leaf rust, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on the NILs Tc and TcLr24 at 0, 12, 24, 48 and 120 hpi with Pt. A total of 202.04 Gb of raw data was generated, yielding 198.88 Gb of clean data after quality control. The sequencing data showed high quality, with Q30 > 91.6%, Q20 > 96.67% and GC content ranging from 53.15% to 57.88% (Table S3). A correlation heat map (Fig. S1) was constructed based on transcriptome data of all samples. High correlation coefficients were observed among biological replicates at the same treatment and time point, indicating good intra-group reproducibility. By contrast, low correlation existed between samples of the same treatment at different time points, revealing distinct transcriptional levels across time periods.

Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome Triticum_aestivum (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/018/294/505/GCF_018294505.1_IWGSC_CS_RefSeq_v2.1/), leading to the identification of 161,142 genes, including 5,680 newly predicted genes (Table S4). Functional annotation was performed using BLAST against public databases, resulting in 59,800, 12,579, 84,745, 94,581 and 41,097 genes being annotated in the Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, Swiss-Prot, Protein families database (Pfam), and Protein-Protein interaction networks analysis (PPI) databases, respectively (Table S5). DEGs were identified by comparing each time point (12, 24, 48 and 120 hpi) to its respective 0 hpi control (TcLr24_0 hpi for TcLr24 and Tc_0 hpi for Tc) using the thresholds |log2(FC)| ≥1 and padj ≤ 0.05. This analysis revealed 55,097 DEGs (Fig. 2) across the compatible and incompatible interactions, spanning multiple time points and genotypes. These results provided a comprehensive transcriptomic resource for dissecting the Lr24-mediated defense response.

Enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO functional annotation of DEGs from the compatible and incompatible interactions was conducted (Fig. 3A). The analysis revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in multiple biological process and molecular function categories related to defense responses, including membrane protein complex, antioxidant activity, peroxidase activity, oxidoreductase activity acting on peroxide as acceptor, response to oxidative stress. These results suggested that the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism played a crucial role in the Lr24-mediated defense response.

Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on TcLr24_12 h vs. Tc_12 h. A total of 10,403 DEGs were identified, comprising 3,109 up-regulated and 7,294 down-regulated. These DEGs were significantly enriched in key pathways including ribosome, plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction and biosynthesis of amino acids (Fig. 3B). Notably, key pathways associated with plant disease resistance—including the MAPK signaling pathway-plant, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction and plant-pathogen interaction—were significantly enriched in DEGs. To visualize the expression patterns of these DEGs, clustered heatmaps were generated for the genes within these pathways (Fig. 3C), revealing wheat genotype-specific transcriptional regulatory networks that were activated as early as the initial stages of infection.

Integrated analysis of metabolomics and transcriptomics

Integrating metabolomic and transcriptomic data to construct a multi-omics regulatory network enabled a more comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of the molecular basis of wheat resistance to Pt. Based on the key pathways—Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ath00940) and Flavonoid biosynthesis (ath00941)—commonly enriched by both metabolites and DEGs, this study illustrated the changes in abundance of key metabolites and the expression dynamics of their corresponding enzyme-encoding genes within these pathways (Figs. 4 and 5). The phenylpropanoid pathway (PPP) was a central plant secondary metabolic pathway that supplied key precursors for the biosynthesis of defense-related compounds, including lignin and flavonoids. The pathway initiated with L-phenylalanine, a core precursor that was up-regulated in both compatible and incompatible interactions at 24 hpi and 120 hpi (Fig. 4), indicating broad activation of this metabolic node in response to pathogen infection. In the PPP, key enzyme-encoding genes—including PAL, C4H, CCoAOMT-1, and CAD—were consistently up-regulated across multiple infection time points in TcLr24, whereas they were down-regulated or showed no significant change in the compatible combination. This contrasting expression pattern revealed a distinct transcriptional activation signature associated with Lr24-mediated resistance. The 4CL was up-regulated in both compatible and incompatible wheat genotypes, with higher expression in the incompatible combination, suggesting a potential role in the resistance response. Moreover, HCT and PER were consistently up-regulated in both interactions, indicating their involvement in basal defense responses. Caffeic aldehyde levels increased in both compatible and incompatible wheat genotypes at 24 hpi. However, they declined specifically in the compatible combination, suggesting that late-phase activation of this metabolic pathway was suppressed under compatible conditions.

In the Flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (ath00941) (Fig. 5), naringenin and hesperetin accumulated in the incompatible combination at 120 hpi, but their levels were reduced at both 24 hpi and 120 hpi in the compatible genotype. Luteolin accumulated in the incompatible wheat genotype at 24 hpi, but its levels were reduced at both 24 and 120 hpi in the compatible genotype. The key biosynthetic enzyme genes CHI and CYP were up-regulated in both compatible and incompatible wheat genotypes at 12 hpi, with higher expression observed in the incompatible combination. These results indicated that Lr24-mediated resistance to Pt involved systemic activation of the phenylpropanoid and associated flavonoid pathways, as evidenced by the accumulation of key metabolites and coordinated up-regulation of relative genes, underscoring the critical role of secondary metabolic reprogramming in wheat defense.

GRN analysis of wheat in response to Pt infection

To elucidate the resistance mechanism of the incompatible wheat variety TcLr24 against Pt, this study employed FIMO software to predict TFs that regulated the relative genes within the Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ath00940) and Flavonoid biosynthesis (ath00941) pathways, as detailed in Table S6. Furthermore, a corresponding GRN was developed and was presented in Fig. 6. This approach facilitated a deeper understanding of the molecular interactions underlying TcLr24’s defense response.

Note: (A) and (B) illustrated the regulatory networks between metabolite synthesis genes and TFs in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ath00940) and flavonoid biosynthesis (ath00941) pathways, respectively. In these networks, “V”-shaped nodes represented metabolite synthesis genes within the metabolic pathways, while circular nodes denoted predicted TFs. The smaller the shape, the lower the regulatory involvement of TFs

As shown in the Fig. 6, the predicted defense-associated regulatory proteins belonged to multiple TFs families known to function in plant defense responses. Within the Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ath00940) pathway, synthase-related genes were regulated by diverse TFs, including members of the MYB, NAC, bZIP and C2H2 families. Notably, within the GRN, TaPER and TaCAD—genes involved in S-lignin biosynthesis—were predicted to be co-regulated by multiple TFs. Among these, TaCAD (123136821) was predicted to be primarily regulated by TFs including bZIP (Traes_5AL_9BC38814C, Traes_6DL_77ACE1279) and NAC (Traes_5DL_B69423A67, Traes_6AL_0D7866801). Furthermore, TaPER70 (123143227) was up-regulated in the incompatible wheat but down-regulated in the compatible, and was predicted to be primarily regulated by TFs including bZIP (Traes_5BL_A85CEE3C1, Traes_2BL_9528AAD7C) and MYB (Traes_5DS_AACA2EBA3, Traes_6DL_991931CF9). In the TcLr24 vs. Tc, TaCCoAMT-1 was up-regulated under incompatible conditions. GRN analysis predicted that its expression was primarily regulated by the TFs bHLH (Traes_5DL_026913547) and bZIP (Traes_5DL_1950C1FC2, Traes_7DL_68B814464) (Fig. 6A). Similarly, within the Flavonoid biosynthesis (ath00941) pathway, synthase-related genes were regulated by diverse TFs families, including ERF (Traes_7DS_433704E8E1, Traes_6DL_DD82BD107), LBD (Traes_1DS_BB8508CC6, TRAES3BF084400010CFD_g), BBR-BPC (Traes_4DL_AE5093871, Traes_4AS_48979CBE1) and C2H2 (Traes_6DS_2B2F9C290, Traes_2BS_DD07C2EE6) (Fig. 6B). These results underscored the pivotal role of TFs in orchestrating distinct metabolic pathways to reinforce wheat resistance against Pt. Meanwhile, the transcriptional levels of four genes HCT3 (123112079), PER2 (123043698), C4H (123077675), PER66 (123055356) and thirteen regulatory TFs were determined (Fig.S2 and Fig.S3). Transcriptome data showed that the expression patterns of these TFs were basically consistent with those of the four genes. q-PCR verification yielded similar results to transcriptome analysis. In conclusion, GRN analysis can be used to predict target genes and their corresponding TFs to a certain extent.

Hesperetin reduced severity of wheat leaf rust and suppressed Pt urediniospore germination

Flavonoid natural products generally possess antibacterial activity. Metabolomic analysis revealed that hesperetin was upregulated in TaLr24, with an upregulation fold of 2.67 at 120 hpi. Relevant antibacterial studies on this compound remain limited. Accordingly, hesperetin was selected for subsequent antibacterial activity assays in this study. In this study, wheat leaves showing chlorotic flecks following Pt inoculation were immersed in hesperetin solutions at varying concentrations for 30 min, then transferred to water-agar plates for incubation. Untreated control leaves were allowed to develop fully before evaluation. As shown in Fig. 7A and B, the number of uredinia was reduced across all hesperetin treatments. Notably, treatment with 1.0 mmol/L hesperetin markedly reduced urediniospore formation on wheat leaves, resulting in a significantly lower ratio of Pt sporulation area to total leaf area compared to the control (P < 0.05).

To assess the direct effect of hesperetin on Pt development, we evaluated urediniospore germination using water-agar media supplemented with varying concentrations of the hesperetin. As shown in Fig. 7C and D, urediniospore germination rate progressively decreased with increasing hesperetin concentration, with statistically significant differences observed (P < 0.05). At concentrations of 0.25 and 0.5 mmol/L, germination rates were reduced to 2.93% and 2.19%, respectively. These findings demonstrated that exogenous hesperetin not only strongly inhibited spore germination but also significantly attenuated wheat leaf rust disease progression. This highlighted the potential of natural compounds like hesperetin as promising candidates for biocontrol strategies.

As illustrated in Fig. 7E-G, treatment with 1 mmol/L hesperetin induced upregulated expression of PR1, PR2 and PR5 genes in TcLr24 at multiple time points in comparison with the control (Tc). Specifically, the transcript level of PR1 remained relatively stable and low within 3–9 h with no statistical significance, and underwent significant elevation at 12 h and 24 h, presenting around 2.5-fold higher expression than the control (P < 0.05). PR2 expression attained the maximum value at 12 h. Noticeable upregulation of PR5 was detected at 6–9 h and 24 h. Collectively, These results indicate that hesperetin can activate SA-mediated systemic acquired resistance.

Discussion

Wheat-Pt interactions had been widely studied [1, 3]. However, a systematic understanding of the regulatory mechanisms and metabolic pathways governing defense responses in compatible and incompatible interactions remained incomplete. In this study, we performed an integrated transcriptomic and quasi-targeted metabolomic analysis using the wheat NILs Tc and TcLr24 inoculated with the Pt to establish compatible and incompatible interaction systems, respectively. This approach enabled the identification of key genes and metabolites associated with disease resistance. Correlation analysis implicated the flavonoid—hesperetin and its biosynthetic genes in the regulation of wheat resistance to Pt. Notably, exogenous application of hesperetin significantly enhanced wheat resistance to leaf rust and strongly suppressed urediniospore germination. These findings provided critical insights into the molecular basis of wheat resistance to leaf rust and established a theoretical foundation for developing natural product-based green control strategies.

Transcriptomic and metabolomic data analyses both revealed favorable reproducibility within each treatment group and distinct discrepancies among different groups (Fig. 1-C and Fig.S1), laying a foundation for subsequent analysis of differentially expressed genes and metabolites. Our results showed that the metabolite L-phenylalanine was up-regulated in both compatible and incompatible wheat genotypes at 24 hpi and 120 hpi, indicating that the PPP can be activated upon pathogen infection regardless of the host’s resistance status. This phenomenon had also been reported in other plant-pathogen systems. Specifically, infection of maize by Puccinia sorghi [27] and of wheat by Pst [19] both led to the up-regulation of L-phenylalanine, thereby activating the PPP. Moreover, the PPP was regulated not only by biotic stresses but also by abiotic stresses. For instance, cold stress [9] and heavy metal stress [39] had been shown to influence the expression of PPP-related genes and the accumulation of pathway metabolites.

Lignin played a critical role in plant defense by providing mechanical strength to cell walls and maintaining structural integrity. As a complex polyphenolic polymer, lignin was derived from the PPP, with p-coumaryl, coniferyl, and sinapyl alcohols serving as monomers that formed the basic hydroxyphenyl (H), guaiacyl (G), and syringyl (S) units, respectively, via dehydrogenative polymerization. In this study, key genes encoding enzymes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ath00940) pathway—PAL, C4H, CCoAMT-1 and CAD—were up-regulated at multiple time points during the incompatible interaction between wheat and the pathogen, suggesting their direct involvement in the synthesis and accumulation of lignin precursors. Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) was a key enzyme in lignin biosynthesis. Previous studies had shown that increased lignin content enhances lodging resistance in wheat [40]. In stem rot-resistant wheat cultivars, the transcription level of TaCAD12 was significantly higher than in susceptible lines [41]. Moreover, overexpression of TaBGI1, which encodes TaCAD1, leaded to elevated lignin content and enhanced resistance to root rot and crown rot in transgenic wheat [42]. Similarly, CAD had been confirmed as a key functional protein regulating lignin biosynthesis and disease resistance in rice [43] and Arabidopsis [44]. Additionally, in the Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ath00940), synthase-related genes were regulated by multiple TFs families, including MYB, NAC, bZIP, and C2H2. Therefore, genetic engineering approaches could be employed to modulate the expression of these genes to alter lignin content and improve agronomic traits in wheat.

Numerous studies had explored the use of plant-derived natural products for disease control and for elucidating plant-pathogen interactions. The alkaloids chelerythrine and chelidonine exhibited strong inhibitory activity against Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV) [45]. Citronellal had been shown to control rice blast with an efficacy of up to 82.14%, primarily by disrupting chitin synthesis, causing hyphal thinning and breakage, thereby inhibiting mycelial growth [46]. Flavonoids, a class of polyphenolic secondary metabolites widely distributed in plants, had received considerable attention for their antimicrobial properties. Notable antimicrobial flavonoids included quercetin [47, 48], luteolin [22], apigenin [49], hesperetin [26] and catechin [50]. These flavonoids can function both as phytoalexins in plant defense systems and as plant-derived pesticides that conferred disease resistance. Research had shown that transgenic overexpression of the wheat Lr34 resistance gene (Lr34res) in sorghum enhanced resistance to both rust and anthracnose. The primarily mechanism involved the up-regulation of gene encoding flavone synthase II, flavonol 4-reductase, and dihydroflavonol reductase in transgenic Sorghum bicolor, leading to elevated levels of 3-deoxyanthocyanidin metabolite [51]. In this study, metabolite profiling revealed that naringenin and hesperetin were significantly up-regulated in the incompatible wheat line at 120 hpi, whereas they were down-regulated at both 24 hpi and 120 hpi in the compatible line. These results suggested that these flavonoids may play a critical role in late-stage defense responses by effectively suppressing pathogen spread, acting as important resistance-associated metabolites in wheat defense. To further investigate the antifungal activity of hesperetin, we performed exogenous application assays. Our results demonstrated that hesperetin significantly inhibited urediniospore germination and markedly reduced disease severity in detached leaf assays, confirming its potent anti-rust activity. Meanwhile, the expression levels of PR1, PR2 and PR5 were upregulated in hesperetin-treated wheat plants. PR genes serve as marker genes of SA-mediated systemic acquired resistance. Elevated expression of these genes indicates the activation of plant innate immune defense pathways. Based on these findings, it is speculated that hesperetin acts as an elicitor, activating the SA signaling pathway to induce high expression of downstream disease-resistant genes, thereby enhancing wheat resistance against Pt.

In summary, this study provided a multi-omics dissection of the molecular mechanisms underlying wheat resistance to leaf rust, identifying key resistance-associated genes and metabolites. Hesperetin, a flavonoid with advantages such as low toxicity and a low risk of inducing resistance, emerged as a promising natural antimicrobial compound. Hesperetin holds broad application prospects in the fields of plant disease resistance and natural antimicrobial agents. Further investigation into its structure-activity relationship and mode of action would provide a solid theoretical basis for developing novel green antimicrobial agents based on hesperetin. Additionally, future studies should focus on deeper integration of metabolomic and transcriptomic data to systematically resolve regulatory networks in resistance pathways, offering more comprehensive and valuable scientific support for the development of novel plant-derived pesticides.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of the wheat NILs Tc and TcLr24—challenged with Pt physiological race THTT—using transcriptome sequencing coupled with quasi-targeted metabolomics. This approach identified the flavonoid hesperetin as a candidate regulator implicated in wheat defense against Pt. Notably, exogenous application of hesperetin not only significantly enhanced wheat resistance to leaf rust but also potently inhibited urediniospore germination. These findings offered valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning wheat resistance to leaf rust and highlighted hesperetin as a potential modulator of plant immunity.
