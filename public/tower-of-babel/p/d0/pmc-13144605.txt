Permeable nanoreactor eye drop for enzymatic cascade-mediated treatment for acute retinal injury model mimicking geographic atrophy
Authors: Jingjing Shen, Haibin Zhao, Yuan Fang, Xinying Lv, Linfu Chen, Yu Miao, Zhiqiang Wu, Nanhui Liu, Qiang Zhang, Yang Yang, Qian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeographic atrophy (GA), the most prevalent form of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), remains clinically challenging due to its complex pathogenesis. Using an acute retinal injury model that mimics GA pathology (oxidative stress-induced retinal apoptosis), we identify enzymatic cascade restoration as a promising therapeutic strategy. To overcome ocular delivery barriers, we engineer zwitterionic nanocages for co-delivering catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Z(CAT&SOD)). These optimized nanocages achieve a remarkable 9.32% ocular penetration rate, which significantly outperforms free peroxidase (0.54%), through conjunctival-sclera-retina and conjunctival-blood-retina penetration pathways, and achieve therapeutic efficacy comparable to intravitreal injection. In the chronic management of GA, the non-invasive Z(CAT&SOD) eye drops show superior therapeutic effects to the current gold standard clinical antioxidants and lutein supplement. Therefore, this work reveals the possible association between retinal peroxidase deficiency and GA progression, and proposes a non-invasive, highly effective zwitterionic nanocage for AMD treatment.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Nanoparticles, Nanobiotechnology, Eye diseases

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive and degenerative disease characterized by dysfunction and death of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells and photoreceptors, leading to irreversible vision loss. It is the leading cause of permanent visual impairment worldwide, affecting more than 180 million people, mainly over the age of 551,2. AMD manifests in two primary forms: geographic atrophy (GA) and choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Among them, GA patients constitute approximately 85% to 90%, and the rate of blindness approaches nearly 20%3. Since the introduction of intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF), patients with CNV have experienced substantial improvements in retinal function. However, despite the effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapies for CNV, these antibodies have proven ineffective in treating GA4. Therefore, there is currently no approved treatment for GA, primarily due to its complex and poorly understood pathogenesis. Clinically, supplementing antioxidants such as lutein appears to be the only viable approach to slowing down the deterioration of GA5. Numerous studies have revealed that the relative pathogenesis of GA involves processes such as complement system activation, disturbances in lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, abnormal autophagy, the chronic inflammation pathways, etc.6–8. In response, various therapeutic strategies have been explored, including the suppression of complement activation, inhibition of angiogenesis, and exertion of anti-inflammatory effects9–11. However, these approaches have not yet demonstrated specific, positive impacts in clinical trials involving GA patients12–14. Therefore, the exploration of alternative therapeutic targets for GA treatment remains crucial.

Oxidative stress disrupts the physiological functions in most biological tissues and can lead to cell death15,16. In the retina, oxidative stress triggers a cascade of events that result in focal loss of RPE and photoreceptors, leading to structural changes in proteins, lipids and DNA, which is increasingly recognized as a major contributing factor to the development of GA12,15,17. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the removal of free radicals from the retina can effectively slow the progression of AMD18. Additionally, in AMD treatment research, strategies targeting the elimination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been proposed, including the use of inorganic nanozymes like Pt, RhZ, and CeO2 to perform antioxidant function19–22. However, these nanozymes face challenges such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and unclear therapeutic efficacy. Endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), neutralize free radicals and maintain cellular homeostasis, with SOD targeting superoxide anion (O2•−) and CAT decomposing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These enzymes play essential roles in defending against oxidative stress, particularly in regulating ROS in inflammatory diseases23–26. However, the specific roles of CAT and SOD in the pathogenesis of age-related macular degeneration, especially during the geographic atrophy stage, require further investigation.

In this study, we found that the cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms were markedly compromised in sodium iodate (SI)-induced acute retinal injury model (ARI), the pathological features of which mimic those of human GA27, as evidenced by a significant reduction in retinal CAT and SOD expression. While supplementation with CAT and SOD partially alleviated retinal atrophy and visual impairment, the therapeutic effects were not satisfactory. These limitations arise from poor ocular delivery and enzyme instability. Hence, we explored the use of zwitterions, which can polymerize in situ on protein surfaces to effectively shield charges, enhance permeability and preserve enzyme activity in complex physical environment28–32. By ingeniously employing zwitterionic materials, we coated the CAT and SOD with sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA), Z(CAT&SOD), a compound widely recognized for its charge regulation, mucosal and tissue penetration and biocompatibility. Remarkably, the Z(CAT&SOD) exhibited improved stability and activity against protease. Moreover, Z(CAT&SOD) demonstrated the ability to facilitate free diffusion across ocular barriers penetration capabilities by promoting clathrin-dependent and caveolae-mediated endocytosis33. As expected, following intravitreal injection of Z(CAT&SOD) in the ARI model, the enzymatic cascade reactions exhibited superior ROS-scavenging capacity and suppressed the activation of NF-κB-HMGB1-ILs inflammatory pathway. Ultimately, they protected RPEs and photoreceptors from ROS-induced apoptosis, which is crucial for preserving retinal function and preventing degenerative changes. Given the chronic nature and long-term treatment requirements of this disease, we attempted to develop a noninvasive peroxidase eye drop formulation, compared with the clinical gold-standard treatment strategy, supplementing with lutein. Unexpectedly, optimizing the components of zwitterionic polymers led to a significant enhancement in the ocular barrier permeability of zwitterionic peroxidase, which achieved an impressive 9.32% ocular penetration (the ocular penetration of free peroxidase was only 0.54%) by increasing the permeability through both conjunctiva-sclera-retinal and blood-retinal pathways. Remarkably, the therapeutic effects of zwitterionic peroxidase eye drops were comparable to those achieved through intravitreal injections in the ARI model, and both of them demonstrated greater efficacy compared to oral lutein therapy. Our research results emphasize that the progression of GA is associated with clear oxidative stress damage to the retina. The innovative and noninvasive eye drop we developed, which is based on a bimetallic ion nanocage coating of peroxidase, provides a transformative and promising therapeutic approach for dry AMD (Fig. 1).

Results

Oxidative stress and impaired enzyme cascade caused retina damage in the acute retinal injury model

SI, an oxidizing agent, is known to induce the ARI model including RPE cell death, geographic atrophy and progressive visual dysfunction. This dosage is well-established to consistently induce oxidative damage in RPE cells and photoreceptors, creating an ARI mouse model that recapitulates key oxidative stress-related cellular events in GA34–36. Previous studies have demonstrated that SI induces intense oxidative stress, which is concomitant with mitochondrial dysfunction in the retina35,36. To demonstrate the successful establishment of the ARI model, NaIO3 (10 mg/kg) was intravenously injected on day 0. Subsequently, the retinal structure and visual function of the mice were assessed at 1 and 2 weeks post-treatment using fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and electroretinography (ERG) (Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2b, in alignment with the pathogenesis of clinical GA, fundus imaging combined with angiographic data revealed that the mice with ARI model exhibited secondary vascular inflammation and significant drusen-like deposits in the retina, and the area of these drusen-like deposits progressively increased over time. OCT imaging provided detailed visualization of the retinal layers, revealing irregular accumulation and atrophy of the RPE layer in both the outer and inner nuclear layers (ONL and INL) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). By the 2-week time points, the ONL had nearly disappeared, accompanied by significant changes in overall retinal thickness. Further analysis of retinal thickness using ImageJ demonstrated an approximate 33% reduction at 2 weeks (Fig. 2c). ERG analysis revealed a marked reduction in both the a and b waves, reflecting impaired activity of photoreceptors and ON-bipolar cells. These reductions were positively correlated with the reduction in retinal thickness (Fig. 2d–f). These findings closely mirrored those observed in clinical GA patients, suggesting the relevance of this model for studying GA.

To assess the underlying mechanisms, we quantified the total ROS levels using fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining and evaluated retinal apoptosis via terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) analysis. As shown in Fig. 2g, h, intense DCF fluorescence signals were observed in both the RPE layer and ONL, indicating substantial ROS generation. Apoptotic signals were also detected, indicating that ROS may induce apoptosis in retinal cells. Additionally, we collected retinas from ARI mice at both 1 week and 2 weeks, and quantified the expression levels of CAT and SOD by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). At 2 weeks, a significant reduction in the expression of both CAT and SOD was observed (Fig. 2i). Enzyme activity assays further confirmed this downregulation, revealing impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms in the retina following SI treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). This impairment of antioxidant enzyme expression and activity coincided with increased oxidative stress and retinal cell apoptosis, thereby exacerbating the progression of retinal injury. Collectively, these results highlight the crucial role of oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of the ARI model. The supplementation with natural antioxidant enzymes may potentially counteract the intense oxidative stress, providing a therapeutic strategy to alleviate retinal damage and visual impairment associated with the ARI model.

Inspired by the above findings, we sought to assess the protective effects of CAT and SOD in the mouse model of SI-induced ARI. Three hours after intravenous injection of SI, 1× PBS (1 μL, Untreated group as negative control), SOD (2 mg/mL, 50,000 U/kg, 1 μL), CAT (2 mg/mL, 80,000 U/kg, 1 μL), or a combination of both enzymes (SOD&CAT, CAT: 1 mg/mL, 40,000 U/g, SOD: 1 mg/mL, 25,000 U/g, 2 μL) was promptly injected into the vitreous cavity. In addition, healthy mice were used as the positive control. The degree of retinal degeneration in each group of mice with ARI was assessed 1 week later using fundus imaging, FFA, OCT, and ERG (Fig. 2j). As shown in Fig. 2k, l, prominent retinal deposits were observed in both PBS- and SOD-injected groups, accompanied by subretinal debris accumulation (likely originating from degenerated RPE and photoreceptors), inflammatory edema, and oxidative stress-induced vascular abnormalities (Fig. S2a). After different treatments, a blinded evaluation was also performed based on fundus imaging and OCT imaging to assess retinal structural integrity and thickness reduction (Fig. S2b). Notable deformation of the RPE and ONL was also evident in these groups. In contrast, mice treated with CAT or SOD&CAT exhibited milder retinal damage, characterized by fewer drusen-like deposits, less microvascular abnormality, and reduced degeneration of the RPE and ONL. Quantitative analysis of retinal thickness using ImageJ from OCT images, revealed that the thickness of retinas treated with CAT or SOD&CAT remained approximately 80% of the original thickness, in sharp contrast to the significant thinning observed in the untreated group and SOD injection groups (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 2c). ERG results further corroborated these findings, showing a significant decline in retinal function following SI induction (Fig. 2n). Analysis of the a- and b-waves, which reflect photoreceptor and ON-bipolar cell activity, respectively, demonstrated partial recovery of electrophysiological responses in mice treated with either CAT or SOD&CAT injections (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Nevertheless, despite these improvements, the retina still did not completely recover to the levels seen in healthy retinas, indicating that oxidative stress-induced retinal damage was not completely alleviated, which warrants further investigation.

Zwitterionic nanocages enhanced the permeability of peroxidases

Due to the limited therapeutic effect of intravitreal injection of peroxidase on the ARI model, we speculated that drug delivery may be limited by complex ocular structures and physiological barriers. Therefore, we further verified the composition of intravitreal and related structural barriers and drug delivery barriers. The vitreous is primarily composed of collagen fibers and acidic mucopolysaccharides, which can easily form coulombic interactions with enzymes36. Additionally, the dense vitreous body membrane, known as the inner limiting membrane, separates the retina from the vitreous cavity, restricting the diffusion of enzymes and limiting their effectiveness37,38. Therefore, it is important to increase the diffusion of drugs in the vitreous cavity and the barrier penetration function for the treatment of GA retinopathy with peroxidase. Considering that zwitterionic platform’s proven ability to effectively penetrate mucosal barriers in the intestinal tract29,31, we aimed to develop zwitterionic nanocages to enhance the permeability of peroxidases (Fig. 3a). The encapsulation process involved enriching acrylamide, acrylic acid, sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA) and bisacrylamide (BIS; a cross-linker) around CAT through noncovalent interactions, followed by the growth of a thin polymer shell around each CAT by in situ polymerization. To evaluate the necessity of individual components for nanocage formation, we established multiple nanocages with various compositions, ranging from Z1 to Z8. Among them, Z1 and Z6 are cationic, Z2 and Z7 are anionic, Z3 contains only SBMA, and Z4 and Z8 incorporate cationic, anionic and SBMA. Moreover, Z6, Z7, and Z8 include crosslinking agent BIS and the initiator APS. We compared these nanocages based on size, surface charge, protein encapsulation ability, and permeability (Fig. 3b). As shown in Fig. 3c, d, different nanocages exhibit different sizes and charges. Notably, attempts to encapsulate peroxidase using Z1 to Z4 were unsuccessful, as nanocages could not be detected. In contrast, formulations Z5 and Z8 maintained stable sizes ranging from 100 nm to 200 nm. To further assess the encapsulation efficiency of Z5 to Z8 nanocages, we conducted gel electrophoresis and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, the results indicated that Z8 nanocages, when formulated with BIS and APS, achieved the most effective enzyme encapsulation. TEM images further confirmed that Z8 nanocages exhibited a uniform particle size distribution of approximately 200 nm (Fig. 3e).

To identify which nanocage formulations could effectively penetrate the ILM barrier, we tested the retinal permeability of Z5 to Z8 in mice after intravitreal injection by tracking the FITC fluorescent signal labeled on the CAT. As shown in Fig. 3f, compared to free CAT-FITC, most fluorescence signals were confined to the vitreous cavity (VC), while those in the Z8 group successfully penetrated the inner membrane and distributed across various retina layers (R). We further optimized the ratio of CAT to zwitterionic components, testing molar ratios of 1:2000, 1:4000, 1:8000, and 1:12000, and observed the resulting changes in particle size. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, at molar ratios of 1:2000 and 1:4000, the encapsulation of peroxidase was either uneven or incomplete. Conversely, at the molar ratio of 1:12,000, the particle size of the peroxidase nanocages increased significantly, suggesting aggregation or gel formation. However, at a molar ratio of 1:8000, the zwitterionic nanocages exhibited a uniform size, indicating the complete encapsulation of CAT. Therefore, we selected the moler ratio of 1:8000 as the optimal ratio and chose Z8 as the polymer formulation for the preparation of zwitterionic nanocages. We further characterized the physical properties and drug-loading performance at this optimal ratio. Dynamic light scattering analysis revealed a nearly neutral surface charge (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the nanocages exhibited excellent drug-loading capacity of 94.84 ± 1.55% with encapsulation efficiency of 35.32 ± 4.43%. Furthermore, circular-dichroism (CD) spectroscopy confirmed that the zwitterionic nanocage encapsulation preserved the native secondary structure of the peroxidases (Supplementary Fig. 5). To further investigate the interaction between zwitterionic nanocages and the vitreous, we measured the surface charge of Z(CAT) nanocages after co-incubation with the isolated rabbit vitreous (Supplementary Fig. 6). We found that the near-neutral surface of the zwitterionic nanocages remained stable in the vitreous environment, facilitating enhanced diffusion of the encapsulated protein drugs. To explore the mechanism by which Z(CAT) enhances permeability across ocular barriers, we conducted a series of tests on pathways such as the paracellular pathway and endocytosis related to ocular barrier permeability. First, we cultured human conjunctival epithelial cells (HConEpiC) to form a tightly connected cell barrier layer. After incubating the cells with Z(CAT) or free CAT for 24 h, we performed immunofluorescence staining for E-cadherin, a key tight junction protein39. As shown in Fig. 3g, the morphology and fluorescence intensity of E-cadherin remained unchanged, suggesting that Z(CAT) does not enhance permeability via the paracellular pathway. Subsequently, we cultured HConEpiC cells in the upper chamber of a transwell to construct the conjunctival epithelial barrier in vitro. Barrier formation was monitored daily using a cell resistance meter until the resistance value plateaued, signifying that the barrier was fully formed. We then added either Z(CAT) or CAT to the upper chamber and incubated for 6 h. Subsequently, we measured transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and observed nearly no significant changes following treatment with either Z(CAT) or free CAT (Supplementary Fig. 7), further confirming that Z(CAT) does not compromise tight junction integrity or utilize the paracellular route for ocular penetration.

To investigate whether Z(CAT) facilitates permeability through endocytosis, we co-cultured Cy5.5-labeled CAT and Z(CAT-Cy5.5) with HConEpiC cells for different time intervals (1, 3, 6 h). As shown in Fig. 3h, j, Z(CAT) demonstrated significantly higher endocytosis compared to free CAT. After 6 h of incubation, the average fluorescence intensity per cell for Z(CAT-Cy5.5) was significantly higher than that of free CAT-Cy5.5. Additionally, we quantified the remaining fluorescence intensity of Z(CAT-Cy5.5) and CAT-Cy5.5 in HConEpiC cells after replacing with fresh medium for different durations (3, 6, 12 h). Three hours later, the fluorescence intensity in cells pre-incubated of Z(CAT-Cy5.5) retained about one-fifth of the original signal, and by 12 h, the fluorescence had nearly disappeared (Fig. 3i, k), indicating that the enhanced barrier permeability of Z(CAT) is primarily due to the enhanced endocytosis. To further elucidate the cellular uptake mechanism of zwitterionic nanocages, we employed a range of endocytosis inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 3l, treatment with amiloride hydrochloride (AM) exhibited a negligible inhibitory impact on cellular uptake, indicating that macropinocytosis may not be an important pathway for the assimilation of zwitterionic nanocages. Notably, treatment with chlorpromazine (CPZ, a clathrin inhibitor) resulted in a substantial decline in cellular uptake. Furthermore, the caveolae-mediated pathway was also instrumental in the endocytosis of zwitterionic nanocages, as evidenced by the marked reduction in the endocytosis when treated with inhibitors, genistein (Geni) and β-cyclodextrin (β-CD). Above all, through clathrin-dependent and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, zwitterionic nanocages could facilitate the effective penetration of peroxidase to break through the eye barriers (Fig. 3m).

To investigate potential organelle-specific effects, we incubated RPE cells with Cy5.5-labeled Z(CAT) for 6 h and performed mitochondrial co-localization analysis using a mitochondrial tracker. Confocal imaging revealed minimal mitochondrial accumulation of the nanocages (Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating that their therapeutic mechanism does not rely on direct mitochondrial targeting. Instead, the nanocage predominantly localized to the cytosol and peri-mitochondrial space, a critical region for ROS diffusion and redox signaling. This spatial distribution is particularly advantageous for catalase activity, as it positions the therapeutic agent near the sites of ROS generation while allowing for effective diffusion throughout the cytosol. To further characterize the cellular response to treatment, we analyzed the activation of endogenous antioxidant pathways in oxidatively stressed RPE cells after Z(CAT&SOD) treatment using qPCR. RPE cells were first treated with 200 μg/mL SI for 6 h to induce oxidative stress, followed by treatment with free CAT&SOD or Z(CAT&SOD) for an additional 6 h. The qPCR analysis revealed that Z(CAT&SOD) treatment significantly downregulated the Nrf2 pathway and its downstream targets (e.g. GPX1, NQO1, GCLC, HMOX-1) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This oxidative stress induction was effectively attenuated by both free CAT/SOD and the zwitterion-protected formulation, with the latter exhibiting superior protective efficacy, likely due to enhanced enzyme stability and improved cellular delivery. To further evaluate mitochondrial protection, we analyzed mitochondrial integrity in stressed RPE cells after Z(CAT&SOD) treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Although the nanocage does not directly localize to mitochondria, its cytosolic ROS-scavenging activity effectively preserved mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced secondary mitochondrial ROS production. These findings highlight the significant organelle-protective mechanism, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of our platform.

Moreover, considering the complex enzymatic metabolic reactions in vivo, we also assessed whether the zwitterionic nanocages could protect peroxidase from enzymatic degradation. To simulate the in vivo degradation environment, we exposed Z(SOD), Z(CAT), free SOD and free CAT to proteinase K at 37 °C. After 3 h, free SOD retained only 51% enzymatic activity and free CAT only 39%, with both activities nearly vanishing after 12 h. In contrast, Z(SOD) maintained 83% enzymatic activity, and Z(CAT) retained 74% even after 12 h (Fig. 3n). We further evaluated the enzymatic stability of Z(SOD), Z(CAT), and their free counterparts under different storage conditions (4 °C, 25 °C and 37 °C). While free enzymes exhibited rapid activity loss at 25 °C and 37 °C, the zwitterionic nanocages-encapsulated enzymes retained most of their initial activity for 4 weeks, indicating their superior protective effect of zwitterionic nanocage (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results demonstrated that zwitterionic nanocages effectively protect protein activity, ensuring that SOD and CAT function as peroxidase in scavenging free radicals within complex physiological environment.

Intravitreal injection of Z(CAT&SOD) for treatment of acute retinal injury

Motivated by the permeability and stability of the enzyme provided by zwitterionic nanocages, we aimed to explore the therapeutic effect of the enzymatic cascade in eradicating ROS within the retina. We first induced ARI in mice using SI on Day 0 and randomly divided them into four groups for timely treatments: Untreated (1× PBS, 2 μL), Z(CAT) (2 mg/mL, 80,000 U/g, 1 μL), Z(SOD) (2 mg/mL, 50,000 U/g, 1 μL), and Z(CAT&SOD) (CAT: 1 mg/mL, 40,000 U/g, SOD: 1 mg/mL, 25,000 U/g, 2 μL). In addition, healthy mice were used as the positive control. Three hours after SI injection, we intravitreally administered PBS, Z(CAT), Z(SOD), or Z(CAT&SOD) into each group. The retina was evaluated using retinal imaging at 1 and 2 weeks post-injection (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 11, PBS- or Z(SOD)-injected mice developed progressive subretinal drusen-like deposits, secondary vascular inflammation, widespread retinal cell apoptosis, and atrophy over time. In contrast, Z(CAT) treated mice exhibited a significant reduction in subretinal drusen-like deposits, milder vascular inflammation and improved retinal cell survival. Surprisingly, the retina of mice treated with Z(CAT&SOD) displayed nearly no subretinal drusen-like deposits, healthy retinal vasculature without edema, clear retinal layers, and no signs of atrophy. The retinas of mice treated with Z(CAT&SOD) intravitreal injections were even comparable to those of healthy retinas. Furthermore, zwitterionic nanocages without peroxidase demonstrated nearly no therapeutic effect on the SI-induced ARI model.

Two weeks post-treatment, we further performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess the structure of the retinal layers. In the Z(CAT&SOD) group, the retinal layers were well-preserved, with high cell density in both the INL and ONL, and clearly defined photoreceptor and RPE layers. In contrast, mice treated with PBS or Z(SOD) showed severe degeneration, including almost complete loss of the INL, photoreceptor layer, and RPE layer. The ONL had sparse cells, reduced thickness, and overall retinal thinning. A blinded evaluation based on fundus imaging and OCT was conducted 2 weeks after treatment (Fig. 4c). Under the influence of SI, both the Untreated and Z(SOD) groups had retinal scores of less than 1, indicating severe damage. In contrast, the Z(CAT&SOD) group showed scores comparable to a healthy retina, with significant improvement over the Z(CAT) group, which exhibited only partial relief. Additionally, we measured retinal thickness using ImageJ software, with results consistent with the scoring system. The Z(CAT&SOD) nanocages, coated with zwitterionic polymers, effectively inhibited retinal atrophy caused by oxidative stress, with significant differences observed compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, we assessed the visual function of mice using ERG. Mice injected with PBS and Z(SOD) exhibited severe visual impairment, with their visual function reduced to one-third of normal levels. Mice injected with Z(CAT) also showed compromised visual function, with a reduction to two-thirds of normal levels. However, mice treated with Z(CAT&SOD) displayed nearly no visual impairment (Fig. 4f, g and Supplementary Fig. 12). The restoration of ERG amplitudes in Z(CAT&SOD)-treated mice, alongside intact ONL and RPE morphology, provides direct evidence that photoreceptor-targeted ROS neutralization is both a mechanistic requirement and an alternative therapeutic strategy for preserving retinal function.

Recognizing that significant optic nerve degeneration in the ARI model is associated with chronic inflammation caused by oxidative stress40,41, we then investigated the regulatory effects of an antioxidant enzyme cascade on inflammation during oxidative stress resistance. One week after intravitreal injection of PBS, Z(CAT), Z(SOD), or Z(CAT&SOD), we assessed retinal apoptosis and inflammatory pathway responses using immunofluorescent staining, flow cytometry and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). DCFH-DA labeling experiments demonstrated that Z(CAT&SOD) significantly reduces ROS levels in the retina, indicating that Z(CAT&SOD) successfully eliminates ROS (Supplementary Fig. 13a). TUNEL analysis further revealed that Z(CAT&SOD) effectively reduced SI-induced retinal damage by inhibiting photoreceptor apoptosis, particularly in the ONL and INL (Fig. 5a). To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying SI-triggered retinal inflammation and cell death, we examined the expression and distribution of NF-κBp65, IL-1β, and HMGB1 in retinal tissues following different treatments. As illustrated in Fig. 5b–d, supplementation with Z(CAT&SOD) resulted in a significant decrease in the inflammatory markers of the NF-κB-HMGB1-ILs pathway, indicating that Z(CAT&SOD) effectively mitigated inflammation. Moreover, chronic inflammation often triggers the release of VEGF family proteins, increasing vascular permeability and potentially leading to vascular swelling and secondary vascular abnormalities40. Therefore, we also evaluated VEGF and ZO-1 protein expression in retinal tissue. As anticipated, the untreated group and those receiving only Z(SOD) or Z(CAT) showed decreased ZO-1 and increased VEGF levels in the retina, suggesting a malignant microenvironment characterized by inflammation and angiogenesis. This increased vascular permeability disrupts the eye’s immune microenvironment, creating a vicious cycle. In contrast, the Z(CAT&SOD) group displayed a more intact retinal structure and lower inflammation levels, comparable to the healthy retinal microenvironment. This dual-enzyme approach not only mitigates inflammation but also helps maintain the integrity of the retinal structure by reducing vascular permeability and preventing secondary vascular abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c).

Additionally, we evaluated the proportions of M1 and M2 macrophage subpopulations in retinal tissue using flow cytometry and measured cytokine levels in the retina by ELISA following different treatments. As shown in Fig. 5e–g, there was a marked increase in CD206+F4/80+ cells in retinas treated with Z(CAT&SOD) and Z(CAT), whereas a decline was observed in those treated with PBS and Z(SOD). Notably, the healthy group, along with the Z(CAT) and Z(CAT&SOD) groups, predominantly exhibited CD206+F4/80+ macrophages, indicative of an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. In contrast, the untreated control group and the Z(SOD) group were mainly characterized by CD80+F4/80+ cells, reflecting a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Furthermore, treatment with Z(CAT) and Z(CAT&SOD) resulted in a significant reduction in retinal inflammatory factor levels, bringing them close to those seen in healthy mice. This was in stark contrast to the untreated and Z(SOD) groups, which maintained higher levels of inflammation (Fig. 5h–k). Importantly, Z(CAT&SOD) demonstrated a more pronounced inhibitory effect on both inflammatory pathways and inflammatory cells and cytokines compared to Z(CAT) alone, indicating that the enzymatic cascade reaction was more effective at eliminating ROS in the retina. Therefore, the enzymatic cascade reaction mediated by Z(CAT&SOD) synergistically reduced key inflammatory markers such as NF-κBp65, IL-1β, and HMGB1 in retinal tissues while promoting macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes. These findings highlight Z(CAT&SOD) as a promising therapeutic candidate for retinal disorders involving oxidative stress and inflammation (Fig. 5l).

Z(CAT&SOD)-based eye drop for acute retinal injury treatment

Motivated by the exceptional permeability of the zwitterionic nanocage, we endeavored to treat GA in a noninvasive manner using eye drops. Considering the unique barrier system of the eye, we tested the capacity of nanocages with distinct charge properties to penetrate the conjunctival and scleral barriers. Among them, Z6 was cationic, Z7 was anionic, while Z5 and Z8 were zwitterionic based on SBMA and Z8 crosslinked with BIS and the initiator APS. Six hours post-application of eye drops in different formulations of Z5 to Z8, the permeability of Z8 obviously surpassed that of both free CAT-FITC and the Z5 to Z7 eye drop groups, with the fluorescence signal of CAT-FITC evident in the conjunctiva, sclera (C/S) and especially in various layers of the retina (R) (Fig. 6a, b). Consequently, the zwitterionic SBMA-constructed nanocages (Z8) exhibit superior tissue permeability compared to their cationic or anionic counterparts, which makes them an ideal choice for formulating peroxidase eye drops and was selected for further investigation for ocular permeability.

We further examined the permeability and localization of the selected Z(CAT-FITC) (Z8) in the eye. Following the administration of Z(CAT-FITC) and free CAT-FITC eye drops to mice, eye samples were collected at intervals of 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 h to measure total fluorescence intensity within the eyeballs. As shown in Fig. 6c, Z(CAT-FITC) achieved a peak eyeball penetration of 9.32% at 6 h post-administration, while that of free CAT-FITC was 0.54%. Even after 12 h, Z(CAT-FITC) maintained a penetration rate of 4.09%, outperforming free CAT-FITC (0.12%). Confocal imaging of whole-eye sections revealed that the zwitterionic nanocages might penetrate the conjunctival-scleral barrier, reaching the choroid and retina (Fig. 6d). Quantitative analysis of CAT-FITC fluorescence signals revealed a conjunctiva/sclera-to-retina ratio of 6.67:1 (Supplementary Fig. 14a), suggesting efficient transscleral delivery. In addition, pharmacokinetic profiling following topical administration of Z(CAT-FITC) eye drops (measured at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h) showed that Z(CAT-FITC) exhibited significantly higher blood accumulation than free CAT-FITC at 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 14b), indicating the existence of a blood-mediated retinal delivery pathway. Additionally, to more directly determine the ocular permeability of zwitterionic peroxidase by measuring the content of peroxidase, we monitored CAT enzyme activity in retinal tissue at different time points (1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 h) post-administration using a catalase activity assay kit. Results indicated significantly higher CAT enzyme activity in the retina of the Z(CAT) group compared to free CAT group during the period from 3 h to 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 14c). This finding prompted us to reflect that the zwitterionic nanocages not only enhanced the permeability of peroxidase in the eye, but also protected it from degradation during delivery, thereby preserving its enzymatic activity. Therefore, it clearly demonstrated that zwitterionic peroxidase nanocages could effectively facilitate peroxidase penetration within complex structural barriers without inactivation.

Leveraging the effective ocular permeation and enzyme activity protection provided by zwitterionic nanocages for peroxidase, we attempted to use this eye drop for treating the ARI model. To benchmark against the clinical gold-standard treatment2, we established a positive control group that receives oral lutein supplementation. After intravenous injection of SI, subjects were randomly divided into six groups: Untreated (1× PBS eye drop, 2 μL twice daily for a week), CAT&SOD eye drops (CAT: 1 mg/mL, 40,000 U/g, SOD: 1 mg/mL, 25,000 U/g, 2 μL twice daily for a week), CAT&SOD intravitreal injection (same concentration, single 2 μL injection), Z(CAT&SOD) eye drops (same concentration, 2 μL twice daily for a week), Z(CAT&SOD) intravitreal injection (same concentration, single 2 μL injection), and lutein oral administration (0.5 μg lutein per day for 1 week). Retinal evaluations were conducted using imaging systems 1 and 2 weeks later (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Unlike the PBS group and the uncoated groups (CAT&SOD i.v.t and CAT&SOD e.d.), which exhibited severe subretinal drusen-like deposits, secondary vascular abnormalities, and reduced retinal thickness, the Z(CAT&SOD) eye drops group demonstrated significantly decreased subretinal drusen-like deposits, reduced vascular abnormality, and maintained normal retinal thickness (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 15b). Clinical scoring indicated that Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop effectively relieved symptoms of mice with ARI and maintained the thickness of RPE and retina (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d), with therapeutic effect comparable to intravitreal injections over time. ERG tests conducted 2 weeks post-treatment revealed that Z(CAT&SOD) effectively protected photoreceptors and ON-bipolar cells, showing no significant difference compared to intravitreal injection (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 15e, f). H&E staining results 2 weeks after treatment confirmed that Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop maintained cell density and thickness across all retinal layers (Fig. 6e). In contrast, the untreated group and the uncoated groups experienced significant reductions in retinal thickness and cell density, particularly in the ONL and photoreceptor layer, correlating with ERG findings of visual impairment. These effects were comparable to those seen with intravitreal injection, indicating that zwitterionic nanocage effectively promoted enzyme penetration across the ocular barrier, facilitating ROS clearance and protecting retina and vascular tissues. Interestingly, the mice with ARI supplemented with daily oral lutein exhibited some improvement in retinal atrophy compared to the untreated group and those supplemented with unmodified peroxidase. This was evidenced by a reduction in subretinal drusen-like deposits, preservation of retinal thickness, and mitigation of retinal cell apoptosis (Fig. 6e, f and Supplementary Fig. 16). This indicated that effective lutein supplementation could indeed alleviate the progression of dry macular degeneration. However, when compared to the coated peroxidase supplementation, lutein’s therapeutic effect was less satisfactory. Mice treated with Z(CAT&SOD) via intravitreal injection or eye drops exhibited the least retinal deposition, the mildest secondary vascular abnormalities, optimal maintenance of retinal thickness, and best preservation of retinal optic nerve function, which may be attributed to the limited ROS-scavenging ability of lutein and its absorption.

Given the high sensitivity of ocular tissues, we conducted a thorough biocompatibility assessment of Z(CAT&SOD) to ensure its safety for long-term use. A battery of ophthalmic evaluations, including slit-lamp microscope, OCT, ERG and intraocular pressure (IOP), was performed on mice 4 weeks after treatment with Z-polymer and Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 17a, b, the corneas of treated mice remained clear and intact, with no observable differences compared to healthy controls. Moreover, prolonged administration of Z-polymer and Z(CAT&SOD) did not obviously alter tear secretion (Supplementary Fig. 17c). Corneal OCT imaging confirmed that neither formation affected corneal thickness (Supplementary Fig. 17d, e), while fundus imaging and retina thickness measurements revealed no structural abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 17f–i). In addition, IOP values remained stable after long-term treatment, indicating that the Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop does not induce ocular hypertension or increase the risk of glaucoma (Supplementary Fig. 17j). To assess systemic toxicity, we performed histological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and eye) via H&E staining. Tissues form Z-polymer and Z(CAT&SOD)-treated mice exhibited nearly no pathological changes compared to healthy mice (Supplementary Fig. 17k). Additionally, hematological analysis of blood samples confirmed that all parameters remained in the normal range, indicating negligible systemic toxicity even after prolonged subconjunctival retention (Supplementary Fig. 17l). Therefore, these findings demonstrate that both Z-polymer and Z(CAT&SOD) exhibit excellent biocompatibility after long-term administration.

Discussion

In the SI-induced retinal acute injury mouse model, we observed pathological features resembling those in clinical GA patients, including subretinal drusen-like deposits and neurodegeneration. Mechanistic investigations revealed that RPE and photoreceptor cell apoptosis were closely associated with the microenvironment characterized by high oxidative stress and diminished peroxidase activity, particularly reduced levels of CAT and SOD11,42. As a proof-of-concept, our findings suggest that timely antioxidant supplementation may preserve residual photoreceptors when oxidative damage is the primary driver of degeneration. However, the coulombic interactions within the vitreous body and the ILM barrier in the vitreous cavity posed significant challenges to the diffusion and penetration of peroxidases into the RPE and optic nerve cells, even when employing intravitreal injection as a benchmark ophthalmic drug delivery modality. To overcome these challenges, we developed zwitterionic nanocages for CAT and SOD to enhance diffusion and barrier penetration by shielding surface charges and promoting endocytosis pathways. Following the intravitreal injection of Z(CAT&SOD), we observed a significant mitigation of retinal damage in the SI-induced ARI model. This was evidenced by reduced ROS-induced damage in RPE and optic nerve cells, suppression of the NF-κB-HMGB1-ILs inflammation pathways. Therefore, the strategy of using zwitterionic peroxidase with intravitreal injection shows promise for slowing the progression of dry AMD.

However, early-stage dry AMD patients often delay therapy due to concerns about traumatic scarring and postoperative infection, thereby missing the optimal treatment window. Moreover, patients with advanced atrophic dry AMD require sustained therapeutic drug concentrations over an extended period. Developing an effective, noninvasive ophthalmic treatment strategy based on peroxidase is of great importance. To address this, we optimized the components of zwitterionic polymers to select the nanocages with the strongest barrier penetration as carriers for a peroxidase eye drop formulation. Unexpectedly, eyeball penetration of Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop was 9.32%, much higher than that of free peroxidase (the eyeball penetration was only 0.54%). Furthermore, after applying the Z(CAT&SOD) eye drop for 2 weeks, we observed a significant reduction in retinal cell apoptosis induced by SI, alongside stabilization of retinal thickness and visual function, achieving comparable therapeutic effects to those observed in the intravitreal injection of Z(CAT&SOD). More interestingly, compared with the therapeutic effects of both intravitreal injection and eye drops of Z(CAT&SOD), lutein exhibited inferior therapeutic efficacy. This may be attributed to the retina being affected by the blood-eye barrier in the systemic circulatory system, resulting in limited absorption and the limitation of the ROS-scavenging ability of lutein.

This study demonstrated that the pathogenesis of ARI is characterized by excessive retinal ROS production and reduced peroxidase activity. Our therapeutic intervention with CAT and SOD exhibits multi-model protection effects by effectively mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation responses while reducing cellular apoptosis and preserving mitochondrial protection. These findings establish a comprehensive antioxidant therapeutic approach for ARI management. Moreover, we employed localized delivery of precisely dosed SOD and CAT co-encapsulated in a protective nanocage, which ensured stoichiometric enzyme delivery to injury sites without genetic alteration. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the zwitterionic nanocage platform enables effective topical delivery of antioxidant enzymes to the retina, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating oxidative stress-related retinal damage. While the SI-induced acute oxidative injury model serves as a valuable tool for investigating oxidative stress, a key driver of RPE and photoreceptor degeneration, it represents a simplified, accelerated system that lacks the chronic, multifactorial complexity of human dry AMD. Future studies will need to validate this proof-of-concept in more physiologically relevant chronic models that better mimic the slow human disease progression. Nonetheless, by addressing the critical challenge of ocular drug delivery, our platform provides a versatile and patient-friendly foundation for developing disease-modifying therapies for dry AMD.

Human conjunctival epithelial cells (HConEpiC) and adult retinal pigment epithelial cell line-19 (RPE19) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), and cultured in DMEM containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Seven-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Nanjing Pengsheng Biological Technology Co. The animals were acclimated in SPF environment for 1 week prior to conducting follow-up experiments. The mice were maintained under a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle, with temperatures ranging from 18 to 23 °C and humidity levels maintained between 40 and 60%. The mice were housed in microisolator cages to ensure the containment of pathogens. All animal procedures complied with relevant laws and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (No. ECSU-2019000198).

Construction and evaluation of acute retinal injury mice model

Sodium iodate (SI) was prepared at a concentration of 2 mg/mL in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at 4 °C until use. SI was administered via tail vein injection to induce the model. Control mice received equivalent volumes of PBS in accordance with standard protocols. To visualize the formation of CNV lesions in the eyes, female or male mice were intraperitoneally injected with 0.1 mL of 2% fluorescein sodium. Fundus images and OCT images were captured using a Phoenix Micron Ⅳ multifunctional high-resolution retinal imaging system (Phoenix, USA). Additionally, ERG was performed under scotopic condition to examine the optic function of both mice with acute retinal injury and healthy mice. The A waves and B waves were recorded, quantified and analyzed to evaluate retinal function.

In vivo imaging (fundoscopy, OCT and ERG): All imaging was performed using a Micron IV retinal imaging system (Phoenix Research Labs) coupled with a high-resolution OCT module and ERG module. Prior to imaging, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine, followed by pupil dilation with topical administration of 1% tropicamide.

Fundoscopy: Color fundus images were captured using the integrated color fundus camera under standard white light illumination.

Spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT): Retinal cross-sectional scans were acquired using the scanning protocol centered on the optic nerve head. Subsequent quantification of retinal layer thicknesses was performed using imageJ system.

Electroretinography (ERG): Full-field ERG recording was performed in accordance with the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) standard protocol for rodents. Following overnight dark adaptation (>12 h), mice were anesthetized as above, and their pupils were dilated under dim red illumination. Scotopic (rod-dominated) responses were first recorded using a series of flashes with increasing intensities (3 log cd·s/m2). For photopic (cone-mediated) responses, mice were light-adapted for 10 min under a 30 cd/m2 background before recording responses to a 1.0 log cd·s/m2 flash stimulus. ERG waveform analysis included: a-wave amplitude (baseline to trough of initial negative deflection), b-wave amplitude (a-wave trough to the peak of subsequent positive wave), implicit times for both components. All recordings and analyses were performed using the integrated ERG module of the Micron IV retinal imaging system.

Measurement of ROS activity and cell apoptosis in the retina of mice with acute retinal injury

ROS production in the retinas of mice with acute retinal injury was quantified using a 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFHDA) assay kit, while cell apoptosis was measured using a TUNEL assay kit, following established protocols. Briefly, frozen eye tissue sections were fixed with eyeball fixation solution for 15 min, washed with PBS three times, permeabilized using freshly prepared 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase solution for 60 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, tissues were incubated with DCFH-DA and DAPI at 37 °C for another 30 min. The fluorescence intensity was examined by a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Determination of CAT and SOD in mice with acute retinal injury

Retinas were extracted from the eyeballs of SI-mice and lysed using a sonic disruptor in Western and IP sample lysates (500 μL) at 0 °C. The supernatant was then centrifuged, and 100 microliters were used for CAT and SOD assay tests. Additionally, RNA expression levels of CAT and SOD were assessed by qPCR analysis. Retinal cells isolated from mice with acute retinal injury were lysed with Trizol, and the RNA was diluted to a final concentration of 200 ng/μL for analysis. Primer and probe sequences were as follows: CAT forward primer: AAATGCTTCAGGGCCGCCTT; CAT reverse primer: GTAGGGACAGTTCACAGGTA; SOD forward primer: AACAATGGTGAAGGCTGTTGCTGT; SOD reverse primer: TTGTGCATGCACATGGAACCCATG; GAPDH forward primer: GTGACGTTGACATCCGTAAAGA; GAPDH reverse primer: GTAACAGTCCGCCTAGAAGCAC. These primes were synthesized by Servicebio.

Preparation of Z(CAT) nanocages

Under continuous low-speed stirring, a 20 mg/mL CAT solution was added gradually into a round-bottom flask. Acrylamide, acrylic acid, SBMA, bisacrylamide (cross-linker) and TMEDA were sequentially added with different molar ratio, with each addition followed by 10 min of stirring to ensure thorough mixing. After all reagents were added, the solution was stirred continuously for an additional 2 h. Unreacted small molecules were removed by dialysis to terminate the reaction. Finally, the concentration and enzymatic activity of Z(CAT)s were determined using the BCA protein assay kit and the CAT activity assay kit. The preparation method of Z(CAT&SOD) nanocages is the same as that mentioned above. The enzymatic activity of SOD was determined using the SOD activity assay kit.

Retina penetration of intravitreal injection with Z(CAT)s

To assess the penetration of Z(CAT)s through the inner limiting membrane (ILM) barrier into the retina, CAT was labeled with FITC. Z(CAT-FITC) nanocages (Z5 to Z8, with CAT concentration at 2 mg/mL, 2 μL) were intravitreally injected into the eyes of anesthetized mice. Six hours post-injection, the mice were euthanized, and their eyes were harvested to determine the location of CAT-FITC using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA). ImageJ was used for semi-quantitative analysis of FITC fluorescence within the retina.

Retina penetration of Z(CAT)s eyedrops

To identify the optimal Z(CAT) formation for penetrating ocular barriers, we evaluated FITC fluorescence in the retina after administration of Z5(CAT-FITC) to Z8(CAT-FITC) eyedrops in mice. Each eyedrop contained Z-CAT nanocapsules (CAT: 2 mg/mL, 2 μL) or free CAT (CAT: 2 mg/mL, 2 μL). The eyedrops were applied to anesthetized mice, and the animals were euthanized at different time points (1, 3, 6, 9, 12 h post-administration). Retina in different groups was collected at corresponding time points, homogenized at 4 °C to isolate proteins, and the fluorescence intensity of CAT-FITC was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA). Additionally, the concentration of CAT in mouse eyes was determined using a CAT-specific assay kit.

Immunocytochemistry analysis of tight junction proteins

HConEpiCs were cultured to form a corneal epithelial layer and then co-cultured with either CAT or Z(CAT) at a concentration of 50 μg/mL. Six hours later, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 20 min, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 20 min. The cells were then blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. Following blocking, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, anti-E-cadherin (1:500). After washing away unbound primary antibodies, the cells were further incubated with the FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using the confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Detection of protein expression related to the endogenous antioxidant system

RPE cells were incubated with Sodium iodate (at an incubation concentration of 200 ug/mL) for 6 h to induce the oxidative stress state of RPE cells. Then, we divided them into three groups: untreated, free CAT&SOD, and Z(CAT&SOD). After another 6-h incubation, we detected the endogenous antioxidant system status of RPE cells. Additionally, RNA expression levels of HMOX1, GPX1, Nrf, NQO1 and RPLP0 were assessed by qPCR analysis. RPE cells after different treatments were lysed with Trizol, and the RNA was diluted to a final concentration of 200 ng/μL for analysis. Primer and probe sequences were as follows: HMOX1 forward primer: CCAGGCAGAGAATGCTGAGTTC; HMOX1 reverse primer: AAGACTGGGCTCTCCTTGTTGC; GPX1 forward primer: GTGCTCGGCTTCCCGTGCAAC; GPX1 reverse primer: CTCGAAGAGCATGAAGTTGGGC; Nrf forward primer:CACATCCAGTCAGAAACCAGTGG; Nrf reverse primer: GGAATGTCTGCGCCAAAAGCTG; NQO1 forward primer:CCTGCCATTCTGAAAGGCTGGT; NQO1 reverse primer:GTGGTGATGGAAAGCACTGCCT; GCLC forward primer: GGAAGTGGATGTGGACACCAGA; GCLC reverse primer: GCTTGTAGTCAGGATGGTTTGCG; β-actin forward primer: GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG; β-actin reverse primer: ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA; RPLP0 forward primer: TGGTCATCCAGCAGGTGTTCGA; RPLP0 reverse primer: ACAGACACTGGCAACATTGCGG. These primes were synthesized by Servicebio.

Cellular endocytosis and mitochondrial co-localization tests of Z(CAT) nanocages

Cellular uptake of Z(CAT) nanocages by HConEpiCs was assessed using flat-bottom, 24-well plates, with a seeding density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were exposed to 50 µg/mL of Z(CAT) or free CAT in serum-free medium. After incubation for 3, 6 and 12 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde and stained with DAPI for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was visualized and analyzed by the confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Based on this, we further investigated whether the two-ion-type nanocages could be co-localized with the mitochondrial organelles. The RPE cells were exposed to 50 µg/mL of Z(CAT) or free CAT in serum-free medium. After incubation for 3, 6 and 12 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, the cells were incubated with Mito-Tracker Green Probe for 30 min. Next, the Mito-Tracker Green staining solution was removed, and the fresh cell culture medium pre-warmed at 37 °C was added. Subsequently, the samples were observed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Mitochondrial damage detection

RPE cells were incubated with Sodium iodate (at an incubation concentration of 200 µg/mL) for 6 h to induce the oxidative stress state of RPE cells. Then, we divided them into three groups: untreated, free CAT&SOD, and Z(CAT&SOD). After another 6-h incubation, we detected the endogenous antioxidant system status of RPE cells. Then, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS, and then incubated with JC-1 (Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit) at staining working solution for 20 min. After the incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed twice with the JC-1 staining buffer. Then, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min, and then observed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

TEER measurement assay

HConEpiCs were seeded into Transwell insert chambers with a density of 5 × 104 cells per well, and the resistivity (R) of the cell layer was measured every 2 days using an Epithelium R/V Meter (RE1600; Beijing King Technology Co. Ltd.). TEER values were calculated using the standard method. Once the TEER value stabilized, CAT or Z(CAT) with equivalent CAT concentration of 50 μg/mL was added to the insert chambers for 6 h, and TEER values were recorded at 0, 6 and 24 h.

The following formula was used to calculate TEER (ohm·cm2).

TEER (ohm·cm2) = (R1-R0) × 0.33,

where R1 is the TEER value of inserts with cells; R0 is the TEER value of inserts without cells; and the insert membrane area in the plate was 0.33 cm2.

Intravitreal injection and eyedrop application of Z(CAT&SOD) nanocages in mice with acute retinal injury

Initially, a dry macular lesion model was induced in mice via intravenous injection of NaIO3. Three hours post-injection, the mice were anesthetized and randomly assigned to four groups: Untreated (1× PBS, 2 μL), Z(CAT) (2 mg/mL, 80,000 U/g, 1 μL), Z(SOD) (2 mg/mL, 50,000 U/g, 1 μL), and Z(CAT&SOD) (CAT: 1 mg/mL, 40,000 U/g, SOD: 1 mg/mL, 25,000 U/g, 2 μL). The respective treatments were administered via intravitreal injections. Live fundus evaluations, including fundus imaging, FFA, OCT and ERG, were conducted 1 and 2 weeks post-treatment. For the evaluation of topical eyedrop treatment, the mice were randomly divided into six groups: After intravenous injection of SI, subjects were randomly divided into six groups: Untreated (1× PBS eyedrop, 2 μL twice daily for a week), CAT&SOD eye drops (CAT: 1 mg/mL, 40,000 U/g, SOD: 1 mg/mL, 25,000 U/g, 2 μL twice daily for a week), CAT&SOD intravitreal injection (same concentration, single 2 μL injection), Z(CAT&SOD) eye drops (same concentration, 2 μL twice daily for a week), Z(CAT&SOD) intravitreal injection (same concentration, single 2 μL injection), and lutein oral administration (0.5 μg lutein per day for 1 week). Live fundus evaluations were conducted again at 1 or 2 weeks post-treatment. Two weeks after different treatments, eyes from different groups were collected for histological examination and immunofluorescence staining using TUNEL kit and IL-1β, ZO-1, NF-κB, HMGB1 and VEGF antibodies.
