Feasibility of high-intensity interval training in older adults with HIV and co-occurring hypertension
Authors: Michael J. Hankes, Taylor L. Taylor, Emily L. Zumbro, Ene M. Enogela, Fitzgerald Dodds, McKenna Tharpe, Silvienne C. Sint Jago, Burel R. Goodin, Amanda L. Willig, Thomas W. Buford, Raymond Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs life expectancy for people living with HIV (PWH) has increased, there has been a rise in age-related comorbidities such as hypertension. Exercise is an important nonpharmacological strategy to counter such adverse effects of chronic HIV infection. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been demonstrated to be safe and efficacious in improving health outcomes in individuals with chronic illness, but data are limited in older PWH and co-morbid conditions. The primary objective of this pilot feasibility study was to assess the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of a 6-week HIIT intervention among older PWH and co-occurring hypertension.MethodsPWH were recruited from a local HIV/AIDS Clinic for a 6-week, center-based HIIT exercise intervention involving twice-weekly aerobic exercise on an upright cycle ergometer, monitored by exercise physiologists. The primary endpoint of the study was the feasibility (e.g., consent rate, attendance, adherence, dropout rate) and participant perceived benefits and barriers assessed via the Exercise Benefits and Barriers Scale. Additional endpoints included cardiorespiratory fitness (6-min walk test [6MWT]), changes in blood pressure (BP), anthropometrics (body mass index [BMI]), and ratings of pain.ResultsA total of 26 PWH were contacted, and 13 agreed to participate and were enrolled in the study. Ten of the 13 participants (76.9%) completed the study, and, on average, the completers were 60.5 years old, 50% Black, and 60% male. Reasons for dropping out of the study include the following: (1) foot pain; (2) distance; and (3) time commitment. Feasibility outcomes were as follows: 50% consent rate; 86.7% attendance rate, 100% protocol adherence; 23.1% dropout rate. The participants perceived exercise more positively and perceived fewer barriers to exercise following the exercise intervention. BP and BMI both decreased following the intervention, while distance walked during the 6MWT declined following the intervention.ConclusionsOverall, this pilot feasibility study suggests that a short-term exercise program is feasible for older PWH and co-occurring hypertension and may improve BP, BMI, and perceptions of exercise. While the program was safe and participants showed some improvements, incorporating approaches to enhance recruitment and adherence is critical. These preliminary findings are exploratory and descriptive in nature and support the need for larger, randomized trials with longer follow-up.Trial registrationThe trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06888596). Background

Significant advancements in HIV treatment and management over the last four decades have enabled people living with HIV (PWH) to live as long as individuals without HIV [1]. However, this increased longevity has led to a sharp rise in aging-related comorbidities, including hypertension, which often develops earlier in PWH compared to those without HIV. Hypertension is the leading cause of preventable deaths worldwide, and in the U.S., roughly 66% of PWH have hypertension, compared to 54% of people without HIV [2]. This elevated prevalence of hypertension in PWH is driven by a complex interplay of factors. Chronic inflammation and immune reconstitution following antiretroviral therapy (ART) contribute to vascular dysfunction, creating a unique pathophysiologic profile and therefore emphasizing the need for tailored strategies to manage hypertension in PWH.

Recent evidence highlights the positive effects of nonpharmacological interventions, such as exercise, in managing chronic conditions. Exercise is the only intervention with evidence to support its use in treating age-related functional declines [3]. Exercise interventions in PWH support the evidence that exercise can improve cardiovascular, metabolic, and functional measures in younger PWH [4]. High-intensity interval training (HIIT), in particular, has been shown to improve aging-related processes, enhance cardiovascular function, and effectively target key mechanisms involved in hypertension management [5]. HIIT is repeated alternating bouts of high-intensity and lower-intensity exercise. Ultimately, the benefits of HIIT extend beyond blood pressure (BP) control, offering improvements in overall fitness, metabolic health, and quality of life for PWH.

Knowledge gap

Many exercise studies have reflected the benefit of 6 to 12 weeks of exercise in motivated participants with good compliance; however, these results demonstrate that adherence is often a limiting factor in the power of results, which is a gap this study aims to address [6, 7].

In real-world situations, adherence to exercise, particularly high-intensity exercise, may fall below desirable levels and hinder the effectiveness of the programs [6]. This may be due, in part, to perceived benefits and barriers to exercise in older PWH. Despite the growing body of research aimed at determinants of adherence to pharmaceutical treatment [8–10], adherence to exercise among PWH remains an under-researched area.

To date, there is limited data on the feasibility and safety of HIIT in older (aged ≥ 50 years) PWH with co-occurring hypertension. The proposed project has broad implications, enhancing participant evaluation and care in the field of aging. By removing critical barriers and addressing key issues of accessibility, the proposed methodology is expected to promote physical, social, and psychological adjustment, independent function, and overall quality of life for older PWH and co-occurring hypertension. This is because the physiological consequences of the aging process are known to be facilitated by HIV and additional comorbidities. Additionally, the proposed study offers a novel target for assessing physiological changes in response to rehabilitative interventions. Thus, the purpose of this pilot feasibility study was to evaluate the efficacy of a HIIT intervention for improving the physical function of older PWH and co-occurring hypertension.

Methods

Study design

A single-group, pre-post intervention study was carried out. PWH and co-occurring hypertension were recruited from a local HIV/AIDS clinic. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval from the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB-300009214) was obtained. The study was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on March 13, 2025.

Participants

Older PWH and co-occurring hypertension were screened and invited to participate if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) at least 50 years of age, (2) on an ART regimen, (3) inactive lifestyle (< 150 min per week of moderate activity on Community Health Activities Model Program for Seniors Questionnaire [11]), (4) CD4 count greater than 200 cells/µL (based on clinic labs from the Center for AIDS Research [CFAR] Network of Integrated Clinical Systems [CNICS] visit closest to study entry), and (5) diagnosed hypertensive and being treated. The study clinician performed a physical exam and extensive medical history for clearance to participate in the exercise study. Individuals were excluded if they did not meet the inclusion criteria and/or had chronic kidney disease, severe cardiac conditions, were taking a beta-blocker, had significant cognitive impairments, or had functional limitations hindering their ability to engage in the exercise intervention. Information collected to describe the sample included the following: age, sex, and race.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of a program of high-intensity cycling on an upright stationary bike. The program was performed for up to 45 min, two times a week, for 6 weeks, for a total of 12 sessions. The intervention was carried out at the University of Alabama at Birmingham Center for Exercise Medicine (UCEM), an exercise medicine research facility, under the supervision of exercise physiologists and the clinical care of a nurse practitioner.

The high-intensity cycling training was based on the requirements for training cardiorespiratory fitness. This consisted of a trial session for the high-intensity protocol and a familiarization with the exercise protocol. Exercise intensity was based on age-predicted maximum heart rate (HRmax). Each training session was preceded and followed by a 5-min warm-up and cool-down, respectively. Heart rate was continuously monitored throughout exercise sessions.

Exercise sessions consisted of 4 sets of 4-min interval training at 80% HRmax, progressing to 90% HRmax over 6 weeks. The high-intensity intervals were separated by a 3-min active rest (~ 60–70% HRmax).

Primary outcomes

The criterion for success of this study is based on recruitment, consent rate, retention, attendance rate, intervention adherence, and safety. Recruitment was measured as successfully meeting the target enrollment within the grant funding period. Consent rate was assessed as the percentage of eligible participants who completed the informed consent. Retention was assessed as the percentage of enrolled participants who completed the study. Attendance rate was assessed as the percentage of intervention sessions that the participant completed. Intervention adherence is the number of participants who completed all aspects of the intervention. Safety was assessed by the number of adverse events.

Secondary outcomes

The following outcome measures were assessed in all participants at baseline and post-intervention: BP, perceived benefits and barriers to exercise (via the Exercise Benefits/Barriers Scale [EBBS]), rating of perceived exertion (RPE; Borg Scale 6–20), body composition (body mass index [BMI]), cardiorespiratory fitness (assessed via 6-min walk test [6MWT]), and physical function (via Short Physical Performance Battery [SPPB]). Maintenance of functional status, determined by measures of physical performance, is a good predictor of cardiovascular outcomes among older PWH with co-occurring hypertension [12, 13]. Pain assessments, inflammation, and immune function will be reported in a separate publication.

The EBBS was developed to assess people’s perceptions on the benefits of exercise and barriers to exercise [14]. The instrument has a four-response, forced-choice Likert-type format with responses ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 4 (“Strongly Agree”). Scores on the total instrument can range from 43 to 172, where the higher the score, the more positively the individual perceives exercise [14]. Additionally, the EBBS is separated into two subscales, a 29-item Benefits Scale and a 14-item Barriers Scale. When using the Benefits Scale alone, scores range between 14 and 56. When using the Barriers Scale alone, the higher the score, the greater the perception of barriers to exercise. Items on the EBBS load onto nine factors: life enhancement, physical performance, psychological outlook, exercise milieu, social interaction, time expenditure, preventive health, physical exertion, and family encouragement [15]. Psychometric evaluation yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 for the Benefits Scale and 0.89 for the Barriers Scale [14]. Test-retest reliability was found to be 0.89 on the total instrument, 0.89 on the Benefits Scale, and 0.77 on the Barriers Scale [14]. As such, the EBBS is sufficiently valid and reliable to justify its use in describing exercise perception of various adult populations.

BMI was assessed using height and weight at baseline and post-intervention. BP was assessed using oscillometry following quiet rest for 5 min sitting and 3 min for standing. Additionally, BP was assessed before and after every exercise session for safety (data not reported). Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using the fast-paced 6MWT, as previously described [16, 17]. The 6MWT is a safe and reliable test of aerobic capacity in older adults. The test has strong reproducibility and good correlation with peak oxygen uptake (VO2 max) [18]. Participants were asked to walk as far and as fast as possible for 6 min on a 40-m course. At the end of the test, the total distance walked was recorded.

Additionally, the SPPB was performed as a measure of functional status through the domains of balance, strength, and gait measurements. The balance tests consisted of standing static balance in three positions; lower limb strength through sit-to-stand from a chair; and walking speed at a normal pace (4-m walk test) [19]. Each test is scored from 0 (inability to perform the task) to 4 points (best performance), with total SPPB scores ranging from 0 to 12 points across all tasks [20]. The SPPB has a strong association with cardiovascular risk among older adults [18, 21, 22].

Data analysis

In accordance with the guidance on feasibility and pilot studies, there was no formal hypothesis testing; therefore, quantitative data are summarized using descriptive statistics. Analyses were conducted using STATA SE 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) for descriptive analysis and are presented as mean (Standard Deviation [SD]) unless otherwise stated. No formal sample size calculation was performed as the study was not designed to show statistical significance or power; however, we estimated sample size based on previous literature recommending 10–20 participants per group is sufficient [23–25]. Hedges’ g effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for changes from pre-intervention to post-intervention for secondary outcomes (EBBS, weight, BP, BMI, 6MWT, SPPB, and 4-m walk test). Data collected in this study will allow sample size calculations to be performed for future randomized clinical trials.

Recruitment and retention

Participants were recruited between June 2022 and March 2024. The trial was stopped at the end of the grant funding period, with the minimum recruitment target being achieved. There are approximately 3700 patients at the local HIV/AIDS Clinic that we recruited from, and nearly 70% of them are 50 years of age or older. Of the 26 participants who were deemed eligible via telephone screening, 13 consented, resulting in a 50% consent rate. Reasons provided for not participating included the following: not interested (n = 8); distance to travel to the facility was too far (n = 3); not enough time (n = 1); and currently taking a beta-blocker (n = 1). Of the 13 participants who consented and successfully enrolled in the study, 10 participants completed all aspects of the study (76.9% completion rate). Reasons for the withdrawals during the intervention were time commitment (n = 1), distance to travel (n = 1), and musculoskeletal complication (i.e., foot pain) (n = 1) (Fig. 1).

Secondary outcomes

Summary data for the secondary clinical outcomes are presented in Table 4. Pre-intervention versus post-intervention weight was 101.5 ± 41.5 kg versus 98.2 ± 35.2 kg. BMI declined pre-to-post-intervention (33.3 ± 12.4 kg/m2 vs. 32.3 ± 10.8 kg/m2; Hedges’ g = 0.1). Pre-intervention systolic BP was 133.4 ± 9.0 mmHg, while post-intervention was 128.1 ± 9.9 mmHg with a medium effect (Hedges’ g = 0.5). Diastolic BP declined pre-intervention to post-intervention (80.2 ± 6.5 mmHg vs. 76 ± 8.6 mmHg) with a medium effect size (Hedges’ g = 0.5). Distance walked in the 6MWT declined following the intervention with a small effect (pre-intervention: 419.7 ± 80.4 m vs. post-intervention: 414.7 ± 51.6 m; Hedges’ g = 0.1). Results from the SPPB showed improvements following the intervention, though the effect size was small (pre-intervention: 9.1 ± 2.0 vs. post-intervention: 9.5 ± 2.2; Hedges’ g = 0.2). Finally, participants completed the 4-m walk test faster post-intervention with a small effect size (pre-intervention: 4.0 ± 0.9 s vs. post-intervention: 3.7 ± 1.0 s; Hedges’ g = 0.3). Discussion

Our goal was to assess the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of a 6-week HIIT program as an intervention for older PWH and co-occurring hypertension. We observed retention and adherence rates of 76.9% and 100%, respectively, in the present study. In completers, we found a decrease in perceived barriers and an increase in perceived benefits of exercise. Additionally, participants exhibited improvements in BMI, systolic and diastolic BP, and SPPB scores, but 6MWT distance decreased, suggesting a potential deterioration in cardiorespiratory fitness due to the onset of fatigue post-intervention [26]. Additional factors contributing to this may be the short intervention duration not allowing full physiological adaptation or muscle recovery and pain [27, 28]. These findings suggest that HIIT is a feasible and safe strategy for older PWH for the management of co-occurring hypertension, but future studies should focus on defining the optimal frequency, duration, and intensity for improving fitness in this population.

Feasibility

This study assessed feasibility based on recruitment, consent rate, retention, and adherence to a structured exercise intervention. Recruitment successfully met the minimum target within the grant funding period, demonstrating feasibility in enrolling older PWH and co-occurring hypertension from a specialized clinic. However, the 50% consent rate highlights barriers such as lack of interest (n = 8) and logistical challenges, including travel distance (n = 3) and time constraints (n = 1). Retention was relatively high at 76.9%, though attrition was influenced by time commitment, travel, and foot pain. Attendance was strong, with participants completing 86% of sessions and achieving 100% adherence to prescribed exercise intensity during high-intensity bouts. Taken together, the completion rate of 76.9% is a strong indicator of feasibility utilizing HIIT training in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension. This completion rate is aligned with previous studies involving exercise in adults living with HIV [29, 30]. These feasibility findings provide support for planning a larger, future trial.

While feasible, challenges remain that may affect future implementation. Recruitment difficulties linked to logistical and personal barriers suggest a need for improved engagement strategies. The recurring issues of travel distance and time commitment emphasize the importance of accessible delivery methods, such as telehealth or localized programs. Addressing health-related concerns through individualized exercise modifications is also critical. Despite these challenges, the study supports the feasibility of implementing structured exercise interventions in this population, with tailored approaches needed to enhance accessibility and optimize long-term engagement.

Safety

Adverse events are common, undesirable occurrences that may or may not be related to the research study. Foot pain was experienced (n = 1) during the exercise sessions within this study. Strategies implemented in our research study to limit adverse events from the exercise intervention included the following: (1) identifying pre-existing conditions that could exacerbate adverse and serious adverse events, (2) implementing a structured supervised exercise protocol, and (3) implementing a progressive exercise intensity ramping protocol. No serious adverse events were reported in the study, supporting the safety of structured, supervised exercise interventions in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension. These findings suggest that the intervention was safe and well-tolerated, supporting the feasibility of using this model in future studies to further explore the benefits of structured exercise in similar populations.

Efficacy

Notably, we found meaningful improvements in BMI and BP over the course of our 6-week-long intervention. Though the effect size was medium (0.1–0.5), these findings highlight the efficacy of HIIT in improving cardiovascular disease risk in older PWH with existing comorbidities.

Exercise is an essential component of cardiovascular health and cardiovascular disease prevention, but barriers do exist [4]. The top barrier reported at pre-intervention was “Exercise tires me.” Further bolstering this claim, the second most frequently reported barrier was “Exercise is hard work for me.” Following the intervention, our participants still reported “Exercise tires me” as their top barrier to participation. However, “I am fatigued by exercise” was the second most reported barrier, and “Exercise is hard work for me” was the third post-intervention. Our findings indicate that older PWH have major concerns about their heightened fatigue and physical capacity to perform strenuous activity. These findings are aligned with previous studies observing the barriers to exercise in PWH, which also highlight fatigue and pain as prominent barriers for this population [31]. These concerns may be further inflated once an exercise program is initiated, suggesting that future interventions should incorporate strategies to combat fatigue during exercise in this population.

Prior to the intervention, participants’ top reported benefit was “Exercise improves the way my body looks.” The other top four benefits were related to exercise-induced improvements in health status and extension of lifespan. Post-intervention, participants’ top reported benefit of exercise was improvements in BP. Other top benefits included enhancements in participants’ sense of self-efficacy and stress relief, as well as improvements in cardiorespiratory and musculoskeletal function. The improved symptoms (i.e., looking better and blood pressure) are consistent with the findings of others who have looked at facilitators to exercise engagement for PWH [31, 32]. We surmise that although the physical benefits of exercise may be the initial source of motivation for older PWH with co-occurring hypertension, psychological and emotional benefits may be more significant after initiation. Future studies may consider these changes in perceived benefits to examine effective health behavior strategies in older PWH with existing comorbidities.

Interestingly, we found an increase in SPPB score from pre- to post-intervention, suggesting exercise-related improvements in balance, lower-limb strength, and walking speed. However, the opposite was found for the 6MWT. Because the 6MWT is associated with cardiorespiratory fitness, these findings imply that exercise may negatively impact VO2max in PWH. Increased perceptions of fatigue with regular exercise may have resulted in decreased exertion during subsequent bouts, ultimately producing declines in VO2max. This is also showcased by the changes in RPE during the exercise interventions, as they increased across the 6 weeks of HIIT. Alternatively, PWH may have unique physiological adaptations to exercise, producing impaired cardiorespiratory responses. Regardless, further research is warranted.

Comparison to literature

The findings of this study align with previous research on exercise interventions in PWH and other chronic conditions. Similar studies have shown that structured exercise programs are associated with improved health outcomes, including better functional capacity, reduced BP, and enhanced perceptions of exercise benefits [4, 7, 33, 34]. However, much of this literature has focused on younger or middle-aged adults, with limited attention given to older PWH and even fewer explicitly addressing co-occurring hypertension. As hypertension is one of the most prevalent comorbidities among aging HIV populations, the present pilot feasibility study sets the foundation for future work on this important topic.

Most exercise interventions for PWH have emphasized moderate-intensity aerobic training, with HIIT being recently a newer topic to focus on [4, 35, 36]. By focusing specifically on HIIT in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension, this pilot feasibility study addresses a critical gap in the literature and provides preliminary evidence that HIIT can be delivered safely and feasibly in this population, while evoking meaningful blood pressure improvements.

Consistent with prior research, barriers such as logistical challenges, time constraints, and health-related issues remain prevalent and may hinder participation; thus, they should be considered for implementation [4, 37, 38]. Notably, adverse events were infrequent and mild, aligning with previous evidence that exercise interventions are associated with fewer and less severe adverse events compared to pharmacologic and surgical approaches [39]. Muscle soreness is the most common adverse event reported in response to exercise interventions [40].

These findings suggest that HIIT may represent a viable and efficient exercise modality for older PWH and co-occurring hypertension, while highlighting the need for targeted strategies to address mobility limitations and logistical barriers. This pilot feasibility study expands the existing literature by demonstrating feasibility in this high-risk population that has been largely excluded from HIIT research, while informing the design of future comparative trials in aging with HIV and other chronic conditions. To our knowledge, this is among the first pilot feasibility studies to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effects of HIIT, specifically in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension.

Limitations

Limitations for this study include, but are not limited to, the small sample size, pilot study design, single-center recruitment, and limited generalizability. This single-group pre-post design with recruitment from a single HIV clinic did not include randomization or a control group, which could have introduced bias and limits the ability to attribute observed changes solely to the exercise intervention. The small sample size of this study limits the generalizability of the findings to the larger HIV population. Additionally, recruitment from a single HIV clinic may introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of the preliminary findings to other PWH receiving care in other settings. Although pilot studies’ research methods can be tested for reliability and feasibility, other results of a pilot study cannot be used to test the efficacy and effectiveness of the intervention. Ultimately, the small sample size pilot study is optimal for testing the strategies of an intervention and the retention rates of the recruited population. Finally, this study was not designed to understand mechanisms underlying the changes observed in blood pressure following the intervention (e.g., pulse wave velocity, flow mediated dilation, inflammation, etc.); however, blood pressure was included in this study as a clinically relevant outcome.

Strengths

Our study had some major strengths. For one, we experienced success in the recruitment and retention of older PWH in a high-intensity exercise intervention. This challenges the widespread belief that perceived barriers to exercise generally prevent the recruitment of PWH in such research studies. Additionally, we found that HIIT was a quick and convenient, yet effective, intervention for improving perceived self-efficacy and physical function in older PWH with co-occurring hypertension. As such, our findings have broad implications for future gerontological, HIV, and rehabilitation science research. Specifically, future research should focus on optimizing exercise recommendations for older PWH to maximize physical benefits whilst considering this population’s distinct perceptions of barriers and benefits to exercise.

Implications and future directions

To scale up and optimize recruitment strategies, future studies should consider implementing additional community-based recruitment initiatives to increase awareness and accessibility. Expanding from the clinic and partnering with local organizations, including HIV/AIDS advocacy groups and healthcare providers, could enhance outreach and foster trust within the target population. Utilizing digital platforms for recruitment, such as social media or patient registries, may also broaden the reach and attract a more diverse participant pool. Additionally, it is important to recognize and address the reported barriers of the participants (e.g., time commitment, travel distance, foot pain) for real-world intervention scalability and implementation. This could be addressed through a remotely delivered intervention and/or alternative exercise modalities to reduce the logistical burden and improve the intervention accessibility.

Overall, the pilot feasibility study was very successful, but we recognize there is always room for improvement. To address barriers, we can incorporate flexible intervention designs, such as hybrid models combining in-person and virtual sessions. This can help accommodate participants with travel or time constraints. Secondly, addressing fatigue as part of the HIV sequelae is important and was a major barrier that participants mentioned during the qualitative measures. Additionally, we can offer transportation assistance or stipends for travel expenses to mitigate the logistical challenges. Ultimately, providing education about the benefits of exercise and addressing misconceptions or concerns can further improve engagement and consent rates. Future studies should evaluate hybrid and digitally supported exercise models, such as remote patient monitoring or mHealth strategies, alongside community-engaged partnerships to improve reach and accessibility.

Conclusions

This pilot feasibility study highlights both the need for more exercise-related data in PWH, as well as the potential benefits of HIIT training in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension. This pilot also shows that a HIIT protocol is feasible and safe in this population. Our findings showcase the need for more innovation in the physical function space for older PWH and co-occurring hypertension to maintain independence and quality of life. While this pilot produced promising results, a fully powered study is warranted as gaps in the literature related to HIIT in older PWH and co-occurring hypertension still exist.
