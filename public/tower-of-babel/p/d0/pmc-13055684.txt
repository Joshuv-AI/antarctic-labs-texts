Hypertension in Overweight and Obesity: Sex‐Specific Role of 4β‐Hydroxycholesterol
Authors: Roosa Rahunen, Heta Merikallio, Valtteri Rinne, Markku J Savolainen, Janne Hukkanen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe have previously proposed novel pathway in overweight/obesity‐induced hypertension involving plasma 4β‐hydroxycholesterol (4βHC), an agonist for LXR (liver X receptor), as a hypotensive factor. The aim was to determine sex‐specific roles of 4βHC in obesity‐induced hypertension in a cohort without blood pressure (BP) medications.MethodsA study population (n=645) with wide body mass index range (19–47 kg/m2) was analyzed for plasma 4βHC. Factors determining BP were analyzed in multiple linear regression analyses. Also, sex‐specific effects of pharmacologically elevated 4βHC, achieved through rifampicin administration, were evaluated.ResultsPlasma 4βHC correlated negatively with systolic BP and body mass index in both sexes. In men (n=282), the correlation between 4βHC and systolic BP was r=−0.19 (P=0.002) and in women (n=363) r=−0.20 (P<0.001). Correlation between plasma 4βHC and BMI in men was r=−0.44 (P<0.001), and women r=−0.37 (P<0.001). In a multiple linear regression with both sexes included, plasma 4βHC along with age, body mass index, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol predicted systolic BP. In men, only age and body mass index were predictors of systolic BP, whereas in women, only age and 4βHC were significant factors. The same sex‐specific pattern regarding 4βHC was detected for diastolic BP and mean arterial pressure. However, in men, the pharmacologic 4βHC elevation strengthened the inverse correlations between BP indices and 4βHC, whereas in women these associations were weakened.ConclusionsPlasma 4βHC was associated inversely with BP, especially in women, whereas pharmacologically elevated 4βHC seemed to affect men more pronouncedly. What Is New?

Our findings suggest that plasma 4β‐hydroxycholesterol, an agonist for liver X receptor and a proposed hypotensive factor, has a sex‐specific role in overweight‐ and obesity‐induced hypertension.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

As the increasing body mass index is known to be more clearly associated with hypertension in women than in men, our finding on the association of overweight/obesity‐repressed plasma 4β‐hydroxycholesterol with blood pressure regulation in women with overweight and obesity may offer a mechanistic explanation for this phenomenon.

According to the World Obesity Federation, obesity (body mass index [BMI] >30 kg/m2) is a chronic, progressive, relapsing disease, and it is justified to state that the world suffers from an obesity epidemic. 1 Obesity is a risk factor for various common diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, cancer, hypertension, stroke, and cardiovascular diseases. 1 On the other hand, hypertension is the primary cause for disability and years of life lost globally, and it is a major risk factor for stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, and end‐stage kidney disease. 1 , 2 , 3 Over a billion people in the world have hypertension. 2 , 4 Individuals with obesity have a 3.5‐fold risk of hypertension, and 60% of hypertension cases are caused by increased adiposity. 5 Proposed mechanisms for obesity‐related hypertension are the activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system mediated by mechanical compression of kidneys, as well as sympathetic nervous system activation elicited by hyperleptinemia, hyperinsulinemia, or obstructive sleep apnea leading to impaired renal natriuresis and vasoconstriction. 6 , 7 Also the metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, dysglycemia, and metabolic syndrome are associated not only with obesity but with inflammation, oxidative stress, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone, and sympathetic nervous system activation and insulin resistance involved in the development of hypertension. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Regulation of blood pressure (BP) and hypertension prevalence are dissimilar in men and women. The incidence of hypertension is lower in premenopausal women compared with men of similar age, but subsequently BP increases more in women than men, leading to similar levels of hypertension in later years, especially after menopause. 15 , 16 In women, the association of hypertension with obesity is stronger compared with men, and increasing BMI has a stronger association with increasing BP levels in women than in men. 17

We have previously proposed a novel pathway for obesity‐induced hypertension involving 4β‐hydroxycholesterol (4βHC), an oxysterol, as a hypotensive factor. Circulating oxysterols are oxidation products of cholesterol, and one of the most abundant oxysterols is 4βHC, formed by cytochrome P450 3A enzymes in the liver. 18 Expression of cytochrome P450 3A is mainly regulated by PXR (pregnane X receptor NR1I2). 19 PXR can be activated by a wide array of xenobiotics, such as environmental toxicants, natural compounds such as flavonoids, 20 and medications that can increase circulating 4βHC concentrations several fold. 21 The 4βHC is an agonist for LXRs (liver X receptors) α (NRH13) and β (NRH12). 22 , 23 LXR has been linked to various homeostatic systems, such as regulation of BP. 24

We have previously shown that plasma 4βHC has an inverse correlation with systolic BP and BMI in a population with a wide BMI range; overweight and obesity repress plasma 4βHC significantly, and lower 4βHC concentrations are associated with higher systolic BP. In addition, elevated circulating 4βHC lowers systolic BP in rats. 25 In this study, the aim was to further explore PXR‐4βHC‐LXR pathway in overweight‐ and obesity‐induced hypertension and especially the sex‐specific aspects of 4βHC‐mediated BP regulation in a study population with healthy volunteers and participants with overweight and obesity, excluding the subjects with any antihypertensive medications. In addition, the sex‐specific effects of PXR activation‐elevated 4βHC were explored.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

A total of 7 previously performed clinical trials and 1 cohort study were combined for this analysis. We used 4 trials with PXR agonist rifampicin (n=62) 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 and patients with obesity from a gastric bypass surgery cohort (n=41). 30 Participants of rifampicin trials included individuals who were healthy and lean or overweight, age between 18 and 45 years and BMI between 19 and 30 kg/m2. Rifa‐1, 26 Rifa‐BP, 27 and Rifa‐Stea 28 trials had a randomized placebo‐controlled crossover design with a minimum of 4‐week wash‐out period. The fourth trial (Rifa‐2), 29 had a 1‐arm design with no control arm. Rifa‐1, Rifa‐2, and Rifa‐Stea had an open design whereas Rifa‐BP was single blind with study personnel blinded. The subjects in all 4 studies were administered 600 mg rifampicin (Rimapen, Orion Inc., Espoo, Finland) once a day for a week. Gastric bypass surgery study individuals (age between 18 and 65 years) had a medically indicated need for the Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery. Before the surgery the mean BMI was 45 kg/m2 (range 36–55 kg/m2). The subjects had a preoperative visit and a postoperative visit at 6 months, including basic anthropometric measurements and laboratory tests. More details of the experimental protocol are described in the previous publication. 25 The placebo arm values of the rifampicin trials (Rifa‐1, Rifa‐2, Rifa‐BP, and Rifa‐Stea) and preoperative values of Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery cohort were combined in a nonintervention cohort (n=103). Only the patients without hypertension diagnosis or BP‐lowering medications were included, therefore 23 subjects from Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery cohort were excluded. Also, control subjects from Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery cohort were excluded (n=7), and 1 subject from rifampicin trials was excluded due to missing 4βHC/4α‐hydroxycholesterol value. After exclusions, n=72 subject from the nonintervention cohort were included in this study. A flow chart (Figure S1) represents the composition of the study population, including the reasons and numbers for subjects excluded.

In addition to the nonintervention cohort, baseline data of 2 trials exploring health behavior change support system (HBCSS)‐assisted weight loss were included in this analysis. The PrevMetSyn (Prevention of Metabolic Syndrome) trial 31 randomized lifestyle interventions for subjects with overweight and obesity (n=532) recruited from the population registry in the city of Oulu, Finland. BMI range of participants was 27 to 35 kg/m2 and age between 20 and 60 years. HBCSS using persuasive systems design and aspects of cognitive behavioral therapy was developed with the aim to help individuals to lose weight. PrevMetSyn trial compared cognitive behavioral therapy‐based group counseling combined with HBCSS and HBCSS alone, and the main outcome was weight loss at 12 and 24 months from baseline. The FeelGood trial 32 explored whether mobile HBCSS is effective in weight reduction without additional counseling. The trial included 200 participants with BMI between 30 and 40 kg/m2 and age 18 to 65 years. Participants were randomized into 2 groups: a group with immediate access to HBCSS and a second group with access after 6 months (wait‐list control). The main outcome was weight loss at 6 months. More details of the experimental protocols are described in previous publications. 31 , 32 All subjects with BP‐lowering medications were excluded (88 subjects from PrevMetSyn trial and 67 subjects from FeelGood trial, Figure S1). Also, a few subjects from FeelGood were excluded (n=4) due to unusually high 4α‐hydroxycholesterol levels, which were used as an indicator of poor sample preservation. 18 One female subject from PrevMetSyn missed BMI value; therefore, the number of subjects is 1 less in BMI analyses. In total, 72 subjects from the nonintervention cohort, 444 subjects from PrevMetSyn, and 129 subjects from FeelGood were combined to construct the population of this study. Total of 645 subjects included 282 men and 363 women (Figure S1). Ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with high resolution mass spectrometry was used as previously described 33 for the measurement of plasma 4α‐hydroxycholesterol and 4βHC at Admescope (Symeres Finland Ltd, Oulu, Finland).

In addition, to explore the sex‐specific aspects of pharmacologically elevated plasma 4βHC on office BP, additional analyses were performed on the data of the participants in the 4 rifampicin trials (men n=40, women n=21). Also, analyses were performed on 24‐hour ambulatory and awake/asleep BP data of Rifa‐BP trial subjects alone (men n=14, women n=7). 27 Office BP was measured twice with 5‐minute intervals and the average of the 2 measures was calculated in all the trials and cohorts included. In Rifa‐BP trial, ambulatory BP was measured with a 24‐hour BP monitoring device Oscar2 (AccuWin PRO version 3 software; SunTech Medical, Morrisville, NC) at the end of each arm on the eighth day for about 24 hours ending on the morning of day 9. The measurements were automatically performed with 30‐minute intervals during daytime and with 60‐minute intervals at night.

As menopausal status affects BP levels in women, additional analyses were conducted separately for pre‐ and postmenopausal women. Without the information of the menopausal status from every participant, we used age of 51 years as a cutoff age, as it is considered the mean menopausal age in many nationalities, including Finnish women. 34 Altogether, 249 women were under the age of 51 years and 114 women were aged ≥51 years.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical computations of clinical data were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (IBM, Armonk, NY), and data illustrations with GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). Descriptive statistics were analyzed for the whole study population, and separately for subsets of studies for men and women (Table S1A and S1B). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to analyze the correlation of 4βHC with hemodynamic parameters and BMI, and scatter plots were compiled to present the results. Analyses performed only for the rifampicin trials and Rifa‐BP trial participants included correlation analyses and paired 2‐tailed Student’s t test to analyze the effect of rifampicin on BP and other hemodynamic parameters. The statistical difference between groups were calculated using 1‐way ANOVA.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to investigate the effects of various independent variables to our variables of interest for men and women separately and together. In multiple regression analyses, the main dependent variable of interest was systolic BP, and the analyses were also conducted for diastolic BP, mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR). Enter‐method were used in analyses. Unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with SE and P values are reported. Normal distributions of dependent variables were verified with histograms, linearity with P‐P plots, and multicollinearity with independent variables were inspected with variance indicator factor; none of the independent variables had this value >5, therefore no variables needed to be excluded due to multicollinearity. For a result to be considered as statistically significant, a criterion P<0.05 was to be met.

Ethics

All participants in the studies provided written informed consent to participate in the research. All studies were approved by the Regional Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Northern Ostrobothnia Hospital District. The trials involving rifampicin administration were also approved by the Finnish Medicines Agency.

RESULTS

Higher Plasma 4βHC Levels Are Associated With Both Lower Systolic BP and Lower BMI

Systolic BP had a statistically significant inverse correlation with plasma 4βHC in both sexes separately and combined (Figure 1A through 1C). In men, the correlation between 4βHC and systolic BP was r=−0.19 (P=0.002), in women r=−0.20 (P<0.001), and combined r=−0.21 (P<0.0001). For between‐group analyses, systolic BP values were divided into 3 classes: <120, 120 to 140, and >140 mm Hg (Figure 1D through 1F, Table S2A). The statistical significance between these classes was P=0.025 in men, P<0.001 in women, and P<0.0001 combined. Within systolic BP classes in men, the mean 4βHC levels decreased 16% from class <120 mm Hg to class >140 mm Hg, and in women, the corresponding decrease was 17%, as in sexes combined. In addition to systolic BP, correlation analyses and scatter plots were carried out for diastolic BP, MAP, and HR (Figure S2A through S2F). Plasma 4βHC correlation with diastolic BP in men was r=− 0.32 (P<0.0001) and in women r=−0.26 (P<0.0001). Plasma 4βHC and MAP correlated r=−0.29 (P<0.001) in men and r=−0.25 (P<0.0001) in women. There was no statistically significant correlation between 4βHC and HR.

We now report the correlations of plasma 4βHC with the systolic BP and 24‐hour systolic BP in rifampicin and placebo arms for both sexes separately (Figure 3A and 3B, 3D through 3E), as well as the previously reported analyses for men and women combined for reference (Figure 3C and 3F). In male participants, there were no statistically significant correlations with plasma 4βHC and systolic BP in either trial arm (Figure 3A). In women, 4βHC and systolic BP correlation in rifampicin arm was almost the same as in men (r=−0.26 versus r=−0.24, both statistically not significant), but correlation in the placebo arm was significantly stronger (r=−0.60; P=0.004 in women; in men r=−0.05, not significant) (Figure 3A and 3B). When the sexes were combined, the correlation of 4βHC and systolic BP in rifampicin arm was r=−0.46 (P=0.0002) and placebo arm r=−0.45 (P=0.0003) (Figure 3C).

In Rifa‐BP data set for 24‐hour systolic BP, male participants did not have statistically significant correlations with plasma 4βHC and 24‐hour systolic BP in either study arm (Figure 3D). In women (Figure 3E), the correlation in placebo arm was high and statistically significant (r=−0.77; P=0.041) whereas in rifampicin arm the correlation was not statistically significant (r=−0.25, P=0.59). The correlation for the sexes combined in rifampicin arm was r=−0.69 (P<0.001) and placebo arm r=−0.70 (P<0.001) (Figure 3F) as previously reported. 25 Rifampicin dosing increased 24‐hour systolic BP in men and women separately and when the sexes were combined as reported.

The same analyses were repeated for diastolic/24‐hour diastolic BP, MAP/24‐hour MAP, and HR/24‐hour HR for the rifampicin trials population and Rifa‐BP population (Figures S3 through S5). For diastolic BP, 24‐hour diastolic BP, MAP, and 24‐hour MAP, men had better correlations in rifampicin arms than in placebo arms, and in women the results were the opposite. In these sex‐specific analyses, the statistical significance was reached for rifampicin 24‐hour diastolic BP (r=−0.73; P =0.003) (Figure S3D) and MAP (r=−0.62; P =0.018) (Figure S4D) in men and placebo office MAP (r=−0.50; P =0.020) (Figure S4B) in women. In addition, awake/asleep systolic and diastolic BP and MAP as well as nighttime BP dipping and their correlations with plasma 4βHC were determined for the Rifa‐BP population (Table S6). When men and women were analyzed together, awake and asleep systolic and diastolic BP and MAP had significant inverse correlations with 4βHC in rifampicin arm and the placebo arm demonstrated materially similar correlations. Similar to 24‐hour BP analyses, the inverse correlations of awake/asleep systolic and diastolic BP and MAP with plasma 4βHC were numerically stronger in men in the t rifampicin arm than in he placebo arm (especially awake), whereas in women the results were the opposite. Plasma 4βHC had no association with nighttime dipping.

Menopausal Status and Correlations of Plasma 4βHC Levels With Systolic BP and BMI

Premenopausal women had mean systolic BP of 123 mm Hg (SD=15), mean BMI of 31.6 kg/m2 (SD=4), and median plasma 4βHC level of 10.7 ng/mL (interquartile range, 8–14). Postmenopausal women had mean systolic BP of 135 mm Hg (SD=17), mean BMI of 31.3 kg/m2 (SD=3), and median plasma 4βHC of 11.3 ng/mL (interquartile range, 9–15). Distinct differences were observed in correlations between plasma 4βHC, systolic BP, and BMI. Premenopausal women had similar or greater correlations as all women analyzed together. In premenopausal women, the correlation between plasma 4βHC and systolic BP was r=−0.32 (P<0.0001), and correlation between 4βHC and BMI was r=−0.47 (P<0.0001). In postmenopausal women, the correlation between plasma 4βHC and systolic BP was r=−0.05 (P=0.6), and correlation between 4βHC and BMI was r=0.02 (P=0.8).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we expand on our previous finding on the role of plasma 4βHC, an LXR agonist, 22 on overweight‐ and obesity‐induced hypertension with a 6‐times larger study population (n=645) with a wide BMI range of 19 to 47 kg/m2 to focus on the sex‐specific aspects of 4βHC‐mediated BP regulation. Plasma 4βHC was inversely associated with systolic and diastolic BP and MAP as well as BMI but not with HR, in both sexes separately. Severe obesity (BMI >35 kg/m2) repressed 4βHC levels as much as 45% in men and 55% in women, when compared with subjects with normal weight (BMI <25 kg/m2). The effects of excess weight on plasma 4βHC were noticeable already in the transition from normal to overweight range (25–30 kg/m2) as 4βHC levels were 26% lower in men with overweight and 45% lower in women with overweight when compared with people with normal weight.

In multiple linear regression analyses, in addition to age, plasma 4βHC was a predictor of systolic and diastolic BP and MAP in women but not in men, suggesting that there may be a sex specificity in the regulation of BP by 4βHC. Importantly, in regression analyses with men and women combined, plasma 4βHC was a more important predictor for systolic BP than BMI or sex, only age being a statistically more significant predictor. Examining the effect of PXR activation by rifampicin, plasma 4βHC elevation induced with rifampicin seemed to have different impacts in men and women, because in men the inverse correlations with BP and plasma 4βHC were strengthened after rifampicin dosing, whereas in women the inverse correlation values during placebo arms, which correspond to normal homeostatic state, were stronger than during rifampicin arms. In addition, menopausal status appears to influence the associations of plasma 4βHC with systolic BP and BMI, with premenopausal women having stronger inverse correlations than postmenopausal women. Thus, our findings suggest that the PXR‐4βHC‐LXR pathway is a part of the pathophysiology and regulation of obesity‐induced hypertension, with previously undiscovered sex‐specific aspects.

LXR is suggested to be a target for atherosclerosis prevention, and it has also been linked with the regulation of BP through the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system. 35 Chronic LXR activation with synthetic agonists, such as T0901317 and GW3965, inhibits experimentally stimulated renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system and lowers BP. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Blaschke et al. 40 demonstrated that treatment with LXR ligand T0901317 inhibited vascular smooth muscle proliferation and neointima formation in balloon‐injured rat carotid arteries, which could indicate a vasodilatory effect of LXR activation. Further, ApoE (apolipoprotein E)−/− mice, treated with LXR agonist T0901317 for 6 weeks, demonstrated improved vasomotoric function in atherosclerotic arteries, verified with KCl/norepinephrine‐induced vasoconstrictive and acetylcholine‐induced vasorelaxation function analysis. 41

We have proposed liver‐produced circulating 4βHC as a factor signaling the hepatic metabolic state to the peripheral tissues as we have previously demonstrated that in human primary monocyte‐derived macrophages and foam cells in vitro, incubation with 4βHC stimulated cholesterol efflux and induced the cholesterol efflux transporters ABC (ATP‐binding cassette transporter) A1 and ABCG1, known LXR targets. 22 , 42 We have also shown that administration of PXR agonist to healthy volunteers and rats elevated circulating 4βHC and induced ABCA1 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and heart, respectively. 42 In addition, we have studied 4βHC as a prognostic factor separately in men and women in ARTEMIS (Innovation to Reduce Cardiovascular Complications of Diabetes at the Intersection) cohort 43 (n=1946) of coronary artery disease patients. Subjects in the highest quartile (highest plasma 4βHC) had the lowest BMI, the best physical performance, and the use of antihypertensives and the prevalence of type 2 diabetes were the lowest. In other words, subjects in the highest 4βHC quartile had the best cardiovascular profile, as assessed with traditional markers. The median follow‐up time was 8.8 years. Surprisingly, in men, subjects in the highest 4βHC quartile had the highest incidence of overall deaths, cardiac deaths, and especially sudden cardiac deaths. In women, the trend of the end point events was in the opposite direction. In addition, these findings suggest distinct 4βHC and LXR effects in men and women. 43

The PXR‐4βHC‐LXR pathway is intriguing as PXR has a multitude of agonists and antagonists in the environment with possibility of both adverse and beneficial downstream effects via this pathway. 44 The effect of 4βHC on BP has not been extensively studied previously. In our previous smaller study consisting of healthy lean individuals and subjects with obesity, obesity repressed 4βHC levels, and plasma 4βHC was the most significant predictor for systolic BP in multiple linear regression analysis. 25 Strong inverse correlations were uncovered between 4βHC and systolic BP and BMI. With rifampicin, a PXR agonist, trial subjects showed significant elevations in systolic BP levels after treatment but negative correlations with plasma 4βHC were present after both rifampicin arm and placebo study arm. The hypotensive effect of PXR activation‐elevated 4βHC was also detected in rats. The ambulatory BP of rats was monitored after pregnenolone 16α‐carbonitrile (rodent PXR agonist) injections for 6 days, which led to 9‐fold increase in serum 4βHC levels, and significant decrease in systolic BP were revealed after pregnenolone 16α‐carbonitrile dosing was terminated and elevated 4βHC was left to act alone on BP.

There are few studies on 4βHC and BP regulation by other research groups. Shimoda et al. 45 administered a diet supplement with cholic acid, the primary 12α‐hydroxylated bile acid, to rats to detect changes in BP. A diet supplement with cholic acid has been demonstrated to induce hepatic lipid accumulation in rats. 46 Rats received diet with cholic acid or control for 13 weeks, and rats with cholic acid supplementation showed increase in systolic and diastolic BP, as well as increase in hepatic lipid concentrations, including 4βHC. In total, 8 oxysterols were analyzed from both groups; 4 showed significant increase in concentration in cholic acid group, and 4βHC had the highest increase in hepatic content. 39 As certain cholic acid metabolites are known to be PXR agonists, 47 these findings support our results on the BP‐elevating effect of PXR activation. However, in the study by Shimoda et al., hepatic 4βHC concentration in rats was positively associated with systolic and diastolic BP. 45 The discrepancy between our finding of an inverse association with plasma 4βHC and BP is probably explained by the analysis of Shimoda et al. combining both control and cholic acid groups in the same correlation analysis, which would reveal the effect of PXR activation by cholic acid and not the effect of 4βHC elevation as such on BP. Oxysterol profile in subjects with obesity differs significantly between men and women. 48 In this study by Tremblay‐Franco et al., circulating oxysterols were measured from subjects with obesity, metabolic syndrome and sex‐matched healthy controls. In men, subjects with obesity had significantly lower 4βHC levels compared with controls; a wide array of oxysterols were analyzed, and only 4βHC levels were lower in subjects with obesity or metabolic syndrome. In women, 4βHC was also lower in obesity and metabolic syndrome, but changes in other oxysterols were also present. 48 Correlation analysis between several oxysterols and fatty acids were calculated; 4βHC correlated with various fatty acids that are end products of de novo lipogenesis, reflecting the previous finding that 4βHC dosing in mice activates lipogenesis via LXR. 49 Furthermore, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease is associated with lower plasma 4βHC, reflecting reduced hepatic cytochrome P450 3A4 expression and activity. 50 , 51 In addition to obesity, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, and metabolic syndrome, chronic low grade inflammatory states, the plasma levels of 4βHC have been observed to be affected by other inflammatory states. In the study performed by Hirai et al. 52 in patients with asthma and controls, lower plasma 4βHC levels were associated with increased concentrations of interleukin‐6, an inflammatory cytokine.

Our study has certain limitations including that we mostly report associations of plasma 4βHC with BP indices and no causal statements are warranted based on our findings reported here. Also of note, the strength of the correlations between plasma 4βHC and BP is weaker in the current full cohort including obesity trial participants in comparison with the correlations that we have reported previously on healthy volunteers. 25 , 27 This may be explained by the inclusion of older patients in the current analysis, with presumably stiffer arteries not as responsive to the possibly vasodilating effects of 4βHC. It is known that both obesity and metabolic syndrome are associated with arterial stiffness. 53 , 54 We also did not have exact information on the menopausal status or measured estradiol, testosterone, or other sex hormones that would have provided more insight into sex‐specific BP regulation. In addition, obesity trial participants had only 1 research visit at the baseline whereas rifampicin trial volunteers had also a separate screening visit. Thus, healthy volunteers were familiar with the trial staff and premises at the trial start, which might have lessened the effect of stress‐induced catecholamines on BP and enabled the effect of 4βHC on BP to be more pronounced. Another limitation is that the number of subjects in rifampicin trials is quite small, especially when analyzed by sex. Although rifampicin trials allow us to infer causality regarding rifampicin dosing‐elicited BP elevation, this same phenomenon negates our ability to make causal statements regarding the hypotensive effect of rifampicin dosing‐elicited 4βHC elevation. Finally, the waist circumference measurements were not performed in the rifampicin trials, and we were thus unable to explore the adipose tissue distribution or waist‐to‐height ratio in relation to plasma 4βHC and BP.

CONCLUSIONS

We demonstrate in a large cohort of persons with a wide BMI range that plasma 4βHC is associated inversely with BP indices, especially in women. For systolic and diastolic BP as well as MAP, plasma 4βHC was a significant predictor in multiple linear regression analyses in women but not in men. However, in men, the pharmacologic 4βHC elevation strengthened the inverse correlations between BP indices and 4βHC. Thus, we propose that PXR‐4βHC‐LXR axis may represent a new sex‐specific pathophysiological mechanism in overweight‐ and obesity‐induced hypertension. As the increasing BMI is known to be more clearly associated with hypertension in women than in men, our findings may offer a mechanistic explanation for this phenomenon. Further trials designed to provide direct evidence on the sex‐specific hypotensive effects of 4βHC are warranted.
