Spontaneous activity of astrocytes is a stochastic functional signal for memory consolidation
Authors: Gabriele Losi, Beatrice Vignoli, Rocco Granata, Annamaria Lia, Micaela Zonta, Gabriele Sansevero, Francesca Pischedda, Angela Chiavegato, Spartaco Santi, Lorena Zentilin, Nicoletta Berardi, Gian Michele Ratto, Giorgio Carmignoto, Marco Canossa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceLosi G., Vignoli B. et al. demonstrate that recurring, spontaneous intracellular Ca2+ fluctuations in perisynaptic astrocytic processes [Ca2+ microdomains (MDs)] are functional signals required for long-term potentiation and memory retention. The inherent stochastic behavior of spontaneous Ca2+ MDs in astrocytes opens new avenues for exploring the contribution of nondeterministic operations in brain functioning.

In recent years, the prevailing neuro-centric view of the brain has been challenged by the growing evidence of a functional role played by astrocytes in multiple brain functions (1–6). Astrocytes respond to synaptic neurotransmitters, such as glutamate, gamma-aminobutyric acid, acetylcholine, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and noradrenaline, with cytosolic Ca2+ elevations mediated by astrocytic G protein–coupled membrane receptors (1, 2). This evoked Ca2+ response can initiate at thin astrocytic processes surrounding synaptic sites, known as Ca2+ microdomains (MDs) (3). From there, the Ca2+ response may either remain confined to these localized regions or slowly spread to main processes and soma, resulting in global Ca2+ elevations (4–8). In response to synaptic neurotransmitters, astrocytes release various gliotransmitters that regulate synaptic plasticity and behavioral states (2, 9–13). Accordingly, the astrocytic Ca2+ response evoked by neuronal activity is commonly believed to represent the key signaling in the functional recruitment of astrocytes to specific brain circuits (1, 14, 15). Astrocytes also display spontaneous Ca2+ activity (16), predominantly at MDs, regulated by a still unclear mechanism involving both Ca2+ influx (8, 17, 18) and Ca2+ release from endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (19) and mitochondria (7, 20, 21). The role of spontaneous MDs is essentially unknown also due to their highly complex spatial-temporal dynamics and stochastic nature (4, 7, 22–24).

Previous studies in the perirhinal cortex (PRC) circuitry have provided clues for understanding the specific relevance of MDs within the broader context of neuron–astrocyte interactions. These studies reveal that following a brief, high-frequency θ-burst stimulation (TBS), neuronal afferents to the PRC release a precursor form of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (proBDNF), which is subsequently taken up by astrocytes via binding to the panneurotrophin receptor p75NTR and internalized through clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the receptor–ligand complex (25). Once internalized, proBDNF is processed into its active cleavage products, prodomain, and mature BDNF, which are then rereleased by astrocytes (26, 27). The following activation of Tropomyosin Receptor Kinase B (TrkB) receptors in postsynaptic neurons leads to long-lasting synaptic strengthening in PRC circuitry and supports recognition memory retention (26, 27). To be effective, however, TrkB receptors must be phosphorylated for a critical time window of several minutes following TBS (26, 28, 29). Accordingly, BDNF must be released by astrocytes for a similar time. We here advance the hypothesis that spontaneous Ca2+ MDs represent in astrocytes functional recurring signals with the requested spatial-temporal properties to warrant the prolonged TrkB phosphorylation (pTrkB) essential to synaptic strengthening and memory function.

Results

Spontaneous and Evoked MD Activity during Long-Term Potentiation (LTP).

We studied Ca2+ signal dynamics in astrocytes from PRC layer II/III after local injections with the adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) AAV-GCaMP6f (AAV5 pZac2.1 gfaABC1D-cyto-GCaMP6f) (30) and AAV-tdTomato (AAV5 pZac2.1 gfaABC1D-tdTomato) (8), transducing the cytosolic Ca2+ indicator GCaMP6f and the reporter tdTomato, respectively. Using two-photon (2P) Ca2+ microscopy in PRC slice preparations, we evaluated astrocytic GCaMP6f fluorescence fluctuations, both at rest and in response to TBS eliciting LTP (28). Imaging data were analyzed with custom MatLab code (Image Gateway), specifically designed to use the statistics of the time series provided by each pixel of the images to extract the spatial-temporal evolution of the Ca2+ events (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2).

Astrocytes, prior to TBS application, exhibit consistent activity at MDs, while Ca2+ elevations at main processes and soma are rare (Fig. 1 A–C and Movie S1). Our statistical analysis reports key aspects of spontaneous MDs, including frequency, amplitude, surface area, and duration (Fig. 1D). These inherent characteristics are similarly observed in in vivo astrocytes from the somatosensory cortex of anesthetized mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C), as 2P microscopy cannot be applied in the inaccessible PRC. We then evaluated whether spontaneous MDs operate independently of neuronal firing activity by blocking neuronal action potentials with Tetrodotoxin (TTX). After perfusing slices with 1 µM TTX, we observed that none of the MD properties is affected (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We also assessed potential intrinsic synchronization among spontaneous MDs by comparing the instantaneous cross-correlation between all possible MD pairs. Our data reveal only a weak correlation among pairs of MDs occurring within a proximity of less than 6 µm (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). This limited correlation is not a result of random positioning of uncorrelated events, as demonstrated by Monte Carlo shuffling of the traces (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). However, we cannot rule out that neighboring correlated MDs may represent portions of an individual MD distributed in different focal planes. Overall, our analysis indicates that spontaneous MDs are autonomous, stochastic events in PRC astrocytes.

The evaluation of Ca2+ dynamics provides insights into how the different astrocytic subregions respond to TBS. The delivery of TBS evokes large Ca2+ transients at astrocytic soma and proximal processes and a highly variable or no response at MDs. We observed that only a subset of MDs, presumably those in contact with activated synapses, responds to the first TBS train (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). The subsequent TBS trains do not consistently recruit the same MDs (Fig. 1C), possibly due to different adaptation and responsiveness among the MD population. In contrast, main processes and somata display a more robust and uniform response to all TBS trains with an amplitude that gradually diminishes with each successive train (Fig. 1B). To quantify this different regional behavior, we identified astrocyte main processes by segmenting the area of brightest tdTomato fluorescence corresponding to the 90th percentile of the signal and found that MDs hardly overlap the outline of the main processes (Fig. 1G). We then computed the cross-correlation of the somatic Ca2+ response to the TBS with the response measured in all pixels belonging to either main processes or MDs. The cumulative distribution of the cross-correlation shows that the Ca2+ responses at the main processes are strongly coupled to those at the soma, whereas the MD responses are fundamentally uncoupled (Fig. 1H).

Immediately after TBS, when the evoked Ca2+-response at main processes and somata is ended, MDs are rare (Fig. 1B). However, within 1 to 2 min, MDs reinitiate their spontaneous activity and fully recover the frequency and all the other characteristics to the levels observed prior to stimulation (Fig. 1 B–D, SI Appendix, Fig. S5C, and Movies S2 and S3). The lack of cross-correlation observed in MDs before TBS also remains unchanged after TBS (Fig. 1 E and F). Altogether, these data indicate that TBS-eliciting LTP does not affect the overall dynamics of spontaneous MDs.

Astrocyte Ca2+ Signals Are Required for LTP Consolidation.

To investigate the role of astrocyte Ca2+ signals in LTP, we injected the mouse PRC layer II/III with two AAVs specifically and efficiently targeting astrocytes without affecting their overall morphology (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–D). The AAV-hPMCA2 (AAV2/5.GfaABC1DmCherry-hPMCA2w/b) (13) transduces the Ca2+ pump isoform type 2 that extrudes cytosolic Ca2+, while the AAV-p130PH (AAV5-pZac2.1-GfaABC1D.cyto-mRFP-p130PH) (31) transduces the Pleckstrin Homology domain of PLC-like protein p130 that selectively disrupts PLC/IP3 signaling pathway by buffering cytosolic IP3 (Fig. 2A). Initially, we tested each construct individually. 2P Ca2+ imaging in mice injected with AAV-GCaMP6f shows that both the spontaneous MD activity (pre- and post-TBS) and the Ca2+ responses evoked by TBS are reduced when either AAV-hPMCA2 or AAV-p130PH is expressed alone, as compared to control astrocytes transduced with AAV-tdTomato (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). However, double transduction with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH more consistently and robustly suppresses MD activity (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–D). In parallel field recording experiments, slices from control AAV-tdTomato mice exhibit the expected long-lasting LTP (L-LTP) in response to TBS, maintaining the synaptic potentiation throughout the entire 180-min recording period (Fig. 2C). In contrast, slices from AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH mice exhibited only a short-lasting potentiation (S-LTP) that persisted for 80 to 120 min (Fig. 2C). Notably, the individual transduction of AAV-hPMCA2 or AAV-p130PH results in a lower success rate as compared to the cotransduction condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B), suggesting a correlation between the extent of MD reduction and LTP maintenance. These findings suggest that LTP consolidation depends on astrocytic Ca2+ signaling in the PRC.

The data reported above also suggest that astrocytic Ca2+ signals may act as critical secretory inputs for the release of gliotransmitters essential for L-LTP. To block Ca2+-dependent gliotransmission, we used Glutamate Aspartate Transporter (GLAST)-CreERT2 mice (32) injected with a lentiviral construct (27) for Cre-dependent expression of the tetanus toxin (TeNT) light-chain (LV-TeNTstop). As a control, we injected the contralateral hemisphere with a lentivirus that transduces GFP (LV-GFPstop) in astrocytes. In slices from the control hemisphere, TBS successfully induces L-LTP, whereas in slices from the hemisphere transduced with LV-TeNTstop, TBS is unsuccessful (Fig. 2F), confirming the requirement of Ca2+-dependent gliotransmission in LTP consolidation.

Spontaneous MDs Control BDNF/TrkB Signaling in Neurons.

Among gliotransmitters, BDNF represents a good candidate to be secreted through Ca2+-dependent exocytosis from astrocytes (Fig. 2D), in a process relying on Vesicle-associated membrane protein 2 that can be targeted by TeNT (25). To investigate the involvement of BDNF in the requirement of astrocyte Ca2+ signals for L-LTP, we performed experiments on slices transduced with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH, which show impaired L-LTP. In these slices, bath perfusion with ectopic BDNF (10 ng/mL) for 10 min, starting immediately after TBS, effectively rescues L-LTP (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that astrocytic Ca2+ events, distinctly acting post-TBS, provide the appropriate signal for the release of BDNF essential to LTP maintenance. Although we cannot exclude that BDNF released from neighboring stimulated neurons (28) may also contribute, our findings demonstrate that astrocyte-derived BDNF is necessary for this process.

To readily explore this further, we disrupted the spontaneous MD activity selectively after TBS, while preserving the spontaneous MDs before TBS and the Ca2+ response to TBS. To this aim, we used a viral vector that transduces the Gq-coupled receptor (AAV-hM3Dq; AAV5/2-GFAP-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry) in astrocytes allowing its selective activation by clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; Fig. 2G). Notably, a recent in vivo study employing this chemogenetic approach in awake mice (33) reported that CNO administration induces in astrocytes an initial transient increase of Ca2+ levels, followed by a prolonged period of silencing. This biphasic response suggests that the subsequent inhibition of astrocytic Ca2+ signaling may result from the depletion of intracellular Ca2+ stores. Here, we provide evidence that Ca2+ from ER stores is indeed depleted following CNO application (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Then, in cortical slices from AAV-hM3Dq mice, we applied CNO starting 2 min post-TBS, i.e. when the Ca2+ response of astrocytes to TBS is concluded and the spontaneous MD activity is not yet resumed (Fig. 1 B and C). We observed an initial increase in both the somatic cytosolic Ca2+ and spontaneous MD activity followed by a long-lasting impairment (Figs. 2 H and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Under these conditions, TBS induces S-LTP only (Fig. 2I). In contrast, slices from control mice transduced with AAV-tdTomato display normal L-LTP (Fig. 2I). Notably, when CNO is applied 10 min after TBS, L-LTP is preserved (Fig. 2I). These data indicate that LTP consolidation requires spontaneous MDs for a restricted time window after TBS.

As reported previously, a sustained BDNF-TrkB signaling is necessary for L-LTP (29). Accordingly, if spontaneous MDs post-TBS drive the astrocytic release of BDNF, their inhibition should block BDNF-TrkB signaling in neighboring neurons. We thus quantified pTrkB, normalized to the total receptor amount through pTrkB/TrkB colocalization (26, 27). In slices from the control hemisphere, pTrkB is significantly increased 10 min after TBS, returning to baseline after 60 min (Fig. 3A). In contrast, in sections from the hemisphere transduced with AAV-hM3Dq, the expected 10 min-increase in pTrkB following TBS is absent when CNO is applied 2 min post-TBS and maintained throughout the recording (Fig. 3A), indicating that spontaneous MDs post-TBS play a crucial role in this process. A comparable effect in pTrkB levels is observed in slices transduced with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH (Fig. 3A), where the suppression of the overall astrocytic Ca2+ signals critically incorporates the spontaneous MDs that occur after TBS.

Following the inhibition of BDNF release mechanism, BDNF is expected to accumulate at perisynaptic astrocytic processes. To address this hypothesis, we performed immunohistochemistry using an anti-BDNF (α-BDNF) antibody on slices transduced with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH. The level of BDNF in astrocytes was quantified by measuring the colocalization of tdTomato and BDNF immunoreactive signals (26, 27) at two specific time points: before and 60 min after TBS. We found that 60 min after TBS, BDNF significantly accumulates in astrocytes transduced with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH (Fig. 3B). In contrast, at the same time point, control astrocytes show no increase in BDNF accumulation (Fig. 3B). Confocal imaging followed by three-dimensional reconstruction also reveals BDNF immunoreactivity as a dispersed, punctate pattern in proximity of the postsynaptic density marker PSD95 (Fig. 3C), confirming the accumulation of BDNF at perisynaptic sites. Structured illumination superresolution microscopy (SIM) further confirms the localization of BDNF at astrocytic perisynaptic structures, such as finger-like extensions and lamellar sheaths (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis using transmission electron microscopy after immuno-gold processing (26, 27) reveals that BDNF gold particles are significantly increased 60 min post-TBS at perisynaptic astrocytes expressing AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH, as compared to controls (Fig. 3E). Finally, BDNF accumulation was analyzed in astrocytes in which the inhibition of Ca2+ signaling was temporally restricted. We patched individual astrocytes transduced with AAV-GCaMP6f with a glass pipette containing the high-affinity Ca2+ chelator 1,2-bis(o-aminophenoxy)ethane-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (BAPTA) (50 mM) that, within 40 min, diffuses into the astrocyte syncytium and inhibits overall Ca2+ activity in astrocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Immunostaining against BDNF shows that 60 min after TBS, BAPTA-filled astrocytes exhibit a significant accumulation of BDNF compared to BAPTA-unfilled astrocytes (Fig. 3 F and G).

Altogether, these results demonstrate that spontaneous MDs after TBS mediate the release of BDNF which, in turn, activates TrkB receptors in neurons leading to L-LTP.

Astrocyte Ca2+ Signaling in Long-Term Memory.

Previous studies reported that the PRC plays an important role in the ability of mice to discriminate familiar from novel objects (34, 35) and that BDNF release from PRC astrocytes mediates recognition memory retention (26, 27). Therefore, we evaluated whether inhibition of Ca2+ signaling in PRC astrocytes impacts familiar-to-novel object recognition memory (Fig. 4A). Mice injected with AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH or AAV-tdTomato were exposed to two identical objects, a learning training in the object recognition test (ORT). After this phase, mice were subjected to two successive memory tests, each consisting of encountering one “familiar” object from the initial training and one “novel” object. A discrimination index (DI) was used to quantify the preferential exploration time of the objects (26). With respect to control AAV-tdTomato mice, in the AAV-hPMCA2/AAV-p130PH mice, DI is comparable at 10 min, but it is significantly lower at 24 h retention time (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). Similar data were obtained using mice injected with LV-TeNTstop (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). Our data indicate that Ca2+-dependent gliotransmitter release, specifically BDNF, is necessary for memory retention.

Next, we implemented chemogenetic interventions utilizing the same approach employed to suppress astrocyte Ca2+ activity in slice preparations (Fig. 2 G–J). Notably, Ca2+ dynamics in in vivo cortical astrocytes transduced with AAV-hM3Dq are suppressed within 10 min of intraperitoneal CNO administration and remain inhibited for at least 1 h (33). In mice injected with AAV-hM3Dq we, therefore, administered CNO (3.5 mg/kg) intraperitoneally, at different memory phases in the ORT: 30 min before the learning training (experiment 1), immediately after it (experiment 2), or 30 min before the 24-h memory test (experiment 3) (Fig. 4D). Our findings indicate that, across all experiments (1–3), the DI at the 1-h retention time remains unchanged in AAV-hM3Dq mice compared to the AAV-tdTomato control mice (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). In contrast, a significant reduction in DI is observed at the 24-h retention interval in experiment 1, whereas no such effect is detected in experiments 2 and 3 (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). By temporally aligning CNO administration with specific phases of ORT, we demonstrate that spontaneous MDs, acting at the time of memory acquisition, exert their function in memory retention.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that spontaneous Ca2+ activity in astrocytes, predominantly expressed by the dynamics of MDs, represents a distinct, functional signaling in the brain. Through their recurring mode, spontaneous MDs incorporate a mechanism that extends gliotransmission (i.e., BDNF) over time, promoting the sustained activation of the synaptic pathways required for the retention of memory traces in the PRC circuitry. With respect to earlier studies focused on rapid, neuron-derived BDNF effects in synaptic strengthening (29), our study unveils the coordinated contribution of neuronal and astrocytic pathways to long-lasting synaptic plasticity. We also demonstrate that the inherent stochasticity of spontaneous MDs is unaffected by neuronal patterned activity, introducing an element of randomness in the control of synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation.

Initial memory traces, generated by episodes of intense neuronal activity, are bound to decay unless they gradually stabilize following the sustained activation of specific signaling pathways (36). Our findings reveal that synaptic strengthening in PRC circuits relies on a functional coupling between a transient neuronal activity, i.e. the TBS, and a sustained astrocytic signaling, i.e., the spontaneous MDs. While TBS alone induces S-LTP, the astrocytic MD activity post-TBS is essential for initiating the molecular processes that consolidate synaptic strengthening leading to L-LTP. At a molecular level, this functional coupling between neurons and astrocytes is orchestrated by BDNF trafficking (37). Neurons initiate this process by the activity-dependent release of inactive proBDNF (25) and astrocytes specifically capture and recycle the neuronal proBDNF to be released back to neurons as active forms (26, 27, 38, 39). We here demonstrate that astrocytic BDNF is released in a Ca2+-dependent manner through a mode regulated by spontaneous MDs. Their recurring activity ensures sustained BDNF/TrkB signaling for the time requested to induce persistent synaptic modifications. Therefore, spontaneous MDs function as astrocytic signals that encode a biochemical memory of recent neuronal activity. This signature in memory retention potentially organizes transcriptional activity, as recently observed in engram neurons and adjacent astrocytes in the basolateral amygdala (40).

The functional recruitment of astrocytes to brain circuits is commonly believed to depend on gliotransmitter release mediated by Ca2+ elevations evoked by synaptic activity (1, 2, 9–12). In our study, we show that the evoked Ca2+ response of astrocytes to TBS is insufficient for inducing L-LTP. Indeed, chemogenetic experiments reveal that when the evoked response is unaffected and the spontaneous MDs post-TBS selectively inhibited, L-LTP is abolished. In this scenario, while the evoked response of astrocytes reflects the organization of synaptic circuits, the spontaneous MDs introduce an autonomous layer of complexity to astrocyte Ca2+ signaling. As shown by our behavioral data, astrocyte signaling is acting during the memory encoding phase, when synaptic strengthening supports memory circuit formation. After this critical time window, astrocytes are no longer required and memory stabilization, which is initiated by astrocytic BDNF signaling, proceeds in memory neurons through the downstream TrkB-mediated intracellular pathway. Given that spontaneous MDs are present across various brain regions, we propose that they likely represent a general signaling mechanism in astrocytes that enables sustained gliotransmitter release tailored to specific synaptic circuits, cortical states, and behavioral contexts.

Through a statistical evaluation of the spatial-temporal features of Ca2+ dynamics, we provide evidence that spontaneous MDs in PRC astrocytes are purely stochastic events. Their inherent stochasticity is not disrupted by TBS-eliciting LTP, providing further support to their autonomous nature. Spontaneous MDs may, therefore, regulate synaptic strengthening by transferring the initial neuronal information to randomly selected synapses, ultimately sculpturing the spatial organization of engram connectivity. We can draw a similarity between the stochasticity of synaptic engram consolidation operated by MDs and allocation studies, in which neurons are recruited in engram circuits based on their random intrinsic excitability (41–48). Both mechanisms resonate with the concept of selective stabilization (49, 50), suggesting that, while the brain generates several potential neuronal and synaptic representations of a memory circuit, only the most effective ones are selected to persist over time. Our data support a role for probabilistic astrocyte operations in shaping the synaptic engrams that are crucial in long-term memory. The uncertainty that is inherent to long-term memory representation finds here a plausible cellular mechanism (51).

In conclusion, our study underscores the requirement of astrocyte spontaneous MDs in memory retention, opening new avenues for understanding the role of neuron–astrocyte reciprocal signaling in synaptic engrams. Furthermore, the stochastic functional action of spontaneous MDs in memory retention unveils the functional relevance of nondeterministic operations in the brain.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Models.

All procedures were performed in accordance with the Italian (D.L. n.26, 14 March 2014) and European Council (2010/63/UE) Directives on Animal Care and approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorizations 76/2020-PR; 754/2018-PR; 773/2020-PR). GLAST-CreERT2 mice were kindly provided by M. Götz (Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität, Munich, Germany). Mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle (7 am – 7 pm light), with a room temperature of 22 °C, humidity of 60%, and unrestricted access to food and water.

Viral Vectors.

The following AAVs were purchased from Addgene (USA): AAV5 pZac2.1 gfaABC1D-cyto-GCaMP6f (AAV-GCaMP6f was a gift from Baljit Khakh (University of California, Los Angeles, USA); Addgene viral prep # 52925-AAV5; http://n2t.net/addgene:52925; RRID:Addgene_52925), AAV5 pZac2.1 gfaABC1D-tdTomato (AAV-tdTomato; was a gift from Baljit Khakh (University of California, Los Angeles, USA); Addgene viral prep # 44332-AAV5; http://n2t.net/addgene:44332; RRID:Addgene_44332), AAV5/2-GFAP-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (AAV-hM3Dq; was a gift from Bryan Roth (University of North Carolina-Chapel Hill, USA); Addgene viral prep # 50478-AAV5; http://n2t.net/addgene:50478; RRID:Addgene_50478). AAV2/5.GfaABC1DmCherry-hPMCA2w/b (AAV-hPMCA2) was obtained from Penn Vector Core (University of Pennsylvania, USA).

Viral vectors production.

The AAV5-pZac2.1-GfaABC1D.cyto-mRFP-p130PH (AAV-p130PH) and AAV5-GfaABC1D-G-CEPIA1er (AAV-CEPIA1er) were prepared, starting respectively from the mRFP-p130PH plasmid (kindly donated by Dr. Shingua Ding, University of Missouri-Columbia, USA) and from pAAV- GfaABC1D-G-CEPIA1er plasmid (kindly donated by Y. Okubo, University of Tokyo, Japan), by the AAV Vector Unit at the International Centre for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology Trieste (https://www.icgeb.org/avu-core-facility/), as described previously (52) with a few modifications. Briefly, infectious recombinant AAV vector particles were generated in HEK293T cell culture in roller bottles by a cross-packaging approach whereby the vector genome was packaged into AAV capsid serotype-5 (pDP5, Plasmid Factory GmbH & Co. KG, Germany). Viral stocks were obtained by PEG precipitation and CsCl2 gradient centrifugation. The physical titer of recombinant AAVs was determined by quantifying vector genomes (vg) packaged into viral particles by real-time PCR against a standard curve; values obtained were 1 × 1012 to 1 × 1013 vg per milliliter. LV-TeNTstop and LV- GFPstop were produced as previously described (26, 27).

Intracranial Viral Injections.

Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6j mice were injected with 1.5 μL of the following solutions containing: for Ca2+ imaging, 50% AAV-GCaMP6f, 25% AAV-tdTomato, 25% sterile Artificial Cerebrospinal Fluid (ACSF) or 50% AAV-GCaMP6f, 25% AAV-p130PH, 25% AAV-hPMCA2w/b or 50% AAV-GCaMP6f, 50% AAV-hM3Dq or 60% AAV-CEPIA1er and 40% AAV-TdTomato; for LTP and behavioral experiments 50% AAV-hPMCA2w/b, 50% AAV-p130PH, or 100% AAV-hM3Dq. Six-week-old GLAST-CreERT2 mice were treated with 1 mg of Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#T5648) dissolved in corn oil twice daily for five consecutive days. On the last day of tamoxifen treatment, mice were injected with LV-TeNTstop or LV-GFPstop with injections alternated randomly between the two hemispheres to avoid lateralization bias. Injections were performed in deeply anesthetized animals (with isoflurane; 4% for the induction and 1.5% for maintenance) after drilling a hole (0.5 mm diameter) into the skull above the perirhinal cortices (coordinates from Bregma: anteroposterior −2 mm, mediolateral ±4.2 mm, dorsoventral −2.8/−2.6 mm). Viral vectors were infused into the PRC of each hemisphere by inserting a pulled glass capillary containing the viral vectors mix and connected to a manual syringe pump or an automated syringe pump (Pump 11 Elite, Harvard Apparatus). After injections, the skin was sutured and mice were revitalized under a heat lamp, returned to their cage and housed in standard conditions until the day of the experiment.

Brain Slices.

Cortical slices were prepared 3 to 4 wk after the intracranial viral injection. The brain was removed and placed in cold oxygenated (95% O2 and 5% CO2) ACSF containing 124 mM NaCl, 4.4 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.3 mM MgCl2, 26.2 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM glucose, and 2 mM L-ascorbic acid. The horizontal cortical slices (300 μm thick) were prepared using a vibratome and maintained in a chamber containing oxygenated ACSF at room temperature. After a minimum recovery period of 1 h, a single slice was transferred into a submersion recording chamber and perfused (3 mL/min) with oxygenated ACSF at 32 °C ± 0.2 °C. Slices were routinely used within 6 h from cutting. Over this time, Ca2+ signal dynamics in astrocytes are unchanged, indicating that Ca2+ signaling remains stable during the time of LTP recordings.

Ca2+ Imaging Experiments.

To image Ca2+ dynamics in GCaMP6f-astrocytes, we used a 2P laser scanning microscope (Multiphoton Imaging System, Scientifica Ltd., UK) equipped with a pulsed laser (Chameleon Ultra 2, Coherent, USA) tuned at 920 nm. Power at the sample was controlled in the range of 10 to 15 mW. Images were acquired with a water-immersion lens (Olympus, LUMPlan FI/IR 20×, 1.05 NA), with a field of view of 120 × 120 μm at 1.53 Hz acquisition frame rate. Each recording was performed in cortical layers II/III for either 2 or 5 min with 5 or 10 min start to start intervals, respectively. Square current pulses (duration 0.2 ms) were applied every 30 s (0.033 Hz) using a stimulus generator (Dagan) connected through a stimulus isolation unit to a concentric bipolar electrode (40 to 80 KU, FHC) positioned in layers II/III on the temporal side of the rhinal sulcus as in local field potentials recordings for LTP studies. Imaging was performed at 250 ± 30 μm anteroposterior from the stimulating electrode. All imaging recordings were performed during 0.033 Hz stimulations to monitor Ca2+ activity in astrocyte MDs except for series with TBS (100 Hz; four sets of stimulations delivered 15 s apart, each one consisting of ten bursts of five pulses at 100 Hz with interburst intervals of 150 ms; first burst starting at frame 20 of the recording).

Analysis of Ca2+ Imaging.

GCaMP6f and astrocytic Ca2+ dynamics.

The presence of GCaMP6f could lead to alterations of the underlying Ca2+ dynamics. Indeed, GCaMP6f is an exogenous Ca2+ buffer that would mainly cause the slowing down of the Ca2+ transient without a major effect on its amplitude. The bandwidth of this low-pass filtering is determined by the time constants of binding-unbinding of GCaMP6f from Ca2+ ions. These time constants are of the order of 100 ms, much faster than the typical mean duration of astrocytic Ca2+ transients. This lack of effect is also proved by the large body of literature reporting that the Ca2+ transients caused by single action potentials can be detected by GCaMP6f. These transients are bandpass limited by the sensor binding/unbinding rates and are at least one order of magnitude faster than the transients observed in our study.

Preprocessing.

Each time series contains a green channel holding the GCaMP6f data and a red channel containing the tdTomato data. We wrote a custom ImageJ macro that exploits the Template Matching plugin (https://sites.google.com/site/qingzongtseng/template-matching-ij-plugin) to align the t-stack. The macro aligns the stack using the red channel as a reference since it has a better S/N ratio and does not vary in time due to the Ca2+ transients. Afterward, the entire stack is binned 2 × 2 to improve signal-to-noise. Finally, the time series Δf(t) is computed to obtain the fluctuation of fluorescence with respect to the mean Fmean (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B): ∆ft = (Ft − Fmean)/Fmean where the terms in bold represent image matrices.

Extraction of Ca2+ transients and definition of the MD footprints.

Next, the Δf(t) stack is binarized by a two-step procedure that is based on the hypothesis that GCaMP6f fluorescence would be normally distributed in the absence of Ca2+ transients. First, the time series for each pixel of the frame is transformed in the Z-score by normalizing for the mean and dividing by the SD as Z(t) = (f(t) − fmean)/SDf. Each time series is binarized by setting it to 1 if Z(t) > 2.5, corresponding to P < 0.01. Then, each voxel is compared with the adjacent voxels and is set to 0 unless there are enough nonzero neighboring voxels. This latest step corresponds to a low pass filter in space and time. Throughout all analysis, each voxel requires at least 1 neighbor in space and 1 neighbor in time to be set to 1. If we assume that noise is uncorrelated, P decreases to P < (0.01)3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C): Thus, each Ca2+ transient is formed by a nonzero voxel in the x, y, t volume. Nonzero voxels are clustered together when they satisfy criteria of topological connectivity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), and each x, y, t cluster is labeled to form individual Ca2+ transients. The footprint on the x, y plane of each event is computed as the border that encloses the max projection on t of all nonzero voxels constituting each Ca2+ transient (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E and F). Some Ca2+ transients might occur in the territory of a MD which can be interested by several events during an acquisition session. Multiple transients occupying the same physical territory form clusters of events that are quantified by exploring the event list and creating clusters of transients that occupy the same territory (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). The footprint of a MD is defined as the union of the footprints of all the events belonging to the same cluster. The outline of the MD can now be used as a region of interest to compute the time course of ΔF/F (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). From these data, we then compute the cross-correlation of the ΔF/F of all couples of MDs belonging to each imaged astrocyte (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I).

Displaying the Ca2+ transient.

The plot of Ca2+ activity shown in Fig. 1 is represented by means of a nonlinear transformation that enhances the visibility of the transients while suppressing the subthreshold fluctuations. This transformation originates from the observation that the ΔF/F time series are described by long-tailed distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C). The ΔF/F time series measured for each MD is first converted to Z-score and cropped to positive only values to ignore fluctuations under the baseline. Then, the Z-score is converted into the probability pG(t) that each time point belongs to the normal distribution. The statistically interesting points have a low probability of belonging to the normal distribution (P < 0.025 indicated by the red contour in the mid panel of SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Finally, the low probability region is stretched by means of a logarithmic transfer function: Mzt = 1 − log10pGt.

Electrophysiological Recording.

Square current pulses (duration 0.2 ms) were applied every 30 s (0.033 Hz) using a stimulus generator (WPI, stimulus isolator A360) connected through a stimulus isolation unit to a concentric bipolar electrode (40 to 80 KU, FHC) positioned in layers II/III on the temporal side of the rhinal sulcus. Evoked extracellular field Excitatory Postsynaptic Potentials (fEPSPs) were recorded using an Axoclamp-2B amplifier (Axon Instruments) with ACSF-filled glass micropipette pulled on a vertical puller (Narishige PC-10, resistance [<5 MΩ]), inserted in layers II/III at around 500 μm from the stimulation electrode, and analyzed using Axoscope 8.0 software. Baseline responses were obtained every 30 s with a stimulus intensity adjusted to induce 50% of the maximal synaptic response. After 20 min of stable baseline, LTP was evoked by TBS (100 Hz; four sets of stimulations delivered 15 s apart, each one consisting of 10 bursts of five pulses at 100 Hz with interburst intervals of 150 ms). fEPSPs were plotted as amplitude. Each point represents the response every 30 s expressed as mean ± SEM. In some experiments, recombinant BDNF (10 ng/mL; Laboratory of Antonino Cattaneo, Scuola Normale Superiore, Pisa, Italy) or CNO (Cayman chemical, Cat#16882; 10 µM) were perfused into the recording chamber.

Immunohistochemistry.

Brain slices were fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h after recording. Slices were treated with 1% Triton X-100 for 20 min, blocked with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in Phosphate-buffered saline for 1 h, and incubated overnight free-floating with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. Slices were washed in Phosphate-buffered saline and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies diluted in a blocking buffer. Slices were eventually counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#D9542) and mounted with Aqua Poly/mount (Polysciences, Inc., Cat#18606).

Antibodies.

The following antibodies were used: chicken α-BDNF (Promega Cat#G1641; RRID:AB#430850, IHC 1:300), mouse α-NeuN (Abcam Cat#ab77315; RRID:AB#1566475, IHC 1:1,000), guinea pig α-NeuN (Millipore Cat#ABN90; RRID:AB#11205592, IHC 1:1,000), rabbit α-pTrkB (Tyr 816) (Millipore Cat#ABN1381; RRID AB#2721199, IHC 1:300), goat α-TrkB (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat#sc-12-G; RRID:AB#632558, IHC 1:300), mouse α-PSD95 (Merck-Millipore Cat#MAB1596; RRID:AB_2092365, IHC 1:500), chicken α-GFAP (Abcam Cat#ab134436; RRID:AB_2818977, IHC 1:1,000), mouse α-GFAP (Abcam Cat#ab10062; RRID:AB_296804, IHC 1:1,000), rabbit α-RFP (Rockland Antibodies Cat#600-401-379; RRID:AB_2209751, IHC 1:1,000), mouse α-DsRed (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat#sc-390909; RRID:AB_2801575, IHC 1:1,000).

BAPTA Experiments.

In slices from mice injected 3 to 4 wk before with AAV-GCaMP6f, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from GCaMp6f positive astrocytes with intracellular solution containing (in mM): BAPTA, 50; KMeSO3, 20; 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid, 10; Na Phosphocreatine, 10; NaCl, 8; Na2ATP, 2; Na2Guanosine Triphosphate, 0.4; ascorbic acid, 3; MgCl2, 1; Alexa Fluor 594, 0.1 or Neurobiotin 488, 0.16; pH 7.3 with KOH. Recordings were performed using a multiclamp-700B amplifier (Molecular Devices). Signals were filtered at 1 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz with a Digidata 1440 s interface and pClamp10 software (Molecular Devices). Typical pipette resistance was 3 to 4 MΩ. Access resistance was monitored throughout the recordings and was typically <25 MΩ. The identity of astrocytes was confirmed by negative resting membrane potential and passive response to square current injections in the current clamp. Astrocytes were maintained in whole-cell configuration at I = 0 for about 30 min at 32 °C to allow diffusion of intracellular solution inside the astrocytic syncytium. Alexa fluor 594 positive astrocytes were observed at 780 nm, and Ca2+ imaging was performed at 920 nm. In experiments with neurobiotin 488, slices were fixed in PFA 4% at the indicated time after TBS.

Confocal Microscopy and Quantitative Image Analysis.

Confocal imaging was performed using a laser-scanning motorized confocal system (Nikon A1) equipped with an Eclipse Ti-E inverted microscope and four laser lines (405, 488, 561, and 638 nm). Z-series images were taken with an interstack interval of 0.5 µm using a 60× oil objective. For each type of quantification, laser intensities and camera settings were maintained identical within the same experiment to allow the comparison of different experimental groups and treatments. Image processing and 3D rendering were performed using the NIS Element software (Nikon). Colocalizations were quantified by calculating Mander’s overlap using the software NIS Element.

Sholl Analysis.

Sholl analysis of individual astrocytes labeled with Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) was performed using a series of concentric circles centered to the soma at a fixed radial interval of 4 µm using SNT-Sholl Analysis Plug in for Fiji (53, 54). The number of intersections between astrocytic processes and each concentric circle generates a Sholl profile that reflects the distribution and complexity of process branching as a function of distance from the soma.

3D-SIM.

3D-SIM was performed using an X-Light V2 confocal spinning disk system completed with a Video Confocal superresolution module (CrestOptics, Italy) with a lateral resolution of 115 nm and an axial resolution of ~250 nm. The system was equipped with a 60×/1.40 NA PlanApo Lambda oil immersion objective (Nikon, Japan), Zyla sCMOS camera (Andor), and Spectra X Lumencor LEDs Light Source with bandpass excitation filters of 460 to 490 and 535 to 600 nm (Chroma Technology, USA). Image stacks were acquired with a format of 2,048 × 2,048 pixels and a z-distance of 150 nm and with 36 raw SIM images per plane (multiple acquisition mode x–y grid scan). The SIM raw data with 16-bit depth were computationally reconstructed using the Metamorph software package. For 3D-image rendering and colocalization analysis, images were processed using NIS-Elements software.

Electron Microscopy.

Sections from electrophysiological recordings were fixed in a solution of 4% PFA and 0.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) for 4 h and washed overnight in 50 mM Tris Buffered Saline (TBS), pH 7.4. Slices were immersed for 2 h in a cryoprotecting solution (25% sucrose and 10% glycerol in 0.05 M PB) before being snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane. After washing in 0.1 M PB, sections were incubated for 1 h in 20% normal goat serum in TBS. Slices were then incubated for three nights at 4 °C in a 3% BSA/TBS solution containing primary α-BDNF. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in 3% BSA/TBS solution containing 2-nm-gold conjugated goat α-rabbit (BBI International). Sections were rinsed in TBS, fixed for 30 min in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, and postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration in an ethanol series followed by propylene oxide, samples were embedded in Epon812 epoxy resin and observed with a JEOLJEM-1011 transmission electron microscope, operated at 100 kV, after cutting ultrathin sections (60 nm thick). Negative controls were performed in the absence of primary antibodies. Astrocytes were identified using 3 structural criteria: i) localization between synapses: The space between synapses is occupied by astrocytic processes. Astrocytes are cellular structures filling all the space between synapses, dendrites, and axons; ii) irregular, stellate shape: The processes of astrocytes surrounding synaptic contacts often adopt convoluted forms. Compared to their flatter neuronal counterparts, perisynaptic astrocytes show an irregular profile; iii) relatively clear cytoplasm: The cytoplasm of an astrocyte differs from that of neighboring objects in the neuropil. An astrocyte cytoplasm is less electron-dense than one of the neurons. Quantification of gold grains was performed manually by counting the number of gold grains labeling astrocytic MDs that surround pre- and postsynaptic structures.

ORT.

ORT was performed in a Y-apparatus with high, homogenous white walls, 30 cm tall: One arm was used as a start arm with a sliding door to control access to the arena; the other two were used to display the objects. The start arm is 26 cm long, the sliding door placed at 13 cm, and the lateral arms are 18 cm long. All arms are 10 cm wide. On the first day (habituation day), mice explored the arena for 20 min. The following day, the learning session (learning training) was performed: For 5 min, the animals were free to explore the arena, which contained two identical objects, one placed in each arm. At 10 min or 24 h after the learning training, the memory test was run; the animals were free to explore the arena containing two objects, a familiar object and a novel one. Arena and objects were cleaned up between trials to stop the build-up of olfactory cues. The DI was calculated as follows: (TNew − TOld)/(TNew + TOld), with TNew and TOld being the time spent exploring the new and the familiar objects, respectively. A video camera was mounted above the apparatus to record trials with the EthoVision software (Noldus). The exploration time was the time during which mice approached the objects with muzzles and paws.

Statistical Analysis.

Sample sizes and statistical tests can be found in figure legends. Statistical analysis was carried out using Graphpad Prism 9 and Origin 2023. Comparison between the two groups was assessed by the unpaired t test, Mann–Whitney, or Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Comparison between two dependent groups was evaluated by the paired t test or Wilcoxon signed rank test. We used two-way RM ANOVA with Holm–Sidak’s (behavioral experiments) or Bonferroni’s (LTP experiments) tests for multiple comparisons. Data were excluded from the analysis following the outlier analysis. The level of significance used was P < 0.05.
