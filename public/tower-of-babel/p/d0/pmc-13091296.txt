Electroporation-Induced Gastric Cancer Models Through Epithelial-Specific Genetic Modifications
Authors: Junya Arai, Masahiro Hata, Yosuke Ochiai, Yoku Hayakawa, Sandra Ryeom, Timothy C Wang, Hiroki Kobayashi, Biyun Zheng, Feijing Wu, Jin Qian, Yi Zeng, Hualong Zheng, Puran Zhang, Shuang Li, Leah Zamechek, Hiroto Kinoshita, Prugo Filko, Kenneth P Olive, Electroporation Study Consortium
Published: 2026

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden and one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Despite improvements in diagnosis and treatment, patient outcomes remain poor, particularly for advanced disease, due in part to the substantial molecular, histological, and etiological heterogeneity that characterizes gastric tumorigenesis. Understanding the early cellular events and the diverse pathways driving tumor progression requires experimental models that accurately reflect the biology of human gastric cancer.

Several types of mouse models have been developed to study gastric cancer, including chemically induced models, infection-based models, xenografts, and genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs).2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Among these, GEMMs have been particularly valuable for elucidating the roles of core oncogenic drivers such as TP53, CDH1, MYC, and KRAS. However, conventional GEMMs require extensive breeding and lack the flexibility needed to rapidly test complex combinations of mutations or to model diverse histological subtypes.

Recently, an electroporation-based somatic engineering method (EPO-GEMM) has emerged as a powerful alternative that allows rapid introduction of multiple oncogenic alterations directly into the stomach mucosa.8 These models generate highly aggressive tumors efficiently, but because the electroporated DNA is not restricted to epithelial cells, the resulting tumors often involve nonepithelial compartments and may not fully recapitulate the stepwise progression and epithelial origin characteristic of human gastric cancer.

To overcome these limitations and better model the epithelial origin of gastric cancer, we sought to generate epithelial-specific somatic models. In particular, we tested whether restricting Myc overexpression and Trp53 loss to gastric epithelial cells could produce tumors more closely resembling human disease. Furthermore, to model undifferentiated gastric carcinoma characterized by signet ring cells, we introduced additional epithelial-specific mutations in Trp53, Cdh1, KrasG12D, and Smad4.2, 3, 4,9

Before testing our modified method, we first reproduced the original electroporation8 model using wild-type mice with Cas9-sg-Trp53 and Myc plasmids (Figure 1A). The tumor incidence was nearly 100%, and both gross morphology and histology revealed highly aggressive gastric tumors developing within a short period after electroporation, covering almost the entire stomach (Figure 1B and C; Supplementary Figure 1A). These findings were consistent with the original model,8 validating our electroporation technique.

We next generated Tff1-Cre;Loxp-stop-Loxp (LSL)-Cas9 mice, expressing Cas9 and Cre specifically in gastric epithelial cells.6,7,10 Plasmids encoding sg-Trp53 and LSL-Myc-tdTomato were electroporated into the epithelium, ensuring that both Trp53 knockout and Myc overexpression were epithelial-restricted (Figure 1D). Electroporation conditions were optimized to maximize epithelial targeting while maintaining tissue integrity.

One and a half months postelectroporation, the tumors exhibited morphological features resembling type 0-IIc lesions of human early gastric cancer, which are restricted to the gastric mucosa, indicating an epithelial origin of these tumors (Figure 1E and F). In mice sacrificed at a later time point (3 months) following electroporation, macroscopic inspection revealed tumors resembling Borrmann type II/III human gastric cancer, with well-demarcated margins, limited ulceration, and lung metastasis (Figure 1G and H; Supplementary Figure 1B). Histopathology showed preserved glandular architecture, consistent with epithelial origin, and features of moderately to poorly differentiated carcinoma with the maintained expression of E-cadherin and upregulation of TROP2 and CD44v9 (Figure 1I–L). Tumor formation rate was approximately 30% at both 1.5 months and 3 months after electroporation (Supplementary Figure 1A), reflecting moderate efficiency of the epithelial-specific system. We also perform electroporation with only LSL-Myc plasmid, which did not develop gastric cancer (Figure 2A), compatible with the original models.9

We then introduced additional mutations reflecting those for human undifferentiated gastric cancer with LSL-KrasG12D-tdTomato along with sg-Tp53, sg-Cdh1, and sg-Smad4 plasmid, which also produced tumors resembling Borrmann type II human gastric cancer (Figure 2B–D). Signet ring cells were clearly observed, recapitulating key features of undifferentiated human gastric carcinoma with the loss of E-cadherin expression and upregulation of TROP2 and CD44v9 (Figure 1E–G and Supplementary Figure 1C). Tumor formation rate was approximately 20% at 3 months after electroporation, reflecting moderate efficiency of the epithelial-specific system and the requirement for several plasmids, whereas there was no clear cancer development at 1.5 months (Supplementary Figure 1A).

Together, these results demonstrate that restricting oncogenic alterations to the gastric epithelium yields tumors that more faithfully reproduce the onset, differentiation states, and histology of human gastric cancer, allowing systematic modeling of diverse subtypes ranging from early-stage lesions to undifferentiated signet ring cell tumors.

We selected the foveolar epithelium–specific Tff1-Cre mouse model because it broadly labels gastric epithelial cells and has been well established to support gastric metaplasia and tumor development.6,7,10 Importantly, oncogenic activation in Tff1+ progenitors in the corpus has been shown to drive gland-wide proliferation, leading to atrophy, metaplasia, and subsequent tumor development, indicating that Tff1+ progenitors can acquire neoplastic potential. Therefore, this broad epithelial targeting was considered optimal for reliably inducing gastric tumorigenesis in our system. In contrast, our pilot experiments using the stem cell–marking Iqgap3-CreERT model failed to produce consistent tumors, likely due to limited Cas9/Cre expression. Although the Cldn18-CreERT model, marking the broad epithelial cells, also induced similar tumors in our pilot studies, its requirement for tamoxifen administration raised concerns regarding toxicity and procedural complexity; therefore, it was not adopted for the main experiments.

This approach has several limitations. Tumor formation efficiency remains moderate, particularly when multiple plasmids are required, making this approach poorly suited for survival analysis, and the full spectrum of tumor microenvironmental interactions may not be captured. Additionally, electroporation parameters may require further refinement to improve consistency across animals.

In summary, by defining the epithelial cell-of-origin and introducing key driver alterations, this platform provides a versatile and physiologically relevant model for studying the initiation and evolution of gastric cancer. It enables rapid modeling of distinct histological subtypes and may serve as a foundation for future studies aimed at early detection and therapeutic development.
