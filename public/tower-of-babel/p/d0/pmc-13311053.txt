Significance of Advocacy Group Meetings for Rare and Intractable Diseases: A Survey of Joubert Syndrome and Related Disorders in Japan
Authors: Hiroshi Mano, Kenichi Kitamura, Wakako Hakiri, Kenji Shimizu, Chihiro Mano
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Joubert syndrome (JS), first described by Canadian neurologist Marie Joubert in 1969,1) is a hereditary disorder characterized by delayed psychomotor development, episodic hyperpnea, abnormal eye movements, ataxia, and cerebellar vermis defects. Arima syndrome, reported by Japanese pediatrician Masataka Arima in 1971,2) is another hereditary disorder presenting with severe developmental delay, visual and renal impairments, and ptosis from early infancy. It is also associated with brain malformations, including cerebellar vermis defects, retinal dystrophy (including congenital cataracts), and lower brainstem dysplasia. Patients with Arima syndrome commonly require renal dialysis and other treatments from childhood. Several disorders related to JS, including Arima syndrome, are collectively categorized under the term Joubert syndrome and related disorders (JSRD), which is defined by the “molar tooth sign” on cerebral magnetic resonance imaging.3) Arima syndrome is considered a severe JSRD, with multisystem involvement, including renal, retinal, and hepatic symptoms.4)

Although few studies on JSRD have been conducted in Japan, a research group under the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) was established in FY2011 to investigate these conditions, primarily involving epidemiological studies by Itoh et al.4) and supported by research grants from MHLW. As a culmination of efforts by the research group, the Clinical Practice Guideline 2018 for JSRD was published.5) These guidelines were developed by a multidisciplinary team, including pediatric neurologists and specialists in respiratory medicine, nephrology, plastic surgery, rehabilitation, and ophthalmology, reflecting the syndrome’s diverse clinical manifestations. Within Japan’s designated intractable disease system, Arima syndrome was recognized and subsequently included as a JSRD condition in FY2018.

In parallel, preparations to establish a patient and family advocacy group for JSRD in Japan began in 2015, with a group officially founded in 2016.6) Since then, patient and family meetings have been held at approximately annual intervals, in conjunction with the research group’s academic symposia. These meetings were initially hosted at hospitals, namely, the University of Tokyo Hospital and Tokyo Metropolitan Tobu Medical (Ryoiku) Center, which are affiliated with the guideline committee’s vice chairs. The former was the alma mater of Dr. Arima, whereas the latter was his workplace as a preparatory director, director, and later director emeritus. Following the 2018 publication of the guidelines and the conclusion of the research group’s activities, the advocacy group became a patient- and family-led initiative. However, the COVID-19 pandemic posed significant challenges to in-person gatherings, resulting in a transition to online information exchange. With the pandemic subsiding, interest in resuming face-to-face meetings has revived. This led to the first meeting outside of Tokyo, held in March 2025 at our facility, upon the group’s request. A questionnaire survey was conducted following the meeting to assess the satisfaction and preferences regarding advocacy group meetings.

CASE

The patient and family advocacy group for JSRD in Japan organized a hybrid (on-site and online) meeting at Shizuoka Children’s Hospital, Shizuoka City, the capital of Shizuoka Prefecture. A pediatric rehabilitation physician and a physical therapist collaborated with group representatives in advance to determine and secure the date, time, venue, and necessary equipment. Overall, 9 families attended in person and 20 families attended online.

The program included a lecture by a board-certified rehabilitation physician member of the JSRD clinical practice guideline development committee. The lecture featured a question-and-answer session, followed by an open information exchange among patients and families. In preparation, a board-certified clinical geneticist from the hospital advised the speaker on topics related to genetic testing and counseling. On the day of the meeting, a board-certified pediatric neurologist member of the JSRD clinical practice guideline committee joined online to contribute to the question-and-answer session. The venue was arranged with accessibility in mind, including wheelchair-friendly pathways (accommodating specialized wheelchairs), designated spaces for patients to lie down, and support for children with complex care needs. These preparations were managed by the rehabilitation physician, physical therapist, and speech-language pathologist.

After the meeting, a survey was conducted to assess satisfaction. The questionnaire was designed for this study. This was an education/practice-oriented descriptive cross-sectional survey, and the questionnaire was not validated. Study participants included all members of the JSRD advocacy group in Japan, encompassing those who attended in person, online, or were unable to attend. As of the meeting date, 63 families were registered with the advocacy group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shizuoka Children’s Hospital (approval number: R7–3).

The survey was administered online using Microsoft Forms; it was developed by the study authors affiliated with Shizuoka Children’s Hospital and was distributed following the meeting. Responses were provided by family members. In cases of families with more than one patient with JSRD, separate responses were submitted for each individual. Informed consent for the academic use of data was obtained from all respondents via the online form. Satisfaction responses were collected only from meeting attendees. Satisfaction with the meeting and preferences for future participation were measured using 5-point Likert scales. Meeting satisfaction was graded as follows: 1, strongly dissatisfied; 2, dissatisfied; 3, neither satisfied nor dissatisfied; 4, satisfied; and 5, strongly satisfied. Similarly, willingness to participate in future meetings was graded as follows: 1, willing to participate; 2, somewhat willing to participate; 3, neither willing to participate nor not willing to participate; 4, somewhat not willing to participate; 5, not willing to participate.

DISCUSSION

This is the first report concerning patient and family advocacy group meetings for JSRD in Japan. Approximately half of registered families participated in the meeting either on-site or online, with attendees joining from across the country. On-site participants were primarily from the Tokyo and Osaka metropolitan areas and the regions between them. Although overall satisfaction with the meeting was high, satisfaction with the meeting location was somewhat lower. Given the rarity of JSRD, holding meetings on a prefectural or regional basis is not practical. A nationwide approach is more realistic, but presents accessibility challenges. Therefore, a hybrid format, combining on-site and online participation, is particularly suitable for rare disease communities such as JSRD.

In general, patient advocacy groups play a vital role in supporting individuals with rare diseases and their families by providing educational resources and fostering a sense of community.7) These groups may also contribute to policy development, research, and drug discovery relevant to their specific conditions. Although evidence regarding the benefits of rare disease support groups remains limited, as well as the perceived barriers and facilitators to establishing and maintaining them, such groups are nonetheless an important source of emotional and practical support for many patients.8) Furthermore, peer support can enhance care for individuals with chronic or rare conditions.9) Although the effects of peer support interventions for parents of children with complex needs remain uncertain, these interventions are generally perceived as valuable.10) Parents of children with JS experience considerable stress and burden.11,12) Maladaptive behaviors in patients and the coping styles of their parents are both associated with increased parental stress and burden. Meetings organized by patient and family advocacy groups can offer valuable opportunities to share information with both fellow members and healthcare professionals, discuss current issues, and disseminate knowledge. Such interactions may help alleviate some of the difficulties faced by JSRD patients and their families. However, organizing these meetings requires significant effort and financial resources from group members. Selecting an appropriate location remains challenging, as indicated by the relatively low satisfaction rate with the venue in this study. Despite these challenges, the meetings are meaningful and should be considered for regular continuation. The involvement of healthcare professionals, to the greatest extent possible, is also desirable. For rehabilitation professionals, participating in patient group meetings for rare and intractable diseases could help them better understand patients’ needs and circumstances, thereby facilitating more effective rehabilitation treatments.

This study was conducted with members of a patient and family advocacy group; the study did not involve all individuals with JSRD in Japan. Therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the status of all JSRD patients. Additionally, the meetings were held in rural venues, and satisfaction results may vary depending on the location and content of each meeting. Furthermore, this survey was an education- and practice-oriented descriptive study based on a descriptive cross-sectional design, and the results reflect the participants’ perceptions and stated needs rather than objective measures of effectiveness. This report should be understood as a case example of a meeting held by a patient and family advocacy group for a rare and intractable disease.
