A cultural explanation for parole decisions in the United States
Authors: Amrita Kaushik Telidevara, Ryan L Boyd, BoKyung Park, Mohammad Atari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRacial disparities in discretionary parole decisions persist, yet their cultural underpinnings remain poorly understood. The US justice system, shaped by White American cultural norms, may implicitly favor parole candidates who explain their crimes through dispositional rather than situational attributions. Importantly, however, findings from studies of cultural psychology suggest that while White Americans tend to frame behavior in dispositional terms, Black, Hispanic, and East Asian Americans are more likely to acknowledge situational contributors. Across four studies, we tested whether these attributional differences can contribute to racial disparities in parole. In studies 1 and 2 (total n = 588), White American mock parole candidates were more likely to use dispositional attributions to explain their hypothetical crimes, while those from racial and cultural minorities either showed no preference or favored situational explanations. In study 3 (n = 512), White American mock parole commissioners perceived candidates providing dispositional (vs. situational) explanations about their crimes as less likely to reoffend. In study 4, analyzing 183 real parole hearings via natural language processing methods, we found that candidates granted parole used dispositional framing of their past criminal offenses more often. These findings highlight a cultural mismatch between minority parole candidates and the expectations of the US justice system, potentially perpetuating racial disparities. Addressing this misalignment may offer a new pathway for reducing systemic bias in discretionary parole decisions.Keywords: culture, racial disparity, parole, attribution, natural language processing

We provide the first empirical evidence that the US justice system favors parole candidates who explain their crimes more dispositionally—emphasizing contributions of their personal attributes—rather than situationally. Specifically, candidates using dispositional framing were more likely to receive discretionary parole. However, while independent cultures (e.g. White Americans) more readily identify dispositional contributors to someone's behaviors, interdependent cultures (e.g. Black, Hispanic, and Asian Americans) more often acknowledge situational factors. Mirroring this, White American mock parole candidates were more likely to align with the justice system's expectations, providing dispositional accounts of their crimes. These findings suggest that racial disparities in discretionary parole may stem partly from a cultural mismatch between parole candidates’ attribution styles and the values reinforced by the justice system.

Introduction

Racial disparities permeate every stage of the US legal system, from arrest all the way through to sentencing (1). The postsentencing phase—particularly discretionary parole decisions—has been relatively less studied (2–4). Yet, some existing literature suggests that it reflects significant racial inequities as well.

As the prevailing system in the country for managing prison release (accounting for 56% of parole releases in 2016 (4)), discretionary parole is currently employed in 33 US states for most sentences (5). Those convicted of felonies are provided with the opportunity to be released from prison earlier than their maximum statutory sentences and after the completion of their mandatory minimum sentences. What distinguishes discretionary parole from other types of parole, such as medical or mandatory parole, is the high involvement of parole commissioners. Typically, two or three commissioners from the state's parole board evaluate the parole candidates’ readiness for parole, ultimately determining their release. Even though objective factors, such as the parole candidate's criminal history, offense severity, rehabilitation efforts, and postrelease plans, are considered (6), due to its discretionary nature, the process is influenced by different extralegal and subjective factors (7). For example, parole likelihood decreases when parole candidates express more negative emotions (e.g. sadness) during parole hearings (8), when parole commissioners have no break (9), and importantly, when similarly qualified parole candidates are racial minorities rather than Whites (10, 11). Specifically, compared with Whites, Black prisoners are more likely to face prolonged incarceration while awaiting parole (12), expected to meet additional qualifications to be paroled (13), more likely to face prosecutor opposition to their parole release (14), and are ultimately less likely to receive parole (14). Similar biases affect other non-White candidates: Hispanic and Asian individuals are often expected to demonstrate more remorse than their White counterparts to secure parole (15). Even after release, probation and parole supervision officers are more willing to conclude that non-White individuals have violated their terms of supervision (16, 17), further entrenching disparities in the system.

Several contributors to racial disparity in the legal system have been identified. These include both institutional and psychological factors ranging across targeted policing and harsher law enforcement on racial minority communities (18), institutionalized practices that allow for exclusion of jurors from racial minorities (19), and implicit racial bias (20). However, how cultural backgrounds of members of our diverse, multicultural society can explain the racial disparities in discretionary parole has been understudied. This can be potentially due to the difficulties in mapping the concepts of race and culture in the same space (see Discussion), the relatively recent emergence of the interdisciplinary research between psychology and law (21, 22), and the lack of investigations into discretionary parole itself (2–4).

By targeting this gap in the literature, we aim to make three important contributions. First, we propose that what the system assumes as a neutral standard in the parole hearing context is actually driven by an epistemic, culturally specific norm. Specifically, what counts as an acceptable explanation that parole candidates should provide about their crimes is shaped by cultural bias, and this may benefit a group that shares the same culture with the justice system but not others. Second, we leverage the classic attributional theory to explain racial disparities in parole granting, extending the laboratory-based social cognition theory into real-world, high-stake decisions about liberty. Third, we aim to bridge two distinctive literatures, cultural psychology and criminal justice research on parole disparities, investigating an understudied mechanism that is distinct from, and potentially complementary to, the heavily studied racial bias mechanism. Race, like culture, is widely recognized as a psychosocial rather than a biological construct (23), and racial minority status often coincides with being a member of a “cultural” minority (24). We thus propose that culture can provide a window to understand one of the contributors to racial disparities in parole outcomes, which can help develop tailored interventions. We specifically investigated the concept of a “cultural mismatch”—a misalignment between the cultural expectations of the US justice system and those of parole candidates who are from racial and cultural minority groups.

Historically, the US justice system has been dominated by White individuals, reinforcing independent White American cultural norms as the standard (25–27). Consequently, the culture that the justice system and parole commissioners thereof endorse as normative is likely to be White American culture. In contrast, parole candidates from racial minority groups, such as Black, Hispanic, and Asian Americans, are more likely to hold interdependent cultural values and perspectives (28–32). This may create a serious mismatch in how they communicate the contributors to their crime and their readiness for release, as well as how these explanations are perceived and interpreted by parole commissioners.

Prior literature demonstrated that one's cultural background promotes certain accounts of one's actions, supporting our idea that cultural mismatch might undermine one's ability to convey a reflection of one's own crimes and a commitment to avoiding re-offense. Specifically, individuals differ in whether they emphasize “dispositional” factors—such as one's own personality, character, and other internal attributes—or “situational” factors, such as the specific context of the behavior, the influence of other people, and one's surrounding environments (33–36). Extensive cultural psychology research has demonstrated that interdependent cultural traditions—such as those of Black, Hispanic, and East Asian cultures (28–32)—emphasize the role of social relationships and the surrounding environment as they relate to the self (37–45). Within these cultural frameworks, behavior is understood as the product of both personal disposition and situational context, making situational attributions central to judgments of actions (38–40). Since there are contributors to the behaviors that are out of one's control, moral accountability is often not equated with taking personal responsibility (46). Instead, providing a comprehensive account of the action, as opposed to emphasizing only one of the factors that gave rise to the action (i.e. taking personal blame), may seem to be a natural and thorough way to communicate about one's behavior (37–39, 41–45).

However, within a parole system shaped by White American cultural norms, emphasizing situational factors may be interpreted as deflecting personal responsibility, potentially reducing a candidate's likelihood of parole approval. Prior literature found that independent White American (i.e. non-Hispanic White) culture defines the self as independent from others and external contexts and perceives one's disposition as driving one's behavior (37–45). Thus, individuals from this culture usually identify strong dispositional, rather than situational, contributors to one's action (37–45). Given that parole commissioners operate within a system shaped by these cultural norms, they may expect candidates to adopt dispositional framing when explaining their crimes, since those would have been—based on their culturally shaped perceptions—driven by dispositions. Making situational explanations about one's crime, on the other hand, may be perceived as not identifying the correct contributors and thus downplaying one's responsibility. Indeed, qualitative interviews with parole commissioners reported that they are prone to attribute crimes to the offenders’ free will or a deficit in their internal characteristics (47–49) and expect the offenders to share this perspective (49, 50). Successfully incorporating this view by making dispositional attributions of their own crimes signals “genuine” remorse, leading to a greater likelihood of receiving parole (49). We aim to provide the initial quantitative evidence that the US justice system favors parole candidates who provide dispositional explanations about their crimes, which may only benefit candidates from certain cultural groups.

The current research examines whether cultural background influences both (i) how parole candidates explain their crimes and (ii) how parole commissioners interpret these explanations. In studies 1 and 2, we examined whether cultural background shapes how mock parole candidates explain their hypothetical crimes. We predicted that White American candidates would be more likely to offer dispositional explanations, whereas interdependent candidates—such as Black, Hispanic, and East Asian Americans—would place relatively greater emphasis on situational factors. However, given prior research showing that interdependent individuals often incorporate both dispositional and situational elements when explaining behavior (51), we did not expect them to overwhelmingly favor situational attributions. In study 3, we examined those on the other end of the parole decision-making process: parole commissioners. We tested whether White American mock parole commissioners—those from an independent cultural background—would perceive candidates who provided dispositional (vs. situational) explanations as less likely to reoffend and therefore more deserving of parole. Overall, our findings indicate that White Americans were more likely than their interdependent counterparts to favor dispositional explanations, both as mock parole candidates and as mock parole commissioners. Lastly, in study 4, we further investigated whether the US justice system's endorsement of White American cultural values and preference for dispositional accounts is indeed reflected in their parole-granting decisions. We specifically predicted and demonstrated that New York State parole candidates who provided more dispositional (vs. situational) accounts about their crimes during their initial parole hearings would be more likely to have been granted (vs. denied) parole.

Results

In study 1, participants imagined that they were currently incarcerated and needed to request parole from their parole board. We varied the participants’ hypothetical crimes to be either arson or drug selling, since racial disparities in sentencing are particularly manifested for property and drug crimes (52), although they disperse across a wide range of crimes (53, 54). We further varied their criminal history to be either a first-time offender or a reoffender, since this is arguably one of the most crucial factors that parole commissioners consider when making their decisions (6; but see also 14). Since crime severity did not modulate the cultural mismatch effect in our preliminary study (Section S1), all participants imagined that they committed a Class E crime, the least severe felony based on the New York State penal code (55).

In study 1, employing a 4 (Participant Culture: White, Black, Hispanic, and East Asian American)×2 (Criminal History: First-time offender and Reoffender)×2 (Crime Type: Arson and Drug selling) between-subject design, participants (80 per cultural group) were randomly assigned to imagine that they were a first-time offender or a reoffender who committed either arson or drug selling. Participants were presented with two offender statements providing explanations about why they committed this crime. These statements mirror offender statements parole candidates provide in their parole packets, wherein they explain and self-reflect upon their crimes (56) (see Section S2 for the full statements). The statements described the same criminal act but explained it in either dispositional (dispositional statement; e.g. “I really wish I had been more proactive at my job” and “I should not have chosen to commit the crime”) or situational (situational statement; e.g. “I really wish there could have been more support at my job” and “I should not have surrendered to the circumstances and committed the crime”) terms. The statements also contained participants’ hypothetical criminal history information (first-time offenders: “This is my first time in prison,” reoffenders: “This is my third time in prison”). After reading both dispositional and situational statements in a random order, participants indicated which one they would like to submit on their own behalf to parole commissioners to request parole (Offender Statement Choice). Since the specific type of crime did not change our findings, it was omitted from the final analyses (Section S3).

A χ2 test of independence was conducted to examine the relationship between Participant Culture and Offender Statement Choice along the different levels of the Criminal History condition. Results revealed significant differences across Participant Culture especially in the Reoffender condition, χ2(3, n = 157) = 8.64, P = 0.035, Cohen's d = 0.47. When participants imagined that they were reoffenders, 71.8% of White Americans chose to submit the dispositional statement, whereas only 60.5% of Black Americans, 41.0% of Hispanic Americans, and 48.8% of East Asian Americans chose to do the same. Only White Americans’ Offender Statement Choice was significantly different from 50%, indicating that they were more likely to submit the dispositional statement over the situational statement (t[38] = 2.99, P = 0.005, Cohen's d = 0.48). Black, Hispanic, and East Asian Americans submitted dispositional and situational statements equally, all P > 0.19. The differences between White Americans and Hispanic Americans (B = 1.30, SE = 0.48, P = 0.007, odds ratio = 3.66, 95% CI = [1.42, 9.42]), and between White Americans and East Asian Americans (B = 0.98, SE = 0.47, P = 0.038, odds ratio = 2.67, 95% CI = [1.06, 6.76]) were significant, while the difference between White Americans and Black Americans was directional (B = 0.51, SE = 0.49, P = 0.298, odds ratio = 1.66, 95% CI = [0.64, 4.31]; Fig. 1). There was no significant association between Participant Culture and Offender Statement Choice in the first-time offender condition, χ2(3, n = 163) = 0.51, P = 0.916 (63.4% of White, 61.9% of Black, 68.3% of Hispanic, and 61.5% of East Asian Americans chose to submit the dispositional statement). These findings persisted when we performed binary logistic regression (Section S3). Thus, when given the opportunity to explain the same criminal act either dispositionally or situationally, White American mock parole candidates were more likely to choose the dispositional accounts rather than the situational accounts compared with members of other racial groups.

One of the limitations of study 1 is that it did not provide participants the opportunity to elaborate on why they committed the crime in their own language. Would these results persist when participants freely describe the contributors to their criminal acts, rather than simply choosing between two laboratory-crafted explanations? In study 2, employing a 4 (Participant Culture)×2 (Criminal History) between-subject design, participants (67 White, 64 Black, 69 Hispanic, and 68 East Asian Americans) imagined that they committed a class E drug-selling crime, either as a first-time offender or as a reoffender. They were provided with a list of hypothetical events from their lives that might have contributed to their crime and wrote an offender statement to request parole. The life events were either dispositional (e.g. “lack of confidence” and “wanted to earn more money than you already did”), situational (e.g. “bullied by peers for one year” and “peer pressure to prove that you can do this”), or ambivalent (e.g. “difficulties in academic performance” and “difficulties in securing a job”; Section S4). Participants were asked to pick the life events that served as the biggest contributors to their crime(s) and elaborate on them as they saw fit to write their offender statements, filling in four blanks. In the first blank, participants were prompted to discuss the event(s) that played the biggest role in leading them to commit the crime. In the second blank, participants were prompted to discuss the event(s) that played the second biggest role in leading them to commit the crime, and so on. We focused on the first blank, given that this would be the most similar to explanations that parole candidates would provide during parole hearings, when there is limited time available (see Section S4 for findings in other blanks).

Given that participants provided open-ended responses, a more generalized natural language processing (NLP) method was necessary to code attributions made in-text (Section S5). An exhaustive list of words reflecting all possible aspects of dispositional versus situational attributions across multiple contexts is not possible, therefore, a comprehensive, top-down, dictionary-based approach to detecting attribution cues was not feasible. As such, we developed a set of general-purpose attribution classifiers in order to code participant responses in a replicable, objective fashion. Each response was tokenized into sentences, which were then vectorized using a contextual embedding model. These semantic vectors were then used to separately infer the presence of self-dispositional, self-situational, other-dispositional, and other-situational attributions in each sentence using binary classification (0: not present, 1: present). Self-dispositional (vs. self-situational) attributions refer to causal factors that are internal (vs. external) to the mock parole candidate (e.g. self-dispositional: “And, due to my own stupidity” and “I have grown so much as a person”; self-situational: “I was bullied in school for one year which felt like forever” and “Back then, my school life was very difficult for me”). On the other hand, other-dispositional (vs. other-situational) attributions refer to causal factors that are internal (vs. external) to other individuals whom the parole candidate refers to (e.g. other-dispositional: “They made it sound so glamorous” and “They frequently boasted about how easy it was to make large amounts of money without working”; other-situational: “As they were too busy with bills” and “The recession then hit which made getting a job harder”). We then found the total number of self-dispositional, self-situational, other-dispositional, and other-situational attributions that each participant provided by summing across all their sentences for each category. We created a “relative overall dispositional attribution” index (M = −3.25, SD = 2.46, range: [−12.00, 0.00]) by subtracting the parole candidates’ total number of situational attributions (i.e. the sum of self-situational, other-dispositional, and other-situational attributions) from their total number of self-dispositional attributions provided in blank 1 of the offender statement.

A 4 (Participant Culture: White, Black, Hispanic, and East Asian American)×2 (Criminal History: First-time offender and Reoffender) Univariate ANOVA on the relative overall dispositional attribution index, controlling for the number of words they used, revealed a Participant Culture × Criminal History interaction, F(3, 259) = 2.68, P = 0.047, ηp2 = 0.03, Cohen's d = 0.35. Among participants who imagined that they were reoffenders, White Americans (M = −2.59, SE = 0.34) were significantly more likely than their Black American counterparts (M = −3.72, SE = 0.36) to provide higher relative overall dispositional attribution, P = 0.023, Cohen's d = 0.42. They were also marginally more likely than East Asian Americans (M = −3.46, SE = 0.31; P = 0.060, Cohen's d = 0.32) and directionally more likely than Hispanic Americans (M = −2.77, SE = 0.29; P = 0.690, Cohen's d = 0.07) to provide more relative overall dispositional attribution. There were no cultural differences for the first-time offender condition, all P > 0.12 (Fig. 2). Thus, even when participants used their own words, White Americans used more dispositional, and less situational, language compared with members of other racial groups (see Section S6 for replications with another NLP approach).

In studies 1 and 2, we focused on the psychology of only parole candidates, i.e. what kinds of explanations they would provide. What about parole commissioners? How would they respond to the dispositional and situational explanations from the candidates, depending on their cultural backgrounds? To answer this question, in study 3, participants (256 White and 256 Black Americans) imagined they were parole commissioners and read two offender statements from two different ostensible parole candidates. We preregistered the hypothesis that White Americans would perceive parole candidates as less likely to reoffend when they provided dispositional (vs. situational) causal explanations about their crimes, compared with Black Americans (https://aspredicted.org/pe68n7.pdf; Section S7). We particularly focused on White and Black Americans in this study because they make up a significant portion of the state and federal prison population (63% of the prison population in 2022 (57)) and show the most prominent racial disparity in the criminal justice system (58). But we found the same patterns with White, Black, Hispanic, and East Asian Americans in our preliminary study (Section S1, Tables S1–S7, and Fig. S1).

Participants were presented with two offender statements, the same ones used in study 1 (see Table S8 for manipulation check). Yet, in study 3, we added another level of crime severity, i.e. class A crime (e.g. selling 3 oz of heroin), in addition to the class E crime (e.g. selling 0.125 oz of heroin) since racial disparities in sentencing are manifested in severe, as well as minor crimes (58–60). We also aimed to explore whether the parole candidates’ race, being either White or Black, would modulate any cultural mismatch effect. We thus informed the participants that the parole candidate was either White (“non-Hispanic White”) or Black (“Black”) and presented images of ostensible parole candidates’ faces, retrieved from public facial stimuli repositories (61, 62). Since participants perceived these faces as differentially trustworthy and attractive (Section S7), these ratings were controlled for in the analyses.

With a 2 (Participant Culture: White American and Black American)×2 (Causal Attribution: Dispositional and Situational)×2 (Parole Candidate Race: White and Black)×2 (Criminal History: First-time offender and Reoffender)×2 (Crime Severity: classes A and E) between-subject design, participants read one arson and one drug-selling statement in a random order, which were worded in either dispositional or situational terms, showed either a White or a Black face, were from parole candidates with the same criminal history, and depicted the same level of crime. Afterward, they rated the likelihood of re-offense of the parole candidates (0: extremely unlikely, 100: extremely likely), as well as how likely they would be to grant them parole (1: extremely unlikely, 7: extremely likely) as mock parole commissioners. Since there were no effects of the different types of crimes (Section S7), we averaged across the participants’ ratings for the two statements and created an aggregated score of likelihood of perceived re-offense and parole granting.

A 2 (Participant Culture) × 2 (Causal Attribution) × 2 (Parole Candidate Race) × 2 (Criminal History) × 2 (Crime Severity) Univariate ANOVA on the participants’ ratings of the parole candidate's likelihood of re-offense revealed a significant Participant Culture × Causal Attribution interaction, F(1,478) = 5.92, P = 0.015, ηp2 = 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.22. This interaction was not modulated by any other effects, all P > 0.26. Corroborating our preregistration, White Americans perceived parole candidates who provided a dispositional account as less likely to reoffend (M = 48.81, SE = 1.70), compared with those who provided a situational account (M = 54.66, SE = 1.59), P = 0.013, 95% CI = (−10.42, −1.26), Cohen's d = 0.22. Black Americans did not differentiate parole candidates based on their attribution styles (dispositional M = 52.09, SE = 1.68; situational M = 49.86, SE = 1.65; P = 0.343; Fig. 3; Table S9). White Americans also rated parole candidates who provided situational accounts as more likely to reoffend than Black Americans, P = 0.037, 95% CI = (0.30, 9.30), Cohen's d = 0.18. Thus, even when they played the role of a parole commissioner, White Americans perceived that those who provided a dispositional account would be at a lower risk of re-offense.

Potentially reflecting people's tendency to overcorrect themselves when making decisions believed to assess one's racial biases (63), analysis of participants’ likelihood to grant parole did not reveal a significant effect involving Participant Culture and Causal Attribution, all P > 0.13 (Table S10). However, we conducted an exploratory moderated mediation analysis using PROCESS macro (model 7 (64)), to examine whether participants’ re-offense ratings could explain their parole-granting decisions. Participants’ parole-granting ratings were entered as the dependent variable. Participants’ culture (White American = −1, Black American = +1) was entered as the independent variable, their re-offense ratings were entered as the mediator, and causal attribution (Dispositional = −1, Situational = +1) was considered as moderating the influence of participants’ culture on re-offense ratings (Fig. 4). We further controlled for parole candidates’ race, their crime severity, and their prior criminal record, as well as the perceived trustworthiness and attractiveness of the parole candidates’ faces.

Confirming our Univariate ANOVA analyses, there was a significant interaction between participants’ culture and causal attribution on perceived likelihood of re-offense, B = −2.04, SE = 0.81, t = −2.53, P = 0.012, 95% CI = (−3.62, −0.45). White Americans perceived parole candidates who provided situational accounts as more likely to reoffend than Black Americans (B = −2.37, SE = 1.12, t = −2.11, P = 0.035, 95% CI = [−4.58, −0.17]), while they were not different in their perceptions about those who provided dispositional accounts, P = 0.143. Moreover, perceived likelihood of re-offense predicted decreased parole-granting decisions (B = −0.02, SE = 0.002, t = −8.54, P < 0.001, 95% CI = [−0.025, −0.015]). Although the direct effect of participants’ culture on parole-granting decisions was not significant, P = 0.247, the overall moderated mediation effect through causal attribution was (Effect = 0.08, SE = 0.03, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.15]). Specifically, the indirect effect of participants’ culture through re-offense ratings on parole granting was significant when the parole candidates provided situational accounts (Effect = 0.05, SE = 0.02, 95% CI = [0.01, 0.09]), but not when they provided dispositional accounts (Effect = −0.03, SE = 0.03, 95% CI = [−0.09, 0.01]). Thus, participants’ culture indirectly drove different parole-granting decisions through their perceived likelihood of re-offense of the parole candidates, when the parole candidates provided situational explanations about their crimes.

Together, studies 1–3 suggest that while White American parole commissioners may perceive parole candidates who provide situational (vs. dispositional) explanations as more likely to reoffend and expect not to receive such explanations from parole candidates, this may be better followed by White American parole candidates. In contrast, those from interdependent cultures may be just as likely to submit situational statements as dispositional ones, perceiving that they are not different in terms of delivering their resolution to not reoffend to their parole boards. Interestingly, when participants were prompted to freely discuss why they chose a specific version of the offender statement over the other in study 1, they were not different in their intention to deliver their accountability and responsibility to the parole board across cultures (Section S3, Fig. S2). This suggests that those from interdependent cultures perceived situational attribution to be just as proper as dispositional attribution to achieve this goal and did not intend to provide an “excuse” of their crimes by making situational attributions.

To examine whether these culturally shaped preferences for dispositional (vs. situational) accounts were associated with real parole-granting decisions, in study 4, we analyzed real parole candidates’ causal explanations of their crimes, provided during New York State parole hearings. Through a Freedom of Information Law request (65) to the New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision (NYS DOCCS), we obtained 183 parole candidates’ transcripts of initial parole hearings conducted between April 2020 and April 2022. Each transcript contains a word-for-word account of the exchange between parole commissioners and parole candidates during parole hearings.

Most parole systems (e.g. 63% of the states studied in (66)) provide face-to-face parole hearing opportunities either virtually or in person (66). For example, in NYS, parole hearings are conducted as follows. After introducing all participants (e.g. parole candidate and commissioners; the presence of other legal agents varies by states), the presiding commissioner confirms that the parole candidate consents to follow through with the hearing. The presiding parole commissioner then questions the candidate about their instant crime(s), providing the candidate with the opportunity to explain why they committed their crime(s). The presiding commissioner also questions them about their criminal history, rehabilitation efforts, and plans postrelease, and supporting parole commissioners ask clarification questions at the end. Parole candidates provide closing statements if prompted and are then excused.

Afterwards, parole commissioners deliberate behind closed doors. The presiding commissioner usually determines the outcome; the most common decisions include “Open Date (parole approved and earliest release date finalized),” “Granted (parole approved but release date not finalized),” and “Denied” (67). The supporting commissioners almost always agree (68). Since reversion of parole decisions by governors is a rarity, it can be said that parole outcomes very often boil down to the parole board's, especially the presiding commissioner's, opinion.

In analyzing the conversation during parole hearings, we focused on the part where the parole candidates provided their causal explanations about their instant crime(s) and their general, non-offense-specific criminal history (e.g. “why so much crime?”), as a response to the commissioners’ question. We employed the same NLP approach as in study 2. The modeling strategy coded each statement provided by each parole candidate as self-dispositional (e.g. “It was all my wrongdoing” and “You know, I’ve always tried to go for higher education”), self-situational (e.g. “I was under the influence at that time” and “I didn’t have no money”), other-dispositional (e.g. “Then, he started crying” and “These kids are crazy”), and other-situational (e.g. “To be completely honest, it's a family domestic issues” and “Well it wasn’t that much different, things happening in the community at that particular time”). As in study 2, we again created the relative overall dispositional attribution index (M = −4.14, SD = 4.39, range: [−21.00, 1.00]), which was submitted to a binary logistic regression on the parole candidates’ parole decisions (denied: 0, granted [including Open Date and Granted]: 1), controlling for the number of words they used.

As predicted, the effect of relative overall dispositional attributions on parole decision was significant (B = 0.10, SE = 0.05, P = 0.045, odds ratio = 1.10; Fig. 5). The odds of being granted parole increased by about 10.0% for each additional dispositional (vs. situational) attribution that was provided by the parole candidate. These findings persisted after controlling for variables that may impact parole decisions, including the severity and crime type of the parole candidates’ most severe instant crime, their total number of prior imprisonments (M = 0.87, SD = 1.17), their age at the time of the hearing (M = 38.5, SD = 12.31), perceived sex (92% male and 8% female), the commissioners who preceded the hearing, usage of a live interpreter (5.7% used interpreter services), success or failure in obtaining an Earned Eligibility Certificate (EEC)—a certificate awarded to offenders who showed satisfactory progress in institutional programs (58.5% of parole candidates for whom we had data earned EECs), and any oppositions from the sentencing judge or the district attorney, which can be submitted prior to the hearing (among those for whom we have data, 3.9% of parole candidates received opposition from the judge; 25.8% received opposition from the district attorney; Section S8, Table S11). As in study 2, these findings were replicated with a different NLP approach (Section S6).

Study 4 usage of attributions. Discussion

What drives racial disparities in discretionary parole decisions? While racial minority status often overlaps with “cultural” minority status (24), the role of cultural factors in discretionary parole decisions has remained largely unexamined. We propose that cultural mismatch between the dominant independent, White American cultural values endorsed by the US criminal justice system and the interdependent cultural values that members of racial minority groups hold may partially explain racial disparities in discretionary parole. In studies 1–3, White American mock parole candidates used more dispositional (vs. situational) explanations than Black, Hispanic, and East Asian American candidates to explain their crimes. Such dispositional explanations were perceived more favorably by White American mock parole commissioners. Given that the justice system has a history of representing White American cultural values (25–27), which parole commissioners may then closely mirror, there is potential for minority parole candidates to be at a disadvantage. Supporting this idea, study 4 revealed that real parole candidates who framed their crimes dispositionally, rather than situationally, were more likely to be granted parole. These findings introduce cultural mismatch—manifested through attributional style—as a novel mechanism that can contribute to racial disparities in discretionary parole, complementing previous research on the role of racial bias in such outcomes (13, 68).

Is the US justice system's expectation, such that parole candidates should provide dispositional explanations about their crimes, communicated with the candidates? Indeed, parole candidates are often advised to explain their crimes using more dispositional and less situational attributions. For instance, US institutions such as the Office of Appellate Defender (OAD) advise parole candidates that outlining too many situational contributors to their crimes (e.g. having a “difficult childhood” or “the wrong group of friends”) during discretionary parole hearings could be perceived as making excuses, thereby harming their chances at parole. Instead, candidates are asked to ensure that they “accept responsibility for everything that happened” to demonstrate remorse (56; p. 16). In his help guide for parole candidates, Jorge Antonio Renaud—a two-time successful parolee in the United States—stresses the importance of referring to one's personal attributes (e.g. using statements such as “I was in love with material things,” “I did not have patience,” etc.) and not providing situational accounts to obtain discretionary parole (69; p. 6).

The limitation is that, even though the justice system's implicit preference is communicated to parole candidates in some form, cultural “code-switching,” or the purposeful modification of one's behavior to fit the system's recommendations, can be difficult (70). Culture provides the implicit and habitual scripts of how people perceive, construe, and behave in their social world (71), even though people may not recognize its influence because they are so immersed in culture (like fish in water (72)). Thus, it is difficult for people to adopt a different way of thinking/behaving that does not match their cultural practices, especially during highly stressful situations where their reliance on habitual and heuristic systems is increased (73, 74). Corroborating this, prior studies reported that culture critically informs people's responses in highly stressful situations such as interacting with an enemy (75), responding to an insult (76), responding to another's death (77), and handling varying forms of conflicts (78, 79). Although our current study 4 data were not suitable to examine this (see Materials and methods), we speculate that parole candidates attending their hearings would fail to effortfully switch their response style in accordance with White American cultural norms. This can be particularly problematic because the current OAD guidance for parole candidates may even reinforce and rationalize parole commissioners’ culturally predisposed preferences for dispositional explanations. Potentially, instead of informing parole candidates about the justice system's expectations, it would be more important to equip the parole commissioners with knowledge about the cultural mismatch effect. While parole commissioners are educated that their parole decisions should not be influenced by parole candidates’ races (80), they are unaware of this implicit cultural bias.

The cultural channel to explain parole outcomes has not been explored, potentially due to the difficulties in mapping race and culture in the same space. Indeed, it is suggested that members of the same race can have vastly different cultures (81–83). Still, research in cultural psychology has often identified that members of the same race are more, than less, likely to share similar cultures (84). Some researchers even argue that sharing a common culture precedes the formation of a racial group (85). Thus, we assumed that our racial minority samples were more likely to share, rather than not share, a common culture within their racial group. We further assumed that across the racial groups, Black, Hispanic, and Asian Americans shared interdependent culture in common as reported in prior literature (86–91), although the processes through which they came to endorse interdependence and the specific nature of interdependence may vary between them (86, 92). We thus leveraged interdependent culture as an interpretive tool to compare the shared cultural components among Black, Hispanic, and Asian cultures against that of White American cultures, although this approach should not warrant the equation of, or the interchangeable use of, race and culture (81–83). Further, even within the same racial group, the degrees of acculturation to independent vs. interdependent cultures, and their attribution styles, would vary between individuals, promoting further investigations.

It should be noted that these findings do not suggest that cultural minority groups are more lenient toward receiving and providing “excuses.” In fact, in study 1, White, Black, Hispanic, and East Asian Americans were all concerned about taking responsibility and showing accountability (Section S3, Fig. S2). These findings promote further investigations into whether delivering a situational, over and above dispositional, account is viewed as one of the ways to express responsibility in interdependent cultures. While taking “personal” responsibility is primary to those from independent cultures to be morally accountable (46), this alone, without acknowledgment of external contributors, may signal only partial accountability to those from interdependent cultures since their actions are not solely driven by themselves (37–39, 41–45).

It should also be noted that these findings do not suggest that the cultural mismatch effect is the ultimate channel to explain racial disparities in parole decisions in their entirety. Many other contributors, such as individuals’ implicit racial bias (20) and system-level policing and harsher sentencing practices that target members of minorities (18), are importantly in play. With that in mind, we aimed to examine one hugely understudied factor, which may provide actionable interventions before the necessary large-scale societal changes take place.

This research raises several important questions for future investigation. First, what are the nuanced influences of the parole candidates’ criminal history and crime severity? The cultural differences were manifested when mock parole candidates imagined that they were reoffenders in studies 1 and 2. When they imagined that they already had prior criminal history, these candidates might have perceived a greater need to deliver their resolution to not reoffend by adopting their culturally valued explanation. Participants in these studies also only imagined that they were parole candidates who committed the mildest felony, class E crime, not a more severe crime. Because people become stressed when the stakes are high and rely more on their habitual mode of thinking (73, 74), we asked participants to imagine a less stressful situation to conservatively assess the influence of culturally shaped attribution styles. Yet, future studies should test if these findings persist with severe levels of crimes, and across different types of crimes that can be regarded as more detrimental, such as mass murder, or crimes that are rarely attributed to situational causes, such as child sexual abuse offenses (93).

Second, what is the interplay between the cultural mismatch effect observed in our studies and racial bias? As the current parole system's efforts to reduce racial biases in their decisions increases (94, 95), the cultural mismatch effect may become even more critical among parole commissioners. In particular, the pressure of monitoring their racial bias may already lead to cognitive depletion. This may in turn increase their reliance on other biases and heuristic processing of information (96, 97), amplifying the cultural mismatch effect. Indeed, parole commissioners’ massive workload (e.g. in NYS, considering 12,000 applications a year, each parole commissioner must conduct about 25–30 parole hearings per day, requiring them to work 12-h shifts (98)), combined with no explicit training for mitigating the cultural mismatch effect, may make them particularly susceptible to this bias. In fact, despite recent reforms by the NYS DOCCS to make the parole board more diverse and improve sentencing guidelines, appeals processes, and implicit racial bias training, racial disparities have continued to widen (99).

Third, these findings highlight the need to reexamine the current parole decision-making process to minimize different forms of biases. Although some states abolished the discretionary parole system, it is still prevailing (4). The lack of federal guidelines and significant variation in the parole decision-making procedure between states leaves the parole commissioners’ discretion vulnerable to the influence of preexisting biases and heuristic judgments (66, 100–103). Many commissioners are not required to have criminal justice experience or formal legal training, and most states lack specific standards about the education or experience required to join the parole board (101, 104). Additionally, guidelines for parole commissioners tend to be vague, with commissioners being instructed to consider broad concepts like “public safety” or “the public good” when granting parole (105). This leads to the discretionary parole process being less governed by objective evidence and protected procedure and more by a subjective assessment of the candidates by parole commissioners, creating room for the influence of cultural and racial biases (6, 106) despite individual parole commissioners’ efforts to not allow it.

Fourth, these findings broaden the scope of analytic approaches in the field of law and science. Most of the decisions in the justice system are discussed, derived, and based on the use of natural language, such as witness testimony in courtrooms and prosecution opening statements. By applying an innovative, rapidly developing NLP approach, this work provides one of the first attempts to assess such data in a scalable and replicable manner.

The cultural contribution to racial disparities in discretionary parole decisions has been largely overlooked. This research represents an initial step in examining how cultural mismatches between the US justice system and the parole candidates can influence parole outcomes. Our findings suggest that US parole commissioners may hold culturally shaped expectations that candidates should provide dispositional (vs. situational) criminal explanations for their crimes—expectations that are more easily met by individuals from White American, independent cultural backgrounds. In contrast, parole candidates from interdependent cultures may be less likely to adopt this attributional style, potentially leading to higher denial rates and further widening racial disparities in parole decisions. As racial disparities in discretionary parole have continued to grow in recent years (100), this work highlights an overlooked factor in legal decision-making, underscoring the urgent need for further research and policy intervention.

Materials and methods

Ethics

All studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at Dallas. All participants provided informed consent prior to their participation.

Participants

In all studies (studies 1–3; Sections S3, S4, and S7), we recruited more participants than the minimum number required to achieve 80% power with α = 0.05 to detect a medium effect, Cohen's f = 0.25 (studies 1 and 2), or a small to medium effect, Cohen's f = 0.13 (study 3) (minimum sample size: studies 1 and 2: 240 participants; study 3: 468 participants). All participants were recruited via Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/). Participants’ racial/ethnic groups were determined based on their responses to a self-report question (“Please indicate your ethnicity [i.e. peoples’ ethnicity describes their feeling of belonging and attachment to a distinct group of a larger population that shares their ancestry, colour, language or religion”]), which Prolific collects at the time of their registration on the platform. All participants were provided with background information about the offense classifications (i.e. the meaning of different severity levels of crimes and specifics about the US discretionary parole review process, e.g. incarcerated individuals become eligible for parole after serving their minimum terms of imprisonment, and factors, such as criminal history, crime severity, and institutional behavior are considered during the decision-making process) at the beginning of each study (Section S1).

Offender statements and ratings

For studies 1 and 3, we developed four offender statements for two types of crimes: arson and drug selling. These statements were created based on a discretionary parole preparation guide for NYS parole candidates (56). They were first-person letters in which ostensible parole candidates causally explained their criminal behavior to request parole, which are reviewed both before and after their hearings. We experimentally manipulated the parole candidates’ explanations of their crime(s) as dispositional or situational, in addition to their criminal history and the severity of their instant crime. A pilot test confirmed that the dispositional statements were evaluated as providing more dispositional, and less situational, explanations about the crimes than the situational statements (Section S2). The parole candidate's criminal history was manipulated such that the current crime was reported as their first one (e.g. “This is my first time in prison”) or third one (e.g. “This is my third time in prison, having served two 1-year sentences for third-degree criminal mischief previously”). The severity level was manipulated to be either class A, violent (e.g. set an occupied building on fire, resulting in the death of a person/sold 3 ounces of heroin) or class E, nonviolent (e.g. set an unoccupied building on fire/sold 0.125 ounces of heroin) felonies, following the NYS Penal Code (55). All other information in the offender statements, including their rehabilitation efforts, future plans, rate of remorseful words used (e.g. “I regret”), their age, disability status, and incarceration length, were kept consistent (Section S2).

Since crime severity did not modulate the cultural mismatch effect in a preliminary study (Section S1), we only used class E crimes in study 1. Participants saw one dispositional and one situational offender statements, which described the same criminal act, either arson or drug selling, and indicated which one they would like to submit on their own behalf to parole commissioners. Findings did not change as a function of the type of the crimes (arson or drug selling; Section S3).

In study 3, participants read two offender statements, one depicting arson and the other depicting drug selling. Except for the specific crime, the statements were identical in terms of how the crime was framed (dispositionally or situationally), the race of the parole candidate who ostensibly wrote the statement (White and Black), the criminal history of the parole candidate (first-time offender or reoffender), and the severity of the crime. After reading each statement, participants indicated the perceived likelihood of the parole candidates’ re-offense as described above, as well as how likely they would be to grant parole to them. Findings persisted when we included class C crimes (e.g. set an occupied building on fire, not resulting in casualties/sold 1 ounce of heroin), in addition to classes A and E crimes in a preliminary study (Section S1).

Facial stimuli

In study 3, we retrieved two White and two Black male faces in their forties from the Face Research Lab London Set (61) (two White and one Black face) and FACES Expanded dataset (62) (one Black face). All faces exhibited a neutral expression. Participants were randomly assigned to view either two White or two Black faces. The order of the faces was counterbalanced across participants. Because participants perceived the faces as differentially trustworthy and attractive based on their cultural backgrounds, these ratings of the faces were controlled for in further analyses (Section S7).

Parole hearing transcripts

We obtained 194 parole candidates’ transcripts of initial parole hearings. We specifically focused on initial parole hearings to account for the practice effect that may be associated with attending multiple parole hearings for the same instant crime. Each of the transcripts was redacted of legally undisclosable and sensitive information, including the parole candidates’ medical information, mental health/substance abuse treatment information, and addresses, prior to being sent to us. Each parole candidate's causal explanation(s) of their instant offense(s), in response to their parole commissioner's nonleading, direct prompt (e.g. “Why were you trying to steal?”) as well as their follow-up request for repetition (e.g. “Okay. So, again, I've got to go back to the basic question which is: why so much crime?”) or clarification (“I'm sorry, you were out there doing what?”) were extracted. Transcripts where the offenders failed to provide any causal explanation of their instant offense(s) (n = 7) or claimed complete innocence (n = 4) were further excluded, leaving us with 183 transcripts in the final sample. We categorized the parole candidates whose parole requests were approved as “granted,” regardless of whether their release date was determined, and those whose parole request was not approved as “denied” in the subsequent analyses. Since our original purpose of this data collection was not centered on the investigation of racial disparities in parole granting, these sampled transcripts do not mirror the racial disparities presented during this period. As a result, candidates from different racial groups used similar dispositional and situational language in describing their crimes, P = 0.071, and were similarly likely to receive parole within this specific sample, P = 0.549, potentially because those from the minorities who provided dispositional explanations were overrepresented in this sample (Section S8).

Training and deploying the attribution classifiers

To classify mock (study 2) and New York State parole candidates’ (study 4) causal explanations of their crimes as dispositional or situational, we built a general-purpose, sentence-level causal attribution classifier.

First, we randomly selected 10,000 comments from the r/AskReddit (i.e. a discussion forum containing conversations on various everyday topics) to sample individuals’ natural attributions about their everyday life events, not constrained to any specific themes or topics. Leveraging Reddit posts has been actively adopted to train large language model (LLM) classifiers in prior research (107). Each Reddit comment was segmented into individual sentences using the Punkt tokenizer from the Natural Language Toolkit (108). Afterwards, each sentence was annotated using ChatGPT-4o-mini (OpenAI) (109), prompted with a custom-designed schema grounded in psychological attribution theory. The schema distinguished between self-attribution (i.e. attributions made about the speaker themselves) and other attribution (i.e. attributions made about another person or entity). If self and/or other attribution was present, the schema assigned a binary coding, 1 or 0, marking the presence of the dispositional (e.g. traits, values, and abilities) and situational (e.g. context and external pressures) attribution. This yielded four binary labels per sentence (self-dispositional, self-situational, other-dispositional, and other-situational). Since human-verifiable LLM annotations are both more consistent and less biased than crowd worker-generated annotations (110, 111), these annotations were manually reviewed for general accuracy by one of the authors (R.L.B.), an expert in the psychology of verbal behavior. This dataset was further vectorized using the all-mpnet-base-v2-negation model for detection of negated constructions (e.g. “I am not guilty”).

Afterwards, we trained four independent logistic ridge regression classifiers, one for each attribution category (112, 113). Each model was trained using an 80–20 train-test split, employing a 10-fold cross-validation approach to assess performance. The models demonstrated strong classification performance, recording area under the receiver operating characteristic curve ranging between 0.807–0.896 (cross-validated) and 0.808–0.893 (test). Precision, recall, and F1 scores reflected robust performance, with models capturing meaningful patterns of attribution from the sentences on Reddit posts (F1weighted range = 0.73–0.84; MAcc range = 0.72–0.81).

We then applied these classifiers to data collected in studies 2 and 4. This approach ensures that no GPT-generated output was ever applied to the main corpus of mock offender statements or parole hearing data. Each sentence from the study 2 mock parole candidate offender statements, and study 4 parole transcripts, was vectorized using the same negation-sensitive embedding model. These vectorized sentences were passed through the trained classifiers to obtain binary labels for each attributional category. These sentence-level labels were aggregated across each participant or each parole candidate to create individual-level attributional profiles and then submitted to the final analyses as described in the Results. Human participants’ manual dispositional and situational ratings on a subset of the study 4 parole transcripts were significantly correlated with the attributional profiles that we created using this approach (Section S9).
