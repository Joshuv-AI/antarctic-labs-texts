A national-scale dataset for neighbourhood-level urban building energy modelling for England and Wales
Authors: Grace Colverd, Ronita Bardhan, Jonathan Cullen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Buildings are significant contributors to global greenhouse gas emissions, accounting for 26% of global energy sector emissions in 2022. Meeting net-zero goals requires a rapid reduction in building emissions, both directly from the buildings and indirectly from the production of electricity and heat used in buildings. National energy planning for net zero demands both detailed and comprehensive building energy consumption data. However, geo-located building-level energy data is rarely available in Europe, with analysis typically relying on anonymised, simulated or low-resolution data. To address this problem, we introduce a dataset of Neighbourhood Energy, Buildings, and Urban Landscapes (NEBULA) for modelling domestic energy consumption for small neighbourhoods (5–150 households). NEBULA integrates data on building characteristics, climate, postcode area, housing density, environment, and socio-demographics, comprising 613,290 samples across England and Wales. It is designed to support evidence-based policymaking across housing, environment, and energy justice domains through integrated spatial-demographic analysis.

Background & Summary

Urban building energy modelling (UBEM) is an important tool for understanding building stocks and their associated energy use. Modern statistical UBEM methods rely on large samples of building-level energy data and associated explanatory building and occupant factors (Ali et al.1). The energy data is typically collected from utility companies, or published by local governments. Privacy concerns surrounding building-level energy data and varying global regulations have led to a lack of open-access building-level energy datasets, as noted by Jin et al.2. The majority of open energy datasets are located in cities in the US, 22 out of a surveyed 332. This concentration of data raises concerns about the potential overfitting of forecasting models to these open datasets (Kazmi et al.3), and limits the national planning of decarbonising building stocks. Of the open datasets reviewed, two datasets were from the UK. The first is the National Energy Efficiency Data Framework (NEED), an anonymised dataset released with representative data on buildings within the UK. The full NEED dataset comprises 4 million samples, including building and occupant characteristics, as well as energy consumption4. Anonymised building datasets like NEED do not enable fine-grained spatial analysis of energy consumption. Buildings within NEED are tagged with their region, but localised analysis is not possible. The second is the Energy Performance of Buildings Data, which provides access to the Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) of buildings in England and Wales. EPCs are a standardised method of assessing the energy efficiency and environmental performance of a building. These certificates, mandated by the European Union in 2003, provide quantifiable data regarding a dwelling’s energy consumption and emissions5. The European Parliament mandate on EPCs allowed them to be calculated either through direct measurement of energy use or via methodological calculation6. EPCs have been mandatory for any house sold or rented in the UK since 2008 (DESNZ7). EPCs suffer from a noted performance gap where actual energy consumption differs from predictions (De Wilde8). Few et al.9 found that EPCS tend to over-predict primary energy intensity, with the over-prediction worsening for lower EPC bands (8% for band C vs. 48% for bands F and G), persisting even when matching assumptions around occupancy and heating styles. Furthermore, Jenkins et al.10 found that EPC ratings for the same building could vary by up to two bands across different assessments.

Closed-source building-level energy datasets also exist in the literature. In the UK, the 3DStock model exemplifies a closed-source approach. It links building-level energy data with Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) along with other data sources to develop 3D building stock models for various areas, including London (Evans et al.11, Godoy-Shimizu et al.12). The potential privacy concerns of the building energy data mean that access to this model is tightly controlled.

The NEBULA (Neighbourhood Energy, Buildings, and Urban Landscapes) dataset presented in this paper offers an alternative: a novel, privacy-preserving, geo-located, open source dataset for energy modelling in the UK at the neighbourhood level (5–150 households). We include domestic energy data for 2022, avoiding the challenges of simulated data or EPC unreliability. Working at the postcode level avoids privacy concerns and enables open-source publication. Our methodology is a true bottom-up methodology, deriving postcode-level attributes relating to the building stock, climate, housing density and socio-demographics. This dataset is targeted towards policymakers, researchers, and practitioners working in energy efficiency, net-zero, urban planning, energy justice and climate policy. The data has already been used to provide energy benchmarks for cities in the UK (Colverd et al.13) and for predictive modelling for retrofit assessment at the postcode level (Colverd et al.14).

After describing the variable generation, we discuss the filters applied to the data to create a data sample suitable for gas-energy modelling. We restrict to only postcodes which are wholly domestic, excluding mixed-use or commercial postcodes. We apply filters on some of the key building stock variables to exclude outliers and anomalies.

Energy

The energy data within NEBULA is drawn from the public records published by the UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ), downloaded at (Energy URL, Postcode level gas statistics: 2022, Postcode level electricity statistics: 2022) (DESNZ15). This data contains annual domestic energy consumption per postcode, with variables including the count of meters per postcode and total, median and mean consumption per postcode, for both gas and electricity. DESNZ provides energy consumption at the postcode level for postcodes with at least five meters, and gas data is regionally adjusted for the temperature to allow for inter-year comparison. Here we use real energy data from 2022.

The gas data is categorised into ‘Domestic’ or ‘Non Domestic’ based on a kWh usage threshold (73,200 kWh). This hard limit means that there will be cases of misclassification, both of small non-domestic users classified as domestic, and cases where multiple properties are served by the same gas meter, which are then classified as Non-domestic. We mitigate this by including only those postcodes which are categorised as wholly domestic based on the building typology, excluding any mixed-use postcodes.

Building Stock

The building stock dataset used in this work is the Verisk ‘UK Buildings’ dataset (The GeoInformation Group Ltd16), published by the Edina Digimap service (https://digimap.edina.ac.uk/). The Verisk data includes key attributes such as building footprint, height, age and typology for all buildings in the UK. We chose this building stock data over open open-source alternatives due to the greater accuracy of building footprints as noted in Krapf et al.17. Verisk have confirmed the publication of the NEBULA dataset as acceptable for open source, given that building-level data is not included.

We use several mapping and lookup files to link postcodes and buildings. For the postcode shapefiles, we use the Ordnance Survey (OS) Code-Point® with Polygons (OS18, https://www.ordnancesurvey.co.uk/products/code-point-polygons, accessed via Edina Digimap Services https://digimap.edina.ac.uk/) under an Education Licence). To connect the building UPRN to Postcodes, we use the ONS UPRN Directory for December 2022 (ONS19, https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/8e8e5386-df9c-4f78-862a-f17e09f14bba/ons-uprn-directory-december-2022, available under an Open Government v3.0 Licence. We link postcodes to the postcode centroids coordinates from the ONS ONSPD Online Latest Centroids (ONS20, https://geoportal.statistics.gov.uk/datasets/ed28dda8b75146288d7d08d56d2290a0_1/explore) available under an Open Government v3.0 Licence.

Before deriving postcode attributes, we implemented cleaning and pre-processing stages. Building heights and ages were cleaned and categorised into discrete groups. Here, height refers to the height of the building, including the roof. While some limited data on roof types is available within Verisk data, the primary attribute provided is roof material rather than shape. Various errors exist within the dataset. We focused on reducing the errors in floor count and height based on thresholds derived from average building parameters. A key variable is the total building floor area within the postcode. We first derive gross external area (GEA) and subsequently, gross internal area (GIA).

Building floor area can be derived either using the storey count variable or the building height variable. To make best use of the data available and enable a confidence assessment of the variable, three methods are used to derived GEA: directly from the raw storey count (GEARaw), a validated storey count method (GEAVal) and directly from the height variable (GEAHeight).

The uncertainty metric U and the confidence metric C identify buildings where GEA estimates diverge significantly. In cases where height and floor count data align closely, a small spread leads to a high confidence, whereas discrepancies between them indicate potential data quality issues and lead to a low confidence.

We then derive an estimate of gross internal area (GIA), from GEA, using EPC floor area assessments. We match Unique Property Reference Numbers (UPRNs) from Verisk building stock to EPC data and calculate the ratio of GEA to Floor Area for any non-flat typologies. We calculate the global means for each combination of typology and age for this scaling factor (filtering to only cases with at least 1000 samples) and use it to scale the GEA for each building based on its typology and age. For cases where the specific typology and age combination does not have a sample scaling factor, we use the global mean scaling factor across all matched buildings, and failing that, the overall average scaling factor of 0.6. For flats, it is not possible to reliably calculate the number of flats within each block at scale, and so the standard scaling factor is used. Further details of the GEA scaling factor are given in the SI. Three GIA metric are derived, based on scaling the three GEA metrics: GIAHeight, GIARaw, GIAVal.

Regions

We match PC to Output Area (OA), and subsequent higher geographic census regions (Lower Super Output Area (LSOA), Middle Super Output Area (MSOA) and Local Authority (LA)). administrative regions using the ONS Postcode Lookup for the 2021 census geographies (ONS23,24, https://geoportal.statistics.gov.uk/datasets/e7c49b62898a417192a336aca17e3a3f/about).

Socio-Demographics

We uses the 2021 census for England and Wales to calculate socio-demographic data per PC (ONS25, https://www.ons.gov.uk/census/planningforcensus2021/ukcensusdata). The raw census data (e.g. count of households with property X) is transformed into percentage attributes at the OA level and then matched to PC, the aforementioned ONS. The 13 themes of demographics are given in Table 2. The census themes were chosen to cover a broad range of socio-demographic factors and include highly predictive attributes identified in prior works. The total number of demographic attributes is 125.

Energy Use Intensity

Energy Use Intensity (EUI) is calculated as annual energy consumption per gross domestic internal floor area (GIA). This is a key metric for assessing energy consumption normalised by building size. We calculate the total heated floor area per PC using the GIA of all domestic buildings (excluding outbuildings), using GIARaw: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,{\rm{EUI}}=\frac{E}{{\sum }_{{\rm{PC}}}\,{{\rm{GIA}}}_{{\rm{Raw}}}}$$\end{document}EUI=E∑PCGIARawwhere E is the total annual domestic energy consumption (gas or electric) and GIARaw is the GEARaw scaled by the associated scaling factor, summed over the postcode.

Final Dataset

After attribute generation, several additional filters were applied to create a clean dataset.

Residential Filter

We filter only to include postcodes that are identified as 100% residential. This is to match the postcode-level gas and electricity data; non-domestic energy data is not available at the postcode level.

Household filter

We filter based on the alignment of the number of gas meters and the count of UPRNs in a postcode. UPRNs refer to addresses and work as a proxy for the count of households. We filter out any postcodes where the difference between the count of UPRNs and the count of gas meters was greater than 40%. This excludes any areas with a high proportion of properties not connected to the gas grid (‘non-gas’ properties). Given our stated aims of a dataset to support energy modelling of gas consumption for policy-makers targeting net zero, this enables us to investigate postcode-level EUI with confidence. Users of this data set should bear this limitation in mind, and future iterations of the data set should expand to include this element.

EUI thresholds

Whilst energy analyses typically exclude based on total energy use, here we follow12 and filter based on annual EUI thresholds (kWh/m2). A gas EUI filter of 5 < EUI < 650 kWh/m2 and an electricity filter of 5 < EUI < 150 kWh/m2 are applied.

Other thresholds

Thresholds were applied to the count of buildings and total building GEA of 1–100 and 50–20,000 m2 respectively. We also exclude postcodes with a percentage of unknown typologies > 25, and postcodes with average gas < 65,000 kWh/yr or total gas < 1,000,000 kWh/yr. We also cap the postcode area at 2.5e+05 m2.

Technical Validation

Morris Analysis

The pre-processing stages detailed in the methodology have corrected various errors in the underlying building stock data. Given the aforementioned prior work within the literature identifying square meters of living space as a highly influential variable influencing energy consumption, we run a sensitivity analysis on our derived variables relating to GEA (Raw, Val, Height). Our approach, informed by Menberg et al.27, employed Morris’ method for parameter screening, which systematically explores the parameter space by applying one-at-a-time variations to input parameters28. This method was chosen for its computational efficiency, given the substantial run time of the data generation model, and the ability to identify the variation caused by each input parameter. For each of the 10 regions in England and Wales, we tested a sample of 5000 postcodes, stratified on the number of buildings per postcode to ensure representativeness. The analysis involved applying perturbations of ±10% to height and floor area, sampling perturbation multipliers from 0.9 to 1.1 for each input.

For a set of k input parameters, Morris’ method generates a sequence of k+1 points, each differing in one coordinate from the preceding one, forming a trajectory, where each point represents a model run. The magnitude of variation in model output due to a pre-defined variation of parameter i is termed the elementary effect (EE), calculated as27,28: 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${EE}_{i}=\frac{Y(X+{e}_{i}{\Delta }_{i})-Y(X)}{{\Delta }_{i}}$$\end{document}EEi=Y(X+eiΔi)-Y(X)Δiwhere Y is the model output, X is the parameter vector, ei is a vector of zeroes except for the i-th parameter that equals 1 and represents an incremental change in parameter i, and δi is the predetermined variation. EE has the same units as the model outcome Y, which for our analysis is m2.

For each postcode, we run 15 Morris trajectories, examining the absolute mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}^{\ast }$$\end{document}μi∗) and standard deviation (σ) of the elementary effects: 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}^{\ast }\,=0.5\,\mathop{\sum }\limits_{t=1}^{r}| {EE}_{it}| $$\end{document}μi∗=0.5∑t=1r∣EEit∣16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma =\sqrt{\frac{1}{(r-1)}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{t=1}^{r}{({EE}_{it}-{\mu }_{i})}^{2}}$$\end{document}σ=1(r-1)∑t=1r(EEit-μi)2where r is the number of trajectories.

Menberg et al.27 noted that the Morris method could be unstable regarding parameter ranking in the context of building energy modelling. Still, that instability was mitigated by using the absolute median and multiple runs. Given the large number of postcodes being analysed relative to the trajectories, we calculate both the mean and median across the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}^{\ast }$$\end{document}μi∗ and σ across the set of postcodes. Given the high computational cost per postcode and the range of postcodes assessed, this method efficiently compromises accuracy and computational feasibility.

Following the classification scheme proposed by Garcia Sanchez et al.29 and applied by Menberg et al.27, we analysed the ratio of μ* to σ to characterise input parameters in terms of (non-)linearity and (non-)monotony. The three GEA estimates show the same pattern of behaviour for premise area, identifying it as the more influential parameter with higher elementary effects and consequently a greater impact on Y. The median results for premise area are lower than the mean, due to the influence of outlier postcodes, a phenomenon also observed by Menberg et al.27. Notably, the premises area consistently falls within the linear regime, indicating a direct linear relationship between variations in the premises area and GEA per postcode, an expected outcome given the model’s algorithmic structure. In contrast, the three methods show distinct behaviour as related to height, as expected given their different derivations. The Height method shows a substantial impact from the height multiplier, with a non-zero median and mean value (median 29 m2 vs. mean 198 m2 for μ), along with significantly higher outlier impact (higher σ). The Raw Floor Count method shows no impact from the height multiplier, reflecting the lack of involvement of height in the methodology. For both the Height method and Validated Floor Count method, the height multiplier demonstrates monotonic to almost-monotonic behaviour, characterised by a substantially lower μ* but a much higher σ than the area multiplier. The Validated Floor Count methodology, by construction, uses thresholds on the acceptable floor heights derived per typology (used to exclude those deemed physically unlikely). Investigations revealed that changing these thresholds affects regional performance and should not be taken as indicating an underlying physical pattern in London beyond a greater presence of buildings near the height thresholds, which trigger validation more often than those in the lower strata of the distribution. Similarly, the Height methodology, which includes using the global average storey count per height, will naturally struggle where buildings are at the extremes of their typologies. Given the method of deriving global storey count for each typology and age, the variation for London likely reflects instability in the height-to-storey heuristic.

Given these regional variations, we recommend using the metrics GEARAW and GIARAW as the defaults for analysis (hence their use in deriving the EUI metrics). The Height and Val metrics are useful for identifying places where height and storey count diverge. These are particularly useful when working within a region. They are also provided for completeness, given their use in the GEA metric agreement U (Eqn. 7). Cross-regional comparisons of the metric U should note the limitations of the methodology in London.

When looking at average electricity per meter the distinction is stronger, with the North East more significantly underconsuming (medium effect sizes) against the top consuming group (South East, London and East of England).

The difference between the EUI metrics and the average per meter metrics reflects that buildings can have more than one meter, and in London, the average meters per building is higher than the national average (mean of 4.6 gas meters per building vs. the national mean of 2). This increased number of gas meters per building reflects larger buildings but lower building count in London, whilst the number of meters per gross internal area for London is on par with the national mean (London mean 1.3e-2 vs. national mean of 1.3e-2 gas meters per m2)

After regional investigations, we conclude that London does show some small differences in behaviour to other regions but it is not a significant outlier. London is a consistent top consumer across all energy metrics but the differences, while statistically significant, are generally small in effect sizes, apart from with respect to the South West and Wales for gas metrics and the North East and Wales for electricity. The consistency in the energy performance of London when filtering on the confidence metric ensures that any bias in the area derivation that leads to higher Morris parameters in London does not impact the validity of the energy results and the extreme results for London Morris results are a feature of thresholds used in the derivation.

Limitations

Several key limitations of this dataset, although mentioned within this paper, are worth highlighting explicitly. Firstly, the geographic coverage is not complete. Within England and Wales postcodes are excluded that are not wholly domestic based on typology of the buildings within. This excludes mixed-use postcodes, which tends to bias to towards exclusions in city centres, as seen in Fig. 3(b) with notable higher exclusions in central London. Postcodes with no gas connections are also excluded, with postcode exclusions occurring when postcodes do not have gas and electricity data. This causes exclusions of some rural communities where no gas connections exist, and can be noted in parts of Wales in Fig. 3(b).

Secondly, the energy data used, as published in the Subnational Consumption statistics, is not the same as smart meter data despite being called ‘real’ energy data. There is still an element of modelling involved, as noted in Calderon et al.31, the method for calculating energy consumption data includes processing ‘Annual Quantity’, in which two meter readings taken 6-18 months apart are used, and used to calculate the amount used by that meter in one year (DESNZ32). The authors argue that this data should be considered as not purely empirical but as a mix between empirical and modelled. The electricity consumption consists of approximately 80% actual readings and 20% estimated readings.
