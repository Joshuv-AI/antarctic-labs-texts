Evolving Trends and Racial Disparities in Alcohol Associated Liver Disease (ALD), Metabolic Dysfunction Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH) and Hepatitis C Viral Infection (HCV) as Etiology of Liver Transplantation: Past, Present, and Future Projections
Authors: Boniface Mensah, Albert E Orhin, Natalie AY Akoto, Seunghee Han, Rachel A Boateng, Ramya Varesiddy, Joseph Atarere, Simardeep Singh, Terrylyna Baffoe-Bonnie, Haider Naqvi, Christopher J Haas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver transplantation remains the definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease (ESLD) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). While hepatitis C virus (HCV) was historically the leading indication for liver transplant, the introduction of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has drastically reduced HCV-related ESLD. In contrast, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) have emerged as predominant causes of liver transplantation. This study evaluates racial and ethnic disparities in liver transplantation indications from 2004 to 2023 and projects future trends through to 2040.MethodsDe-identified data from the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research (STAR) files of the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database were analyzed to assess temporal trends in the proportion of ALD, HCV, and MASH as etiologies of liver transplant indications using Joinpoint regression. The annual percentage change (APC) in the proportion of etiologies of liver transplantation were reported with a significance level at a p-value of 0.5. Future projections were estimated using the Box-Jenkins methodology of Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) modelling. Trends were stratified by race/ethnicity to evaluate disparities.ResultsThe proportion of liver transplantation attributed to ALD has risen significantly across all racial groups, with American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN) individuals experiencing the steepest increase. By 2040, ALD is projected to account for 80% of liver transplantation in AIAN populations. MASH-related liver transplantation is also increasing, particularly among Hispanic and White individuals, with projections reaching approximately 40% by 2040. In contrast, HCV-related liver transplantation has sharply declined across all racial groups likely due to the impact of DAAs, however, the Black population continues to experience disproportionately higher rates of HCV-related ESLD.ConclusionThe shifting landscape of liver transplantation etiologies highlights the growing burden of ALD and MASH, emphasizing the need for targeted prevention strategies. Persistent racial disparities in liver transplant access and outcomes underscore systemic healthcare inequities. Addressing these disparities through equitable healthcare policies, early intervention programs, and improved transplant access is crucial to ensuring fair distribution of transplant resources and improved outcomes for all populations.Keywords: Liver transplant, Racial disparities, Trend

Introduction

Liver disease remains a major global health burden, accounting for significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Liver transplantation is the definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease and selected cases of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), offering a lifeline to patients with irreversible damage. In 2023, the United States performed a record 10,660 liver transplants, an 11.9% increase from the prior year and the 11th consecutive record-setting year.1 By 2024, this number rose further to 11,458, a 7.5% increase, highlighting the expanding capacity of the U.S. transplant system.2

The etiology of liver disease in transplant populations has shifted dramatically over the past decade.3 Hepatitis C virus (HCV) has declined as a primary indication, from 28% in 2013 to only 4% in 2022, largely due to the introduction of direct-acting antivirals in 2014.4,5 In contrast, alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) have surged.6 ALD is now the predominant non-HCC indication, rising from 23% in 2013 to 48% in 2022, while MASH increased from 19% to 27%. Among HCC patients, MASH grew from 10% to 31% during the same period.4

The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated ALD-related mortality. Between 2017 and 2019, ALD deaths rose by 1.1% per quarter, but during 2020 this jumped to 11.2%, with the steepest increase in 2021. Mortality from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) also rose, from 1.9% prepandemic to 6.6% during the pandemic.7 Similarly, a UNOS-based cohort study found a 106.6% increase in waitlist additions for acute alcohol-associated hepatitis (AAH) and a 210.2% increase in transplants performed for these patients during the pandemic, both highly significant (P < 0.001).8,9

Despite overall growth in transplantation, disparities persist in access and outcomes. Socioeconomic status, psychiatric comorbidities, and regional variation all affect selection and survival.10,11 Consistent with prior findings (Wong et al., 2020), our study confirms the rising burden of ALD and MASH as leading transplant indications by 2019, while uniquely projecting racial and ethnic disparities through 2040.

Methods

2.1. Study design and data collection

This study investigated the trends in liver transplantation etiology due to alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection from 2004 to 2023 and provided future projections extending to 2040. Data on liver transplant recipients was extracted from Standard Transplant Analysis and Research (STAR) files from the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database. The STAR files contain deidentified data on all waiting list registration and transplants performed and reported to the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) since 1987. Data is publicly available and therefore exempt from IRB approval.

2.2. Study population

Study participants aged 18 years and above who received a liver transplant for ALD, MASH, and HCV were included in the study. Participants were identified using the primary diagnosis at listing. Patients who had not received liver transplant but were on the waiting list were excluded from the study.

2.3. Statistical analysis

2.3.1. Descriptive and temporal trend analysis

The annual percentage of liver transplants attributed to ALD, MASH, and HCV was calculated relative to the total number of liver transplants from 2004 to 2023. Temporal trends in etiology were analyzed by calculating the annual percentage change (APC) using Joinpoint regression. Joinpoint regression detects points in time where significant changes in trends occur, fitting linear segments to identify shifts in rates, such as increases or decreases in outcomes over time. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value<0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

2.3.2. Time series modeling and forecasting

To project future trends in liver transplant etiology from 2024 to 2040, we employed Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) modeling following the Box-Jenkins methodology.

The Box-Jenkins methodology is a systematic approach to develop an optimal time series forecasting model. The Box-Jenkins methodology is a validated and often used tool that ensures a rigorous, data-driven approach to forecasting, facilitating reliable and interpretable predictions.12 It consists of a four-stage process including model identification, model estimation, model diagnostics, and model forecasting.

2.3.3. Model identification

The initial step of model identification involves determining the time series is stationary with the mean and variance of the variable remaining constant with time. The annual percentages were log transformed if the transformation better explained the time series. Time series stationarity was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. If non-stationarity was detected, differencing (d) was applied to achieve stationarity. The autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) were examined to determine the autoregressive (p) and moving average (q) parameters. The ARIMA model (p, d, q) was then obtained.

2.3.4. Model estimation and diagnostics

The optimal ARIMA model, which was stationary and parsimonious but fit the data well was selected based on maximum likelihood estimate, estimate of the error variance (SigmaSQ), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Diagnostics to assess model accuracy were then performed. Residual diagnostics, including Ljung-Box test, tests for stability and invertibility of ARMA and visual inspections of residual plots, were performed to confirm model adequacy. If necessary, refinements were made by adjusting p, d or q values.

2.3.5. Forecasting

The final ARIMA model was applied to predict the proportion of liver transplant cases attributed to ALD, MASH, and HCV from 2024 through to 2040. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for all projections to reflect variability in predictions. Projecting trends to 2040 provides valuable insight into the potential future burden of etiologies of liver transplantation, enabling policymakers, healthcare systems, and researchers to proactively allocate resources, implement preventive strategies, and address health disparities before they widen.

2.4. Software and statistical tools

Analysis on temporal trends were conducted using Joinpoint Regression Program, Version 5.3.0.0 November 2024. ARIMA modelling and forecasting were performed using StataNow (version 18.5, StataCorp, TX).

Results

3.1. Distribution of ALD, MASH and HCV among racial/ethnicity groups

A total of 137,869 patients aged 18 years or more underwent liver transplantation over the study period. Of these, 72,115 patients (52.3%) had ALD, MASH, or HCV listed as the primary diagnosis for liver transplantation. The rates of ALD, MASH, and HCV varied across racial groups. Of the 3 etiologies, ALD was the most common indication among American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN) (52.23%), White (41.58%), and Hispanic (42.11%) patients. In contrast, HCV was the predominant etiology among Black (68.85%) and Asian (47.98%) patients. MASH accounted for the highest proportion of liver transplants in AIAN (26.28%) and White (26.01%) populations but was markedly lower among Black individuals (6.63%). Overall, across all racial groups, ALD (40.36%) and HCV (35.27%) were the leading causes of liver transplant with MASH contributing to 24.37% of cases over the entire study period as shown in Fig. 1.

3.2. Trends in alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) as etiology of liver transplantation by race/ethnicity

The proportion of liver transplants from 2004 to 2023 attributed to alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) varied across racial/ethnic groups (Fig. 2) with a projected rise from 2024 to 2040 in all groups (Fig. 2). Among American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN) individuals, the proportion of alcohol-associated liver transplants declined from 2004 to 2013 (APC = −6.85, p-value>0.05) but subsequently increased sharply from 2013 to 2023 (APC = 17.60, p-value <0.05). The proportion of ALD as etiology of liver transplant among AIAN is projected to continue to increase with a projected proportion of 80% by 2040 as shown in Fig. 2.

(a) Trends in percentage of ALD as primary diagnosis of liver transplant per race/ethnicity. (b) Actual and projected percentage of ALD as primary diagnosis of liver transplant (2004–2040).

In the Asian population, there was a steady and significant increase over the entire study period (2004–2023) with an APC of 11.48 (p < 0.05), reflecting a consistent upward trajectory. The proportion of ALD as etiology of liver transplantation is projected to continue to rise steadily with a proportion of about 30% by 2040 from about 19% in 2024. In Blacks, there was an initial decline from 2004 to 2010 (APC = −6.51, p-value <0.05), followed by a significant increase from 2010 to 2023 (APC = 15.76, p < 0.05). ALD as cause of liver transplantation is projected to continue to rise with projected proportion of about 40% by 2040. Hispanic individuals showed a moderate but non-significant increase from 2004 to 2013 (APC = 2.02, p-value> 0.05) followed by a pronounced rise from 2013 to 2023 (APC = 9.07, p < 0.05) with a projected proportion of about 50% by 2040. In contrast, the White population displayed a more complex pattern with two joinpoints: minimal non-significant change from 2004 to 2012 (APC = 0.02), a sharp increase from 2012 to 2021 (APC = 10.95, p < 0.05), and a plateau from 2021 to 2023 (APC = 1.29). The proportion of ALD as cause of liver transplant by 2040 is projected to be similar to that of Hispanic population as shown in Fig. 2. Fig. 2 also demonstrates that the trend in actual proportions of liver transplantation due to ALD rises sharply from 2020 across all racial groups, due to the effect of COVID-19 pandemic on alcohol use.

3.3. Trends in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) as etiology for liver transplantation by race/ethnicity

Liver transplants due to Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH) have increased significantly across all racial/ethnic groups as shown in Fig. 3. The AIAN population showed a substantial increase from 2004 to 2023 (APC = 9.19, p-value <0.05). There was a substantial increase among Asian population as well from 2005 to 2023 (APC = 17.69 p-value<0.05). In the Black population, the APC from 2004 to 2023 was 18.15 (p-value< 0.05), indicating a steady rise in MASH-related liver transplants. Among Hispanic individuals, the trend was more dynamic, with an APC of 55.84 from 2004 to 2007 (p-value<0.05), followed by a sustained increase from 2007 to 2019 (APC = 14.44, p-value<0.05) and a slower rise from 2019 to 2023 (APC = 2.28, p-value>0.05).

(a) Trends in percentage of MASH as primary diagnosis of liver transplant per race/ethnicity. (b) Actual and projected percentage of MASH as primary diagnosis of liver transplant (2004–2040).

The White population exhibited a rapid early increase (APC = 84.41, p-value<0.05 from 2004 to 2006), followed by a slower but continued rise from 2006 to 2018 (APC = 11.52, p-value<0.05), and stabilization from 2018 to 2023 (APC = 0.87, p-value> 0.05), suggesting a possible plateau effect. The proportion of liver transplants due to MASH is projected to continue to increase in all racial groups. In Whites and Hispanics, the projected proportion is around 40% by 2040 as shown in Fig. 3. The projected proportion among Asian and AIAN populations by 2040 is around 25% with Black population having the lowest projected percentage of about 10%.

3.4. Trends in hepatitis c infection as etiology of liver transplantation by race/ethnicity

The proportion of liver transplants attributed to Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection declined across all racial/ethnic groups (Fig. 4). Among AIAN individuals, there was a marked increase from 2004 to 2008 (APC = 30.40, p-value <0.05), followed by stabilization from 2008 to 2016 (APC = −2.90 p-value> 0.05), and a sharp decline from 2016 to 2023 (APC = −31.73, p-value< 0.05). The Asian population demonstrated a steady decline in Hepatitis C infection-related liver transplant cases, with a significant APC of −18.15 from 2015 to 2023 (p-value< 0.05). Similarly, the Black population exhibited an initial, non-significant increase from 2004 to 2015 (APC = 2.06, p-value>0.05), but experienced a steep decline from 2015 to 2023 (APC = −19.96, p-value< 0.05).

(a) Trends in percentage of HCV as primary diagnosis of liver transplant per race/ethnicity. (b) Actual and projected percentage of HCV as primary diagnosis of liver transplant (2004–2040).

Among Hispanic individuals, the trend remained relatively stable from 2004 to 2014 (APC = −1.37, p-value> 0.05) before a significant decrease from 2014 to 2023 (APC = −18.86, p-value< 0.05). The White population displayed a multi-phase significant decline, with APC values of −19.63 (2014–2018, p-value> 0.05), −25.82 (2018–2021, p-value>0.05), and −8.77 (2021–2023, p-value>0.05), consistent with the impact of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) and improved HCV management.

Projection of the percentage of HCV as cause of liver transplantation among AIAN, Asian, Hispanic and White population shows a significant decline with projected percentage in 2040 almost at 0%. However, among Black population, despite the significant decline, the projected percentage of HCV as etiology of liver transplantation is still above 5% at 2040 as shown in Fig. 4. Fig. 4 also demonstrates the sharp decline of liver transplantation due to HCV from 2013 to 2015 likely due to the introduction of direct-acting antiviral (DAAs) for HCV treatment across all racial groups.

Discussion

Our study highlights a significant shift in the etiologies of liver transplantation over the past two decades, with alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) surpassing hepatitis C virus (HCV) as leading indications. Despite overall improvements in outcomes, persistent racial disparities reflect systemic inequities in healthcare access, disease prevention, and treatment. Addressing these disparities is essential for equitable liver disease management and improved transplant outcomes.

4.1. ALD as the leading indication

ALD-related transplantation has risen across all racial and ethnic groups, with American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN) individuals experiencing the steepest increases. Projections suggest ALD will account for 80% of transplants in this group by 2040. Contributing factors include socioeconomic challenges, limited addiction treatment, and historical inequities.13 The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated alcohol misuse, with surging alcohol sales driving increases in cirrhosis and ESLD.8,14 Early intervention for alcohol use disorder (AUD) in the community hospital setting, including behavioral therapies and medications like naltrexone, remains critical.15

4.2. Declining HCV-related transplantation

Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) have sharply reduced HCV-related transplantation across all groups, with near-zero rates projected by 2040 for AIAN, Asian, Hispanic, and White populations. However, Black individuals remain disproportionately affected, with HCV still projected to account for >5% of transplants. Historically, Black patients have had higher HCV prevalence, lower spontaneous clearance, and poorer interferon-based treatment response.16,17 DAAs achieve cure rates >90% across races,18 yet disparities in access persist. Medicare-based analyses show low-income patients had significantly lower odds of receiving DAAs, and restrictive Medicaid policies further limit timely treatment.18,21 National data confirm Black Americans continue to face higher HCV incidence, mortality, and transplant listing rates.19,20

4.3. The rise of MASH

MASH is among the fastest-growing transplant indications, particularly in Hispanic populations, partly due to the PNPLA3 rs738409-G allele, which increases susceptibility to steatosis and fibrosis.22,23 Combined with high obesity and diabetes rates, this genetic risk accelerates ESLD progression. Currently, no FDA-approved treatments exist; lifestyle interventions and metabolic disease management remain central. GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, have shown promise in reducing liver fat and slowing fibrosis,24,25 but access is inequitable. Over 80% of commercial insurers restrict coverage for weight-loss drugs, and Medicare excludes them, disproportionately affecting low-income and minority patients.26 Bariatric surgery may also mitigate MASH progression and reduce transplant burden.

4.4. Racial disparities in access and outcomes

Disparities persist in transplant waitlisting and survival. Black patients are less likely to be listed despite comparable disease severity and experience higher post-transplant graft failure.27 Contributing factors include implicit bias, reduced access to transplant centers, organ quality differences, and inequities in post-transplant care. National data demonstrate that Black patients are disproportionately affected by barriers at the referral and waitlisting stages, even after adjustment for clinical factors.20 Additionally, structural inequities in insurance coverage, continuity of care, and access to specialty follow-up further compound disparities in post-transplant outcomes.27 Targeted interventions aimed at standardizing referral pathways, improving access to transplant evaluation, and strengthening post-transplant care coordination are critical to mitigating these persistent inequities.

4.5. Limitations

Despite elucidating clear temporal and racial trends in liver transplantation etiology, several critical knowledge gaps remain that warrant further investigation. Future research should focus on disentangling the upstream social, structural, and biological drivers underlying the disproportionate rise of ALD among AIAN populations, including the roles of historical trauma, alcohol availability, access to addiction treatment, and community-level prevention resources. Similarly, mechanistic and implementation-focused studies are needed to understand why Black patients continue to experience a residual burden of HCV-related transplantation despite highly effective direct-acting antivirals, with particular attention to screening uptake, linkage to care, insurance restrictions, and treatment delays. As MASH emerges as a dominant transplant indication, prospective studies examining gene–environment interactions (including PNPLA3 and other metabolic risk alleles), differential disease progression by race/ethnicity, and real-world access to emerging pharmacologic therapies are urgently needed. Additionally, future work should integrate transplant waitlist data, referral patterns, and post-transplant outcomes to better characterize disparities across the full continuum of care rather than transplantation alone. Finally, policy-focused research evaluating the impact of Medicaid expansion, insurance coverage for anti-obesity and AUD medications, and culturally tailored prevention programs will be essential to inform interventions capable of altering the projected trajectory of liver transplantation inequities through 2040.

4.6. Implications for policy, systems, and prevention

The projected dominance of alcohol-associated liver disease and MASH as leading indications for liver transplantation reflects fundamental limitations of current intervention strategies. The success of direct-acting antivirals in reducing HCV-related transplantation underscores that population-level impact is achievable when effective therapies are paired with systematic screening, broad access, and policy support.4,18 In contrast, ALD and MASH are driven by upstream social, behavioral, and metabolic determinants for which existing interventions remain fragmented, inconsistently implemented, and inequitably accessible - particularly in community hospital and safety-net settings where most patients initially present.6,10,11

Addressing these projected trends will require targeted policy changes and system-level reforms rather than reliance on individual behavior modification alone. Evidence supports expanding insurance coverage for medications for alcohol use disorder, integrating addiction treatment within primary care and hepatology practices, and reducing structural barriers that delay referral for advanced ALD.11,15 Similarly, emerging pharmacologic therapies for MASH, including GLP-1 receptor agonists, demonstrate meaningful metabolic and histologic benefit,24,25 yet restrictive insurance policies and lack of Medicare coverage limit their real-world effectiveness, particularly among low-income and minority populations.26 Systemic reforms should also prioritize standardized referral pathways from community hospitals to transplant centers, bias mitigation in transplant evaluation, and multidisciplinary models of care that integrate hepatology, addiction medicine, and metabolic disease management.

Conclusion

Our study highlights a dramatic shift in liver transplantation indications, with ALD and MASH surpassing HCV as dominant drivers of transplantation. However, persistent racial disparities in liver transplantation access, disease progression, and post-transplant outcomes remain a major public health concern. Addressing these disparities requires equitable healthcare policies, targeted prevention programs, and systemic reforms to ensure that advancements in liver disease management benefit all populations.
