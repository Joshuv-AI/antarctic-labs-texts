Effects of Intubation Duration on Trachea: A Histopathological Study
Authors: Osman Kilic, Serdal Celik, Cemal Gundogdu, Berina Slipcevic, Ayse Yasemin Gunduz, Hamdi Sirac Gundogdu, M. Tayyar Kalcioglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo analyze tracheal specimens obtained during tracheotomy in patients with prolonged intubation and investigate the relationship between intubation duration and the development of histopathological damage.MethodsForty‐six adult patients undergoing surgical tracheotomy were categorized into three groups based on intubation duration: 7–14 days (Group A), 15–28 days (Group B), and ≥ 29 days (Group C). The control group comprised 20 patients with laryngeal cancer who underwent laryngectomy and tracheotomy. Tracheal specimens obtained during routine procedures were subjected to histopathological evaluation.ResultsSignificant differences were observed between the intubated groups and the control group in terms of ulceration, inflammatory cell infiltration, granulation tissue formation, microabscess formation, dilatation, neovascularization, desmoplasia, degeneration, perichondritis, and calcification. The incidence of dilatation and perichondritis increased significantly with longer intubation duration.ConclusionProlonged endotracheal intubation is associated with various histopathological changes in the trachea. Given that histopathological injury and complication rates increase with intubation duration, performing tracheotomy within the first 14 days may be considered.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

In recent years, dramatic advances in intensive care medicine have led to an increased use of endotracheal intubation in many critically ill patients at some point during their treatment. This procedure is one of the most frequently performed interventions in intensive care units. It secures the airway and provides mechanical ventilation support. Although life‐saving, as with all medical procedures, it also predisposes patients to various complications. Some of these arise from trauma occurring during intubation. Vocal cord edema, hematoma, laceration, paralysis, and even arytenoid dislocation may develop due to the procedure itself, leading to symptoms such as hoarseness and dysphagia. However, a much greater number of complications arise from prolonged intubation [1]. Prolonged intubation is defined as endotracheal intubation lasting longer than 7 days. Well‐established complications in such cases include ventilator‐associated pneumonia, vocal cord paralysis, pressure ulcers, tracheomalacia, and laryngotracheal stenosis [1].

Congenital tracheomalacia results from abnormalities in the development of the trachea. Acquired tracheomalacia may develop in a normally formed trachea due to factors such as inflammation, trauma, surgical interventions, malignancy, compression from within or outside the trachea, or positive‐pressure ventilation [2, 3]. Therefore, acquired tracheomalacia may also be iatrogenic in origin. The most frequently reported cause of acquired tracheomalacia to date is the placement of a cuffed endotracheal tube within the trachea [4]. In cases of long‐term tracheal intubation, it is believed that mechanical friction and pressure lead to impaired blood flow, inflammation, and necrosis. This process can weaken the supportive tracheal cartilage, ultimately resulting in dynamic collapse at the cuff level [3, 4]. The introduction of newer, high‐volume, low‐pressure cuffs, which replaced older cuffs requiring high pressures (180–250 mmHg) to stabilize their position by deforming the tracheal contour, has been reported to markedly reduce the incidence of post‐intubation tracheal stenosis [5]. These cuffs require only the equivalent of airway pressure for inflation, thereby conforming to and stabilizing against the tracheal wall contour at this low pressure [6]. Nevertheless, pressure necrosis may still occur despite the use of such cuffs and tracheomalacia may eventually develop [5]. Our review of the literature revealed that studies examining tracheal changes histologically are limited, with those available predominantly focusing on tracheal stenosis.

When prolonged orotracheal intubation is anticipated in patients requiring invasive ventilatory support, tracheotomy is usually performed within 10–21 days to facilitate weaning and reduce intensive care unit length of stay and mortality rates, as it also facilitates the maintenance of oral and pulmonary hygiene [6]. The reported rate of tracheotomy in critically ill patients ranges between 10% and 20% [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, the optimal timing of tracheotomy remains controversial.

This study aims to conduct a thorough histopathological analysis of tracheal samples collected during tracheotomy procedures in intensive care patients requiring prolonged endotracheal intubation. The study also seeks to clarify the relationship between intubation duration and the development of histopathological injury. Through this analysis, we intend to provide insight into the optimal timing of tracheotomy in patients with prolonged intubation in relation to tracheal structural changes. This will provide a novel perspective on existing literature on this subject.

Materials and Methods

This prospective observational histopathological study involved 46 adult patients (aged 18 years or over) who were under endotracheal intubation in the intensive care unit at Istanbul Medeniyet University Göztepe Prof. Dr. Suleyman Yalcin City Hospital, as well as 20 control patients. The study was conducted between July 2023 and January 2025. The intubated patients requiring prolonged airway management underwent surgical tracheotomy by the Department of Otorhinolaryngology. Approval for the study was granted by the local ethics committee (approval no. 2023/0456, July 26, 2023) and the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for tracheotomy was obtained from the first‐degree relatives of all patients.

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1). Based on an effect size of 0.50, an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 55 participants for the planned four‐group comparison. Since 66 participants were included in the study, the final sample size exceeded the required sample size.

Prior to undergoing a tracheotomy, patients were categorized according to the duration of their endotracheal intubation. They were categorized as follows: Group A, 7–14 days; Group B, 15–28 days; and Group C, ≥ 29 days. These subgroup thresholds were selected based on clinically relevant time points reported in the literature on prolonged intubation and tracheotomy [11]. Previous studies have used cut‐off values ranging from 10 to 29 days to define prolonged intubation, with 14 days being one of the most frequently applied thresholds. Patients were therefore categorized into groups of 7–14 days, 15–28 days, and ≥ 29 days in order to investigate the progression of duration‐dependent histopathological changes and to evaluate whether longer intubation periods were associated with more advanced tracheal histopathological injury. Patients with bone, cartilage, or connective tissue disorders; metabolic diseases, such as calcium metabolism disorders; or a history of previous prolonged intubation, neck radiotherapy, tracheal stenosis, previous tracheotomy, or tracheal surgery were excluded from the study. In accordance with the standard intensive care unit protocol, endotracheal tube cuff pressures were routinely monitored and maintained within 20–30 cm H2O throughout the intubation period.

The control group comprised 20 patients with laryngeal cancer whose tumors did not involve the cricoid cartilage. These patients underwent laryngectomy with tracheotomy after less than 1 day of intubation. Patients with a history of previous head and neck surgery, neck radiotherapy, prolonged intubation, tracheotomy, or tracheal surgery were excluded from the control group. The following data were recorded for all included patients: age, sex, duration of tracheal intubation prior to tracheotomy, endotracheal tube diameter, indication for intensive care admission, and the presence or absence of pneumonia during their stay.

Surgical Procedure

The standard surgical steps for the procedure were followed during the tracheotomy. During the procedure, the operating surgeon directly visualized the position of the endotracheal tube cuff relative to the tracheal wall. As the cuff generally extends across multiple tracheal rings, the excised tracheal tissue was obtained from the region where the cuff makes contact with the tracheal wall. In most patients, this corresponded to the third tracheal ring. This tissue, typically removed during the procedure, corresponds to the level of the endotracheal tube cuff and was collected and fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde.

In the control group, tracheal specimens were obtained from patients with laryngeal carcinoma who were undergoing total laryngectomy. Samples were collected from the anterior tracheal wall at a level that corresponded as closely as possible to the third tracheal ring, in order to ensure anatomical comparability with the study group. Care was taken to ensure that none of the specimens showed any macroscopic or microscopic tumor invasion of the trachea. Patients with tracheal involvement were excluded from the study. All samples were collected and fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde before being sent to the Department of Medical Pathology at Medicana International Istanbul Hospital for histopathological examination.

Histopathological Examination of the Tracheal Specimen

All histopathological evaluations were performed by a pathologist who was blinded to the clinical characteristics of the patients. The biopsy specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde for 1–2 days. As part of the routine pathology laboratory protocol, the tissues were dehydrated using a series of graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. They were then blocked in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm using a microtome, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined histomorphologically under a light microscope.

For the histomorphological assessment, the parameters described by Zouk et al. were used [12]. All histopathological parameters were qualitatively assessed and recorded as either present or absent. No semi‐quantitative grading or severity scoring system was applied. This method primarily evaluates the mucosa, submucosa, and chondroid tissue. In the mucosa, the presence of epithelial erosion/ulceration, atrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, and granulation tissue was assessed. In the submucosa, vascular alterations such as acute and chronic inflammation, microabscess formation, dilatation, congestion, and neovascularization were evaluated. In the chondroid tissue, fibrosis, cartilage degeneration, perichondritis, cartilage necrosis, and calcification were also evaluated. The findings obtained were statistically analyzed across the study groups.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and disease‐related characteristics were compared between the intubated group (n = 46) and the control group (n = 20). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean values, standard deviations, minimum and maximum values, as well as numbers and percentages. The Chi‐square test (χ 2) was used to compare categorical variables between the two groups. For continuous variables, an independent samples t‐test was performed following assessment of normality. Additionally, the distribution of disease parameters within the intubated group was evaluated according to intubation duration (7–14 days, 15–28 days, and ≥ 29 days). The Chi‐square test was employed to analyze categorical variables across the three intubation duration groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the SPSS version 27.0 statistical software package. To further assess the adequacy of the sample size, a post hoc power analysis was conducted based on one of the main significant histopathological outcomes (dilatation) between the intubated groups and control group. Based on the observed proportions, the effect size was calculated as Cohen's w = 0.49. With a total sample size of 66 patients and an alpha level of 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 0.98.

Results

In the intubated group (n = 46), 43.5% (n = 20) of patients were female and 56.5% (n = 26) were male. In the control group (n = 20), 40.0% (n = 8) were female and 60.0% (n = 12) were male. There was no statistically significant difference in sex distribution between the two groups (p = 0.505).

The mean age was 62.5 ± 18.74 years (range, 20–89) in the intubated group and 63.4 ± 13.27 years (range, 41–89) in the control group. There was no statistically significant difference in age between the two groups (p = 0.847).

In the intubated group, the mean intubation duration was 22.98 ± 9.45 days (range, 5–44 days), with an average endotracheal tube size of 7.59 ± 0.41 mm (range, 6.0–8.0 mm). The distribution of endotracheal tube sizes across the intubation duration groups was also evaluated. Overall, one patient (2.2%) was intubated with a 6.0‐mm tube, six patients (13.0%) with a 7.0‐mm tube, 22 patients (47.8%) with a 7.5‐mm tube, and 17 patients (37.0%) with an 8.0‐mm tube. There was no significant difference in tube size distribution among the intubation duration groups (χ 2 = 1.63, p = 0.950), indicating similar tube size distribution across all groups. Discussion

The increasing number of patients requiring intensive care has inevitably been accompanied by a rise in associated complications, particularly those related to prolonged endotracheal intubation. Compared with prolonged endotracheal intubation, tracheotomy offers several advantages, including reduced laryngeal injury, decreased airway resistance and dead space, improved secretion clearance, reduced sedation requirements, and shorter durations of both mechanical ventilation and stay in intensive care. Despite these benefits, the optimal timing of tracheotomy remains controversial. In 1989, the American Association of Respiratory Care recommended endotracheal intubation for patients expected to require ventilation for less than 10 days and tracheotomy for those requiring more than 21 days [6]. While some authors advocate early tracheotomy, others argue that there is insufficient evidence to demonstrate its overall superiority over endotracheal intubation [6]. Following intubation, patients may develop hoarseness and dysphagia; however, a greater number of and more severe complications are associated with prolonged intubation [1]. Prolonged intubation, commonly defined as intubation lasting more than 7 days, is associated with complications such as ventilator‐associated pneumonia, vocal cord paralysis, pressure ulcers, tracheomalacia, and laryngotracheal stenosis [1].

In a meta‐analysis of 22 studies, Boni et al. reported that although early tracheotomy performed within the first 7 days was associated with a reduced incidence of ventilator‐associated pneumonia, the strength of the evidence was limited [10]. In addition, the same study did not demonstrate a clear mortality benefit of early tracheotomy [10]. Nevertheless, clinically significant laryngeal injuries have been reported even after relatively short periods of endotracheal intubation, suggesting that tissue damage may begin early in the course of airway instrumentation [13]. Therefore, the aim of our study was to characterize the histopathological changes associated with prolonged endotracheal intubation while also evaluating their relationship with intubation duration to help determine the optimal timing of tracheotomy from the perspective of tracheal histopathological injury.

Tracheomalacia develops when the structural integrity of the tracheal wall is compromised. Acquired cases are most commonly associated with endotracheal tube or tracheostomy tube cuffs. Although the exact mechanism is unclear, it is thought that chronic cuff‐related injury, repeated intubation, and impaired tissue perfusion disrupt the normal tracheal architecture. This can lead to increased airway compliance, resulting in collapse or abnormal dilatation. A retrospective study of patients with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection requiring prolonged intubation during intensive care showed that the risk of tracheomalacia increased over time [14].

Our study provides a novel histopathological perspective on the potential benefits of early tracheotomy by showing significantly lower dilatation and perichondritis rates in patients who underwent tracheotomy within the first 14 days of intubation. These results are in line with the favorable outcomes regarding early tracheotomy reported in the literature.

Consistent with our results, Badr El Din et al. found that inflammation and ulceration were the most common histopathological findings in a study of 35 patients intubated for 1 week [15]. Cartilage necrosis and fibrosis were observed more frequently in patients intubated for 7 days [15]. However, no statistically significant difference in cartilage necrosis was observed among the groups in the current study.

Our literature review identified only a limited number of studies investigating tracheal changes from a histological perspective. In a study involving intubated rabbits, Lee et al. developed a tracheal stenosis model as a result of intubation [16]. They suggested that the intubation period required for tracheal changes to occur was approximately one week. Initially, prolonged cuff pressure was associated with mucosal ulceration and diffuse inflammation, and in some cases, focal necrosis within the trachea. Subsequently, granulation tissue formation was observed in some cases [16].

As the early mechanisms underlying the development of tracheal stenosis are not fully understood, the effect of intubation duration on the extent of tracheal injury requires further investigation. Our study identified significant differences between the intubated group and the control group with respect to ulceration, the presence of neutrophils and macrophages, granulation tissue, acute and chronic inflammatory patterns, microabscess formation, dilatation, congestion, neovascularization, desmoplasia, degeneration, perichondritis, and calcification. Collectively, these findings may provide valuable insight into this area.

Due to the limited data available on structural changes in tracheal stenosis, Zagalo et al. analyzed surgical tracheal specimens and reported mucosal thickening related to fibrosis and associated with chronic inflammation [17]. Plasma cells were the predominant inflammatory cells present, while epithelial and glandular structures remained intact [17]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed a greater number of histopathological features of chronic inflammation compared with controls.

Approximately 60 years ago, autopsy data from 30 patients who had received respiratory support via cuffed endotracheal tube or tracheostomy tube revealed tracheal injuries at the cuff level. The severity of injury increased with longer intubation duration [18]. In the early stages, mucosal hemorrhage and ulceration were shown to deepen over time, extending to the level of the cartilage. Ultimately, full‐thickness injury developed in the unsupported tracheal segment. If tissue viability was preserved, subsequent fibrosis could lead to tracheal stenosis [5]. In our study, we found that ulceration and acute and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration were significantly higher in the study group than in the control group.

Jahshan et al. described a rat‐based animal model for simulating tracheal mucosal injury following prolonged intubation [19]. In this model, widespread impairment of the ciliary mechanism was observed in the study group compared to the control group. Furthermore, increased collagen deposition within the trachea was observed in rats subjected to prolonged intubation [19]. Consistent with these findings, desmoplasia was significantly more prevalent in the study group in our histopathological evaluation.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the group sizes were unequal, which may have impacted the statistical power of certain comparisons. Secondly, the single‐center design, coupled with the fact that all histopathological evaluations were performed by a single pathologist who was unaware of the study groups, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Thirdly, despite the application of strict exclusion criteria, such as the exclusion of patients with tracheal tumor invasion, previous head and neck surgery, radiotherapy, prolonged intubation, tracheotomy, or tracheal surgery, the use of laryngectomy specimens as controls may have introduced selection bias. However, because obtaining tracheal tissue from healthy individuals was not ethically feasible, laryngectomy specimens were the most appropriate available control tissue. Differences in smoking history, nutritional status, comorbidities, and other patient‐related factors between the study and control groups may have influenced tracheal histopathological findings independently of intubation duration. In addition, several clinically relevant variables, including diabetes mellitus, chronic steroid use, cuff inflation duration, duration of mechanical ventilation, reintubation history, prone positioning, frequency of tracheal suctioning, and nasogastric tube use, were not systematically documented and could not therefore be included in the analysis. As these factors may affect tracheal mucosal injury, the inflammatory response, tissue perfusion, and cartilage integrity, their potential confounding effects cannot be completely excluded. Future prospective studies that incorporate these variables and clinical outcome measures would provide a more comprehensive understanding of intubation‐related tracheal injury.

Conclusion

Prolonged endotracheal intubation is associated with various histopathological changes, including epithelial inflammation, ulceration, dilatation, and perichondritis. The frequency of these changes tend to increase with the duration of intubation. Our study showed that tracheal dilatation and perichondritis, in particular, increased significantly after 14 days of intubation.

These findings suggest that performing tracheotomy within the first 14 days of intubation may have a protective effect from a histopathological perspective. In clinical practice, we suggest that ventilatory requirements and the risk of tracheal injury should be considered when deciding the optimal timing of tracheotomy.
