Equatorward upper-ocean heat transport from the Southern Ocean boosted interglacial warming
Authors: Ce Yang, Haowen Dang, Jian Xu, Xiaolin Ma, Xingxing Wang, Yu Ren, Hongrui Zhang, Chen Li, Peng Zhang, Haijing Chen, Franck Bassinot, Yair Rosenthal, Zhimin Jian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Mid-Brunhes Transition (MBT) signifies a pivotal shift in glacial–interglacial cycles with intensified interglacial warming, yet its underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Targeting the most sensitive component of oceanic heat uptake, the southern-sourced sub-surface waters, the post-MBT ocean heat dynamics are investigated using reconstructed deep and bottom thermocline temperatures from the Western Equatorial Pacific. Our results show that, since the MBT, the deep thermocline temperature has been gradually declining in association with cooling within the (sub)tropical Pacific upper ocean circulation. In contrast, the bottom thermocline temperature shows a pronounced rising trend independent of the calibration-derived uncertainties in its absolute estimates. Such post-MBT bottom thermocline warming is consistent with an enhanced advection of southern-sourced subsurface waters, as indicated by strengthened Southern Ocean meridional thermal gradients and concurrent warming in the southern subtropics. It is thus suggested that enhanced equatorward transport of southern-sourced subsurface waters, as a cross-hemispheric heat-redistribution pathway, may have elevated the background thermal state of post-MBT interglacials, highlighting the potential role of tropical-Southern Ocean connection in shaping climate trajectories.Subject terms: Palaeoceanography, Palaeoclimate

Introduction

The ocean stores more than 90% of the excess heat from anthropogenic forcing, making it the dominant reservoir of the present energy imbalance1–3. The way of the heat being partitioned within the thermocline and intermediate layers of the ocean influences energy exchange between hemispheres and hence climate feedbacks in modern days4–6, while paleo records show that meridional heat transport between the poles and the equator has influenced climate sensitivity on orbital to millennial timescales7–9. At the center of ocean heat redistribution is the Southern Ocean10–12, where formation and subduction of the Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW) and Subantarctic Mode Water (SAMW) ventilate the ocean interior with varying heat, carbon, and nutrients13–15. These water masses connect high-latitude surface forcing to low-latitude upper-ocean ventilation. Their past variability, therefore, provides key constraints on how ocean heat redistribution has shaped long-term climate transitions.

Modern observations highlight the importance of mode and intermediate waters in global heat uptake and reveal a strong hemispheric asymmetry3,16, with most ocean heat gain occurring in the Southern Hemisphere5. Yet instrumental records are too short to resolve their role over large-amplitude climate shifts. The Mid-Brunhes Transition (MBT), occurring around ~430 thousand-years ago (ka), marks a fundamental shift toward warmer interglacials with higher atmospheric CO2, such as during the Holocene17,18. Although the MBT is not a direct analogue for modern warming, it provides a process-based reference for examining how internal reorganization of the climate system amplified interglacial warmth within the range of natural boundary conditions in the Quaternary (Fig. 1a, d, e). Given the relatively consistent orbital configurations over the past million years19, warmer interglacials after the MBT (post-MBT; ~430 ka onward) are generally attributed to stronger internal feedbacks than those before the MBT ( ~ 800–430 ka)20 (Fig. 1d, e), especially the processes of heat distribution within the ocean-atmosphere system.

Upper-ocean heat storage in the Pacific, shaped by wind-driven subtropical cells and thermocline dynamics, regulates the distribution of heat and moisture across the ocean-atmosphere system, thereby influencing global climate variability4,21,22. The Indo-Pacific Warm Pool (IPWP) is a key region where the upper-ocean heat, coupling with intense atmospheric convection, modulates the ocean-atmosphere moisture budget on orbital time-scales8,23–25. Given such importance, the upper-ocean heat dynamics across the (sub)tropical Pacific are supposed to contribute to the post-MBT climate amplification. Previous studies have paid greater attention to deep-ocean ventilation26 and carbon cycling27, whereas the role of upper- to intermediate-ocean heat transport has been less explored. However, direct evidence linking variability in SAMW and AAIW formation to equatorward heat transport on orbital timescales remains limited.

Here we examine the changes in upper- to intermediate-ocean circulation through MBT, particularly involving SAMW and AAIW, to assess the associated effect of equatorward heat transport for post-MBT interglacial warming. To test this hypothesis, we reconstructed deep- and bottom-thermocline water temperatures (DTT and BTT, respectively) from International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) Site U148928 (2°07.19’N, 141°01.67’E, water depth 3421 m), located at the center of the IPWP, spanning the past ~670 kyr (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1a). The reconstructions are derived from Mg/Ca ratios of two planktonic foraminiferal species, Globorotalia tumida and Globorotalia crassaformis, which calcify at ~200 m (i.e., deep thermocline) and ~330–600 m (i.e., bottom thermocline)29–32, respectively (see Methods and Supplementary Material 1). Since available Mg/Ca-temperature calibrations for G. crassaformis were developed from Atlantic Ocean, all reported temperature variations focus on their relative variations and long-term trends that are little affected by the choice of calibrations32 (detailed in Supplementary Materials 1–3). The BTT variations are emphasized because of their direct linkage to the penetration depth and thermohaline properties of SAMW and AAIW, while the DTT record is used alongside to illustrate the thermocline dynamics across the MBT. By linking these changes in IPWP thermocline structure to southern-sourced intermediate-water circulation, we identified a mechanism of equatorward heat redistribution with implications for interglacial climate evolution.

Results and discussion

Divergent deep and bottom thermocline changes

The DTT record of Site U1489 documents significant cycles with dominant 100-kyr and 23-kyr periodicities (above 90% confidence interval, Supplementary Fig. 2a, c), showing warmer values aligned with precession minima, especially during interglacial peaks (e.g., peaks at Marine Isotope Stages (MIS) 5, 9, 13, and 15; Figs. 1d, 2a; Supplementary Material 4). These patterns match with the shallower thermocline water temperature records from the IPWP and indicate a common modulation of precession-paced inter-hemispheric summer insolation gradient on the Pacific Subtropical Cells (STCs)8,21. Our results reaffirm a similar precession-driven STCs mechanism governing the thermal condition throughout the shallow to deep waters within the IPWP thermocline.

The mean values of our DTT show a slight cooling trend by ~0.9°C ± 0.2 °C (1σ, from 18.6 °C to 17.7 °C) starting from ~470 ka, around the boundary between MIS 13 and 12 (Fig. 1b). This DTT cooling exhibits correspondence with the sea surface temperature (SST) trends in the center of the IPWP33,34 (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b) and with thermocline water temperature reconstructions across the western and eastern equatorial Pacific, pointing to a basin-wide shoaling and cooling of the tropical Pacific thermocline across the Pleistocene35,36 (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Such changes were likely mediated by variations in the Equatorial Undercurrent and upwelling dynamics37,38.

The DTT cooling (Fig. 2b) is further corroborated by a weak cooling trend in the mid-latitude North Pacific39, and a southward migration of both the Northern Hemisphere westerlies and the North Equatorial Counter Current during the same interval40,41 (Fig. 2c, d), thus indicating a cooling and equatorward contraction of Pacific STCs (Fig. 2e). The associated cooling trend in the subtropical North Pacific would enhance thermocline ventilation along isopycnals21,42, thereby facilitating subduction of subtropical cold anomalies into the tropical upper-waters. Both modeling and observational studies indicate that temperature anomalies originating from the subtropics are transmitted equatorward to imprint on the equatorial thermocline, through pathways in the interior and western boundary of Pacific43,44. These lines of evidence allow us to attribute the long-term deep thermocline cooling in the Western Equatorial Pacific since ~470 ka to an intensified northern-sourced cold-water supply through a contracted STCs system. Independent reconstructions of G. tumida from the eastern tropical Pacific (ODP Site 1236) are consistent with our results and confirm the observed long-term DTT cooling as basin-scale thermocline changes rather than a site-specific feature or ecological artifacts45 (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Differing from DTT, the BTT is dominated by obliquity cyclicities (41-kyr; Fig. 1c, e and Supplementary Fig. 2b, d), indicating sensitivity of the IPWP bottom thermocline to higher-latitude processes, which are more dominated by obliquity-paced cross-latitude insolation-gradient forcing46. Notably, variations in all the proxy records of G. crassaformis display relatively small amplitudes before MIS13 but enlarged variations after MIS12 (Supplementary Fig. 4e–g). The obliquity signal in BTT also strengthens since 400 ka and may be linked to the post-MBT intensification of obliquity forcing47, indicating an increased influence of higher-latitude processes on BTT variability.

Since the MBT, BTT rose on average by ~1.8 ± 0.1 °C (1σ), from its prior-MBT foothold (from ~670 to 470 ka) with smaller-amplitude variations (~1 °C) (Fig. 1c). Sensitivity tests among Mg/Ca-temperature calibrations confirm the increase in G. crassaformis Mg/Ca ratios as the primary origin of such post-MBT BTT warming, and the choice of calibrations merely determines the baseline of absolute temperature estimates (Supplementary Material 3 and Supplementary Table 1). To explain the BTT warming trend since ~470 ka, one might speculate a long-term upward movement of the calcification depth of G. crassaformis. However, the δ13C of G. crassaformis closely parallels that of G. tumida and maintains a consistent offset from shallower-dwelling species (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Material 4), indicating stable biogeochemical and thermohaline gradients vertically across their habitat depths. The δ18O of G. crassaformis does show a progressive negative shift, but it agrees largely with the BTT warming (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Moreover, given the consistent inter-species δ18O gradients from the mixed layer to the deep thermocline across the MBT, the progressive lightening of G. crassaformis δ18O may not be derived from a shoaling of its habitat depth but warming and/or freshening of the water-mass (Supplementary Fig. 5c–e). Collectively, the coherent trends since MIS 13/12 among the δ13C, δ18O, and the Mg/Ca-derived BTT at Site U1489, argue against any progressive habitat-depth shift of G. crassaformis, instead indicating changes in the thermohaline properties of the bottom-thermocline water mass.

Southern origin of the IPWP bottom thermocline warming

The substantial warming of bottom-thermocline waters in the center of the IPWP since ~470 ka (Fig. 3a) may be explained by two orthogonal scenarios: (1) vertically increased mixing from the shallower warmer waters, and (2) advectively from an enhanced equatorward transport of warmer waters via the SAMW and/or AAIW. The first scenario contradicts other proxy reconstructions. If downward mixing had dominated, the δ18O gradient between surface- and thermocline-dwelling foraminiferal species would have diminished. However, δ18O records from G. ruber, P. obliquiloculata, and G. tumida at Site U148948 varied parallel to one another, with little systematic change in the δ18O gradient between mixed layer and deep thermocline since ~670 ka (Supplementary Fig. 5c, e, and Supplementary Material 4). In addition, downward mixing would imply thickened warm-water pile-up in IPWP and thereby enlarged thermal contrast zonally across the tropical Pacific. However, the zonal SST gradient has slightly weakened since MIS12 (Fig. 2f), which conflicts with this scenario. Hence, a downward vertical mixing scenario is not supported by the available proxy evidence.

The second mechanism, advection of southern-sourced waters, is supported by multiple independent lines of evidence. An increase of 2–4 °C in the SST gradients between the southern Subtropical Front (STF) and Subantarctic Front (SAF) since the MBT (Fig. 3a, c, d, Supplementary Fig. 6) is consistent with a strengthening of Southern Hemisphere westerlies (based on a conceptual estimate, see Supplementary Material 6) and supports a Southern Ocean source for the onset of the BTT warming. Stronger westerlies between the SAF and STF would deepen the mixed layer and enhance the Ekman pumping north of the SAF and thus strengthen the SAMW subduction49. Model experiments further suggest that poleward intensification of westerlies can more than double the rates of SAMW/AAIW subduction50,51. Proxy records from higher latitudes also show stronger subtropical flow of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) across the Agulhas Plateau52, an intermediate-water cooling in the southwest Pacific associated with a poleward shift of the ACC53 (Fig. 3e), and intensified deep western boundary currents off New Zealand since ~450 ka54. Taken together, these observations indicate stronger subsurface water export from the sub-Antarctic zone and a more vigorous South Pacific overturning, supporting an intensified AAlW formation in accordance with the observed BTT warming.

After correcting for the temperature and ice-volume effects, the δ18Osw-ice of G. crassaformis still shows a trend toward more negative values (Supplementary Fig. 5d and Supplementary Material 5), likely reflecting an increase in buoyancy in its source water. Concurrently, SST in the southern Subtropical Front region has significantly increased since the MBT46 (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 7). Similar to modern warming scenarios55–57, warmer and shallower SAMW were likely produced with its subduction zone shifted poleward, due to warming-induced buoyancy increases58,59, thereby facilitating advection of the SAMW/AAIW along isopycnals into the IPWP bottom thermocline under strengthened westerlies. Modern observations also suggest that the equatorward heat transport associated with Southern Ocean warming60, would be enhanced by intensified zonal flows61 and a deepened mixed layer north of the SAF62. Accordingly, together with an associated strengthening of the upper-layer circulation of the subtropical gyre63, the equatorward advection of the SAMW/AAIW has been further enhanced since the MBT.

It is noted that a “young, high-δ13C” signature in the bottom thermocline, as expected from well-ventilated Southern Ocean–sourced waters, is absent in our G. crassaformis record, largely due to the limitation of this δ13C tracer in this region64,65. This is mainly because, as SAMW and AAIW are transported northward, they experience mixing with subtropical and equatorial water masses64–66, and extensive biogeochemical modifications including remineralization of organic matters and transformations of inorganic carbon species67–70. These processes progressively attenuate the original δ13C signal. Consistent with this interpretation, such an expected “young, high-δ13C” signal can only be detected partially in some benthic δ13C records at mid-latitudes71, and may be further diminished equatorward into the tropical Pacific subsurface.

In summary, the rise in BTT temperature since the MBT at Site U1489 coincided with SST warming in the Southern Hemisphere STF region and a strengthening of the Southern Hemisphere westerlies (Fig. 3a, c, d), suggesting a linkage to the source of bottom thermocline waters. Based on available evidence, we propose that enhanced subduction of a warmer SAMW, together with intensified AAIW advection and South Pacific overturning, facilitated transport of warmer waters from the subtropical Southern Ocean into the IPWP bottom thermocline.

Equatorward upper-ocean heat transport boosted warmer interglacials

The BTT and DTT records of Site U1489 suggest accelerated upper- to intermediate-ocean circulations towards the equator after the MBT. Consonantly, during the post-MBT interglacials, the southern trade winds may have been weakened, and the southern STF migrated southward, as supported by a warming in the Southern mid-latitudes and a reduced SST gradient between the southern subtropics and the IPWP72 (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 7). Also, an equatorward shift of northern westerly winds41 and a southward displacement of the IPWP since MIS12 (Fig. 3b) has been detected, in concert with our DTT cooling. These together indicate an interhemispheric asymmetry in the Pacific upper-ocean circulation, which is supposed to have facilitated heat uptake in the southern mid-latitude Pacific and strengthened the linkage between the IPWP and the Southern Ocean. This leads us to propose enhanced heat storage in the Mode Waters during the post-MBT interglacial periods, in tandem with elevated atmospheric pCO2 and orbital insolation heating26,73.

Since the MBT, the difference between our DTT and BTT decreased, while the δ18Osw-ice difference between G. tumida and G. crassaformis at Site U1489 increased (Fig. 4a, b, see Methods). These changes may reflect reduced thermal but enhanced salinity gradients between the two, which is consistent with an intensified AAIW subduction in the Southern Ocean since MIS13/12 ( ~ 470 ka) (Fig. 5). The relatively stable global deep-water temperatures74 (Fig. 4e), contrasted with warming in the mid- to high-latitude Southern Hemisphere53,75–79 (Figs. 3c and 4c, d), further support this interpretation. The sub-surface ocean circulation and heat export are often regarded as drivers of decadal variability in modern-climate studies4–6,80, whereas the high-latitude dynamics of AAIW and SAMW in isolation have been largely examined by palaeoceanographic and observational studies14,81,82. By extending these perspectives from short-term variability to orbital timescales, our records suggest that Southern Ocean forcing operates within a dynamically coupled hemispheric heat-redistribution system, in which subsurface waters of the IPWP transmit high-latitude variability to the tropics. This equatorward subsurface transport increased tropical heat content and elevated the background thermal state, providing a key mechanism that could have contributed to the amplification of interglacial warmth after the MBT.

The proposed feedback that stronger Southern westerly winds intensify upper- to intermediate-ocean circulation and sustain northward ocean heat transport resonates with the observed post-industrial trends such as warming and expansion of subtropical western boundary currents55–57, increased Southern Ocean heat uptake10–12 and the strengthened pole-to-equator heat transport62. These insights provide specific targets for numerical model experiments, particularly sensitivity tests on the subduction rate and equatorward heat transport of SAMW/AAIW under varying climate scenarios. In any case, our findings underscore that Southern Ocean subsurface-water dynamics can effectively absorb the excess heat and yield cascading effects on the tropical Pacific oceanic heat structure and global climate state.

Methods

Samples and chronology

The studied cores from International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) Site U1489 (02°07.19ʹN, 141°01.67ʹE, 3421 m water depth) were drilled during Expedition 363 on the western slope of the southern Eauripik Rise, within the Caroline Basin north off Papua New Guinea28 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). A total of 332 sediment samples were collected from cores of the site at every ~5 cm interval from the upper 15.31 m core composite depth below seafloor (CCSF), covering the past ~670 kyr with a time resolution of ~2 kyr.

Site U1489 age model follows the published framework based on high-resolution Globigerinoides ruber δ18O48. A preliminary age model and baseline sedimentation rate were first defined using magnetic reversals from Hole D, owing to their consistency in depth scale. We aligned glacial–interglacial boundaries to the LR04 global benthic δ18O stack to define stratigraphic tie points and identify individual glacial cycles. These tie points were then used as age controls for constructing the age–depth model, with linear interpolation applied to all samples between 0 and 15.31 m. The resulting age model yields a relatively stable mean sedimentation rate of 2.1 ± 0.7 cm/kyr (1σ), generally higher during interglacials (1.0–4.0 cm/kyr; Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Subsurface water masses

In the western equatorial Pacific, shallow thermocline waters are ventilated from the subtropics through the Pacific Subtropical Cells (STCs)21,45 via the North and South Equatorial Currents83. While the Equatorial Undercurrent transports subsurface waters eastward across the basin, thereby linking hydrographic conditions between the western and eastern Pacific37. For the deeper part of the thermocline, the North Pacific Intermediate Water (NPIW) extends southward to ~8°N but is largely blocked from the equatorial band by the stronger influence of the Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW)84. The latter is advected through the Solomon Sea along the north coast of Papua New Guinea and extends to ~15°N85–87, where it dominates the bottom thermocline of the western equatorial Pacific. The Subantarctic Mode Water (SAMW) forms north of the Subantarctic Front (SAF) by wintertime convection and is subsequently advected into the thermocline of the southern subtropical gyres as a key component of the upper limb of the Pacific overturning circulations14,88,89. The SAMW originates mainly from the east and central South Pacific, where it mixes with surrounding waters during formation and northward transport, often together with the AAIW, before entering the subtropical thermocline65,90,91 (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

At Site U1489, the Equatorial Pacific Intermediate Waters are mainly influenced by AAIW sourced from the southeast Pacific, with additional contributions from the Coral and Tasman Seas and direct Southern Ocean inflow64,65. These circulation pathways make Site U1489 particularly suitable for reconstructing thermocline- and intermediate-water variations and their interactions with high- and low-latitude processes. Consistent with previous studies92,93, the GLODAP δ13C distribution shows that newly subducted AAIW and SAMW exhibit relatively high δ13C values, reflecting quick ventilations from the air-sea interface94 (Supplementary Fig. 1d). In contrast, the deep and bottom thermocline in the western Pacific warm pool have lower δ13C, indicating the attenuation of source water signals due to addition of respired carbon and mixing with older waters.

Planktonic foraminiferal proxies

The subsurface-dwelling species Globorotalia tumida, which calcifies at ~200 m in the western equatorial Pacific29 (detailed in Supplementary Material 1), is commonly used as an indicator of deep thermocline conditions36,95,96. Globorotalia crassaformis calcifies at ~330–600 m in the tropical ocean30,31, and generally above the intermediate waters across ocean basins30–32, so it has been extensively applied to reconstruct deepest thermocline/intermediate-water properties in both the Pacific and Atlantic since the Pliocene30–32,97. Accordingly, we use G. tumida as a proxy for the deep thermocline and G. crassaformis as a proxy for the bottom thermocline, defined relative to their calcification depths (Supplementary Material 1). Proxy records from these species, together with sea surface temperature anomaly (SSTA) stacks (detailed in Supplementary Material 6), therefore provide a means to examine connections between the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool (IPWP), Pacific STCs, and Southern Hemisphere mid-latitude processes mediated by the AAIW and SAMW.

Stable isotope and Mg/Ca-temperature analyses

Between 25 and 30 well-preserved specimens of G. tumida and G. crassaformis were hand-picked from the 355–425 μm and 250–425 μm size fractions for stable isotope and Mg/Ca measurement, respectively. All specimens are well preserved based on scanning electron microscopy examinations (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Material 2).

Stable isotope analyses were conducted on a Finnigan MAT253 mass spectrometer with a Kiel IV carbonate preparation device at the State Key Laboratory of Marine Geology, Tongji University, following ref. 98. Long-term measurement standard deviation is 0.07‰ for δ18O and 0.05‰ (1σ) for δ13C. Results were calibrated to the Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB) scale using the NBS19 standard. The average sampling resolution is ~2–3 kyr for both isotope and Mg/Ca records.

For Mg/Ca analyses, we used a composite cleaning protocol based on the established procedures99 with steps of silicate removal100 and reductive cleaning101. The protocol included sequential removal of clays, oxidative and reductive cleaning, weak acid leaching, and multiple rinses with ultrapure water to eliminate detrital clays, Fe–Mn oxides, and organic matter. Mg/Ca ratios were measured on an Agilent 5110 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) at the Marine Elements Analysis Laboratory, Northwest University, China. Instrumental drift and analytical precision were monitored using ECRM 752–159102, analyzed after every 11 samples. Replicate analyses (n = 30) yielded reproducibility of 5.1% for G. tumida and 3.2% for G. crassaformis.

Cleaning effectiveness was assessed by correlations between Mg/Ca and Al/Ca, Fe/Ca, and Mn/Ca ratios (Supplementary Fig. 9a–h). In G. tumida, a weak positive correlation between Mn/Ca and Mg/Ca was detected (R2 = 0.26). In contrast, Mn/Ca and Mg/Ca of G. crassaformis shows a strong correlation (R2 = 0.74, Supplementary Fig. 9i–n), primarily due to elevated Mn/Ca values after ~270 ka (Supplementary Fig. 9f), with no apparent link to BTT warming. This relationship, consistent with previous reports of Mn–Mg coupling103–105, is attributed to Mn-rich surface coatings. Following ref. 106, a Mn correction (assuming Mg/Mn = 0.2 mmol/mol) was applied.

Because Site U1489 lies at a depth of ~3421 m, both dissolution and Mn-corrections were applied following refs. 106,107. The Mg/Ca ratio in G. tumida was then converted to deep thermocline temperature (DTT) using a species-specific calibration for the 355–425 μm size fraction from western equatorial Pacific29 (Mg/Ca = 0.33exp (0.097 × DTT)). This calibration was selected after evaluating results from alternative equations against modern hydrographic data (Supplementary Figs. 1b, 10a, 11a–d; see Supplementary Material 3 for details). For G. crassaformis, specific calibration for the western equatorial Pacific has not been established. The Atlantic-based calibration, Mg/Ca = 0.658exp (0.122 × BTT)32 with corrections for Mn-contamination and dissolution106,107, gives the most reasonable BTT estimates, in terms of the comparable Late-Holocene BTT values with respect to the modern temperatures at 330–600 m depth in the western equatorial Pacific (Supplementary Figs. 1b, 10b, 11e–h). Uncertainties were calculated following ref. 108, yielding mean errors of 1.7 °C for DTT and 0.6 °C for BTT (1σ), respectively.

Calculation of seawater δ18O

Seawater δ18O (δ18Osw) was calculated using the equation from ref. 109: δ18Osw (VSMOW, ‰) = (T(°C) −16.5 + 4.8×δ18Ocalcite (VPDB, ‰))/4.8 + 0.27. The global ice volume effect, as reported by ref. 110 was then removed to obtain δ18Osw-ice, which reflects local/regional freshwater (or salinity) budget. The δ18Osw-ice errors (see ref. 108 for details) are 0.35‰ for G. tumida and 0.17‰ for G. crassaformis (1σ).

Regional SST stacks and gradients

We compiled subsurface/thermocline records from the IODP Site U1489 and SST reconstructions from Western and Eastern Equatorial Pacific, and Southern Ocean frontal zones (STF and SAF). All datasets, proxies (Mg/Ca or alkenone), coordinates, water depths, and references are listed in Supplementary Table 2. For regional stacks, individual series were linearly interpolated to 4-kyr, adjusted by subtracting the maximum value over the entire time window, smoothed with a 20-kyr running mean, and then averaged. When combining Mg/Ca and alkenone, we used anomalies to minimize method-specific offsets (detailed discussion see Supplementary Material 6). Zonal equatorial Pacific SST gradient is calculated by the difference of SST anomaly between western and eastern equatorial Pacific (ΔSSTAW−E = SSTAMD97-2140 − SSTAODP846), and meridional frontal gradient by those between STF and SAF (ΔSSTASTF−SAF = SSTA stack (STF) − SSTA stack (SAF); detailed in Supplementary Material 7).

Time series analyses

To evaluate the orbital pacing and long-term trends, all SSTA records shown in the main text were first linearly interpolated at 4-kyr intervals, and then smoothed with a 20-kyr running average after. Records from IODP Site U1489 were processed using a three-point running average and LOESS smoothing with a Gaussian fit (span = 0.5), followed by further LOESS smoothing (smoothing factor = 0.3) to obtain their long-term trends. Spectral analysis was carried out using the REDFIT procedure111 as implemented in PAST v4.03112. Rectangular windows were applied with an oversampling factor of 6, and the data were divided into two equal-length segments to estimate the red-noise spectra. Wavelet analysis of the temperature reconstructions was performed using Acycle v2.8, following standard procedures113. RAMPFIT regressions were applied to identify changing points in long-term trends114.
