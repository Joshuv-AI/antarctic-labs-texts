Exploring communication between pharmacists and infection control staff: a qualitative study in ministry of health hospitals, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdullah U. Althemery, Abdullah Alfaifi, Ahmed Alshehri, Nehad Ahmed, Saad Alaeena, Abdullah Aljahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear and effective communication between infection control staff and healthcare professionals is an essential component in supporting the development and consistent implementation of infection control protocols within hospital settings.MethodsThis qualitative study examined communication between infection control workers and pharmacists at MOH hospitals through semi-structured interviews (n = 9, 30–45 min) and two FGDs (4–5 participants each). Purposive sampling ensured representation, with thematic saturation reached by the ninth participant—consistent with similar studies (6–12 participants). Qualitative methods captured nuanced dynamics, using interviews for individual insights and FGDs for group perspectives. Data were analyzed via Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis. Ethical approval (H-01-R-009) was obtained on May 28, 2019, with written consent from all participants.ResultsThis study identified four key themes from 9 participants (6 male, 3 female) regarding antimicrobial stewardship communication challenges: (1) Patient Safety & Communication - emphasizing protocol adherence, interdisciplinary rounds, and preference for formal channels over WhatsApp; (2) Interdisciplinary Collaboration - where leadership engagement and face-to-face committees enhanced decision-making; (3) Healthcare Delivery Challenges - particularly staffing shortages, excessive workloads, and unclear medication protocols; and (4) Training & Development - revealing gaps in feedback systems, limited educational resources, and reactive crisis communication. Critical findings included the need for structured antibiotic monitoring, COO-level support for stewardship programs, resolution of role ambiguities, and implementation of proactive training initiatives to optimize infection control practices.ConclusionsStructured communication—through regular meetings, stewardship rounds, and clear antibiotic guidelines—significantly enhanced infection control outcomes by fostering collaboration between pharmacists and infection control teams. This approach improved adherence to protocols, optimized antibiotic use, and strengthened emergency preparedness, directly contributing to patient safety. However, persistent challenges like staffing shortages and training gaps must be addressed to maximize these benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13556-1.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) emerges when microorganisms develop resistance to antimicrobial agents, rendering treatments ineffective and allowing infections to persist and spread [1]. The World Health Organization identifies AMR, including multidrug resistance (MDR), as a major global health threat that is growing in both healthcare and community environments [2]. Globally, 3.7% of new tuberculosis (TB) cases and 20% of previously treated cases involve multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) [3]. In the United States, antibiotic-resistant infections account for approximately 2 million cases and 23,000 deaths annually [4], while in Europe, resistant bacterial infections contribute to over 25,000 deaths each year, with associated healthcare costs and productivity losses exceeding $1.6 billion [5]. Projections suggest that by 2050, cumulative economic losses attributable to AMR in the European Union could reach $2.9 trillion [5]. The growing threat of AMR underscores the urgent need for coordinated efforts between pharmacists and infection control teams to optimize antimicrobial stewardship, enforce prescribing guidelines, and mitigate resistance development through effective communication and surveillance.

In Saudi Arabia, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and healthcare-associated infections present a significant public health challenge. Alanazi et al. reported that 94% of isolated bacterial strains exhibited multidrug resistance (MDR), with particularly high resistance to β-lactams (≥ 94%) and intermediate resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (71.6%) and gentamicin (59.7%) [6]. Halwani and Tyforet found 70% penicillin resistance among Streptococcus pneumoniae isolates from respiratory samples [7]. Thabit et al. demonstrated alarming resistance patterns in Acinetobacter baumannii, with 100% MDR, 52% extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and 42% pandrug-resistant (PDR) isolates [8]. Similarly, Ahmed et al. documented substantial resistance in Staphylococcus aureus (98% resistant to penicillin/ampicillin) and Enterococcus faecalis (50% resistant to rifampin) at a Riyadh military hospital [9]. These escalating AMR trends underscore the critical need for enhanced collaboration between pharmacists and infection control teams to implement robust antimicrobial stewardship programs, optimize prescribing practices, and prevent transmission of resistant pathogens in healthcare settings.

Saudi Arabia is well aware of the growing problem of antibiotic resistance and is pulling out all the stops to tackle it. Back in 2017, the Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH) launched the Saudi National Action Plan for Combating Antibiotic-resistant Bacteria. This plan takes a multi-pronged approach, setting up specialized committees like the Lab Monitoring Committee for Antibiotic-resistant Bacteria, the Infection Control Committee, and the Optimization of Antibiotic Use Committee. These groups oversee all healthcare facilities, government agencies, and private sectors [10]. Saudi Arabia isn’t going it alone, either. The country is an active member of the Gulf Cooperation Council Centre for Infection Control. Together with other Gulf Health Council (GHC) countries, they’ve made infection control a top priority, recognizing it as a key part of global biosecurity. In 2018, they even released the third edition of the Infection Prevention & Control Manual to keep practices up to date [11]. In line with the criteria set by the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI), the Ministry of Health (MOH) requires hospitals to establish an infection control department or an equivalent unit with similar responsibilities [12]. To promote communication, MOH hospitals provide infection control departments within their buildings with a unified portal [13]. There is still some variation, though, as every MOH hospital has its strategies, initiatives, and goals [14].

Infection control departments encounter various challenges globally [14–17]. Insufficient infrastructure in developing nations’ private and public healthcare sectors is a significant impediment to effective infection control. Inadequate staffing and disorganized control programs adversely affect infection prevention efforts. Moreover, communication barriers between infection control personnel and other healthcare providers pose challenges [15–17]. Halwani et al. demonstrated that enhancing communication between infection control staff and clinicians improved infection control protocols in hospitals associated with the MOH in Jeddah [14]. This study aims to evaluate and optimize communication pathways between infection control staff and pharmacists in Ministry of Health (MOH) hospitals to enhance antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention. The investigation addresses two core research questions: (1) What are the key incentives and systemic barriers influencing pharmacists’ adherence to infection control protocols? (2) What effective communication strategies and implementation challenges do infection control teams experience when engaging with pharmacists?

Methods

Design

This qualitative study employed face-to-face interviews and two focus group discussions (total participants = 9) to evaluate communication between infection control staff and pharmacists. Thematic analysis was used to examine the data. The first focus group comprised five infection prevention and control specialists from MOH hospitals, while the second included four hospital pharmacists. All participants were employed by Ministry of Health (MOH) hospitals in Riyadh and Alkharj, Saudi Arabia. Individuals unable to provide informed consent were excluded from participation. The study protocol received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval prior to data collection.

Data collection and analysis

The lead researcher contacted potential participants through telephone, email, and in-person visits to explain the study. Non-respondents received follow-up reminders after three weeks. Initial recruitment packages included an invitation letter, consent form, reply slip, and participant information sheet. Interested individuals were contacted by the principal investigator to address questions and schedule sessions. Face-to-face interviews (n = 9) lasted 30–45 min each, while focus group discussions (2 groups, total n = 9) ran for 60–75 min per session. All focus groups were moderated by two trained researchers: one facilitated discussion using the structured topic guide while the other managed logistics and note-taking. Sessions were audio-recorded with participant consent. The focus group topic guide was developed using Barnlund’s Transactional Model of Communication (TMC) [18–21], which conceptualizes communication as a dynamic, reciprocal process between senders and receivers. The research team refined the guide through pilot testing, ultimately including nine core questions that explored communication patterns, challenges, and improvement strategies between pharmacists and infection control staff.

Interviews were conducted with nine individuals, and the focus group session was held in person and recorded with an encrypted recorder. All interviews were recorded and transcribed, with files stored on password-protected university cloud storage. Transcripts and recordings were only indexed by the study ID number to maintain participant anonymity. The interviews lasted roughly two hours and thirty minutes. The data were thoroughly analyzed using inductive and deductive approaches. A deductive approach based on the transactional model of communication was used to identify pre-existing subthemes about communication practices. An inductive approach was then used to facilitate the discovery of unanticipated themes [18–21].

This study implemented Braun and Clarke’s (2014) six-phase thematic analysis framework, with NVivo 14 (QSR International) facilitating systematic coding of verbatim transcripts. Methodological rigor was ensured through Lincoln and Guba’s trustworthiness criteria [22], complemented by reflexivity practices [23]: the primary analysis was conducted by Abdullah Althemery [PhD, Clinical Pharmacist] and Abdullah Aljahan [PhD, Clinical Pharmacist], with potential biases mitigated through (1) maintained reflexive journals documenting analytical decisions and preconceptions, (2) structured peer debriefing sessions with an independent qualitative methods expert, and (3) systematic negative case analysis to challenge emerging themes. This dual focus on structured analysis and researcher reflexivity enhanced the study’s confirmability while ensuring faithful representation of participant perspectives.

Results

A total of 15 participants were invited to participate in the study, with 9 participants attending the focus group (6 males and 3 females). This study brought up four major themes that describe some communication challenges within healthcare settings, particularly concerning antimicrobial resistance. The first theme was patient safety and communication, which discussed the importance of effective treatment, side effect monitoring, and patient education. The second theme was the interdisciplinary collaboration. Challenges in healthcare delivery have revealed clear hurdles, indicating that limited resources may prevent extremely effective communication from being realized, resulting in a bigger loss. Finally, the training and development theme underlines the need for continued education in communication skills development techniques.

Theme 2: Interdisciplinary collaboration

The theme of interdisciplinary collaboration highlights the essential significance of cooperative relationships among healthcare professionals, emphasizing the importance of teamwork with the infectious disease team and the structured dialogues conducted through committee meetings in enhancing patient care outcomes.

Effective interdisciplinary collaboration in antimicrobial stewardship was achieved through three key mechanisms: (1) formal leadership engagement, where COO-level authorization empowered clinical pharmacists to modify antibiotic regimens (Participant 6); (2) structured committee dialogues, with participants emphasizing face-to-face meetings as superior to electronic communication for complex decision-making (Participant 5); and (3) documented accountability systems, including mandatory pharmacy representation and distributed meeting minutes (Participants 1, 4). However, collaboration remained limited by inconsistent participation in committees, over-reliance on hierarchical approval processes, and the absence of standardized cross-departmental protocols. Notably, while leadership endorsement enhanced stewardship authority (Participant 6), the dependence on high-level approvals revealed systemic vulnerabilities when such support was unavailable. Discussion

The objective of this research was to analyze the collaboration between the infection control team and the pharmacy department across the MOH hospitals with a critical focus on how such collaboration can be enhanced further. The themes developed contained the following aspects: patient safety and communication, interdisciplinary collaboration, barriers in healthcare delivery, and communication skills enhancement through continuous training and development.

The patient safety and communication theme underscores the critical relationship between clinical interventions, side effect monitoring, and effective communication to optimize safety outcomes. Participants emphasized that successful collaborative care requires strict adherence to established protocols and interdisciplinary coordination, particularly for antibiotic stewardship. The study highlighted how communication facilitates timely management of drug toxicities and necessary treatment modifications. Supporting these findings, Ebben et al. (2018) demonstrated that guideline adherence enhances care quality and reduces practice variability [24], while Baur et al. (2017) confirmed that optimized antibiotic prescribing improves treatment efficacy and infection prevention [25]. These align with Barlam et al.‘s (2016) evidence that antimicrobial stewardship programs yield better patient outcomes, reduced adverse events, and more efficient resource utilization [26].

The research characterized antibiotic stewardship rounds as valuable to collaboration. Although formal tools such as emails and meetings were the communication mediums of choice, informal tools such as WhatsApp were also utilized. Monthly meetings also played a pivotal role in bridging the communication gap between the pharmacy and infection control teams. Recent evidence highlights that structured multidisciplinary approaches, such as Lean Six Sigma-driven antimicrobial stewardship programs, can effectively optimize antimicrobial use and enhance collaboration across healthcare teams [27]. In the same vein, Seidelman et al. (2022) found that the extensive engagement required in the implementation of weekly antibiotic stewardship rounds significantly reduced the amount of antibiotics used, supporting the idea that indirect effects from the rounds led to declines in ICU antibiotic use [28].

The theme of interdisciplinary collaboration of the specialists involved calls for engagement of healthcare professionals, especially the members of the infectious disease team in the patient care process. The participants pointed out the involvement of leadership in the area of antimicrobial stewardship. The prevailing practice in infection control is that the office emails are not a preferred means of communication between team members since verbal discussions are more beneficial. Sending and posting minutes of the meetings for the department managers comply with the responsibility in charge of infection control. According to McLaney et al. (2022), interprofessional collaboration in particular promotes more cooperative, team-centered approaches that result in better outcomes and higher-quality care [29].

The quality of antimicrobial stewardship interventions can be enhanced through a collaborative practice approach. As defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), collaboration means that health professionals from different sectors work in partnership alongside patients, their families, and their communities to deliver optimum care [30]. Ashley and Spires stated that medicine is best practiced in a teamwork environment, with responsibility in all aspects of patient care. Antimicrobial stewardship, which combines the knowledge of all team members in antibiotic use with the co-leadership of pharmacists and doctors, is a strong example of collaboration in this area [31].

The barriers to effective infection control and antibiotic stewardship are highlighted in this study, including insufficiencies in resources, inadequate workforce, and poor communication. Firstly, poor communication between healthcare workers contributes to ineffective infection control and antibiotic use. Wang et al. (2023) added other factors influencing the effectiveness of clinical guidelines such as weak networks and time as well as motivation deficiencies [32]. Balea and Glasdam (2024) note the role of systemic factors such as insufficient regulation and diagnostics in promoting antimicrobial resistance, endorsing limited clinician autonomy as a solution. This research argues that local guidelines are ineffective in addressing the misuse and resistance issues as they do not provide robust monitoring. The staff also see high workloads and medication safety as issues, negatively affecting compliance with protocols set forth [33]. Wang et al. (2023) identified insufficient resources and supplies as the primary reasons for poor infection control in hospitals. The high staff requirements and protocol non-compliance contributed to the ineffective infection control and management of infections [32].

The training and development theme stresses the deficits in the feedback processes, the lack of training services, and the need for health providers to have better preparedness for emergencies. Participants underscored the need for organized communication where pharmacists are part of the infection control teams, report generation, and survey administration to improve involvement and infection management. They pointed out gaps in education processes concerning infection control communication extending to the broader staff excluding the pharmacy and the physicians who will have comprehensive oversight. Regular consultations and feedback were also regarded as a means of improving coordination efforts especially when services are restored after disruption. Other participants commented on emergency communication and called for the development of more proactive communication strategies in readiness for emergencies. Bonacaro et al. (2024) demonstrated that there is a great relationship between the lack of inconsistent education and poor adherence to the protocols [34]. According to WHO (2015), one of the targets of the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance is to increase awareness through effective communication, education, and training [35]. Samuel et al. (2021) demonstrated that continuous professional development (CPD) programs have a significant impact on healthcare workers’ practices and patient outcomes [36].

While this focus group study provides valuable insights, several methodological constraints should be acknowledged. First, the sample size (n = 9) and recruitment from only MOH hospitals in Riyadh and Alkharj may limit generalizability to other regions or private-sector settings. Second, conducting only one focus group per staff type (pharmacists/infection control) might not fully capture inter-group dynamics or the spectrum of professional perspectives. Third, unrecorded participant characteristics (e.g., years of experience, specific job roles) could influence interpretation of findings. Fourth, the format inherently prioritizes group dialogue over in-depth individual responses, with potential for dominant voices to overshadow quieter participants. Additionally, reliance on self-reported perceptions introduces possible recall or social desirability bias. While these limitations constrain broader applicability, the findings remain valuable for understanding communication challenges in similar public healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

This study identifies effective communication and teamwork as critical for optimizing patient care and infection prevention, particularly through antibiotic stewardship. Structured communication mechanisms (e.g., stewardship rounds, monthly meetings) were essential for collaboration, though informal platforms like WhatsApp facilitated rapid responses. Key challenges included staffing shortages, inadequate prioritization of infection control, and training gaps. The findings underscore the need for clear antibiotic policies, pharmacist-physician collaboration, improved feedback systems, and emergency preparedness. These results highlight two actionable priorities: (1) implementing standardized interdisciplinary communication protocols across MOH hospitals, and (2) developing competency-based training programs to address identified knowledge-practice gaps. Future research should explore the impact of such interventions on antimicrobial resistance rates and patient outcomes. Ultimately, the study demonstrates that structured communication systems are foundational for enhancing infection control and patient safety.
