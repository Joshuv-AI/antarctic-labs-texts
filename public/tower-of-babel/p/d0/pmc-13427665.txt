A rapid rhPCR–lateral flow assay for authenticating royal red and Argentine red shrimp in the U.S. seafood supply chain
Authors: Samuel Kwawukume, Nethraja Kandula, Leqi Cui, Prashant Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the United States, approximately one-third of shrimp sold are misrepresented. DNA barcoding requires robust infrastructure and one to three days to generate results, limiting enforcement utility. We hypothesized that RNase H2-dependent PCR (rhPCR) primers targeting cytochrome oxidase subunit I (COI) sequence-specific sites, coupled with lateral flow detection, could enable rapid species authentication of royal red shrimp (Pleoticus robustus) and Argentine red shrimp (Pleoticus muelleri). Species-specific rhPCR primers labeled with FAM and biotin were designed and evaluated using crude DNA extracts from 65 barcoded shrimp specimens representing seven commercially traded species. The assays were further validated using 20 blinded retail samples marketed as royal red shrimp. Both assays demonstrated high specificity comparable to DNA barcoding results, and an analytical sensitivity of 0.01 ng/μL. Analysis of retail samples revealed 60% of samples were not royal red shrimp. Total time-to-results for 20 samples was approximately 150 min, demonstrating a rapid, simpler, and sequencing-free alternative for regulatory enforcement.

Introduction

Species substitution is one of the most economically consequential forms of seafood fraud in the global seafood supply chain (FAO, 2026). In the United States (U.S.), shrimp is the most consumed seafood, with per capita consumption up to 5.9 pounds (Ansai et al., 2025; NFI, 2023). Domestic shrimp production in the U.S. remains limited and insufficient to meet consumer demand. In 2024, domestic shrimp landings were about 104,000 metric tons, while the U.S. imported 762,000 metric tons to meet market demands (NOAA, 2025). Within this import-dominated market, a smaller but economically important segment comes from domestic wild-caught shrimp that supply distinctive and high-value species, most notably the deepwater royal red shrimp (Pleoticus robustus).

In 2024, the total reported landings for royal red shrimp in the U.S. was 81 metric tons (NMFS, 2025). It is a deep-water shrimp species with a unique taste and flavor that requires specialized equipment for harvesting them, thus making it one of the most expensive and rare shrimp species on the market (Balcom, 1996; Cascorbi, 2004; Zack, 2007). This makes it a target for fraudulent substitution with less expensive alternatives, particularly Argentine red shrimp (Pleoticus muelleri), which is visually similar in coloration and less distinguishable after processing (Warner et al., 2014).

The scale of shrimp food fraud in the U.S. is well documented as peer-reviewed publications reported a species substitution rate of 30% across all traded species (Warner et al., 2013). Korzik et al. (2020) observed a shrimp species substitution rate of 34% in samples collected from North Carolina while a substitution rate of 21% prevailed in Southern California (Rivers et al., 2024). Ahles et al., 2025 estimated an overall seafood fraud rate of 39.1% from a meta-data study in the U.S. Furthermore, recent unpublished data from testing conducted by SeaD Consulting in collaboration with the Southern Shrimp Alliance has shown substantial increases in retail substitution rates for shrimp species. Their testing showed 90% and 96% substitution rates in Charleston (South Carolina) and Tampa (Florida), respectively (Southern Shrimp Alliance, 2025). Furthermore, Warner et al. (2014) found that all Gulf samples labeled as royal red shrimp were substituted with P. muelleri or Hymenopenaeus debilis.

Accurate identification and distinguishing of royal red shrimp from substituting shrimp species based on morphological features is challenging due to close similarities with other deepwater shrimp (Korzik et al., 2020; Ortea et al., 2012). Reliable authentication of shrimp relies on molecular methods, with DNA barcoding of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene serving as the current regulatory gold standard due to high interspecific sequence divergence coupled with low intraspecific variability, which enables robust taxonomic assignment (Handy et al., 2011; Hebert et al., 2003). However, despite its analytical robustness, DNA barcoding is operationally constrained by the need for laboratory-based sequencing infrastructure, specialized expertise, multi-day turnaround times, and reduced performance with degraded DNA from processed products. These limitations restrict its use for rapid, point-of-enforcement applications, underscoring the need for faster molecular authentication approaches suitable for enforcement laboratory deployment (Liberty et al., 2025; Pascoal et al., 2008).

Alternative DNA-based methods include conventional PCR with gel electrophoresis, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), and probe-based assays that offer faster results but still require extensive laboratory infrastructure and post PCR processing steps (Silva, 2021). PCR coupled with lateral flow assay (PCR-LFA) detection replaces gel electrophoresis with a simple, instrument-free dipstick readout. Fluorophore-labeled amplicons are captured by antibody-conjugated particles on a nitrocellulose membrane, producing a visible test line within minutes of amplification. PCR-LFA assays have been developed for the authentication of multiple seafood species, including Atlantic cod, Atlantic white shrimp, Pacific white shrimp, red snapper, octopus, and swimming crabs (Taboada et al., 2017; Kwawukume et al., 2023, 2024; Kandula et al., 2025; Moorthy et al., 2025).

A key analytical challenge in molecular species authentication is achieving sufficient specificity when target and substitute species are closely related. RNase H2-dependent PCR (rhPCR) addresses this by using primers containing an internal RNA base and a 3′ blocking modification, which gets cleaved by RNase H2 enzyme only upon perfect primer-template hybridization (Dobosy et al., 2011). When combined with lateral flow detection, rhPCR provides a rapid and species-specific assay and has successfully been applied to seafood species authentication (Kandula et al., 2025; Kwawukume et al., 2023).

COI gene sequences of P. robustus and P. muelleri contain species-specific sequences that distinguish them from each other, and from commonly co-traded shrimp species. We hypothesized that rhPCR primers targeting these positions would provide sufficient mismatch discrimination to eliminate cross-amplification from non-target species, and the resulting amplicons from crude DNA extract amplification could be reliably detected by the lateral flow assay. The aim of this study was to design, optimize, and validate species-specific rhPCR–lateral flow assays for the identification of P. robustus and P. muelleri, and evaluate their application on retail market samples collected under regulatory surveillance.

Materials and methods

Sample collection: Sixty-five packaged shrimp samples were obtained using a targeted sampling approach for analytical validation, with each specimen representing an independent biological replicate. Shrimp samples were from seven commercially important species collected from wholesale distributors operating in Alabama, Florida, Georgia, and Texas. The species included Argentine red shrimp (Pleoticus muelleri, n = 13), Atlantic white shrimp (Litopenaeus setiferus, n = 8), brown shrimp (Farfantepenaeus aztecus, n = 4), Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei, n = 9), pink shrimp (Farfantepenaeus duorarum, n = 4), royal red shrimp (Pleoticus robustus, n = 20), and tiger shrimp (Penaeus monodon, n = 7). An additional 20 unmarked samples were collected by the state Food Safety Division (Alabama Department of Agriculture and Industries), which included raw and processed shrimp. Samples were obtained from shrimp boats, grocery stores, and seafood markets. Chain of custody was maintained from collection through delivery. Five grams of tissue from each sample was transferred into 2 mL cryotubes containing 1 mL of DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) and stored at −20 °C for preservation.

Genomic DNA extraction, quality control, and DNA barcoding: High-purity genomic DNA for primer screening and assay optimization was extracted from the 65 thawed shrimp tissue samples collected previously using DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) was assessed with a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Extracted genomic DNA was diluted to 10 ng/μL working stock using nuclease-free water (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) for the DNA barcoding protocol described by Handy et al., 2011. The universal fish barcoding primers FISHCOILBC_ts: CACGACGTTGTAAAACGACTCAACYAATCAYAAAGATATYGGCAC and FISHCOIHBC_ts: GGATAACAATTTCACACAGGACTTCYGGGTGRCCRAARAATCA (Handy et al., 2011) were used for this protocol, generating an approximately 650 bp amplicon. PCR amplicons were cleaned up with the Omegabio kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcoss, GA, USA) to eliminate primer dimers and excess reaction components, ensuring high-quality, single-template sequence reads. Cleaned samples were transported to the molecular cloning facility at the Department of Biological Science, Florida State University (Tallahassee, FL, USA), where Sanger sequencing was performed using an Applied Biosystems 3730 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Resulting sequences were used for species identification using the BOLD Systems database and NCBI GenBank BLAST searches, with species identities assigned at a minimum pairwise identity threshold of ≥98%. The 20 blinded market samples were not barcoded prior to assay testing.

Crude DNA extraction: To simulate low resource conditions, approximately ≤10 mg of tissue was collected using sterile toothpicks from each of the sixty-five commercially obtained and barcoded samples, as well as 20 unidentified market samples into 0.2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Each sample was treated with 100 μL of Extracta™ DNA Prep for PCR (Quanta Biosciences, Beverly, MA, USA) and incubated at 95 °C in a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Singapore) for 30 min to lyse tissue samples. Lysed samples were cooled at room temperature and centrifuged at 2000 ×g using a Corning® 6770 microcentrifuge (Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA) for five minutes to pellet the coagulated protein. 10 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a clean 2 mL tube and diluted to 1:10 in nuclease-free water. Crude DNA concentration and purity was quantified using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

COI sequence retrieval, Alignment and Primer Design: COI gene sequences for P. robustus (NSILRRS-12), P. muelleri (BOLD-3MX8KZS11), and other commonly marketed shrimp species; F. duorarum (NSILPS-20), F. aztecus (NSILBS-16), L. setiferus (ULLZ-11629), Litopenaeus vannamei (FDA_252–2), P. monodon (ULLZ-8719) and H. debilis (MT387297.1) were retrieved from the FDA Reference Standard Sequence Library for Seafood Identification, BOLD database, and NCBI GenBank databases. Multiple sequence alignment was performed and visualized using Jalview® software (version 2.11.5.1) (Troshin et al., 2011) and species-specific regions were identified for P. muelleri and P. robustus. Variations were highlighted using the percentage identity coloring scheme and manual inspection of the consensus string to isolate these sites. These unique regions were targeted for candidate primer pairs targeting these conserved positions using Primer3 software (Untergasser et al., 2012). Specificity was verified in-silico using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool against the nucleotide database as well as alignment in Jalview®. Primers were selected for low predicted cross-reactivity with any shrimp or crustacean sequence in the database and synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), 2025 (Coralville, IA, USA). Primer optimization using qPCR: A gradient PCR was performed to determine the optimal annealing temperatures for the designed royal red-specific and Argentine red-specific conventional primers using a LightCycler® 96 instrument (Roche Diagnostics Corp., Indianapolis, IN, USA). Each 20 μL PCR reaction contained 10 μL of 2× Apex qPCR GREEN Master Mix (Genesee Scientific, El Cajon, CA, USA), 0.20 μM of forward and reverse PCR primers, 20 ng/μL of genomic DNA, and 7.4 μL of nuclease-free water. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 900 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at a temperature range of 55–65 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. A melt curve analysis was performed after amplification with the following steps: 95 °C for 10 s, 65 °C for 60 s, and 97 °C for 1 s. Real-time PCR data was analyzed using LightCycler® 96 SW 1.1 software (Roche Diagnostics Corp., Indianapolis, IN, USA). The annealing temperatures yielding consistent amplification and a specific single melting peak were selected for the development of the assays.

rhPCR primer design and optimization using conventional PCR: Conventional PCR primers showing amplification potential and specificity were converted to rhPCR primers following IDT's recommendations for the rhPrimer GEN1 design by incorporating a single RNA base into the primer sequence (Table 1). The rhPCR 5′ end was labeled with 6-FAM fluorophore and reverse primers were labeled at the 5′ end with biotin. Both forward and reverse primers carried a 3′ C3 blocker. All oligos for this study were synthesized by IDT (Coralville, IA, USA). The rhPCR reaction was performed using crude DNA extracts diluted 1:10 with nuclease-free water and used directly as the template. Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 μL Apex qPCR GREEN Master Mix, 0.2 μM each of forward and reverse rhPCR primers, 4 mU of RNase H2 enzyme (P. muelleri assay) and 8 mU of RNase H2 enzyme (P. robustus assay) prepared using manufacturer-supplied dilution buffer (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA), 20 ng/μL of diluted crude DNA template, and 7.4 μL (P. muelleri assay) and 7.2 μL (P. robustus assay) of nuclease-free water. The optimized thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 900 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C (P. robustus assay) or 63 °C (P. muelleri assay) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 300 s. Amplification was performed on a T100™ thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Singapore). Amplicons were confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis (2% agarose, ethidium bromide staining) and lateral flow strip detection.

Lateral Flow Assay Detection: PCR amplicons were detected using single-target lateral flow strips designed for FAM/biotin-labeled amplicons (Attogene, Austin, TX, USA). Briefly, 100 μL of lateral flow assay buffer from the manufacturer was added to a microcentrifuge tube. A 10 μL aliquot of PCR product was added, briefly vortexed for 5 s. A lateral flow strip was inserted into the mixture and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Strip results were interpreted visually by two bands, i.e., control line (top line from vertical position) and the test line indicating a positive result and a single control line indicating a negative result. The absence of the control line was treated as an invalid result. Lateral-flow strip images were recorded using a digital camera (iPhone 15) under standardized lighting conditions. To assess inter-operator reproducibility, a blinded subset of 20 strips (10 positive, 10 negative) was independently interpreted by three laboratory personnel without prior knowledge of sample identity. Agreement among readers was assessed, and all three readers reached identical interpretations for all 20 strips, confirming consistent visual readout under the conditions assessed.

rhPCR–lateral flow assay validation: Assay performance was evaluated using crude DNA from all 65 barcoded reference samples and the 20 blinded market samples using the protocol developed in the previous steps. Each sample was tested in triplicates per assay, and blinded market samples were tested in duplicates per assay. For the 65 barcoded samples, a binary confusion matrix was calculated using DNA barcoding species identification as the reference standard. A positive result on each assay for a confirmed target sample was classified as a true positive (TP); a negative result for any non-target sample was classified as a true negative (TN). False positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) were defined accordingly. Results from the 20 blinded market samples were reported descriptively (species calls per assay).

Limit of detection: The limit of detection (LOD) was determined using serial tenfold dilutions of crude DNA extracts starting from a 100 ng/μL standardized lysate. Dilutions for both assays were prepared from 100 ng/μL to six dilution points: 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 ng/μL. Each dilution point was assessed in triplicate across three independent experiments. LOD was defined as the lowest concentration at which a visible test line was observed.

Assay performance and statistical analysis: The sensitivity, specificity, false-positive rate, false-negative rate, positive-predictive value, negative-predictive value, and test accuracy of the assays were calculated as previously described by Bosilevac et al., 2019. DNA concentration and purity metric (A260/A280) obtained from the two extraction kits were compared using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples, as both methods were applied to DNA extracted from the same tissue specimens (n = 65). Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Alignment of COI sequences from P. muelleri, P. robustus, five non-target shrimp species included in this study, and H. debilis identified species-specific nucleotide positions suitable for the rhPCR primer design (Fig. S1). These positions enabled the selective targeting of each species while minimizing the likelihood of non-specific amplification. The final primer pairs (Table 1), designed to amplify the COI gene of P. muelleri and P. robustus were further evaluated in silico using sequence alignment and database comparison tools. The analysis confirmed high specificity, with no predicted cross-amplification against any of the non-target shrimp species included in this study. Collectively, these results support the robustness of the primer design strategy and demonstrate its suitability for accurate, species-specific discrimination in downstream rhPCR-based assays.

Crude DNA yield was significantly higher with the Extracta™ kit than with the Blood & Tissue® kit (195.83 vs 39.97 ng/μL; p < 0.0001, Table S1) in Fig. 1a. However, A260/A280 ratios (1.83 vs 1.87; p = 0.4742, Table S1) indicated similar purity (Fig. 1b), supporting the use of crude lysates as a reliable, rapid alternative for the development of our assays. These findings are consistent with previous studies (Jafar et al., 2024; Kusnadi et al., 2023) that demonstrated the suitability of crude DNA lysate to yield acceptable A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 for molecular applications, comparable to genomic DNA extracted using standard kits or extensive protocols. Gradient real-time PCR experiments for the designed oligos showed amplification of the target species while all non-target species produced no detectable fluorescence signals. The optimal annealing temperature of 58 °C was specific for amplifying royal red genomic and crude DNA extracts, with mean Cq values of 20 and 28 respectively (Fig. 2a). Whereas an annealing temperature of 63 °C generated specific amplification for the Argentine red assay with genomic and crude DNA extracts having mean Cq values of 18 and 22 respectively (Fig. 2b). A shift in Cq values was observed, with an increase in Cq from genomic to crude DNA extracts. The observed Cq shift is attributable to inhibitory substances present in the crude lysate. Crude extraction methods often co-isolates proteins and complex polysaccharides, which are known to interfere with Taq DNA polymerase activity and reduce PCR amplification efficiency. In contrast, purified genomic DNA undergoes removal of these inhibitors, resulting in improved enzyme performance and more efficient amplification, as reflected by lower Cq values (Kermekchiev et al., 2009; Schrader et al., 2012). Agarose gel electrophoresis of the developed royal red shrimp assay produced amplicons of about 350 bp for P. robustus from both crude and genomic DNA, with no amplification in non-target species. Band intensity was stronger for genomic DNA, due to inhibitors in crude extracts and the larger amplicon size reducing amplification efficiency (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the Argentine red shrimp assay generated a smaller 168 bp amplicon for P. muelleri from both DNA types, with no visible bands in non-target species, indicating high specificity. These results support previous findings that crude DNA extracts are suitable for COI-based PCR amplification in seafood authentication, even in processed samples (Fig. 2d). Similar findings in published literature have previously reported the use of crude DNA extracts for amplifying COI gene targets in PCR reactions to identify seafood specimens, including processed and variously cooked samples, demonstrating the practical applicability of crude DNA extracts for seafood species identification (Tagliavia et al., 2016; Kwawukume et al., 2023, 2024; Kandula et al., 2025; Pappalardo et al., 2025).

Following conversion to FAM- and biotin-labeled rhPCR primers, amplification was observed only in the presence of RNase H2. Enzyme concentration of 4 mU and 8 mU was sufficient to enable primer cleavage and target-specific amplification for P. muelleri and P. robustus, respectively, with PCR products that were detectable on lateral flow sticks. This concentration was in the range recommended by the manufacturer and previous studies (IDT, 2025; Kandula et al., 2025). All crude DNA samples from P. robustus (Fig. 3a, n = 12 & Fig. S3a, n = 8), and P. muelleri (Fig. 4a) tested positive, producing both test and control bands, confirming successful amplification. While rhPCR primers ensure species-specific amplification of DNA sequences, labeled primers enable reliable detection of amplicons on lateral-flow test strips, particularly in resource limited settings. This combination of rhPCR and lateral-flow strips offers the food industry a reliable, faster, and cheaper DNA-based species authentication tool that has shown potential for use in seafood fraud mitigation. The standardized assays achieved a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.01 ng/μL using crude DNA extracts (Fig. 5a, b), which is comparable to reported LOD for PCR–lateral flow assays in seafood authentication. A LOD of 0.1 ng/μL has been reported for Pacific white shrimp assay (Kandula et al., 2025). Similarly, a LOD of 0.5 ng/μL was reported for Alaska pollock and Pacific cod assay, and 0.05 ng/μL for Atlantic cod and ling assays (Taboada et al., 2017), 0.01 ng/μL for Pacific white shrimp (Saetang et al., 2024). The LOD observed in our study was higher than the LOD expected for real-time PCR-based diagnostic assays. This could be due to the use of crude DNA lysate. However, this limitation did not affect the assay's diagnostic performance. The method was developed specifically for qualitative identification of our target shrimp species in unknown single-species samples, where sample DNA is always available in abundance. As a result, minor variations in sensitivity do not significantly affect interpretation or reliability. This was further supported by the rhPCR–lateral flow assays exhibiting maximum performance with no observed misclassification errors in the performance matrix (Table S2&3), demonstrating complete qualitative agreement with reference identification and strong suitability for enforcement-oriented species authentication.Fig. 5(a). The complete workflow comprises three steps: (1) crude DNA extraction by thermal lysis; (2) rhPCR amplification which takes approximately 100 min; and (3) lateral flow strip incubation and visual readout in 5 min. Time to result for simultaneously testing of 20 samples was approximately 150 min. The standardized testing workflow used a standard thermocycler and no post-amplification instrumentation. The assay buffer, lateral flow strips, and DNA extraction reagents are stable at room temperature, reducing cold chain requirements relative to probe-based qPCR assays.

The primary intended users for the assays standardized in this study are enforcement agency laboratories, state departments of agriculture, FDA field labs, and seafood industry quality control operations with access to a standard thermocycler. Relative to DNA barcoding, the rhPCR–lateral flow assay trades sequence-level resolution for rapid turnaround, minimal infrastructure requirements, and high-throughput suitability. Future directions of multiplexing both assays into a single test would expand the testing capabilities and minimize any operational cost and complexities associated with the developed method.

Conclusion

Shrimp species substitution remains a persistent form of food fraud that undermines economic value, compromises food safety, and erodes consumer confidence in the United States. Developing efficient, rapid laboratory authentication methods is essential to safeguard the integrity of the seafood trade. In this study, we developed a simplified and time-efficient PCR–lateral flow assay for detecting and differentiating royal red and Argentine red shrimp. The assays demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity, capable of identifying shrimp from crude DNA extracts. Testing of 20 samples labeled as royal red shrimp, obtained from seafood markets and restaurants, revealed a high prevalence of species substitution. They are consistent with prior reports of elevated shrimp and royal red shrimp mislabeling in U.S. retail markets, though broader geographic and multi-season sampling would be required to estimate national prevalence or characterize trends over time. This work provides a practical workflow for large-scale or rapid laboratory detection to authenticate shrimp species and support regulatory decision-making.
