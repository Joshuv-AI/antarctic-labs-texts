Foreclosure, memory decline, and dementia probability: A longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Min Hee Kim, Gabriel L Schwartz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONHousing insecurity is rising among older adults; health researchers know little about how this may impact their cognitive health. We investigated links between foreclosure and older adults’ memory and probability of dementia.METHODSUsing the Health and Retirement Study (2008 to 2018), we fit mixed models comparing the memory and dementia probability scores of 249 older adults who experienced foreclosure (treated) with 15,645 who did not. Baseline covariates included sociodemographics and health. Models were stratified by age group.RESULTSForeclosure was associated with faster memory decline among middle‐aged (50 to 64) older adults (−0.007 standard deviations/year, 95% confidence interval: −0.13, −0.001). Compared to average memory decline among middle‐aged older adults who were stably housed, foreclosure equated to 3.7 additional years of aging over 10 years. Among those 65+, differences between those who were and were not foreclosed upon were short‐lived and less clear, potentially driven by depletion‐of‐susceptibles bias.DISCUSSIONForeclosure may endanger older adults’ memory.Highlights Housing instability is a key determinant of cognitive aging.We examined foreclosure and levels and changes in memory and dementia probability scores in the US older adult population.Foreclosure was associated with faster memory decline among middle‐aged (50 to 64) older adults, equivalent to 3.7 additional years of cognitive aging over 10 years.Foreclosure yielded sharp memory declines and increases in dementia probability among older adults 65 and above.Foreclosure imposes a greater risk for older adults’ cognitive decline. Keywords: cognitive function, cohort, dementia, foreclosure, housing insecurity, longitudinal, social epidemiology

BACKGROUND

Housing is a key social determinant of health: among the most foundational, it enables social, psychological, and financial stability, healthy behaviors, and access to healthcare. 1 Housing may be especially important for older adults, among whom “aging in place” – the ability to remain in one's home and community in later life – is thought to support healthy aging. 2 Unfortunately, older US adults face unique challenges for housing stability, including limited incomes, 3 a high chronic disease burden, 4 social isolation, 5 and financial scam victimization. 6 These challenges increasingly push US seniors into housing precarity.

Prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic, for example, the percentage of adults over 65 with outstanding mortgage debt was roughly double what it was 30 years ago, as were their average mortgage balances. 7 Foreclosure rates were similarly elevated, about 50% higher than they were as recently as 2007. Since the onset of the pandemic, 4.4% of adults 65 and older reported being behind on their mortgage; another 1.5% had no confidence that they could make next month's mortgage payment. 8 In addition, among older renters, one in every 50 people over 65 faces an eviction filing each year, amounting to roughly 168,000 people. 9 Older adults are accordingly one of the fastest‐growing homeless subgroups. 10 In California today – home to a third of the US homeless population – nearly half of single adults living outside are over 50; of these, more than 40% experienced homelessness for the first time as older adults. 11

Still, epidemiologists know little about how housing instability impacts older adults’ well‐being, especially their cognitive health. Only two studies have examined foreclosure and cognition among older adults, both finding foreclosure was associated with worse cognitive performance. 12 , 13 Past studies, however, either were cross‐sectional 13 or used area‐level foreclosure rates as their exposure, 12 complicating their utility for individual‐level causal inference. In particular, reverse causality looms large: poor financial decision‐making is a known indicator of prodromal cognitive impairment, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and older adults with cognitive impairment are at greater risk of financial fraud and exploitation, 18 suggesting that dementia may cause foreclosure, not the other way around. Further, addressing selection bias may be critical: both foreclosure and dementia could plausibly increase mortality 19 or other forms of study attrition. Finally, prior studies lumped older adults together, across a wide age range. This may obscure important heterogeneity between middle‐aged (50 to 64) and older (65+) older adults, driven by differences in the prevalence and financial consequences of foreclosure in these two groups (eg, more of middle‐aged adults’ wealth is concentrated in their housing) 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 or differences in their cognitive health. 24 , 25 Dementia, for example, manifests largely in later old age, such that the link between foreclosure and cognitive health among middle‐aged older adults might be better studied by examining memory decline more broadly.

First, they call for richer investigations of housing instability and dementia, including assessing whether evictions or even high housing cost burdens similarly drive dementia risk. Second, research investigating the potential mediators we describe would yield important data for developing interventions to safeguard older adults’ cognition following foreclosure.

We address those gaps here, using the 2008 to 2018 waves of the Health & Retirement Study (HRS) to estimate longitudinal associations between foreclosure and two cognitive outcomes: (1) continuous memory scores and (2) composite dementia probability scores developed by Wu and colleagues. 26 We create stratified models by age – comparing associations in middle age versus ages 65+ – while accounting for baseline cognition by using “change in memory scores from baseline” as our outcome, excluding those with probable dementia at baseline, accounting for potentially differential study attrition, and clustering of longitudinal responses. We focus on foreclosure because of its extreme consequences for individuals and the strong biological plausibility of potential impacts on cognition, including via (1) direct biological effects of foreclosure‐induced stress; 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 (2) indirect effects through worsened health behaviors increasing vascular dementia risk; 34 , 36 , 37 (3) displacing people from their communities, thereby increasing social isolation and speeding cognitive decline; 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 or (4) lowering access to credit, thereby worsening financial hardship. 45 , 46 , 47

In secondary analyses, however, we additionally consider a third level of treatment: having a difficult time keeping up with mortgage payments (ie, housing unaffordability). Untenably high housing cost burdens could activate similar pathways (especially, pathways 1 and 2 mentioned previously, in addition to high housing cost burdens competing with household spending on healthcare, utilities, or healthy food 48 , 49 ). Separating out those having difficulty making mortgage payments also helps in drawing a sharper contrast between foreclosure and housing security.

METHODS

Study design and inclusion criteria

We examined longitudinal associations between foreclosure and two cognitive health measures: memory scores and probability of dementia scores. Our data set‐up took inspiration from methodological research encouraging epidemiologists to imagine dividing a larger cohort study into a sequence of longitudinal cohort studies that could then be pooled together to provide an overall estimate of the association between some exposure (foreclosure) and health. 50 Here, we conceptualized each wave of HRS as the beginning of a separate cohort study testing the impact of foreclosure on memory and dementia probability.

To construct the dataset for each “cohort study,” we first identified HRS participants in the 2008 to 2018 waves capable of experiencing foreclosure (ie, had a mortgage). We then constructed five distinct cohorts – our 2008 to 2010, 2010 to 2012, 2012 to 2014, 2014 to 2016, and 2016 to 2018 cohorts, each representing a different “cohort study” – and followed their memory and dementia probability scores.

Cohorts were constructed as follows. For the 2008 to 2010 cohort, we began with all eligible respondents with foreclosure data in 2008 and 2010 (N = 6,891). We then excluded respondents who (1) experienced foreclosure in 2008 (were already exposed at baseline) (N = 20); (2) lacked cognition data (N = 197) or had probable dementia (a dementia probability score ≥ 0.6, calculated per Wu et al.) 26 (N = 198) in 2008; or (3) lacked any follow‐up memory scores (N = 65) or were missing covariates (N = 4). Respondents were considered “treated” if they reported experiencing foreclosure in the last 2 years in the subsequent, 2010 wave (ie, they were foreclosed upon between 2008 and 2010). We considered respondents censored for all subsequent observations if they lacked memory scores in a given follow‐up wave, attritted for any reason, or were foreclosed on after 2010 (for our comparison group only, ie, comparison group members within a given cohort were considered censored if they changed treatment states during that cohort's follow‐up). Respondents were followed until censoring or the end of follow‐up (2018).

We repeated this procedure to construct our remaining cohorts (treating 2010 as “baseline” and then constructing a 2010 to 2012 cohort, treating 2012 as “baseline” and then constructing a 2012 to 2014 cohort, and so forth), then appended them all together. Respondents could thus have been included in multiple cohorts. The final pooled analytic sample included 15,766 individuals – 249 treated respondents and 15,645 not foreclosed on (including 1,092 who had housing cost burden). Collectively, they contributed 173,973 observations, with a median follow‐up of four calendar years (min‐max: 2 to 10).

Measures

Exposure

Among those who had a mortgage at baseline or had a home at baseline but lost it by the first follow‐up (those at risk of foreclosure), our binary exposure = 1 if respondents experienced foreclosure in the past 2 years, and 0 otherwise.

In secondary analyses, we further defined a third treatment level based on HRS’ questions on mortgage payments; specifically, HRS asked whether respondents had ever been more than 2 months behind on their mortgage payments at any time in the past 2 years. We treated as housing insecure (ie, as having a distinct, third level of treatment) those who reported ever being so behind but who had not been foreclosed on in the past 2 years in either baseline or follow‐up waves.

Outcomes

We examined two outcomes: a composite memory score and a dementia probability score (assessing the probability that a given person has dementia at a given time point) developed by Wu and colleagues. 26 Wu et al. (2017) used core HRS questionnaire items (ie, word recalls, subtraction problems, and proxy respondents’ assessments of cognition for memory score) and calibrated against the Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study (ADAMS) sample, a subset of HRS participants who underwent detailed in‐person neuropsychological batteries, including direct memory assessments and formal evaluation of clinical dementia ratings. 51 These developed scores were compared against participants’ prior‐wave HRS core interview performance. Using the models derived from the ADAMS cohort, memory score and dementia probability were estimated for all HRS participants based on the HRS core component measures. Importantly, Wu harmonized measures across respondents who could take memory assessments themselves and those who required a proxy to facilitate measurement (common among those with dementia); these harmonized scores were standardized memory scores for ease of interpretation. In our outcome models, we further modified memory scores using change in those scores from baseline as our outcome. This helped account for the fact that those who experienced foreclosure may have had lower memory scores to begin with (ie, these differences in baseline memory are “baked in” to our change score calculations).

Confounders

Potential confounders measured at baseline were selected a priori because they shape access to both financial and health resources (ie, could shape foreclosure risk and memory): age (in years, entered as a continuous variable as well as the square of that variable); binary sex (male vs female, reflecting limitations in HRS’ data collection); marital status (married/partnered vs single); race/ethnicity (a proxy for racism exposures in education, employment, healthcare, and housing – non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, all others); education (< high school, high school diploma, some college, or college and above); per‐capita household income; non‐housing wealth (calculated as total non‐housing wealth minus non‐housing debt); and chronic conditions (the sum of eight binary self‐reported medical diagnoses: high blood pressure, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, and arthritis).

Statistical analysis

We fitted mixed‐effects linear regression models with person‐ and household‐level random intercepts to account for autocorrelation across repeat observations and between individuals living together, using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. These models included a main effect for foreclosure as well as interactions between foreclosure and time, allowing us to assess foreclosure‐related changes in sample members’ rates of cognitive decline. For easier interpretation, we translated these time × foreclosure change estimates into “excess years of cognitive aging over a 10‐year period,” as per Weuve. 52 We present all results across subgroups by age (50 to 64 vs 65+). We further provide results from models with inverse probability censoring weights to account for potential differential study attrition across treatment, calculated using logistic regression. 53

One concern from these models is that some people who did not experience foreclosure may nonetheless have been housing insecure. To help provide better contrasts between foreclosure and housing security, in secondary analyses, we contrasted housing‐secure older adults with two different levels of treatment: (1) foreclosure and (2) no foreclosure but more than 2 months behind on mortgage payments (either at baseline or in the subsequent year).

Finally, we ran several sensitivity analyses. These included calculating standard errors via wild cluster bootstrapping instead of via random intercept models (repeatedly sampling individuals–i.e., clusters of observations nested within individuals–to generate a sampling distribution from which to make inferences), incorporating random slopes (which more thoughtfully address the correlation structure of repeated observations, though in these models, we could not incorporate inverse probability of censoring weights), incorporating interactions between time and all of our covariates (which may reduce confounding bias in the slope at the expense of precision/statistical power, as well as increasing the risk of overfitting, given our small sample of individuals who experienced foreclosure), and additionally adjusting for a measure of childhood socioeconomic status (SES), a plausible confounder even after adjusting for adult SES. These last models, adjusting for childhood SES, were relegated to sensitivity analyses because validated measures of childhood SES, calculated by Vable, 54 were not available for a large portion of the middle‐aged older adults in our sample, limiting power, and because we already adjusted for adult income, education, non‐housing wealth, and homeownership (through restriction to those with mortgages). There are also documented issues with measurement error when using retrospective reports of childhood SES in HRS. 55

RESULTS

A total of 249 individuals (contributing 253 person‐trials across cohorts) experienced foreclosure (Table 1); 78% were <65 at the time of foreclosure (198 person‐trials among those aged 60 to 64 vs 55 person‐trials among those aged 65 and older). Within each age subgroup, HRS participants who experienced foreclosure were generally younger, more likely to be racial/ethnic minorities (except for the older non‐Hispanic Black category among those 65+), and more likely to be married/partnered; they were also more likely to have no college degree, lower household incomes, and more chronic conditions. Baseline memory scores were similar across foreclosure status for middle‐aged older adults but at later ages were slightly lower for those who experienced foreclosure.

Among middle‐aged older adults, foreclosure was associated with faster longitudinal cognitive decline (−0.007 standard deviations [SDs] per year; 95% confidence interval: −0.013, −0.001), such that disparities in cognition emerged only over time (Figure 1). Compared to secular trends among those not foreclosed on (−0.019 SDs per year), this translated into 3.7 excess years of cognitive aging over 10 years. As expected in this younger age group, associations between foreclosure and dementia probability scores were closer to 0 and statistically insignificant (Table S1).

In contrast, among those aged 65+, foreclosure associations were more complex. At 2 years after foreclosure, foreclosed older adults presented with a greater decline in memory scores and greater increase in dementia probability scores from baseline. However, their scores eventually converged with the average memory and dementia probability trends of our comparison group, with widening confidence intervals over the course of follow‐up.

Results were robust to inverse probability censoring weights (Appendix Table S1).

Sensitivity analyses

Results were consistent across nearly all of our sensitivity analyses, including the use of bootstrapped standard errors, addition of random slopes, models with interactions between all of our covariates and time, and additionally controlling for childhood SES (Appendix Tables S3 to S6). Models with bootstrapped standard errors yielded very slightly wider confidence intervals, and models additionally controlling for childhood SES were very slightly attenuated for our estimate of the association between foreclosure and cognitive decline among middle‐aged adults; but in the latter case, we also lost a substantial portion of the sample due to missing childhood SES data.

DISCUSSION

We found that foreclosure was associated with faster memory decline in late middle age (50 to 64). Particularly for current older adults, many of whom were over 50 during the peak of the foreclosure crisis in 2010, the population burden of foreclosure on memory may be substantial.

Our analysis is associational. Existing epidemiologic and social science research, however, supports the biological plausibility of a causal relationship between foreclosure and cognition. Evidence currently points to at least four potential mediating paths.

Foreclosure‐induced stress may directly impede memory and speed cognitive decline

Prolonged, heightened levels of stress – including increases in circulating glucocorticoids like cortisol – impede memory formation and cognitive performance. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 These effects may be transient and resolve if stress is abated (e.g., if the housing insecurity experienced by foreclosed‐upon older adults resolves over time). Human and animal models demonstrate, however, that chronic stress is associated with structural changes in the brain that precede Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 This could induce longer‐lasting increases in dementia risk.

Social epidemiologic research has found that eviction and foreclosure are linked to exactly the kind of heightened mental distress and poor mental health that could drive these changes, including several quasi‐experimental studies that leverage housing policy changes 32 or random assignment to more or less tenant‐friendly judges 33 as natural experiments. These studies rely not only on self‐reported mental distress 32 , 34 but also on administrative data on mental health hospitalizations 33 and biomarker data showing that foreclosures are associated with higher cumulative cortisol levels as detected in hair follicles. 35

Foreclosure could increase risk of vascular dementia via worsening health behaviors and reduced access to medical care

Foreclosure may also contribute to dementia via potential effects on the cardiovascular system, as strokes and less acute reductions in oxygen to the brain can lead to dementia either alone or in combination with Alzheimer's disease. 56 The stress of homelessness or housing insecurity – as well as the economic toll housing instability imposes – may lead to a higher prevalence of coping and survival health behaviors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, or poorer diet, all cardiovascular disease risk factors. 34 , 36 , 37 Combined with increased stress (another risk factor for cardiovascular disease), it is unsurprising that neighborhoods with higher levels of foreclosure also suffer higher rates of cardiovascular illness, 57 though current designs exploring this are associational.

Simultaneously, housing instability can impede access to healthcare and health insurance, thereby worsening cardiovascular disease risk factors while simultaneously reducing patients’ ability to manage those risks. A study of Medicaid patients in New York City, for example, found that, compared to matched patients with similar healthcare utilization prior to an eviction, eviction was associated with substantial increases in Medicaid coverage loss, fewer prescription medication refills, and heightened acute (but lower primary) care utilization. 58 (Medicaid eligibility must be regularly recertified, a process that often uses mailed recertification forms, a unique challenge for recently displaced patients. Research among families with young children in Baltimore and Philadelphia has similarly found that families evicted in the past year were more likely to experience disruptions to Medicaid and public nutrition assistance program benefits. 59 ) Related research found that eviction was associated with a lower ability to take human immunodeficiency virus medication as prescribed, 60 as patients are displaced away from known healthcare providers and pharmacies and struggle to prioritize their healthcare in the face of homelessness. Indeed, interviews with people experiencing homelessness who have diabetes indicate that housing needs often directly compete with diabetes management, 61 a crucial driver of cardiovascular disease risk. 62

Experiencing foreclosure can impede social contact, removing a protective buffer against memory decline

Finally, foreclosure may drive social isolation. Frequent social contact is thought to prevent the onset of cognitive impairment by preserving cognitive reserves. 38 , 39 Stable housing facilitates such contact: qualitative research with older adults displaced from public housing projects shows that geographically rooted communities facilitate social connectivity and meaning‐making, 40 creating an environment in which older adults have not only friends and neighbors but also a social role in their neighborhoods as respected elders. 41 , 42 , 43 When those geographically rooted communities are lost, many (now far‐flung) older adults express profound grief and social isolation, as well as difficulty reforging connections in their new neighborhoods. 43 , 44

Foreclosure lowers access to credit, increasing risk of poverty and material deprivation

Foreclosure severely damages credit scores, often by hundreds of points. 45 It also represents a major loss of wealth against which older adults can borrow when they are in good standing, such as via a reverse mortgage. Foreclosure can thus damage access to revolving credit and lower older adults’ ability to weather economic shocks. A lack of financial buffers threatens food security 46 and access to medical care that requires out‐of‐pocket expenditures, 47 potentially both directly contributing to cognitive decline and exacerbating many of the pathways discussed earlier.

Of course, these paths interrelate: worsening social isolation and access to credit (paths 3 and 4) can drive stress and unhealthy coping behaviors (paths 1 and 2), for example. Future work both replicating our findings with stronger causal designs and examining (quantitatively and qualitatively) the potential paths above is needed.

Results for high housing cost burdens (as proxied by falling behind on mortgage payments) were null. This suggests that, if high housing cost burdens speed cognitive decline, falling behind on one's mortgage at any point in a given 4‐year period may be an insufficient measure for detecting such effects. Future work with larger samples and richer measures of housing insecurity would help clarify the issue – such as via detailed, longitudinal question batteries on housing cost burden among both homeowners and renters.

Our study used longitudinal memory and dementia probability scores as outcomes, one of the important ways to study dementia risks when direct dementia incidence information is lacking. Future studies evaluating the causal effects of foreclosure on dementia incidence, such as those leveraging administrative data or electronic health records, 63 would be fruitful for building evidence in the housing–dementia research realm.

Why we might see sustained, faster memory decline among middle‐aged older adults, but only temporary associations among those aged 65+, is unclear. We hypothesize that it is due to selection bias – in particular, differential depletion of susceptibles. 64 It is possible that the most socially excluded (and, therefore, disproportionately medically vulnerable) people may have already died before age 65, such that lower‐income people who survive to this age (who are more likely to be foreclosed upon) may be disproportionately resilient to social–biological hazards like foreclosure. Relationships between foreclosure and cognition would be biased away from a harmful effect, a problem not shared by middle‐aged older adults.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings support the hypothesis that avoiding foreclosure has cognitive health benefits. As such, mortgage relief programs for older adults may benefit a population's cognitive health (and, thus, potentially generate cost savings for Medicare and other healthcare payers), as may social support programs for older adults following a foreclosure. For healthcare providers, collecting data on patients’ housing experiences may help clinicians identify older adults in need of targeted cognitive health support.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has important limitations. Most obviously, unmeasured confounding and information bias from self‐reported confounders loom large, problems we hope to address in future work using study designs that better support causal inference. Similarly, our censoring weights only function to eliminate selection bias if we have correctly measured and modeled all determinants of potentially differential attrition 53 ; these assumptions may not perfectly hold, though we note that our results were remarkably robust to censoring weights. Our treatment group was small, limiting statistical efficiency and precluding detailed subgroup analyses. HRS also lacks a meaningfully large sample of older renters at high risk of eviction, nor does it include a sufficient battery of eviction questions to measure evictions holistically. 65 As a result, we were not able to examine the potential cognitive impact of eviction and housing cost burden in the older renting population. Finally, though we focus on within‐person changes in memory and dementia probability scores and exclude those with probable dementia at baseline, the possibility of reverse causation remains: foreclosure–memory score change associations could plausibly arise from pre‐existing declining memory scores before baseline, even among those without probable dementia. We note, however, that our findings are of concern regardless of the direction of the causal arrow:it would be worth intervening if foreclosure merely added to the hardships faced by older adults with cognitive impairment, even if not all of the association we observe is explained by foreclosure causing cognitive decline.

Our analysis also benefits from critical strengths, both substantively and methodologically. Substantively, our longitudinal, nationally representative data cover a period of intense foreclosure activity in the US, and we newly investigated important effect heterogeneity by age in the nascent field of housing and cognition. Methodologically, we directly addressed reverse causality by excluding those with probable baseline dementia and analyzing change in memory scores from baseline, as well as applying rigorous methods for handling differential study attrition. Our study thus offers robust, novel insights into the social determinants of cognitive aging, points to promising directions for the integration of housing policy and healthcare policy in the United States, and opens up new paths toward better safeguarding older adults’ cognition.
