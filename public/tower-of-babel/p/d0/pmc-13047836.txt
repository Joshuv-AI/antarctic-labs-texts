The best solution is the simplest: advances in surgical and minimally invasive management of aneurysmal and simple bone cysts
Authors: Abdulrahman Alaseem, Abdullah Addar, Mishari Alanezi, Fahad Alshayhan, Waleed Albishi, Ibrahim Alshaygy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) and simple bone cysts (SBCs) are benign osseous lesions predominantly affecting children and adolescents, typically presenting with pain, deformity, or pathologic fracture. Despite their frequency, optimal management remains debated. This review synthesizes current evidence on therapeutic strategies, efficacy, recurrence, and safety. For ABCs, intralesional curettage remains the standard surgical approach, though recurrence rates vary and are reduced with adjuvants such as high-speed burring, cryotherapy, or cementation. En bloc resection offers the lowest recurrence; however, it is restricted by procedural morbidity and poor functional outcomes. Minimally invasive modalities, including sclerotherapy, selective arterial embolization, endoscopic curettage, and image-guided ablation, consistently achieve high rates of healing, pain relief, and functional recovery, often with fewer complications. Management of SBCs ranges from observation to intervention in symptomatic or unstable lesions. Elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN) provides both decompression and structural stability, with high healing rates. Overall, advances in minimally invasive and biologic therapies are reshaping treatment paradigms, yet standardized outcome measures and head-to-head comparative trials remain needed.Keywords: Aneurysmal bone cysts, Simple bone cysts, Unicameral bone cysts, Intralesional curettage, Denosumab

Introduction

Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) and simple bone cysts (SBCs) represent two of the most common benign osseous lesions encountered in orthopedic practice, particularly among pediatric and adolescent populations [1, 2]. Though both are non-cancerous, their clinical behavior, underlying pathophysiology, and management strategies differ substantially, posing distinct challenges for clinicians. ABCs are expansile, osteolytic lesions characterized by blood-filled cavities separated by fibrous septa, with a tendency for local aggressiveness and bone destruction [3]. In contrast, SBCs are developmental or reactive unicameral cavities filled with serous fluid [4]. Despite their benign nature, both lesions are clinically significant due to their association with pain, skeletal deformity, and a heightened risk of pathological fracture in young patients.

Management strategies for ABCs and SBCs have evolved substantially over the past two decades, driven by improved biologic understanding and the expansion of minimally invasive options [5–8]. In ABCs, recognition of USP6 rearrangements has refined diagnostic certainty and supported development of targeted approaches [9], while percutaneous modalities (e.g., sclerotherapy and SAE) have increasingly been adopted to reduce morbidity in anatomically complex lesions [10, 11]. Systemic therapy (most notably denosumab, a receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand [RANKL] inhibitor) is now used selectively for aggressive, recurrent, or surgically inaccessible ABCs. However, outcome definitions and comparative evidence remain heterogeneous, limiting standardization of treatment selection and sequencing [12–15].

Given the persistent clinical challenges and the rapid evolution in the understanding and management of these lesions, a comprehensive review is warranted. The objectives of this review are (1) to summarize the distinct pathophysiology, molecular mechanisms, and diagnostic criteria of ABCs and SBCs; (2) to critically evaluate the spectrum of established and emerging treatment modalities, from minimally invasive techniques to open surgical procedures and targeted medical therapies; and (3) to highlight specific management challenges, particularly in pediatric populations and anatomically complex sites.

Epidemiology

ABCs have an estimated annual incidence of 0.14–0.32 per 100,000 individuals, with a slight female predominance and a median age of 10–13 years. Approximately 60% arise in long bones, most commonly in the metaphyseal regions of the femur, tibia, and humerus, although the spine and pelvis are also frequent sites [16–18].

SBCs, also known as unicameral bone cysts (UBCs), have a comparable annual prevalence of approximately 0.30 per 100,000 but have a stronger male predominance (male-to-female ratio up to 1.96:1) and typically present at a slightly younger median age of 9–11 years [17]. They most commonly involve the proximal humerus and femur and are frequently identified incidentally or after a pathologic fracture [19]. Both ABCs and SBCs are rare in the craniofacial skeleton and jaws; when present, they primarily affect young individuals and are usually asymptomatic [20, 21].

Diagnostic modalities of ABCs and SBCs

A precise diagnosis is the cornerstone of effective treatment for bone cysts. Understanding the distinct pathophysiology, molecular underpinnings, and characteristic imaging features of ABCs and SBCs is crucial for differentiating them from each other and, most importantly, from malignant mimics.

Histopathology and molecular pathogenesis

ABCs are blood-filled, multiloculated cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa. Histologically, these septa contain fibroblast-like stromal cells, numerous osteoclast-type giant cells, and reactive woven bone, often with osteoblastic rimming [18]. The cyst walls lack a true endothelial lining, though flattened endothelial-like cells may be present. The seminal discovery in ABC pathogenesis is the consistent presence of a USP6 gene rearrangement in approximately 63–82% of primary cases [22–24]. Its most critical role is in distinguishing primary ABC from secondary “ABC-like changes” that can occur in a variety of other benign and malignant bone tumors, including giant cell tumor of bone, chondroblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, and, most importantly, telangiectatic osteosarcoma. These secondary changes mimic the histology of an ABC but lack the underlying USP6 driver mutation [18]. In ABCs, USP6 oncogenic activation occurs via promoter swapping, where a chromosomal translocation juxtaposes the entire coding sequence of USP6 with the highly active promoter of a partner gene [25].

SBCs are fluid-filled cavities that are lined by a thin fibrous membrane composed of spindled to ovoid cells, with scattered osteoclast-like giant cells, foamy histiocytes, hemosiderin deposits, and cholesterol clefts. Fibrin-like collagen deposits and occasional woven bone may be present in the cyst wall [26]. Unlike ABCs, SBCs lack the prominent blood-filled spaces and septa with abundant giant cells. Recent molecular studies have identified EWSR1 or FUS gene rearrangements, particularly FUS: NFATC2 fusions, in a subset of SBCs, suggesting a neoplastic component in their pathogenesis [27, 28]. However, SBCs remain less cellular and less vascular than ABCs, and their histopathology is generally less complex [26].

While the precise cause is unknown, the dominant hypothesis attributes SBCs to a focal imbalance in skeletal development and intracystic fluid dynamics. It is hypothesized that an obstruction of intramedullary venous drainage leads to elevated intracystic pressure [29]. This increased pressure causes bone resorption by osteoclasts and stimulates the production of interstitial fluid, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of cyst expansion. Supporting this, analysis of SBC fluid has shown elevated levels of inflammatory mediators and enzymes, including prostaglandins and lysosomal enzymes, as well as increased concentrations of RANKL, which promotes osteoclast activity [30–32]. SBCs are clinically classified by their proximity to the physis, which predicts growth behavior and risk [33]. Active cysts lie within 1 cm of the growth plate, exhibit greater growth potential, and carry higher risks of pathological fracture and recurrence. As the bone elongates, the cyst migrates diaphyseally and becomes latent; these lesions have lower growth potential and are more likely to heal spontaneously, particularly as skeletal maturity nears [26]. Radiographic features

Plain radiography is the initial imaging modality of choice and often provides strong clues to the diagnosis. ABCs typically present as expansile, lytic lesions with well-defined margins, cortical thinning, and often a soap-bubble or multiloculated appearance due to internal septations [34]. They frequently cause bone expansion and may show periosteal reactions. Computed tomography (CT) can further delineate the extent of bone destruction, cortical breach, and internal septations; however, it is less sensitive than MRI in evaluating fluid characteristics and soft tissue involvement [35]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is particularly valuable for ABCs, as it often reveals multiple fluid-fluid levels within the cystic spaces, reflecting blood of different ages, and shows septal enhancement after contrast administration, features that are highly suggestive of ABC [36]. MRI also provides superior assessment of soft tissue extension and helps distinguish ABCs from malignant lesions [37, 38].

On the other hand, SBCs usually appear on plain radiographs as well-circumscribed, unilocular, radiolucent lesions with thin sclerotic margins, most commonly in the metaphysis of long bones [39]. They are often discovered incidentally or after a pathological fracture, which may result in a fallen fragment sign, a cortical bone fragment lying dependently within the cyst [40]. CT can help confirm the unilocular nature and assess cortical integrity. On MRI, SBCs typically show a single fluid-filled cavity [26].

Clinical course and natural history

ABCs typically manifest as swelling, pain, or pathological fracture, though some cases are discovered incidentally on imaging [18]. The clinical course of ABCs is unpredictable, with some lesions growing rapidly and causing substantial bone destruction, while others remain stable or regress spontaneously [18]. Recurrence after treatment is relatively common, especially in younger patients and when intralesional curettage alone is performed, with reported rates ranging from 10% to 30% depending on the location and completeness of excision [41]. Secondary ABCs, which arise in association with other bone lesions, tend to have a higher recurrence rate and may require more aggressive management [42].

SBCs may resolve spontaneously, particularly following a fracture; however, persistent or enlarging cysts can predispose to recurrent fractures or progressive bone deformity [4]. Compared with ABCs, recurrence after intervention is less frequent, and the overall prognosis is favorable, with most patients achieving complete recovery and subsequent bone remodeling over time [43].

Indications for interventions

Intervention for ABCs is indicated in cases of pain, rapid lesion expansion, risk of pathological fracture, and neurological symptoms [18]. Intralesional curettage, with or without adjuvant therapy, remains the standard approach; however, minimally invasive modalities such as percutaneous sclerotherapy, SAE, or systemic therapy are increasingly considered in surgically challenging locations [44]. Gross total resection is preferred when feasible, as incomplete excision is associated with higher recurrence rates [45].

In contrast, SBCs are often managed conservatively when asymptomatic, as many resolve spontaneously [4]. Surgical intervention is indicated in cases of persistent pain, risk of fracture, failure of healing after fracture, or when the lesion is large, expanding, or located in a weight-bearing bone where structural integrity is compromised [4]. Treatment options include intralesional curettage with or without bone grafting, elastic intramedullary nailing, and corticosteroid injection, with the choice determined by cyst size, anatomical location, and patient-specific factors [43].

Established and emerging treatments for ABCs and SBCs

The treatment of ABCs is guided by the lesion’s size, location, and the patient’s symptoms. The primary goals are to eradicate the lesion, prevent recurrence, restore bone integrity to prevent fracture, and preserve function [46]. The therapeutic landscape has evolved from exclusively open surgical approaches to include a variety of effective minimally invasive options.

Established treatments of ABCs

Surgical interventions of ABCs

Intralesional curettage: Intralesional curettage has historically been the standard of care for ABCs [47]. The procedure involves creating a cortical window, removing the cystic contents and membranous lining, and often abrading the cavity with a high-speed burr to eliminate microscopic disease [5]. However, curettage alone is associated with substantial and heterogeneous recurrence across reports [7], with individual series describing recurrence rates in the 30–40% range [47–49]. Furthermore, a systematic review by Bavan et al., analyzed 17 studies involving 594 patients, reported an aggregate failure or recurrence rate of 14.4% for curettage (with or without adjuvants), with rates varying from 0% to 40% across different studies [6]. Nevertheless, functional outcomes are usually favorable, with most patients returning to normal activities. Complications, though uncommon, can include growth plate injury and deformity [50].

Intralesional curettage with adjuvant therapy: The addition of adjuvant therapies to curettage aims to improve local control and reduce recurrence. The rationale is to destroy any residual neoplastic cells left behind on the cavity walls, effectively sterilizing the surgical bed [5]. Meta-analyses highlight that curettage combined with autologous cancellous bone grafting yields better healing and lower recurrence than curettage alone [51]. Other adjuvants, including high-speed burring, penalization, cryotherapy, argon beam coagulation, and bone cement, have been associated with improved local control in selected series, although reported effects vary across studies [52–54]. Preoperative SAE has also been shown to further decrease recurrence risk [55]. Overall complication rates are generally low, but modality-specific risks should be considered during patient selection and surgical planning [56].

En bloc resection: En bloc resection, which entails complete removal of the cyst with a margin of healthy bone, provides the highest cure rates for ABCs, with recurrence rates approaching zero in many series [57]. This radical approach is most suitable for lesions in surgically accessible and expendable bones such as the fibula, clavicle, or ribs, where resection causes minimal functional loss [6, 57]. However, its application in weight-bearing bones is limited due to the high surgical morbidity, need for complex reconstructions, and risk of functional compromise [58]. Across published series and systematic reviews, resection consistently demonstrates superior local control versus extended curettage, albeit with the trade-off of greater technical complexity and possible functional impact in selected locations [59–62] (Table 2).

Minimally invasive and percutaneous interventions for ABCs

Percutaneous steroid injection: Percutaneous steroid injection, often combined with calcitonin, has emerged as a minimally invasive and effective treatment for ABCs. Several retrospective and prospective series showed that patients achieve meaningful clinical improvement and radiographic healing, although non-responders appear at higher risk of subsequent recurrence and therefore warrant close monitoring [70–72]. A meta-analysis of 25 studies involving 729 patients showed that calcitonin plus methylprednisolone had comparable efficacy and safety to other sclerotherapy agents, with a low recurrence rate of 3.5% [10]. Collectively, these findings support steroid-based injections as a strong option for ABCs.

Percutaneous bone marrow injection: Percutaneous bone marrow or allogenic graft injection is a minimally invasive technique designed to stimulate healing in ABCs by introducing osteogenic cells and scaffolding materials directly into the cystic cavity [73]. This approach leverages the regenerative capacity of autologous bone marrow, which is rich in mesenchymal stem cells, and combines it with adjuncts such as demineralized bone matrix (DBM), platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), or allogenic bone particles to promote ossification and cyst resolution [74]. Clinical evidence supports its utility, with comparative studies showing up to 80% healing rates after a single injection of bone marrow concentrate combined with DBM and PRF, outcomes comparable to curettage with bone grafting [75]. Case series have reported similar results, with healing rates around 79% for percutaneous DBM and bone marrow injections [76]. Collectively, these findings indicate that percutaneous bone marrow or allogenic graft injection is a safe, effective, and repeatable treatment option that provides a valuable alternative when open surgery is undesirable or in high-risk settings.

Injectable bone substitutes: Injection of bone substitutes such as calcium sulfate, calcium phosphate, or biphasic ceramic materials has emerged as a minimally invasive option for treating ABCs [77]. The rationale is to provide immediate mechanical stability, fill the cystic cavity with an osteoconductive scaffold, and promote gradual replacement by native bone while minimizing long-term complications. Clinical evidence supports this approach, with case reports and series showing encouraging results. A reported case of a C2 ABC treated with a single biphasic ceramic injection achieved complete ossification, cortical remodeling, and restoration of spinal canal anatomy within six months, without complications [78]. These findings suggest that bone substitute injection is a safe, effective, and repeatable strategy that can achieve durable cyst healing and functional recovery.

Sclerotherapy: Sclerotherapy is a minimally invasive technique for treating ABCs that involves percutaneous injection of sclerosing agents directly into the cyst cavity under imaging guidance [79, 80]. The mechanism is based on inducing endothelial damage, thrombosis, and fibrosis within the lesion, which ultimately promotes ossification and healing [79]. This approach is particularly valuable for anatomically complex or surgically high-risk sites, including the pelvis and spine [10]. Clinical evidence consistently demonstrates its efficacy, with prospective and retrospective studies showing high healing rates, rapid pain relief, and functional recovery. For example, a polidocanol-based study in 26 patients achieved 92.3% ossification and a substantial pain reduction, with only two recurrences successfully managed by a repeat injection [81]. Larger series have reported disease control rates of 96–100% after two to four injections, with minimal complications such as transient nausea, ecchymosis, or local discomfort [82, 83].

Selective arterial embolization (SAE): SAE is a minimally invasive technique increasingly used in the management of ABCs, especially in highly vascular sites such as the spine, pelvis, and sacrum [35]. The procedure involves angiographic identification of feeding arteries followed by targeted occlusion with embolic agents. By devascularizing the lesion, SAE induces thrombosis and promotes cyst regression and ossification, reducing pain, controlling growth, and lowering the risk of intraoperative bleeding if later surgery is required [84, 85]. In a cohort of 36 patients, effectiveness reached 94% with only minor complications such as transient paresis or limited skin necrosis [86]. Another series of 18 patients reported an 88.9% response rate, no major complications, and only two recurrences managed surgically [85] (Table 3).

Comparative effectiveness and recurrence across treatment modalities for ABCs

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and cryoablation: RFA and cryoablation are minimally invasive, image-guided thermal ablation techniques that have gained increasing use in the management of ABCs [87]. RFA uses high-frequency alternating current to generate heat and induce coagulative necrosis within the lesion, while cryoablation uses rapid freezing, typically with argon gas, to form an ice ball that destroys tumor cells [88]. Both procedures are performed percutaneously under CT or MRI guidance, ensuring precise targeting and sparing adjacent tissues. Their primary aims are local tumor control, pain reduction, and functional recovery, achieved with lower morbidity than open surgery. Clinical studies have shown encouraging results. In a cohort of 20 patients managed with RFA (with or without cementation), pain scores dropped dramatically from a mean VAS of 8.4 to 0.2, with no recurrences or complications after one year of follow-up [87]. Cryoablation, used either alone or with cementoplasty, has similarly demonstrated high rates of radiological healing and symptom resolution in both pediatric and adult populations, including spinal and recurrent cases [89, 90].

Endoscopic curettage: Minimally invasive curettage via endoscopy is an emerging approach for managing ABCs, designed to lower surgical morbidity while ensuring effective local control. Using arthroscopic or endoscopic guidance, surgeons access the cyst through small incisions, enabling direct visualization and precise curettage until healthy bone is exposed [91]. This method reduces blind spots, prevents unnecessary bone loss, and helps preserve vital structures such as the physis in pediatric patients. Compared with open surgery, the technique causes less soft tissue disruption, carries a lower risk of infection, and yields improved cosmetic outcomes. The defect is typically filled with autograft, allograft, or synthetic bone substitutes to promote consolidation [92]. A series of 30 patients demonstrated a 10% recurrence rate, solid union at an average of 3.2 months, with excellent functional recovery [91].

Box 2. Treatment algorithm for simple bone cysts.

Conservative management

Conservative therapy for SBCs typically involves observation or minimally invasive interventions, especially in cases with non-displaced pathological fractures or asymptomatic lesions. Studies showed that most SBCs, particularly those in the upper extremity, can be managed conservatively, with spontaneous healing observed in many cases [58]. Conservative approaches may include immobilization and close radiographic follow-up, as the majority of pathological fractures treated by this approach heal within six weeks, and secondary deformities are rare [102]. Intralesional steroid injections have also been used as a conservative, low-cost technique, promoting bone formation and preserving vital structures, with documented efficacy in long bone SBCs [103, 104].

Surgical management of SBCs

Intralesional curettage: Intralesional curettage is the standard surgical treatment for SBCs, aiming to remove the cyst lining and contents to stimulate bone healing. Clinical outcomes of intralesional curettage for SBCs and other benign osseous lesions demonstrate relatively high recurrence rates, with studies reporting 27–51% recurrence and some series noting a median of 47% [105, 106], largely influenced by the adequacy of cyst wall removal [107]. Greater benefits have been observed with the use of adjuvant therapies such as high-speed burring, cryotherapy, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement, or chemical agents. Meta-analyses report that recurrence rates decrease to approximately 11.9% with high-speed burring and around 20% with PMMA, although the efficacy of chemical agents such as phenol or hydrogen peroxide remains inconsistent [108, 109]. Overall, meticulous curettage remains the critical determinant of success, with grafting providing structural support and adjuvants offering additional protection against recurrence, particularly in aggressive lesions.

Cyst decompression and elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN): Cyst decompression techniques for SBCs primarily involve creating a channel between the cyst cavity and the medullary canal to relieve intracystic pressure and promote healing [110]. Methods such as percutaneous drilling or decompression with cannulated pins have demonstrated high healing rates, with some studies reporting successful outcomes in up to 89.5% of cases with low recurrence rates [111]. These minimally invasive approaches allow for early mobilization, reduced surgical trauma, and preservation of bone integrity, making them suitable for pediatric patients. However, although cyst decompression alone can be effective, some patients may experience partial healing or residual cysts, and recurrence risk tends to be higher in younger children or in lesions located near the growth plate [112].

Elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN) is a widely used decompression technique that provides both continuous drainage of cystic fluid and mechanical stability [113]. Studies consistently show that ESIN leads to high rates of complete or partial radiographic healing, ranging from 66% to 100%, with very low recurrence and complication rates. ESIN is particularly advantageous in cases with pathological fractures, as it stabilizes the bone and allows for early return to activity [114–116]. Functional outcomes are excellent, with most patients regaining full range of motion and experiencing minimal pain postoperatively. Complications are rare and generally mild, such as nail irritation or minor deformity, and the need for reoperation is low [117, 118]. Other adjunctive techniques, such as combining ESIN with steroid injections, bone grafting, or demineralized bone matrix, have also been explored. ESIN combined with intralesional steroid injection has shown 100% complete or partial healing with no recurrences in some series, while ESIN with bone grafting or bone marrow injection also yields excellent radiological and functional outcomes [119, 120]. Overall, the addition of adjuvant therapies to decompression techniques can optimize outcomes, particularly in complex or recurrent SBCs (Table 4).

Intralesional injections

Intralesional corticosteroid injection for SBCs typically results in rapid pain relief and functional improvement, with significant reductions in pain scores observed as early as 6 weeks post-injection. In musculoskeletal applications, corticosteroid injections have been shown to reduce pain scores by up to 50% at 6 weeks and 3 months, with the effect often diminishing by 6 months [121, 122]. However, recurrence rates for SBCs treated with corticosteroids alone are reported to range from 30% to 50% at 1–2 years, indicating that while initial symptomatic control is excellent, long-term cyst resolution is less reliable. Complications are rare, with most studies reporting no serious adverse events, but minor risks include local tissue atrophy or infection [121, 122].

Emerging therapies for ABCs and SBCs

Denosumab (RANKL inhibition)

Denosumab is a recent pharmacologic advance for ABCs and may be considered in cases where conventional surgical or interventional options are inadequate [131]. It is a fully human monoclonal antibody that binds RANKL with high affinity and specificity, thereby inhibiting a key pathway that drives osteoclast differentiation, activation, and survival [132, 133]. By blocking RANKL–RANK signaling, denosumab suppresses osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, limits progressive bone destruction, and can promote structural stabilization of the lesion [132, 133]. This mechanism is biologically relevant in ABCs, which are enriched in osteoclast-like giant cells and exhibit heightened RANK/RANKL pathway activity contributing to osteolysis and aggressive behavior [3].

Denosumab is primarily used as a second-line or adjunctive option for surgically challenging ABCs, including unresectable lesions, recurrent disease refractory to conventional interventions, and tumors in anatomically complex/high-risk locations where surgery would carry substantial morbidity [134]. It is particularly reported in spine and pelvis lesions, where it may relieve pain, improve neurological deficits, and stabilize the lesion while reducing the need for high-risk surgery and associated complications such as hemorrhage [135, 136].

Clinical outcomes and challenges: The efficacy of denosumab is often rapid and profound. Studies consistently report high rates of positive clinical response, including significant pain relief and functional improvement within weeks of treatment initiation [15, 137, 138]. Radiologically, this corresponds to progressive ossification, formation of a sclerotic rim, and a reduction in the cystic components of the lesion. Beyond its role as primary therapy, denosumab is also valuable as a neoadjuvant agent. Preoperative administration can downstage large, aggressive tumors, reduce their vascularity, and induce a sclerotic shell, thereby facilitating a safer, less extensive, and more effective surgical resection.

Despite demonstrated effectiveness, denosumab use in ABCs, particularly in pediatric patients, raises important safety and durability concerns, including recurrence after treatment discontinuation and the need for re-treatment in some cases [139]. A key risk in skeletally immature patients is severe rebound hypercalcemia after cessation, likely reflecting a surge in osteoclast activity and higher baseline bone turnover, necessitating prolonged post-treatment calcium monitoring [67, 140]. Additional uncertainties relate to potential long-term effects on the growing skeleton, including reports of sclerotic metaphyseal bands and possible impacts on bone modelling and linear growth, although long-term consequences remain incompletely defined [141].

Role of denosumab in SBCs: In contrast to its role in ABCs, there is currently no substantive evidence or established biological rationale to support the use of denosumab for the treatment of SBCs. The pathophysiology of SBCs is believed to be related to a localized disturbance in venous drainage, leading to fluid accumulation and pressure-induced bone resorption, rather than a neoplastic process driven by osteoclast-like giant cells. The cellular and molecular environment of an SBC does not involve the RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis that denosumab targets. Therefore, its use is not supported by existing literature and is not a recommended treatment modality for this condition (Table 5).

Special considerations and management challenges

Although a wide range of treatment modalities exists for SBCs and ABCs, their application is not uniform. Management decisions must be individualized, taking into account patient-specific factors and the biological and anatomical characteristics of each lesion. Pediatric patients, lesions situated in complex anatomical regions, and cases characterized by recurrence each present distinct clinical challenges. These scenarios require carefully tailored therapeutic strategies.

Pediatric patients

The vast majority of ABCs and SBCs are diagnosed in children and adolescents, a demographic that introduces several layers of complexity to treatment planning.

Higher recurrence rates

Younger age is one of the most consistently reported independent risk factors for treatment failure and lesion recurrence for both cyst types. For SBCs, patients younger than 10 years have a significantly higher risk of recurrence following treatment [144]. Similarly, for ABCs, age below 10 has been associated with lower recurrence-free survival after surgical treatment [47].

Growth plate (Physeal) proximity

Many of these lesions arise in the metaphysis, immediately adjacent to the physis (growth plate) [19]. This proximity presents a dual risk. First, an aggressive, expansile lesion like an ABC can directly damage or disrupt the growth plate, leading to premature closure, angular deformities, or limb length discrepancy [145]. Second, the treatment itself can cause iatrogenic physeal injury. Aggressive intralesional curettage, or the use of chemical or thermal adjuvants, can damage the delicate chondrocytes of the growth plate if not applied with extreme caution [107, 146].

Anatomically challenging locations

Anatomical site largely dictates the risk and feasibility of managing bone cysts, with the spine, pelvis, and skull base being particularly challenging. Spinal ABCs, which account for approximately 15% of cases [147], threaten neural structures and stability, making surgery high-risk, with potential for dural tears, neurological injury, and spinal deformity [148]. Management often combines SAE, denosumab, and, when unavoidable, complex instrumented fusion [149]. Pelvic ABCs are difficult to access and carry major hemorrhagic and neurovascular risks [145], so minimally invasive approaches, SAE, percutaneous sclerotherapy, and denosumab are commonly favored to avoid open surgery [136].

Management of recurrent lesions

Recurrence represents the most common form of treatment failure for both ABC and SBC [18]. The management of a recurrent lesion often requires a step-up in therapeutic aggressiveness [63]. The initial choice of treatment, patient age, and lesion location all influence the approach to recurrence [150]. Options include repeating the initial therapy, escalating to a more invasive procedure, or switching to an entirely different modality [6]. For SBC that recurs after steroid injection, a surgeon might proceed to curettage and bone grafting [151]. For an ABC that recurs after simple curettage, a repeat procedure with the addition of a potent adjuvant may be indicated to achieve better local control [55]. In cases where recurrence occurs in a location where further surgery is unfeasible or would be too morbid, alternative modalities become critical [14, 152].

Unmet needs and critical gaps in the management of ABCs and SBCs

From our perspective, management of ABCs and SBCs is limited chiefly by two gaps: diagnostic certainty and high-quality comparative evidence. In terms of diagnosis, distinguishing primary ABC from secondary ABC remains imperfect in routine practice; while USP6 rearrangements strongly support primary ABC, molecular testing is not universally available, and fusion partners are expanding, complicating test selection and interpretation [18, 153]. Imaging-based criteria for activity/healing also vary, which undermines trial comparability and day-to-day decisions such as when to stop sclerotherapy or resume sport. For SBCs, even basic questions (natural history near the physis, true fracture risk, and the definition of healed) lack consensus; legacy radiographic scores and heterogeneous endpoints limit meta-analyses and practical guidance [26]. Evidence quality is another core unmet need. For ABCs, most data on SAE, sclerotherapy agents, and denosumab come from small series, single-center cohorts, and systematic reviews that highlight promise but also underscore ongoing uncertainty regarding long-term oncologic durability and the risk of relapse following treatment discontinuation [154]. In pediatric patients, the role of denosumab remains particularly uncertain. Reports describe favorable responses in inoperable spinal and pelvic lesions but also document episodes of rebound hypercalcemia. These findings underscore the absence of standardized dosing or tapering protocols and the lack of long-term safety data [140, 155]. For SBCs, decades of clinical practice continue to rely on small or methodologically limited studies comparing treatments such as steroid injection, decompression, curettage with bone grafting, intramedullary nailing, and sclerotherapy. Even the sole Level I trial was modest in size, and durable superiority of any single modality, or treatment sequence, remains unproven [156].

Management and follow-up pathways remain highly fragmented. In anatomically complex ABCs involving the spine, pelvis, or skull base, a careful balance must be maintained between the risks of bleeding, neurological compromise, and recurrence against procedure-related morbidity. SAE and other percutaneous techniques are being adopted with increasing frequency; however, prospective treatment algorithms and head-to-head comparisons with surgical approaches remain scarce, particularly for pelvic lesions, where complication and reoperation rates continue to be substantially high [152]. For SBCs, unanswered questions include when to intervene versus observe; how to individualize care by age, cyst activity, and proximity to the physis; as well as whether to prioritize single-stage definitive surgery or staged minimally invasive care [26]. Across both entities, there is a need for: core outcome sets that integrate pain, function, time-to-healing, growth-plate complications, and return-to-activity; standardized imaging follow-up schedules and healing criteria; comparative-effectiveness trials; and validated risk tools using age, location, genetic markers, and cyst biomechanics to predict fracture and recurrence and to guide sequencing of treatments.

Future directions

Future studies should move beyond isolated case series toward prospectively designed programs that integrate biology, biomechanics, and precision intervention. Molecular stratification, quantitative imaging phenotypes, and biomechanical risk scoring could be combined to personalize therapy sequencing and to proactively prevent deformity in juxta-physeal lesions.

Technique innovation should prioritize growth-plate–sparing approaches, and smarter percutaneous regimens. In parallel, pragmatic, multi-center platform trials can test modular care bundles (e.g., SAE followed by sclerotherapy vs. sclerotherapy-alone vs. denosumab-bridging) with shared core outcomes and time-to-function endpoints, while embedded health-economics analyses clarify value in real systems.

Conclusions

Management of ABCs and SBCs has evolved toward risk-adapted strategies that balance local control against morbidity, growth-plate preservation, and functional outcomes, although most comparative evidence remains low-level and heterogeneous. For ABCs, an anatomy- and morbidity-stratified pathway is most actionable: surgically accessible lesions can be managed with extended intralesional curettage and appropriate reconstruction, whereas anatomically complex or high-morbidity sites are well suited to minimally invasive first-line options such as image-guided sclerotherapy, with SAE used as definitive or adjunct therapy to reduce vascularity and facilitate safer intervention; escalation to en bloc resection is reserved for carefully selected refractory cases. Denosumab should remain a reserved option for aggressive, recurrent, or inoperable ABCs, with structured safety monitoring.

For SBCs, a fracture-risk–centered approach is recommended: asymptomatic, low-risk lesions may be observed, whereas symptomatic or higher-risk cysts warrant bone-preserving percutaneous strategies. In mechanically vulnerable locations, particularly the proximal femur, prophylactic stabilization should be considered to mitigate fracture consequence and support healing, with curettage and grafting/substitute (with fixation when needed) reserved for persistent lesions, repeated failure of percutaneous management, or progressive structural compromise.
