Nrf-2/HO-1 activation protects against oxidative stress and inflammation induced by metal welding fume UFPs in 16HBE cells
Authors: Mengchao Ying, Yun Yang, Qian Huo, Jingqiu Sun, Xinyu Hong, Feng Yang, Yamin Fang, Lingyi Lu, Tingfeng Mao, Ping Xiao, Gonghua Tao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As one of the main occupational hazards, welding fumes can cause oxidative damage and induce series of diseases, such as COPD or asthma. To clarify the effects of the metal fume ultrafine particulates (MF-UFPs) of welding fumes on oxidative damage, UFPs were collected by melt inert gas (MIG) and manual metal arc (MMA) welding, and the composition was confirmed. Human bronchial epithelial 16HBE cells were treated with 0–1000 µg/cm2 MF-UFPs to analyse the cytotoxicity, oxidative stress and cytokines. The protein and mRNA expression of Keap1-Nrf-2/antioxidant response elements (AREs) signalling pathway components were also analysed. After 4 h of treatment, the cell viability decreased 25% after 33.85 and 32.81 µg/cm2 MIG/MMA-UFPs treated. The intracellular ATP concentrations were also decreased significantly, while LDH leakage was increased. The decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and increased ROS suggested the occurrence of oxidative damage, and the results of proteome profiling arrays also showed a significant increase in IL-6 and IL-8. The expression of AREs which related to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory were also increased. These results indicate that the MF-UFPs can cause oxidative stress in 16HBE cells and activate the Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway to against oxidative damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74599-8.

Introduction

As one of the most common occupational hazards, welding fumes expose millions of workers and are classified as Class I carcinogens1. Due to the increased attention on the occupational health, understanding the molecular mechanism of welding fumes toxicity has become increasingly important. Depending on the welding material, welding fumes are complex variable and usually include metal oxides and toxic gases. Epidemiological studies have shown that the workers who exposed to long-term welding fumes can suffer from pneumoconiosis, systemic inflammation and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)2–4. Tokaç et al. reported that the expression levels of GSH, CAT, SOD and GPx were decreased and the MDA, 8-OHdG, and GR were significantly increased in welding workers compared to the general workers5. Dement et al. also reported that there was a statistically significant association between COPD and welding6. These results suggest that the welding fume has a close relationship with oxidative stress, DNA oxidative damage and lung diseases.

Oxidative damage is mainly induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). These oxidants are controllable and beneficial in normal cells and serve as important signalling molecules that regulate cell division, inflammation, immune function, autophagy and other stress responses. However, once the active oxidant is out of control, it will cause oxidative stress, cell damage, cancer, chronic disease, or other negative effects7. Oxidative damage also plays a major role in the mechanism of COPD because it can cause inflammation, stimulate fibrosis and emphysema8. The NF-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf-2) is a member of the CNC-bZIP (cap’n’collar basic-region leucine zipper) subfamily transcription factors and plays an important role in the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway. It is one of the most important signalling pathways for defending against oxidative damage9. Kensler et al. reported that Nrf-2 knockout mice exhibit increased sensitivity to chemical and oxidative toxicity. The Nrf-2 agonists that induce the expression of Nrf-2 can also protect animals from oxidative stress and diseases such as cancer, hepatotoxicity, respiratory distress and inflammation10–12. Under the normal conditions, Nrf-2 is combined and inhibited with Keap1 as a dimer in the cytoplasm. Once the cells are under oxidative stress, the Nrf-2 is released to the nucleus and increases the expression levels of antioxidant response elements (AREs)13. In recent years, the results of chromatin immunoprecipitation have provided abundant genome-wide analysis data for Nrf-2 and its target genes. Many studies have shown that AREs are involved in regulating cellular protective proteins such as glutathione, antioxidant proteins or enzymes, exogenous metabolic enzymes, transporters and other transcription factors, thereby accelerating exogenous metabolism and elimination and inhibiting oxidative states to maintain intracellular stability14–17.

Usually, we focus on the large size of welding fume particles because they can be deposited in the upper respiratory tract and lungs. However, ultrafine particles (UFPs) can also cause more severe cytotoxic effects, such as injuring the lung tissue directly or into other organs through systemic circulation18. Due to their nanoparticle size, UFPs can translocate to the alveolus pulmonic and spread by Brownian diffusion. It can also break the air-blood barrier and enter the blood circulation, where it is ultimately deposited into organs such as the liver, spleen, heart, or other important organs19,20. In this study, we analysed the metal composition of welding fume UFPs and confirmed their effects on cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and the exchange of the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway.

Methods

Cell material and growth conditions

The human bronchial epithelial 16HBE cell line was obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were seeded in a cell culture flask, and the passage numbers were kept at 5–30 for tests.

Particle collection and component analysis

The metal fume ultrafine particulates (MF-UFPs) samples of melt inert gas (MIG) and manual metal arc (MMA) were collected from a simulated welding process using a MOUDI impactor, which had 10, 5.6, 3.2, 1.8, 1.0, 0.56, 0.32, 0.18, 0.10, and 0.056 nominal cut points. The impactor was set 50 cm away from the welding worker, and the samples were collected on quartz fibre filters (Millipore, Germany). The samples below 0.10 μm were extracted from the filters by sonication for 60 min in methanol and 120 min in water. All the eluted samples were combined and concentrated by lyophilization and then resuspended into water before use. An atomic absorption spectrophotometer was used to analyse the metal components.

Cell viability assay and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

MTT and LDH were used to confirm cell viability and cell membrane integrity after MF-UFPs treated. 16HBE cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well for 24 ± 0.5 h and then incubated with different concentrations of MIG and MMA MF-UFPs from 0 to 1000 µg/cm2 for 4 h. All the cell supernatants were collected after treatment for the LDH assay, and the cells were washed with PBS one time, followed by the addition of 100 µl/well fresh medium with 0.1 mg/ml MTT solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, the medium was removed, and 150 µl/well DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added into the plate. All the plates were transfer to the microplate shaker for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm and used to calculate the cell viability. For the LDH assay, the collected supernatants were centrifuged at 2000 r/min and measured with a Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

Cellular mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The MF-UFPs treated cells were stained with an Enhanced Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit with JC-1 (Beyotime, China) for 20 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS three times, and fluorescence was detected (λex/em = 490/530 nm) by using a SpectraMax multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

Reactive oxygen species assay

After MF-UFPs treated, the cells were washed with PBS and then stained with DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, China). The cells and probe were coincubated for 20 min at 37 °C and washed three times with FBS-free medium to ensure that the probe was completely removed. The ROS concentration was measured at λex/em = 488/525 nm. The SpectraMax multimode microplate reader was used to analyse the fluorescence intensity.

ATP and catalase assay

Total protein was extracted from MF-UFPs treated cells with cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). Then, the samples were determined with BCA Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) and diluted with ATP or catalase assay buffer (Beyotime, China) to the same concentration. The ATP standard solution (0.5 mM) was diluted from 0.01 to 10 µM to construct the standard curve. Then, 100 µl of ATP working solution was added to the 96-well microplates and incubated at room temperature for 3–5 min to ensure that all of the background ATP was consumed. 20 µl of samples and standard were added and mixed well with ATP assay working solution, and the RLU was measured to calculate the ATP concentration. For the CAT assay, 0 to 3.75 mM hydrogen peroxide solution was used as the standard curve. 40 µl of the sample was pipetted into a 1.5 ml EP tube, 10 µl of 250 mM hydrogen peroxide was added, and the tube was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. After incubation, 450 µl of stop solution was added to all the samples, which were mixed well by vortexing. After vortexing, 40 µl of Catalase Assay Buffer was mixed with 10 µl of the reaction sample, and then 10 µl of the mixture was pipetted onto a 96-well microplate. Before measurement, 200 µl of chromogenic working solution was added to each well, and the A520 was measured after incubation at 25 °C for 15 min.

Proteome profiling arrays

The RayBio® Human Cytokine Array C Series (RayBiotech, Inc.) was used to detect the expression levels of cytokines after treated with MF-UFPs. Total protein samples were extracted from MF-UFPs treated cells and determined with BCA Assay Kit. For each array membrane, 200 µg protein samples were used to incubation at 4 °C overnight. The bound cytokines were detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions and detected by the Doc Molecular Imager (Bio-Rad, USA). The area under the curve (AUC) was determined by ImageJ to quantify the bands. The information of cytokines was list in a Supplementary Information (SI).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted with cold RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China) and then measured with BCA Assay Kit. All the protein samples were treated with 4x LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen, USA) at 95 °C for 5 min. After treatment, the samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking with skim milk, the membranes were incubated with diluted Nrf-2, Keap1, NQO-1, HO-1, and GAPDH primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the membranes were washed with 1× TBS-T three times for 15 min and then incubated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies for 1 h. The ECL substrate (Thermo Scientific, USA) and Doc Molecular Imager (Bio-Rad, USA) were used to detect the bands. GAPDH was used as the reference protein and the entire antibody were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA. The area under the curve (AUC) was determined by ImageJ (ver. 1.54b, NIH, USA) to quantify the bands.

Gene expression analysis by qRT‒PCR

Total RNA was isolated from treated cells using a TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (TAKARA, Japan), and the concentration was measured with NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). For first-strand cDNA synthesis, 2 µg of total RNA was incubated at 65 °C for 5 min to dissolve the secondary structures. Then, the samples were annealed with 5x RT Master Mix at 37 °C for 15 min, 50 °C for 5 min and 98 °C for 5 min using ReverTra AceTM qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan). cDNA (100 ng) was used as the template for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), which was conducted with QuantStudio 7 Flex (Applied Biosystems, USA) and PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mixes (Applied Biosystems, USA). The qRT-PCR program was as follows: 50 °C for 2 min; 95 °C for 2 min; and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The program was completed at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s to check the specificity of the amplified products. All the qRT-PCR data were evaluated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method21. The gene-specific primers used were provided by the NRF2 pathway PCR ARRAY plate (WcGene Biotech, China), and a heatmap was drawn with the HemI heatmap illustrator22. The information of NRF2 pathway PCR ARRAY plate was list in a Supplementary Information (SI).

Benchmark dose analysis and statistical analysis

The Benchmark dose analyses used the BMDS23, which was released by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA). BMD was calculated using the continuous model, and the Hill model was used for the curve models. All the BMDs were calculated based on a 0.95 confidence level.

All of the data were expressed as the form of mean ± standard deviation (SD) and the results of three individual experiments were used statistically by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test to determine the differences between groups. The p-value < 0.05 was considered as a statistically significant difference.

The cytotoxicity of MF-UFPs

Due to the main exposure pathway of MF-UFPs, 16HBE cells were used to evaluate their cytotoxicity. The cell viability, LDH leakage, and ATP concentration were determined after 4 h and 0-1000 µg/cm2 MF-UFPs treated. The cell viability (CV) and ATP concentration decreased in a dose-dependent manner in both of MIG and MMA treatment groups (Fig. 1A, D). LDH leakage rates also increased after exposure with MF-UFPs (Fig. 1C). The CV was calculated with a polynomial equation, and the EPA BMDS was used to predict the dose that should be considered disadvantageous to cell viability (Fig. 1B). The CV75, CV50 and BMD results are shown in Table 2. For MIG and MMA MF-UFPs, the exposure doses that caused 25% cell death were 33.85 and 32.81 µg/cm2, and the doses caused 50% cell death were 151.42 and 142.12 µg/cm2, respectively. Compared with MIG MF-UFPs, the CV75 and CV50 dose of MMA MF-UFPs was lower, which suggests that the cytotoxicity of MMA MF-UFPs was greater than that of the MIG MF-UFPs. Similar to these results, the BMD values represent the predicted dose with a 0.95 confidence level. The BMD of MIG was 10.78 µg/cm2, whereas that of MMA was 7.05 µg/cm2. Both the CV and BMD values suggested that the cytotoxicity of MMA was greater than that of MIG MF-UFPs.

The effect of MF-UFPs on oxidative stress

Mitochondria play an important role in oxidative damage. Damaged or aged mitochondria produce toxic by products such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) to cause oxidative damage. After treated with MF-UFPs, JC-1 was used to stain mitochondria and confirm changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). The results showed that the JC-1 monomer increased after both MIG and MMA MF-UFPs treatment, which means that MMA was significantly reduced in MF-UFPs treated cells (Fig. 2A).

We also used the cell-permeable fluorogenic probe DCFH-DA to measure the ROS levels (Fig. 2B) and the amount of hydrogen peroxide decomposed by catalase in the samples of MF-UFPs treatment. As shown in Fig. 2C and D, MF-UFPs upregulated ROS production and catalase activity. These results indicate that both MIG and MMA MF-UFPs induced oxidative damage in 16HBE cells.

Oxidative stress and inflammation are both major factors that cause lung diseases. The results of the antibody array (Fig. 3) showed that the expression levels of a number of cytokines and growth factors in MF-UFPs treated cells, which including BMP-4, BMP-6, EGF, Eotaxin, IFN-γ, IGFBP-1, IGFBP-2, IGFBP-4, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-6R, IL-7, IL-8, MIP-3-alpha (CCL20), TIMP-1 and TIMP-2, were induced by MF-UFPs.

MF-UFPs active the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway in 16HBE cells

As an important antioxidant signalling pathway, the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway can regulate antioxidant response elements (AREs) to protect against oxidative damage and inflammation. As shown in Fig. 4, the expression of Nrf-2 increased approximately 1.78-fold in the MIG treated grout though had not significantly change after MMA treatment. The expression of Keap1 also significantly decreased, which suggested that Keap1 was degraded and that the Nrf2:KeKe1 complex dissociated. Compared with that in the control group, the expression level of HO-1 increased after expose with both MIG and MMA. CAT was only upregulated significantly in the MIG treatment group, while the expression of NQO-1 decreased in both treatment groups.

MF-UFPs induced AREs in 16HBE cells

We aimed to clarify how the expression of Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway genes was regulated after MIG and MMA MF-UFPs treatment. Nrf-2 target genes, which include antioxidant proteins, phase 1 proteins, phase 2 proteins, transcription factors, growth factors and receptor and transport factors, were detected in the MF-UFPs-treated groups (Fig. 5). The NFE2L2 gene expression level increased under CV75 treatment condition (33.85 µg/cm2 for MIG and 32.81 µg/cm2 for MMA) but decreased under CV50 treatment condition (151.42 µg/cm2 for MIG and 142.12 µg/cm2 for MMA). The gene expression levels of CBR1, CBR3, CES1, CES5A, EGR1, GSTA1, GSTM1, GSTM2, GSTP1, HBEGF, HGF, HMOX1, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSPA1A, MAFF, MAFG, PPARD, PRDX6, SLC6A1, TXNRD3, UGT1A1 and UGT2B7 increased, while those of ABCC4, BLVRB and GSTM4 decreased after treated with MF-UFPs. Similar to those of NFE2L2, the expression levels of GSTM3, ME1, NRG1, SERPINA1, SLC39A1, TGFA and TGFBR2 also increased under CV75 treatment conditions but decreased under CV50 treatment conditions. In contrast, the expression levels of CYP4A11, DNAJB1, GCLC, GPX2, UGT1A6, EPHA2 and EPHA3 decreased under CV75 but increased under CV50 treatment conditions.

Discussion

As a common occupational hazard, welding fumes are composed of a large number of different metal mixtures. Fumes can enter the respiratory system through respiration and cause inflammation and oxidative damage. In recent research, the damage to the lungs and airways of people or animals was shown to be affected by the metal composition and particle size24. Usually, particles between 1 and 5 μm in diameter can be inhaled25. However, recent research has indicated that particles with diameters smaller than 1 μm are also in the appropriate size range and may cause severe damage to the lung26.

In this study, we collected and investigated the metal components of MIG and MMA MF-UFPs (diameter ≤ 0.01 μm). The contents of metal components in descending order were Zn > Mn > Ba > Fe > Al > Ti > W > Mo > Ni > Cu > Cr > V > Cd > Co in the MIG and Zn > Mn > Ba > Fe > Al > Cu > Mo > Cr > W > Ni > Co > Cd > V > Ti in the MMA. Chromium and nickel, which are common factors that cause cytotoxic and oxidative stress, respectively, accounted for 0.12% (0.068% for Cr and 0.052% for Ni) of the MMA and 0.064% (0.021% for Cr and 0.043% for Ni) of the MIG.

According to the relevant inhalation exposure research, the average volume of inhaled air per minute under moderate physical activity is 46 L/min27. The National Occupational Health Standards of the People’s Republic of China (GBZ 2.1–2007) also prescribe that the limit of exposure to welding fume is 4 mg/m3. In our previous study, UFPs accounted for approximately 10% of the total welding fume particles; thus, we exposed 16HBE cells to 0–1000 µg/cm3 MF-UFPs (equivalent to 0–0.927 mg/m3 for workers) and both CV75 and CV50 treatment condition was within the limit exposure concentrations. There also has relevant report shown workers exposed to welding fumes for 4 h can experience metal fume fever28. Our results also showed exposure of 16HBE cells to MF-UFPs for 4 h can also cause significant cytotoxicity. The exposure dose of 75% and 50% cell viability were 33.85 and 151.42 µg/cm2 for MIG and 32.81 and 142.12 µg/cm2 for MMA, respectively. We also used a Hill model to calculate the BMD range for the toxic dose of 16HBE cells. The BMD values for MIG had a ranged from 6.62 to 15.91 µg/cm2, and those for MMA ranged from 4.00 to 11.16 µg/cm2. Both of these results showed that the MF-UFPs of MMA had slightly stronger cytotoxicity than MIG. The LDH leakage results also supported this conclusion. The increased LDH leakage rate suggested cell membrane damage, and LDH leakage was 2.43-fold greater in the MMA treatment group than in the control group, while LDH leakage in the MIG treatment group was 2.27-fold greater.

As ATP is a marker of cell damage, a decrease in ATP concentration indicates cellular ATP depletion, cell damage, and dysfunction or a decrease in the membrane potential of mitochondria, thereby causing oxidative damage. To clarify the effect of MF-UFPs on oxidative damage, JC-1 staining was used to detect the MMP (∆Ψm). When the ∆Ψm is high in normal cells, JC-1 forms red fluorescent JC-1 aggregates. However, the ∆Ψm decreases when mitochondria are damaged, and JC-1 cannot aggregate in the matrix and exists as a green fluorescent monomer. The results showed that the number of JC-1 aggregates significantly decreased and the number of JC-1 monomers increased in the MF-UFPs-treated groups. This result indicated that mitochondrial function was impaired; thus, the ATP concentration decreased, and ROS production increased29. DCFH-DA and CAT activity assays also demonstrated that the level of oxidative stress was increased after MF-UFPs treatment. As the main factor that induces asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, there is an inseparable relationship between oxidative damage and inflammation. Exogenous oxidants such as atmospheric particulate matter, cigarette smoke, and welding fume can induce ROS production through mitochondrial oxidative stress or inflammatory cells, and oxidative stress can also amplify inflammation8. Our results showed that the levels of a series of cytokines and growth factors, especially BMP-4, Eotaxin, IFN-γ, IGFBP-1, IGFBP-2, IGFBP-4, IL-6, IL-6R, IL-8 and MIP-3-alpha (CCL20), were increased by exposure to MF-USPs. These cytokines are closely related to the pathogenesis mechanism of COPD or asthma, and some of them can also be regulated by the Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway30–35. The Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway is one of the most important pathways involved in preventing oxidative damage36. In normal cells, Nrf-2 is inhibited by Keap1 in complex form. Once cells suffer oxidative damage, Keap1 can be modified with electrophilic agents such as ROS, and the Keap1:Nrf-2 complex will quickly dissociate, which means that the pathway is activated. To clarify how MF-UFPs affect the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway, whole-cell lysates were extracted for western blot analysis. When the 16HBE cells were exposed to the MIG-UFPs, the Nrf-2 protein expression levels were upregulated under the 151.42 µg/cm2 exposure conditions, but there was no significant change after MMA-UFPs treatment. However, both of these two MF-UFPs can decrease the expression of cytoplasmic Nrf-2 (data not shown) and Keap1, which function as inhibitors of Nrf-2. Thus, Nrf-2 can be released and transferred to the nucleus to activate AREs. The increased expression of HO-1 and CAT also indicated this result. Similar results were also shown in other studies related to welding fumes24.

After MF-UFPs treated, the NFE2L2 was upregulated under CV75 but decreased under CV50 treatment conditions. Similarly, MAFF and MAFG, which heterodimerize with Nrf-2 in the nucleus and recognize AREs37, were also significantly upregulated under CV75 compared with the control but were less upregulated under CV50 treatment conditions. The gene expression levels of Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway ARE genes such as CBR1, CBR3, GSTA1, GSTM1, GSTM2, GSTP1, HBEGF, HGF, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSPA1A, PRDX6 and TXNRD3, which relate with antioxidants or cytoprotective, were also upregulated after MF-UFPs treated38–45. Other upregulated genes, such as CES1, CES5A, EGR1, PPARD, UGT1A1 and UGT2B7, are closely related to detoxification and inflammatory lipid metabolism46–51. Compared with that in the MIG treatment group, the western blot results revealed that HO-1 expression was much greater in the MMA treatment group under CV75 treatment condition, but exhausted under CV50 treatment though the HO-1 was continuously upregulated in the MIG treatment group. We also noted that some ARE genes’ expression patterns were differed between the MIG and MMA treatment groups, such as CES4A, FLT, GPX3, PDGFB, PGD, SQSTM1, TGFB2 and TXNRD1. These genes were upregulated under CV50 treatment conditions in MIG but downregulated in the MMA treatment groups, and both of them were upregulated under CV75 treatment conditions. Most of these genes are related to hydrolysis, macroautophagy, GSH, macroautophagy and TGF-beta52–56, which may suggest that the cells in the MMA treatment group may suffer more damage than those in the MIG group. The major components of these two types of welding fumes were similar, but the Cr and Ni contents were 2-fold greater in the MMA MF-UFPs than in the MIG MF-UFPs. Furthermore, MIG may generate less metal oxide than MMA because of the use of inert gas for protection during welding, which may be one of the reasons why the cytotoxicity of MMA was slightly greater than that of MIG. These findings may explain the differences in the Keap1-Nrf-2/ARE signalling pathway between MIG and MMA.

In conclusion, our results revealed five major findings related to MF-UFPs and their effects on 16HBE cells: (1) the major components of MF-UFPs were Zn, Mn, Ba and Fe; (2) two types of welding fumes induced similar cytotoxicity with decreased cell viability, ATP levels, and increased LDH leakage; (3) oxidative stress and inflammation were induced by MF-UFPs; and (4) cytokines and growth factors were significantly increased, which may resulting in metal fume fever, even COPD, asthma or cancer. (5) The Nrf-2/HO1 signalling pathway was activated to combat oxidative damage and inflammation, which might protect the lung from these adverse reactions or other diseases.
