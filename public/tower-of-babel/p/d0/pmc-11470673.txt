Prevalence and contributing factors associated with tricuspid regurgitation among patients underwent echocardiography assessment
Authors: Jianyi Zheng, Xing Yu, Dazhuo Zhou, Mingcan Fan, Zhanyi Lin, Jiyan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTricuspid regurgitation (TR) is common in patients evaluated by echocardiography. However, the prevalence and contributing factors of the disease remain limited. This hospital-based study was designed to analyze adult patients first diagnosed with tricuspid regurgitation by Doppler echocardiography to determine the prevalence and characteristics of clinically meaningful TR.MethodsA total of 22,317 patients over the age of 18 who underwent echocardiography at the Cardiac Ultrasound Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University from July 1, 2015 to December 31, 2019 were collected. We collected basic information about the patients, including age, gender, history of heart disease, etc. Patients with valvular heart disease were assessed by transthoracic echocardiography. According to the degree of regurgitation and regurgitation, TR was divided into 6 grades (0–5). Pericardial effusion was recorded and bilateral atrial and ventricular diameters were measured. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess risk factors for significant TR (≥ grade 2 reflux).ResultsA total of 2299 significant TR cases were found in people over 18 years old, accounting for 10.3% of the total population. The occurrence of TR was found to be closely related to age. The prevalence rates of significant TR in different groups were: 3.3% in the younger than 45-year-old group, 4.1% in the 46-55-year-old group, 5.8% in the 56-65-year-old group, 10.1% in the 66-75-year-old group, and the prevalence of significant TR rose directly to 22.3% in patients over 75-year-old group. Further logistic regression analysis showed that male, age, pacemaker, congenital heart disease, pericardial effusion, pulmonary hypertension, mitral regurgitation, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and aortic regurgitation were associated with the occurrence of significant TR. Both RVD and RA-1 were effective predictors of significant TR, with RVD ≥ 33.5 mm having a sensitivity of 0.638, specificity of 0.675, and ROC curve area of 0.722. The sensitivity of RA1 ≥ 45.5 mm was 0.652, the specificity was 0.699, and the area under the ROC curve was 0.736.ConclusionsTR is common in people undergoing echocardiography. Gender, age, pacemaker implantation, congenital heart disease, pericardial effusion, pulmonary hypertension, mitral insufficiency, and aortic insufficiency are the influencing factors of TR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04178-2.

Introduction

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a common condition that was previously overlooked. The tricuspid valve is also known as the “forgotten valve” because it is generally well tolerated and the onset of symptoms is delayed in TR patients [1]. With the increasing prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF) and the use of implantable intracardiac pacemakers, the prevalence of TR continues to rise and is gradually gaining the attention it deserves [2, 3].The tricuspid valve is no longer the “forgotten valve”. Current studies have shown that patients with severe TR have a poor prognosis, and with the progression of TR, the survival rate and quality of life of patients have declined seriously [4, 5].However, little is known about the prevalence of TR, especially in Asian populations. There is an urgent need to assess the clinical features and prevalence of TR. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and demographics of TR in patients who underwent echocardiographic evaluation at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University between July 1,2015, and December 31, 2019. We recruited patients regardless of symptoms and reasons for referral to assess the prevalence of TR in the hospital population and tried to identify the contributing factors associated with tricuspid regurgitation.

Methods

Study population

From July 1, 2015, to December 31, 2019, a total of 30,630 cases of admission examinations with data older than 18 years were collected, of which 8,230 cases were repeated results of the same patient, and only 22,400 cases were the first echocardiography results and were included in the analysis. Excluding 83 cases with missing basic information, 22,317 cases of different case data were collected for further analysis (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University(2021-JS-41), All participants included in this study understood and provided informed consent.

Patient Selection Process

Clinical parameters

Record the basic information of the patient, including ID card, name, age, gender. Collect the medical history of heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and pacemaker implantation.

Doppler Echocardiography

All patients underwent comprehensive two-dimensional and Doppler echocardiography using multiple windows during the same examination. Patients were instructed to breathe normally, and all tricuspid valve and right ventricular(RV) measurements were the average of inspiratory and expiratory measurements for at least 5 sinus rhythms and 8 atrial fibrillation (AF) cycles. In echocardiographic examination, the diagnosis of TR typically involves the integrated application of multiple Doppler techniques, including Color flow mapping, Pulsed-Wave Doppler (PWD), and Continuous-Wave Doppler (CW). Our echocardiography examination was conducted by experienced sonographers with over 10 years of professional knowledge. The aforementioned techniques were flexibly combined and utilized by them based on the specific conditions and examination requirements of each patient. Additionally, the individual data from these three modes were preserved, allowing for a more accurate diagnosis of TR. According to the guide [6], the grading of TR was visually assessed using a comprehensive semiquantitative approach, including assessment of color Doppler jet area, tricuspid valve morphology, right atrium (RA) and RV size, inferior vena cava size, jet density, and contour color Doppler (None = 0, mild = 1, mild to moderate = 2, moderate = 3, moderate to severe = 4, severe = 5) [6].Significant TR was defined as equal to or greater than mild to moderate.

Severity of left-sided valvular disease, including mitral regurgitation (MR) or mitral stenosis (MS), aortic regurgitation (AR) or aortic stenosis (AS), is classified according to current recommendations [7, 8]. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured according to the modified biplane Simpson’s rule. We used LVEF < 50% as a method to classify significant systolic dysfunction. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) estimated from right ventricular systolic pressure calculated from peak TR velocity by continuous wave Doppler using a simplified Bernoulli equation and combining this value with estimated right atrial pressure (RAP) : PASP = 4(V) 2 + RAP [9]. Pulmonary hypertension was defined as PASP ≥ 50mmHg based on the results of transthoracic echocardiography [10].

Pericardial effusion was considered significant if it persisted throughout the cardiac cycle and was graded as small if measured < 1 cm in diastole, moderate if 1-2 cm, and if > 2 cm, it was classified as large [11]. For assessment of left ventricular diastolic function, pulse wave Doppler peak early mitral flow velocity (E) and late diastolic peak flow velocity (A), and tissue Doppler imaging early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e’) are record it. In this study, we used the ratio of E/e’ as an indicator of LV filling pressure and e’ as an indicator of early LV diastole or LV stiffness .

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies or proportions for categorical variables. One-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with post-hoc test by Bonferroni was used to examine the differences. A logistic regression model was used to assess factors associated with significant TR. For multivariable analysis, those with a P value < 0.1 were included and subsequently removed if the Wald test P value was > 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package SPSS for windows (version 21.0, SPSS, Chicago, USA) and reported P-values are two-sided for consistency. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A total of 22,317 transthoracic echocardiographic studies were retrieved for analysis. The mean age of the study population was 63.61 ± 15.44 years old, and 11,198 (50.2%) were male. Prevalence of significant TR

All cases were divided into 5 groups by age. The prevalence of significant TR in the under-45 group was only 3.3%. The prevalence rate increased to 4.1% in the 46–55 year old group, 5.8% in the 56–65 year old group, 10.1% in the 66–75 year old group, and the prevalence of significant TR increased further to 22.3% in the over 75-year-old group. The P value for the comparison between groups was less than 0.05. It is suggested that the prevalence of significant TR increases significantly with age. (Fig. 2)

Discussion

Early tricuspid regurgitation usually has no obvious signs and symptoms. Signs and symptoms associated with right heart failure, including gastrointestinal symptoms, lower extremity edema, hepatomegaly, and jugular venous dilatation with pulsation, are only present in the setting of severe tricuspid valve insufficiency. The lack of specific clinical signs and symptoms of tricuspid regurgitation makes imaging particularly important [12]. Echocardiography is the most important method to diagnose tricuspid regurgitation [13].

Tricuspid regurgitation is very common in patients undergoing echocardiography, and the Framingham Heart study first reported that the prevalence TR of more than or equal to mild severity was 14.8% in men and 18.4% in women [14].Ox-Valve study conducted a large cross-sectional epidemiological analysis at Oxford. The prevalence of moderate or greater TR was 2.6% (95% CI 2.3 to 2.9) in the community over 65 years old population. At present, there is a lack of relevant epidemiological studies in Asia. A hospital-based study found that 54.7% of patients with mild TR, of whom 8.4% had TR greater than moderate [10], which is similar to our findings. In this cohort, we found that the prevalence of TR in patients with equal or greater than mild TR was 34.7%. The prevalence of TR in patients with equal and greater than mild-moderate was 10.3%.

Further studies found that age, female, and pacemaker implantation were the main risk factors for the occurrence of TR. The prevalence of TR increased significantly with age, and the prevalence of moderate tricuspid regurgitation was as high as 22% in people over 75 years old, indicating that age is an important risk factor for TR. Previous studies have found that the number and type of collagen fibers in the tricuspid annulus will change with age, leading to dilatation of the tricuspid annular surface and a gradual change in the shape of the tricuspid annulus from triangular to oval [15]. Elderly patients are likely to be complicated with atrial fibrillation. The current studies have confirmed that atrial fibrillation is closely related to the occurrence of TR and is a major risk factor for functional TR [16, 17]. In addition, we found that females are also a risk factor for TR, which may also be related to differences in the tricuspid annulus between males and females. The fibrous skeleton of the female tricuspid valve is less developed than in males. Furthermore, histological differences may play an important role in susceptibility to tricuspid regurgitation and tricuspid annular dilatation in women, but not in men [15].

The relationship between pacemakers and tricuspid regurgitation has been discussed in the past. After implantation of a cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED), the prevalence of lead-related significant tricuspid regurgitation (≥ grade 2) and increased grade ≥ 1 tricuspid regurgitation ranged from 7–45% [18]. The specific mechanisms include: (1) the guide wire is positioned between the valve leaflets, which interferes with the closure of the valve leaflets; (2) the guide wire strikes the valve leaflets to cause fibrosis and scarring, and the guide wire sticks to the valve; (3) the guide wire is wrapped around the valve leaflet; (4) leaflet perforation, injury, or annular dilation; (5) injury to subvalvular structures, such as wire and tendon tangles, papillary muscle perforation [18–20].

Valvular heart disease usually involves multiple heart valves, and previous studies have suggested that tricuspid regurgitation may be secondary to the left heart valve [21, 22]. This was also confirmed in our study. The results suggest that tricuspid regurgitation is associated with mitral stenosis, mitral regurgitation, and aortic regurgitation, but not with aortic stenosis. A possible explanation for this is that both mitral regurgitation and aortic insufficiency increase the pressure load in the left atrium, which can be transferred to the right ventricle through the pulmonary circulation, resulting in increased pulmonary pressure. This leads to dilatation of the right atrium and right ventricle, which in turn leads to tricuspid regurgitation.

Previous studies have suggested that TR induced by atrial fibrillation can cause tricuspid annulus to dilate laterally of the right ventricular free wall, resulting in tricuspid regurgitation. In contrast, TR due to left heart disease or pulmonary hypertension is often characterized by a dilated right ventricle, which pulls the anterior tricuspid valve forward through the papillary muscles and chordae tendineae [23, 24].

The relationship between pericardial effusion and tricuspid regurgitation is rarely reported, but it is closely related to pulmonary hypertension. Pericardial effusion is very common in patients with pulmonary hypertension [11]. In fact, pericardial effusion is associated with severe right ventricular insufficiency. Khan et al. found that pulmonary hypertension leads to the increase of right ventricular preload, which may lead to the reconstruction of right ventricular cavity with the increase of right atrial pressure [25].

Right atrial pressure was found to correlate strongly with pericardial effusion in all patients with pulmonary hypertension who underwent right heart catheterization. During the occurrence and development of TR, TR is often accompanied by an increase in pulmonary artery pressure and atrial pressure, which is the cause of pericardial effusion. There is no significant difference in the volume of pericardial effusion between patients with isolated pulmonary venous hypertension or systemic hypertension, but in the presence of both, the volume of pericardial effusion increased due to obstruction of pericardial venous return due to elevated right atrial pressure [26, 27]. In this study, we found an association between pericardial effusion and moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, but further associations between them require further validation in relevant studies.

We also found that left ventricular diastolic dysfunction was associated with the occurrence of TR, but the decrease of EF was not a risk factor of TR. The relationship between left ventricular diastolic insufficiency and tricuspid regurgitation has been confirmed by many studies, tricuspid regurgitation with the maximum velocity > 2.8 m/s has been newly integrated into the diagnostic criteria for left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction [28].Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction is.

often associated with elevated left atrial pressure, which may be the initiating factor for secondary tricuspid regurgitation [28, 29].

In patients with advanced heart failure, left ventricular systolic insufficiency is often associated with tricuspid regurgitation. Tricuspid regurgitation is present in up to 30% of patients with LVSD [30], and the presence of tricuspid regurgitation can increase the mortality of LVSD patients [31]. However, these patients often have mixed factors such as advanced age, atrial fibrillation, mitral valve disease and aortic valve disease, which often cannot independently reflect the separate relationship between left ventricular systolic insufficiency and tricuspid regurgitation [32, 33].

In this study, no correlation was found between left ventricular systolic dysfunction and tricuspid regurgitation, suggesting that in the absence of risk factors for tricuspid regurgitation such as mitral regurgitation and atrial fibrillation, tricuspid regurgitation insufficiency does not increase the prevalence of early tricuspid regurgitation in LV systolic insufficiency.

Limitations

Firstly, the subjects of this study were individuals who underwent color Doppler echocardiography in a tertiary hospital. Therefore, the prevalence of TR observed in this study may not accurately reflect the prevalence in the general community. Nevertheless, the trends observed in age and gender, supported by a sufficiently large sample size, provided reliable insights. Secondly, as this was a retrospective study, there were inherent limitations related to unmeasured confounding variables that could potentially influence the results.For example, the collection of risk factors in this study did not include common risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, especially atrial fibrillation. Previous research had demonstrated a strong correlation between atrial fibrillation and the occurrence of TR. The absence of this critical information limited the comprehensiveness of our analysis.Furthermore, in terms of echocardiographic data collection, studies had indicated that parameters like tricuspid annular plane systolic dispersion and TAPSE were valuable in the diagnosis and grading of TR. Future epidemiological studies are encouraged to explore these and other potential risk factors for a more comprehensive understanding of TR.
