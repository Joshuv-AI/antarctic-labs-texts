Characterizing spatiotemporal spread of infectious diseases using ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots: application to dengue virus outbreaks
Authors: Pei-Sheng Lin, Chun-Hong Chen, Wei-Liang Liu, Tzai-Hung Wen, Yu-Chun Lu, Li‑Wei Chen, Hsiang-Yu Yuan, Yi-Hung Kung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

In epidemiology, identifying a transmission hotspot (i.e., an emerging hotspot) is important for the understanding of disease transmission as well as the development of prevention policies [1]. A transmission hotspot is typically defined as a collection of spatial units where transmission rates of an infectious disease are significantly higher than those in the surrounding areas. Particularly, targeted intervention at transmission hotspots in community-based size can be highly efficient in reducing the disease burden [2]. For convenience, in this paper, we refer to a geographic cluster in community scale as a micro-cluster. The members of micro-clusters with little within-group variation in the transmission rate are ideal targets for such interventions. Understanding the attributes of specific micro-clusters may simplify the prediction of their evolution over time. Additionally, the analysis of micro-clusters can rapidly reveal multi-dimensional patterns of event locations, providing a useful tool in the big-data era. In this article, we propose a method to identify micro-clusters and transform them into ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots to predict disease propagation.

Incidence rates of emerging infectious diseases, such as dengue virus infection, typically increase rapidly in rural areas [3]. For such diseases, characterizing the hotspots associated with community-based spatial units may help explore the risk factors for disease propagation, such as environmental changes or human–animal interactions [2]. This study was motivated, in part, by observations on dengue virus infections in Taiwan. Dengue fever, a mosquito-borne viral disease, has been flagged by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a major infectious disease of global health concern [4–5]. The dengue virus is transmitted by infected female Aedes mosquitoes. Dengue fever poses a substantial threat in southern Taiwan, which has a warm and humid climate, providing an ideal breeding ground for such mosquitoes. In Taiwan, dengue epidemics usually start in late August, in areas with both old bungalows and new apartment buildings, and subsequently spread to downtown Kaohsiung [6]. As Aedes mosquitoes typically remain within a few hundred meters of their breeding site [7], a surveillance system based on micro-cluster models could assist decision-makers in implementing appropriate policies in the early stages of disease propagation to prevent potential outbreaks.

According to the WHO [3], implementing prediction strategies for dengue transmission is a key public health measure. In this regard, geographic information system (GIS) technology is a valuable tool to visualize the spatiotemporal (ST) patterns of dengue transmission [8–11]. By modeling the distribution of infections with suitable clustering algorithms, a GIS-based surveillance system can be used to predict transmission hotspots—representing high-risk areas—and examine their evolution [2]. Transmission hotspots can be used to guide targeted interventions, such as mosquito breeding-site clearance, and to optimize resource allocation. Ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots, characterized by location, dispersion, and orientation, are particularly suited to the abovementioned predictions. For example, the degree of dispersion in the ellipse can be used to decide range of the target area for environmental cleaning measures, while its orientation may indicate the direction of disease propagation. Thus, ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots based on dynamic GIS data and micro-cluster modeling could be used to aggregate mapped information and continuously update propagation patterns [12].

Although some popular clustering algorithms, such as the density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) and kernel density estimation (KDE) methods, can also be used to classify observations for transmission hotspots, these traditional approaches could struggle to analyze data sets with varying densities (e.g., data with mix of dense clusters and sparse clusters) [13]. As can be seen from Fig. 4, this situation would often happen in the early stage of epidemics. To address this issue, in this study, we aimed to integrate a GIS-based surveillance system and data-driven models to identify and characterize transmission hotspots. We applied several machine-learning algorithms, including the Knox test for ST interaction and a quadratic tree clustering algorithm for spatial aggregation, to identify micro-clusters. For each micro-cluster, we transformed the ST coordinates of infected cases into eigen-values and eigen-vectors to visualize the potential dispersion of the disease. The proposed GIS-based surveillance system with transmission hotspot analysis enables deeper exploration of GIS surveillance data through statistical inference, revealing patterns and correlations between the disease dynamics and the surrounding environment. In this article, we introduce the statistical and computational methods used to construct ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots based on micro-clusters. Thereafter, dengue epidemic data from Kaohsiung is used to illustrate how the proposed method can be applied to explore transmission hotspots in the early stages of epidemics. Finally, we discuss the potential for future research.

Methods

Data collection

The data on the dengue epidemics in downtown Kaohsiung in 2014 and 2015 were provided by the Department of Health of the Kaohsiung City Government. The ST point data comprise geographic coordinates (according to the Taiwan geodetic datum) and notification dates for confirmed cases. In total, 13,582 and 17,607 cases were confirmed in 2014 and 2015, respectively, in 12 administrative districts. Exponential growth periods for training and validation sets

We used the following procedures to identify the exponential growth periods during the early stages of the epidemics for setting training and validation sets. For the 2014 data, we utilized a k-means clustering algorithm [14] to classify weekly numbers of cases into five periods, namely, the pre-growth, slow growth, exponential growth, plateau, and decline periods. This algorithm was implemented using the stats package in the R software. For the 2015 dengue epidemic, we adapted a change-point analysis method [15] to identify the exponential growth period using weekly numbers of cases, because the k-means clustering algorithm could not identify suitable consecutive weeks for the exponential growth period for 2015. The exponential growth periods of 2014 and 2015 comprised 2,434 and 1,082 cases, respectively. Details of the exponential growth periods are presented in the Results section.

ST thresholds by Knox tests

We estimated an ST threshold, which is defined as an ST distance such that a pair of cases with a distance less than the threshold would have strongest clustering effects. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{0}$$\end{document} denote the null hypothesis that no ST interaction exists among cases, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{1}$$\end{document} denote the alternative hypothesis that the cases are positively correlated within certain distances. The Knox test [16] is widely used to examine whether pairs of cases are more clustered than would be expected under \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{0}$$\end{document}. We conducted a series of Knox tests for various combinations of spatial distances and time lags. The p-values (adjusted using Bonferroni’s correction) were computed for all combinations, and ST threshold was chosen as the combination with the lowest p-value. (The significance level was set at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upalpha}=0.05$$\end{document}.) The surveillance package in the R software was used for Knox test.

A quadratic tree algorithm for clusters

We classified pairs of cases with distances less than the ST threshold as micro-clusters. To facilitate rapid searching for nearest neighbors, we adapted a quadratic tree algorithm [17] to systematically reduce the search space. This algorithm recursively partitions the study region to form quaternary-tree structures, reducing the computational burden [18]. Specifically, for a given query point rather than comparing it with all other points, we restricted comparisons to points within the same spatial partition. Meanwhile, the pre-selected ST-threshold was applied to determine whether two cases should be connected by an edge. Once the edges were established, all cases that were connected were grouped together into a cluster. The FNN package in the R software was used to implement the quadratic tree algorithm.

Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise

The DBSCAN method, an unsupervised algorithm, was also used to identify geographic clusters of spatial points for comparison with the proposed method. For DBSCAN, two parameters—the radius of the densest region (ε ) and the minimum number of points (p)—must be pre-assigned to achieve significant aggregation of points. In this study, the preset values were as follows: ε =100, 200, or 400 (based on flying distances of Aedes mosquitoes) and p= 3 (as an ellipse requires at least three points). The dbscan package in the R software was used to conduct the DBSCAN analysis.

Results

The 2014 dengue epidemic data were used to train the model to characterize the diffusion patterns of the 2015 dengue epidemic. Prediction was performed following the procedure shown in the flowchart (Table 1), in which “past data” refers to the 2014 data. The pre-determined ST threshold, estimated using the 2014 dengue data, was used to classify the 2015 dengue data points into micro-clusters. Elliptical regions were computed based on the geographic coordinates of the cases within the micro-clusters. For model validation, these results were compared with those obtained using DBSCAN. Further details are presented below.

We first used weekly data to identify the early stages (i.e., exponential growth periods) of the 2014 and 2015 dengue epidemics. The k-means clustering algorithm was applied with an elbow criterion to characterize the 2014 epidemic into five periods: pre-growth, slow growth, exponential growth, plateau, and decline (Fig. 1; the classification results were presented in Table A5 in the Supplementary Materials). The exponential growth period for the 2014 epidemic was from weeks 31 to 39, for which the weekly numbers of reported cases were 106, 143, 138, 225, 217, 270, 374, 413, and 548, respectively. For the 2015 data, we computed Z-scores based on changes in the weekly numbers of cases [15] to identify the exponential growth period. (Applying the k-means clustering algorithm failed to classify consecutive weeks as an exponential growth period for the 2015 epidemic.) Fig. 2 depicts a time series of the weekly numbers of dengue cases in 2015. The exponential growth period (weeks 32 to 37) is framed in red (weekly Z-scores used in the change-point analysis are presented in Table A6 in the Supplementary Materials). Notably, the exponential growth periods for 2014 and 2015 were almost the same.

For model calibration (step 2 in Table 1), the ST threshold was estimated using data from the exponential growth period of 2014 (the ST threshold estimated using all of the 2014 data was too large to use for micro-clustering). Specifically, for confirmed cases in the 2014 exponential growth period, we computed p-values via the Knox test under various combinations of geographic distances (d = 50, 100, 150, or 200 m) and time lags (t = 7 or 14 days). In total, 2,960,961 pairs of cases were evaluated using Knox testing. Figure 3 presents a heat map of the Bonferroni-corrected p-values. Although all the combinations were significant, the lowest p-value (p \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=4\times {10}^{-233}$$\end{document}) was calculated for the combination of 200 m and 14 days (i.e., pairs of cases with spatial distances within 200 m and time lags within 14 days had the most significant clustering effect). Therefore, for the 2015 dengue data, we grouped pairs of cases with ST distances below this threshold into micro-clusters using the quadratic tree algorithm. The grouping procedure was sequentially applied to each week of 2015, although we show only the results for the exponential growth period of 2015 in the main text.

Discussion

Applying ST analysis to GIS data can help explore transmission hotspots for infectious diseases. In this study, we first combined the Knox test (with p-values corrected by Bonferroni’s correction) and quadratic trees (as one of machine-learning clustering algorithms) to construct clusters. Observations (or cases) in the clusters were then expressed as the mathematical model in Eq. (1) by decomposing geographic coordinates into eigen-values and eigen-vectors, which were used to construct ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots. These ellipses correspond to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100\left(1-\alpha \right)\mathrm{\%}$$\end{document} confidence regions in statistical testing, thus providing a theoretical foundation for their validation through probability theory.

The primary purpose of this study is to conduct rapid detection and geometric characterization for early-stage local hotspots in surveillance systems. The GIS-based surveillance system in combination with ellipse-shaped transmission hotspots enables visualization of the evolution of the dispersion area, which may lead to timely impact assessment for possible epidemics. The proposed method is expected to be useful during the time when outbreak start to spread exponentially. At this stage, the constructed ellipses help identify the target region and prioritize the direction for disease control. For example, in Fig. 4, the growing ellipse (bolded black color) generally showed the same directions. The boundary near longer axis indicates a larger at-risk area, which may require larger resources for control than shorter axis during this period. On the other hand, when the number of infected cases becomes very large, the resulting cluster size may expand excessively, limiting the ability of the ellipse representation to provide guidance for control policies.

We conducted simulations with two scenarios to: (i) compare performance of identification for ST clusters between the proposed method and the DBSCAN algorithm, and (ii) evaluate whether the Knox test is suitable for choice of the threshold in Fig. 3. Details for the simulation study can be seen in “Simulation Studies” of the Supplementary Material. By the simulation results shown in Table A3 and A4 of the Supplementary Material, we found that, when the numbers of ST points in the clusters were less than the number of “random” points, the proposed method was significantly better than the DBSCAN algorithm in identification of clusters. This result agreed with other research works [13], indicating that the DBSCAN algorithm could struggle to properly cluster observations for data with mix of hot spots. We also learned from Table A1 and A2 of the Supplementary Material that the Knox test used in Fig. 3 had significantly better performance than the other method in choice of suitable thresholds.

Although our ellipse-shaped transmission hotspot was applied to dengue fever data, the computational algorithm can easily be adapted for application to other infectious diseases. In rural areas in particular, emerging infectious diseases typically occur in nonrandom clusters. Our ellipse-shaped micro-clustering method may facilitate the early control of infectious diseases with different transmission routes, thus helping public health departments to implement adequate control measures.

While DBSCAN is commonly used to characterize clustering patterns, this approach generally lacks a well-defined threshold for setting the distance ε, one of the important cluster parameters. Hence results can vary greatly depending on the chosen values, limiting its use by prevention units to devise a control strategy for infectious diseases. Similar to the DBSCAN algorithm, our proposed method uses the quadratic tree algorithm to partition study regions to cluster observations with similar characteristics. Nevertheless, our proposed method takes several further steps for statistical validation. For example, based on the testing hypothesis framework, the Knox test can pre-determine a clustering parameter by p-values. Additionally, we use eigenvalues and eigenvectors to characterize elliptical transmission hotspots, which is the same as confidence regions (a confidence interval in one-dimensional spaces) in multivariate analysis of statistics.

In our data analysis, under the same parameter settings, the hotspots identified using DBSCAN were similar to those generated using the ellipse-shaped method, if clusters were sufficiently isolated. This result is not surprising, as these methods both incorporate a nearest-neighbor algorithm to group observations. Further analysis (Fig. A2 in the Supplementary Material) shows that DBSCAN tended to merge nearby hotspots, allowing the resulting shapes more irregular and hence less straightforward to interpret (see, e.g., cluster C2 in week 34 and cluster C3 in week 37 in Fig. 4). In contrast, the ellipse-shaped representation provides clearer orientation of diffusion patterns and more intuitive visualization of the attributes of confirmed cases within each hotspot. This approach therefore enhances the ability to track and interpret the evolution of individual clusters over time.

We limited the ST threshold to just two weeks and 200 m in this study. From the perspective of dengue control, identifying transmission hotspots based on micro-clusters can help decision makers to implement more efficient environmental cleaning measures. The ST threshold used in this paper matches the commonly recognized infection period and range of Aedes mosquitoes. So, at the micro-cluster scale, dengue transmission may be closely related to localized mosquito-human interaction and short-range activity patterns than to broader inter-district mobility flows.

In the early stage of epidemics, the immediate analytical priority is to identify whether statistically significant local aggregation has emerged. Although human mobility may contribute to cluster formation, especially for transmission across neighborhoods or larger urban areas, its role cannot be readily distinguished from other localized transmission processes without explicit movement or activity-space data [19]. How to incorporate more human mobility and city topology, such as the transportation networks and river barriers, into the current framework is left for future research.

The proposed ellipse-shaped method has several limitations. First, although micro-cluster analysis is useful in the early stages of epidemics, it would be difficult to apply when the number of cases increases dramatically. In this study, the number of ellipse-shaped clusters increased substantially from week 38 onward in 2015 (Fig. A1 in the Supplementary Material), rendering them infeasible for the prediction of diffusion patterns. Second, this method does not incorporate the impacts of risk factors, such as urbanization, meteorological variables and human mobility. An integrated surveillance system, with different layers of risk factors, can enable the visualization of the dynamic patterns on a risk map via machine-learning algorithms. This would help public health departments to improve resource-allocation planning for high-risk areas, warranting future research.

Conclusion

This study provides statistical and computational methods that strengthen the traditional clustering application such as DBSCAN for emerging infectious diseases. In addition, the constructed ellipse‑shaped transmission hotspots of dengue fever allow public health departments to implement appropriate vector control measures.
