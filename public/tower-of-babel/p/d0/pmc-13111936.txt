Risk Factors for Adverse Circulatory and Respiratory Events in Patients Undergoing Esophageal Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection Under Dexmedetomidine-Based Sedation
Authors: Kenshi Matsuno, Hideaki Miyamoto, Sayoko Tayama, Kotaro Waki, Akira Yamasaki, Yoki Furuta, Ryosuke Gushima, Hideaki Naoe, Yasuhito Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsSufficient sedation is important when performing an endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for an esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and dexmedetomidine (DEX) is being increasingly used. ESD is often performed in elderly patients and those with comorbidities. Therefore, adverse events (AEs) in the circulatory and respiratory systems remain a clinical concern. Despite this, limited data exist on these AEs, so this study was conducted to investigate this issue.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 526 patients who underwent ESD for ESCC under DEX-based sedation from 2016 to 2023. The study assessed the incidence of AEs in circulatory and respiratory systems, as well as the risk factors associated with these events. Various clinical factors, including the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), were analyzed as candidates.ResultsCirculatory AEs occurred in 55 cases (10.5%), including bradycardia (7.2%) and hypotension (4.2%). Univariate and multivariate analyses revealed significant associations of lower PNI (<45) and prolonged procedure time with circulatory AEs (p=0.023 and p=0.008, respectively). Respiratory AEs occurred in 12 cases (2.3%), including respiratory depression (1.0 %) and post-ESD pneumonia (1.5 %) with one fatal case (0.2 %). An analysis of respiratory AEs showed that the elderly (≥80 years) and lower PNI were significant in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis (both p=0.07).ConclusionsWhen performing ESD for ESCC under DEX-based sedation, special caution is needed for patients with lower PNI and the elderly, who are more likely to experience circulatory or respiratory complications.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is widely accepted in Japan as a minimally invasive and curative treatment for superficial esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).1-4 It enables en bloc resection regardless of the tumor size but requires precise endoscopic manipulation because of the narrow operative field and thin esophageal wall. Therefore, maintaining an appropriate depth of sedation is essential for safety and procedural success.5

Benzodiazepines are commonly used for sedation in endoscopic procedures, but their efficacy may be reduced in patients with a history of heavy alcohol consumption, which is frequent among ESCC cases.6 Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a highly selective α2-adrenoreceptor agonist, provides sedation and analgesia without significant respiratory depression, making it well-suited for non-intubated sedation in gastrointestinal ESD.7-12 Previous studies have confirmed its effectiveness in upper GI endoscopy, including esophageal ESD.

Nevertheless, DEX can cause hemodynamic effects such as bradycardia and hypotension.13 Despite its increasing use, particularly in elderly patients or those with comorbidities, limited evidence exists on the safety profile of DEX-based sedation in esophageal ESD. In particular, few studies have examined the risk factors for circulatory and respiratory adverse events (AEs) under this protocol. Therefore, a retrospective analysis was conducted to evaluate the incidence of sedation-related AEs in esophageal ESD with DEX and identify the clinical factors associated with the increased risk.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

1. Patients

This was a single-institution, retrospective study conducted at Kumamoto University Hospital in Japan. This study included patients who underwent ESD for ESCC using DEX for sedation from January 2016 to December 2023. Consent for ESD was obtained from all participants, and the Institutional Review Board of Kumamoto University Hospital approved the study protocol (No. 2455). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2. Medication and monitoring

In the authors’ institute, DEX has generally been used in combination with midazolam (MDZ) and pethidine hydrochloride for sedation during esophageal ESD since January 2016. The target sedation depth was set for deep sedation based on the Ramsay sedation score of 5–6.14 According to the drug package insert, DEX was administered via a 6 μg/kg/h infusion for 10 min as a loading dose followed by a 0.2–0.5 μg/kg/h continuous infusion. The loading doses of MDZ and pethidine hydrochloride were 0.01–0.03 mg/kg and 17.5–35 mg, respectively. An additional 0.5 mg of MDZ or 17.5 μg of pethidine hydrochloride was administered if the target sedation depth was not reached during the procedure. A non-anesthesiologist assistant doctor adjusted each drug to achieve the required level of sedation.

During the procedure, the blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO2), end-tidal carbon dioxide, and the electrocardiogram were monitored, with BP measured every 5 min. The DEX dose was decreased or discontinued if the systolic BP decreased to <80 mmHg, and 2 mg of etilefrine hydrochloride was administered. Atropine sulfate hydrate was administered, and the DEX dose was decreased or discontinued if the HR decreased to <40 beats per minute. Oxygen was administered via nasal cannula when the oxygen saturation fell below 90%. The ESD procedure was discontinued if the BP, HR, and SpO2 did not stabilize, and antagonists (naloxone, flumazenil) were administered; the operator made the decision.

3. ESD procedure

ESD was performed as previously described,1,2 and the indications for ESD were based on the Japanese Guidelines.15-17 The tumor margin was confirmed using a high-definition endoscope (GIF-H260Z, H290Z, XZ1200; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) with magnified narrow-band imaging followed by Lugol staining. A single-channel endoscope (GIF-Q260J, H290T; Olympus) was used throughout the procedure, from injection to completed dissection, with a transparent hood mounted on the endoscope tip. A hyaluronic acid solution (MucoUp®; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) with indigo carmine was used for the subepithelial injection. For marking, incision, and dissection, electrosurgical knives such as the Dualknife (KD-650 L; Olympus), the Clutch Cutter (DP2618DT; FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), the ProKnife (ORISE ProKnife; Boston Scientific), or the Endosaber (Sumitomo Bakelite, Tokyo, Japan) were used as necessary. The electrosurgical unit VIO300D (Erbe, Tübingen, Germany) and VIO3 (Erbe) were used for these procedures. The overtube was generally not used.

At the authors’ institution, antibiotics are not administered routinely before and after ESD. Chest X-rays or computed tomography (CT) scans were not performed routinely the day after ESD but were obtained as needed, such as when a fever was observed.

4. Definition

The AEs of the circulatory system were defined as low blood pressure (systolic BP <80 mmHg) or bradycardia (HR <40 beats per minute) that required a discontinuation of DEX or administration of circulatory agonists (etilefrine hydrochloride, atropine sulfate hydrate). AEs of the respiratory system were also defined as respiratory depression (SpO2 <90%) requiring discontinuation of DEX, or the incident occurrence of post-ESD pneumonia with infiltrates on post-ESD chest X-rays or CT that were not observed preoperatively. The largest lesion was designated as the representative lesion when multiple lesions were resected. The procedure time was defined as the time from insertion to removal of the endoscope. Perforation was defined as a visible hole in the esophageal wall recognized during the procedure or free air detected by chest CT after the procedure. Postoperative bleeding was defined as bleeding with hematemesis or melena that required urgent endoscopy for hemostasis. A R0 resection was defined as an en bloc resection with tumor-free lateral and vertical margins. Endoscopists with at least 10 years’ experience in endoscopic therapy and who had performed >50 cases of esophageal ESD were defined as expert endoscopists.

5. Outcomes

The incidence of perioperative complications during ESD was investigated with a particular focus on AEs involving the circulatory and respiratory systems. In addition, this study examined the factors associated with the occurrence of these complications. The candidate factors for AEs included the following: age ≥80 years (elderly), body mass index (BMI) <18.5, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) ≥3, prognostic nutritional index (PNI) <45, total drug dose (MDZ and pethidine hydrochloride), the operator (expert or non-expert), the procedure time, and lesion-specific variables (size, circumference and location). The third quartile was used as the cutoff value for the total drug dose, procedure time, and lesion size.

6. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with R version 3.3.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). The continuous variables are presented as the mean and standard deviation or the median and range, as appropriate for their distributions. The clinical outcomes were analyzed using a Mann–Whitney U test, Student’s t-test, Fisher’s exact test, or chi-square test, as appropriate. Multiple logistic regression was used for multivariate analysis. The probability values for statistical tests were two-tailed, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Variables that were statistically significant in univariate analysis were then entered into the multivariate model.

RESULTS

Six hundred and twenty-nine patients with ESCC underwent ESD at the authors’ institute between January 2016 and December 2023. Of these, 37 cases under general anesthesia and 66 cases without DEX were excluded, leaving 526 cases for analysis. General anesthesia was used primarily for cervical esophageal lesions or near-circumferential/circumferential lesions. The decision to avoid DEX in non-general anesthesia cases was typically based on concerns about bradycardia because of electrocardiogram abnormalities (e.g., sinus bradycardia, atrioventricular block). Fig. 1 presents the study flow chart.

Table 1 lists the characteristics of all patients and lesions. The median age of the cases was 70 years (range 42–90 years); 13.5% were elderly cases (≥80 years), and 87.8% were male. The median BMI, median PNI, and median CCI were 22.2 (range 11.5–35.3), 50.0 (range 27.9–65.4), and 2 (range 0–8), respectively. Approximately 13.5%, 16.4%, and 25.3% of patients had a lower BMI (<18.5), a lower PNI (<45), and a higher CCI (≥3), respectively. Table 1 lists the technical outcomes. Briefly, the median procedure time was 70 minutes. The en bloc resection rate was 99.6%, and the R0 resection rate was 90.0%. No perforation cases occurred, and three cases of postoperative bleeding (0.6%) were encountered as complications related to the resection procedure. The third quartile was set as the cutoff value, “high dose use of MDZ” for cases using 4 mg or more of MDZ, “high dose use of pethidine” for cases using 100 mg or more of pethidine, “prolonged procedure time” for procedure times longer than 102 minutes, and “larger lesion size” for lesion length diameters greater than 30 mm.

AEs of the circulatory system occurred in 55 cases (10.5%), and Table 2 lists the details (with overlap): bradycardia in 38 cases (7.2%), and low blood pressure in 22 cases (4.2%). Circulatory agonists (etilefrine hydrochloride and atropine sulfate) were administered in 25 cases (4.8%), and DEX was discontinued in 48 cases (9.1%). In one case (0.2%), the ESD procedure was discontinued because of low blood pressure and respiratory depression. Table 3 lists the results of univariate and multivariate analyses of the factors associated with AEs of the circulatory system. Univariate analysis revealed a lower PNI (<45) and prolonged procedure time to be significantly associated with AEs of the circulatory system (p=0.032, p=0.013, respectively), and multivariate analysis also confirmed these associations (p=0.023, p=0.008, respectively). The case in which ESD was discontinued because of AEs also had a lower PNI. No significant associations were found for other factors, including age, BMI, CCI, total drug dose (MDZ and pethidine), the operator (expert or non-expert), and lesion-specific variables (size, circumference, and location).

The AEs of the respiratory system occurred in 12 cases (2.3%), and Table 2 presents the details (with overlap): respiratory depression in five cases (1.3%), and post-ESD pneumonia in eight cases (1.5%). Most patients with post-ESD pneumonia recovered after a few days of antibiotic therapy, but one case (0.2%) resulted in a fatal outcome. Table 4 lists the results of univariate and multivariate analyses of the factors associated with AEs of the respiratory system. Although lower PNI (<45) and age ≥80 years were significant in univariate analysis (p=0.032, p=0.036, respectively), neither remained significant in multivariate analysis (both p=0.07). Other factors were not significantly associated with AEs of the respiratory system.

DISCUSSION

This study provides important insights into the safety of esophageal ESD performed under a DEX-based sedation protocol using DEX, MDZ, and pethidine hydrochloride. The observed AEs may reflect the combined effects of these agents rather than DEX alone, but the median doses of MDZ and pethidine hydrochloride were relatively low. By focusing on AEs under this sedation protocol, lower PNI and prolonged procedure time significantly increase the risk of hemodynamic instability. Furthermore, elderly patients and lower PNI were significantly associated with AEs of the respiratory system, but the latter associations were observed only in univariate analyses because of the limited number of events. These findings highlight the clinical significance of assessing PNI as a simple and practical marker for identifying high-risk patients undergoing esophageal ESD.

Several studies have reported the association of lower PNI and poor prognosis of gastrointestinal malignancies, including superficial ESCC.18-20 The PNI was originally designed to evaluate the nutritional status of patients undergoing surgical treatment for gastrointestinal diseases.21 Onodera proposed the modified PNI using a simpler index,22 which was calculated using the serum albumin level and lymphocyte count in the peripheral blood. A cutoff value of 45 is commonly used,19,20 and this threshold was applied to define a lower PNI in the present study. A lower PNI may be linked to hemodynamic instability through mechanisms such as hypoalbuminemia, which reduces the colloid osmotic pressure and drug-binding capacity, lymphopenia reflecting impaired immunity, and malnutrition limiting skeletal muscle reserves. In patients with low PNI, circulatory instability may be more likely because of the procedural stress from esophageal ESD, and the circulatory effects of DEX may be more pronounced in these patients. The association between low PNI and circulatory AEs should be interpreted as resulting from the combined effects of patient frailty, procedural stress, and sedation-related hemodynamic changes, rather than as DEX-specific toxicity. Similarly, higher CCI (≥3) has been identified as a predictor of a poor prognosis in superficial ESCC patients.20 PNI and CCI are simple indicators that do not require special tests.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to examine the risk factors for AEs of the circulatory and respiratory systems during gastrointestinal ESD, making it novel. A prospective study using DEX for sedation during esophageal ESD revealed more circulatory side effects: bradycardia 35% and hypotension 37%.10 In contrast, the incidence of circulatory events in the present study was lower, likely because AEs were defined as events requiring intervention, such as discontinuation of DEX or administration of circulatory agonists, which reflects their clinical significance. Therefore, a direct comparison of AEs rates across studies should be interpreted with caution. Although the total DEX dose was not analyzed because of missing values, DEX was administered continuously according to the package insert, which correlates with the procedure time and body weight. This may explain why the prolonged procedure time was associated with the higher incidence of circulatory AEs. Identifying patients at high risk of circulatory instability in advance could enable preventive measures, such as careful adjustment of the DEX dosages and preparation of circulatory agonists.

Although the incidence of post-ESD pneumonia is low, it can be fatal in patients with significant comorbidities. Several studies have investigated post-esophageal ESD pneumonia. A recent report summarizing multicenter analysis of complications showed a 1.8% incidence of post-ESD pneumonia,23 which aligns with these findings. In that report, elderly patients (>80 years) were significantly associated with post-ESD pneumonia. Another study identified a low pharyngeal/esophageal pressure associated with sarcopenia as a risk factor for post-ESD pneumonia.24 Nevertheless, measuring these pressures requires specialized equipment, and the diagnosis of sarcopenia requires some work. In contrast, the lower PNI (<45) identified in this study is an easily measurable marker that can predict post-ESD pneumonia in routine clinical practice. Anticipating high-risk patients in advance enables preventive measures. For example, a previous multicenter study reported that traction was inversely associated with post-ESD pneumonia,23 and that attaching a liquid-suction catheter to the endoscope was effective in preventing post-ESD pneumonia.25 Although perioperative antibiotic therapy has not been reported to be effective in preventing post-ESD pneumonia,26 it may be beneficial in high-risk patients. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed the efficacy of ultrashort-acting sedatives, such as propofol and remimazolam,27,28 during endoscopic procedures. The rapid awakening associated with these agents may reduce the risk of post-ESD pneumonia, even though caution is needed when used by non-anesthesiologists.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis of procedures carried out at a single center. Second, cases in which DEX was not used were excluded, potentially introducing selection bias. Third, the association between the cumulative DEX dose and AEs could not be analyzed because of substantial missing data. As the cumulative DEX dose is a critical confounding factor when interpreting sedation-related AEs, this represents a major limitation of the present study and may limit the interpretation of the association between a prolonged procedure time and circulatory AEs. Fourth, post-treatment chest X-rays or CT scans were not performed routinely in this study. Therefore, asymptomatic or mild cases of post-ESD pneumonia may have been overlooked, and the true incidence of respiratory AEs could have been underestimated. Fifth, as no established risk factors have been reported, variables significant in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. Nevertheless, for respiratory AEs, the limited number of cases may have led to overfitting. Sixth, the continuous variables were categorized using the third quartile as the cutoff, intended to capture relatively higher exposure within the study population, but this may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Despite these limitations, this study had the strength to identify the easily measurable risk factors for AEs of the circulatory and respiratory systems using a large number of ESCC cases.

In conclusion, esophageal ESD using DEX sedation is generally safe, but careful monitoring is warranted in patients with lower PNI and elderly patients because they are more vulnerable to DEX-associated circulatory and respiratory complications. These findings support the importance of individualized sedation strategies and preoperative risk assessment in DEX-sedated endoscopic procedures.
