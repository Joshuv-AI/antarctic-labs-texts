Targeting the PPAR-γ-ABC transporter axis ameliorates secondary pulmonary alveolar proteinosis induced by indium oxide nanoparticles
Authors: Soyeon Jeon, Gyuri Kim, Wan-Seob Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare lung disease with primary (usually autoimmune) and secondary forms. Unlike the autoimmune type, which can be treated with cytokine therapy or whole-lung lavage, the secondary form associated with occupational particulate exposure, such as indium compounds, has no established therapy and remains challenging to treat.ResultsPAP was experimentally induced in rats through intratracheal exposure to indium oxide nanoparticles (In2O3 NPs). To assess potential therapeutic interventions, we administered four pharmacological agents: aspirin and naproxen, which are expected to attenuate inflammation, and pioglitazone and indomethacin, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)-γ agonists anticipated to restore impaired surfactant homeostasis. The efficacy of their amelioration of In2O3 NP-induced PAP and the underlying mechanisms were evaluated by bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis, histopathology, and gene expression profiling. Treatment with pioglitazone and indomethacin markedly attenuated In2O3 NP–induced PAP by reducing foamy macrophage accumulation and restoring the expression of PPAR-γ and its downstream ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, such as ABCG1 and ABCG4. In contrast, aspirin and naproxen only slightly decreased neutrophil infiltration. Among the tested agents, indomethacin showed relatively pronounced effects on several PAP-related endpoints, suggesting its potential to improve macrophage lipid handling and surfactant balance.ConclusionTargeting PPAR-γ–dependent macrophage lipid regulation may serve to attenuate In2O3 NP-induced secondary PAP-like pathology by reducing foamy macrophage accumulation and improving surfactant clearance. Background

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare lung disease characterized by the abnormal accumulation of surfactant within the alveoli, primarily due to defective clearance by alveolar macrophages, leading to impaired gas exchange and dyspnea [1, 2]. It is broadly classified into primary and secondary forms [3]. Primary PAP comprises autoimmune and congenital types [1]. The autoimmune form, which is most common, results from the production of neutralizing autoantibodies against granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [1]. In contrast, the congenital form is associated with mutations in GM-CSF receptor signaling or surfactant-related proteins [4]. These primary cases are driven by disrupted GM-CSF-dependent macrophage function and/or inherited defects in surfactant clearance [1, 4]. In contrast, secondary PAP develops without GM-CSF autoantibody involvement and arises from acquired dysfunction of alveolar macrophages due to hematologic disorders, infections, or occupational exposure to inhaled particles such as silica and aluminum dust [4–6].

Complementing this clinical background, preclinical studies have shown that indium oxide nanoparticles (In2O3 NPs) induce persistent PAP-like pathology in rats, characterized by surfactant accumulation and foamy alveolar macrophages [7, 8]. Mechanistically, these PAP-like responses are associated with prolonged biopersistence in the lung and disruption of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ)/liver X receptor (LXR) signaling, as well as downstream ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, particularly ABCG1 and ABCG4, thereby impairing phospholipid efflux and recycling [7, 9]. In addition, in vitro overload studies in rat and human alveolar macrophages support the relevance of macrophage dysfunction under high particle burden [10]. Despite different initiating causes, clinical PAP and NP-induced PAP-like pathology share key features, including the accumulation of foamy macrophages and surfactant overload in the alveoli.

NPs possess diverse physicochemical properties (i.e., size, shape, surface charge/chemistry, composition, and solubility) that influence their biological effects in the lung [10]. Among these properties, poorly soluble particles with high surface reactivity, pronounced toxicity toward alveolar type I epithelial cells (whose death leads to compensatory proliferation of type II cells), and the capacity to impair PPAR-γ–dependent phospholipid efflux in alveolar macrophages have been shown to induce a PAP-like phenotype characterized by the accumulation of foamy macrophages and surfactant overload [9, 11, 12]. Representative examples include nickel oxide, indium oxide, and certain rare-earth oxide nanoparticles [8, 9]. In contrast, highly soluble particles such as zinc oxide and copper oxide dissolve rapidly into ions and are cleared, and have not been shown to cause PAP-like responses under similar exposure conditions [8]. This distinction suggests that secondary PAP is driven not only by particle insolubility, but also by the combined effects of particle persistence, surface reactivity, and disruption of macrophage lipid-handling pathways [6, 13, 14]. Therefore, promoting the rapid clearance of NPs and restoring normal alveolar macrophage function are critical therapeutic strategies for NP-induced PAP [15].

Current treatments for PAP, such as whole-lung lavage (WLL) and inhaled GM-CSF, are mainly used in autoimmune forms but remain limited by invasiveness, variable efficacy, and the need for repeated interventions [15–17]. Pharmacological alternatives that provide sustained benefit with fewer risks are therefore highly desirable. PPAR-γ agonists such as pioglitazone have been experimentally applied to autoimmune PAP, with case reports suggesting clinical benefit; however, their therapeutic use remains preliminary and not established [18]. Importantly, no effective pharmacological strategies have been identified for secondary PAP, particularly that induced by inhaled NPs, despite its distinct pathogenesis involving disruption of PPAR-γ signaling and ABC transporter–mediated lipid efflux in alveolar macrophages [6, 19]. In this context, we investigated the therapeutic potential of pioglitazone and indomethacin (both are PPAR-γ agonists) in a rat model of indium oxide nanoparticle (In2O3 NP)-induced PAP. To provide comparative insight, we also evaluated two conventional non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin and naproxen, within the same experimental framework.

Methods

Physicochemical properties of In2O3 NPs

In2O3 NPs were purchased from Nanostructured & Amorphous Materials Inc. (NanoAmor, Houston, TX, USA). The physicochemical properties, including primary size, shape, hydrodynamic size, and zeta potential, were characterized. The primary size and shape of NPs were determined by field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The primary size was calculated by counting 300 particles. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of test NPs were measured under various dispersion conditions, including distilled water (DW), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and PBS supplemented with 3% heat-inactivated alloantigenic rat serum, using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The absence of endotoxin contamination in the highest dose (50 µg/rat) of In2O3 NPs was verified using the limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA).

Preparation of In2O3 NP suspension and rationale for dose selection

We dispersed the In2O3 NPs in PBS using heat-inactivated alloantigenic serum (i.e., Wistar rat) according to our previously described method [20]. Briefly, a stock suspension of In2O3 NPs was prepared in DW at 1 mg/mL and sonicated for 10 min using a bath sonicator (Saehan-Sonic; Seoul, Korea). Then, serum was added to the stock suspension at 3% of the final working volume to ensure proper dispersion, and the mixture was sonicated for 10 min using a bath sonicator (Saehan-Sonic). Finally, the mixture was diluted with PBS to the desired working concentration and sonicated for 10 min. Based on our previous findings that a dose of 7.5 cm2/rat (50 µg/rat) of In2O3 NPs was sufficient to induce PAP [7], this dose was selected for the present study. The administered mass was estimated using the Multiple-Path Particle Dosimetry (MPPD) model (version 3.01). For the simulation, the hydrodynamic size of the test particles (0.25 μm) was applied as the aerodynamic diameter, the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) recommended exposure limit (REL) for indium (0.1 mg/m3) was set as the aerosol concentration [21], and an exposure duration of 6 h/day was used. Under these conditions, the model predicted that the instilled dose corresponded to the alveolar retained burden accumulated over approximately 62 days of nose-only inhalation. Therefore, the selected dose was considered appropriate for modeling lung burdens relevant to occupational exposure scenarios.

Induction of PAP by intratracheal instillation of In2O3 NPs in rats

Six-week-old female Wistar rat was purchased from Orient Bio (Gyeonggi-do, Korea) and housed under controlled conditions (humidity: 40–60%, temperature: 21–23 °C, and a 12-h light/dark cycle). The animals were provided with standard laboratory chow and water ad libitum throughout the study. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Dong-A University (approval No. DIACUC-23-09). The rats were randomly allocated to each group, with five animals per group. The rats used in this study were acclimated for 7 days prior to experimentation. To induce PAP by In2O3 NPs, we conducted intratracheal instillation using the method previously described [20]. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized using a rodent anesthesia system (VetEquip, Pleasanton, CA, USA) with isoflurane. Then, a 16-gauge plastic catheter was inserted into the trachea, and 500 µL of In2O3 NP suspension was instilled.

Study design for preventive and therapeutic evaluation in In2O3-induced PAP

This study was designed to evaluate both the preventive and therapeutic potential of candidate drugs against In2O3 NP-induced PAP. Our previous study suggested that PAP induced by In2O3 NPs began approximately 3 days after exposure and continued to worsen for more than 6 months [7]. Based on these findings, day 28 post-exposure was selected in this study as the reference point for fully established PAP. The drugs tested in this study for the prevention or treatment of In2O3-induced PAP included two PPAR-γ agonists (pioglitazone and indomethacin) and two NSAIDs (aspirin and naproxen). Notably, indomethacin, although an NSAID, was separately considered due to its known activity as a PPAR-γ agonist [22]. All drugs were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA). To evaluate the preventive effects, the test drugs were administered by oral gavage twice daily, starting 2 days before In2O3 NP exposure and continuing for 28 days during the period of PAP development. To evaluate therapeutic effects, the drugs were administered by oral gavage twice daily from day 28 post-exposure (when PAP was fully established) and continued for 28 days. Thus, the treatment duration was similar in both experiments. However, in the preventive protocol, drugs were administered during disease progression, whereas in the therapeutic protocol, they were administered after disease establishment, during the period when PAP persists for several months. The drugs for oral gavage were prepared in PBS with 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which was also used as the vehicle control. The doses of drugs (i.e., aspirin and naproxen: 20 mg/kg; pioglitazone: 10 mg/kg; indomethacin: 1 mg/kg) were selected based on previous studies that demonstrated no toxicity, taking into account the half-lives of the drugs [23–25].

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis

Twenty-four hours after the last drug administration, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane using a rodent anesthesia system (VetEquip) and euthanized by abdominal vein incision. The thoracic cavity of the rat was opened, and the trachea was tied to an 18-gauge stainless steel catheter using an elastic thread. Then, 8 mL of cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) was instilled and gently massaged to collect. The BALF collection was repeated a total of 4 times. The BALF was centrifuged at 875 × g for 5 min, and then the cells were resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 (Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Corning). The first BALF was separately collected for measurements of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total protein, cytokines, and phospholipids.

The levels of LDH and total protein were measured by the LDH assay kit (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) and bicinchoninic acid kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. The levels of cytokines, including GM-CSF, interleukin (IL)-1β, cytokine-induced neutrophil chemoattractant (CINC)-3, monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, were measured using a DuoSet ELISA assay kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The levels of phospholipids were measured using a phospholipid assay kit (Bioassay Systems, Hayward, CA, USA). The cell pellets from 4 lavages were pooled and used for differential cell counts. The number of total cells was counted using a NucleoCounter (Chemometec, Allerød, Denmark). The 4 × 104 cells were attached to the slide glass using a cytospin (Hanil, Seoul, Korea) and then stained with Diff-Quik staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Differential cell counts were performed on BALF cells by randomly selecting 3–4 microscopic fields per slide, and approximately 300–350 cells were counted under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) according to morphological criteria. All counts were performed in a blinded manner. Moreover, cytospin slides from the therapeutic potential study were additionally examined by dark-field microscopy to evaluate particle-like signal localization in alveolar macrophages.

Histopathological analysis

The lung and liver were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and processed for paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The lung sections were also stained using Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) to evaluate the accumulation of PAS-positive intra-alveolar material associated with PAP-like change. For histopathological evaluation, tissues were collected from one animal per group. The histopathological sections were evaluated under blinded conditions and in a randomized order. The left lung and the right superior lobe were examined as representative lung regions, and the left lateral lobe of the liver was examined for hepatic evaluation. Representative sections from these regions were qualitatively evaluated for histopathological changes.

Real-time PCR analysis of alveolar macrophages isolated from BALF

Gene expression levels of ABC transporters and PPAR-γ in alveolar macrophages isolated from BALF were analyzed, as these genes were identified in our previous study as key biomarkers involved in the development of PAP [9]. After excluding the portion of BALF cells used for cytological analysis, the remaining cells were seeded into 6-well culture plates (Life Science, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) and incubated for 2–4 h in a CO2 incubator, allowing alveolar macrophages to adhere to the plate surface. The cells were then washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, and only the adherent cells were visually confirmed to remain. The adherent cells were treated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems) with gene-specific probes for ABCA1 (assay ID: Rn00710172_m1), ABCB4 (assay ID: Rn01529224_m1), ABCG1 (assay ID: Rn00585262_m1), ABCG4 (assay ID: Rn01421889_m1), and PPAR-γ (assay ID: Rn00440945_m1), and β-actin (assay ID: Rn00667869_m1) as the reference gene. Reactions were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol using 10 ng/µL cDNA, TaqMan probes, and TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and run for 40 cycles on a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1; La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical analysis, data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. Group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess differences between multiple groups. Spearman correlation was performed to examine the relationship between lung inflammation parameters and PAP induction, using data pooled from all experimental groups. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The qPCR data shown in the figures are presented as log₂-transformed values. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physicochemical properties of In2O3 NPs

FE-TEM and SEM analysis revealed that the In2O3 NPs were predominantly spherical in shape (Fig. 1A and B), with an average primary diameter of 60.2 ± 16.6 nm and a narrow, unimodal size distribution (Fig. 1C). Because NPs needed to be well-dispersed in physiological media for in vivo administration, their dispersion stability was evaluated under different dispersion conditions. When dispersed in DW, the In2O3 NPs exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 800 nm, indicating partial aggregation (Fig. 1D). In PBS, severe agglomeration was observed, resulting in an increased average size of 1246.6 ± 356.2 nm (Fig. 1E). Such aggregation could interfere with evaluating the effects of nanoscale particle effects; therefore, the particles were further dispersed in PBS containing 3% heat-inactivated rat serum. Under this condition, the hydrodynamic size decreased to 251.9 ± 2.6 nm, and the size distribution data showed a narrow, unimodal pattern, indicating markedly improved dispersion stability (Fig. 1F). The zeta potential values were − 5 mV in DW, − 29 mV in PBS, and − 12 mV in PBS with rat serum (Fig. 1G). Endotoxin contamination was not detected in the suspension of In2O3 NPs prepared for in vivo administration (data not shown).

Activation of PPAR-γ signaling attenuates the progression of In2O3 NP–induced PAP

To evaluate whether pharmacological activation of PPAR-γ could prevent or inhibit the development of PAP induced by In2O3 NPs, rats were administered the test drugs by oral gavage for 2 days before intratracheal instillation of In2O3 NPs and continuously treated for 28 days thereafter (Fig. 2A). This dosing regimen was designed to maintain sustained PPAR-γ activation throughout the period in which PAP develops following In2O3 NP exposure. At the end of the 28-day treatment, BALF and lung tissues were collected to assess surfactant accumulation, inflammatory responses, and the expression of PPAR-γ–related genes in alveolar macrophages.

Attenuation of lung inflammation through PPAR-γ activation during PAP development

In PAP induced by In2O3 NPs, bronchoalveolar inflammation was evident by increases in inflammatory cells, LDH, total protein, and phospholipids in BALF (Fig. 2). The total cell count was significantly elevated in the positive control (In2O3 NP–only) and in the aspirin- and naproxen-treated groups compared with the vehicle control, indicating pronounced inflammatory cell recruitment to the alveolar space. In contrast, the PPAR-γ agonist–treated groups (pioglitazone and indomethacin) showed total cell numbers comparable to those of the vehicle control (Fig. 2B). Notably, the pioglitazone group exhibited a significantly lower total cell count than the positive control, suggesting that PPAR-γ activation effectively suppressed cellular infiltration in the lung. The number of macrophages significantly increased only in the aspirin-treated group compared with the vehicle control (Fig. 2C), while no significant differences were observed among the other drug-treated groups relative to the positive control. Neutrophil counts in BALF were significantly increased in the positive control, the aspirin- and naproxen-treated groups compared with the vehicle control. However, the counts were reduced by approximately 52%, 70%, and 65% in the aspirin-, pioglitazone-, and indomethacin-treated groups, respectively, compared with the positive control, whereas the naproxen-treated group showed levels comparable to the positive control (Fig. 2D). Compared with the vehicle control group, LDH and total protein levels were significantly increased in the positive control and in all other drug-treated groups, whereas no significant change was observed in the indomethacin-treated group (Fig. 2E and F). Compared with the positive control, only the indomethacin-treated group showed a significant reduction in both parameters, while the other treatment groups exhibited similar levels to the positive control.

Restoration of surfactant homeostasis via PPAR-γ activation

Phospholipid levels were significantly increased in all In2O3 NP–exposed groups compared with the vehicle control. However, relative to the positive control, only the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups showed significantly lower levels, whereas the other treatment groups exhibited similar levels to the positive control (Fig. 2G). Turbidity, an indicator of PAP severity, showed a pattern identical to that of phospholipid levels (Fig. S1, see Supporting Information). Representative BALF cytospin slides from the PAP model showed proteinaceous material and foamy macrophages characterized by abundant multivacuolated cytoplasm. These cytomorphological features were more prominent in the positive control, aspirin-, and naproxen-treated groups and less prominent in the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups (Fig. S2, see Supporting Information). To identify BALF toxicity parameters associated with the increase in phospholipid levels (a key marker of PAP), Spearman correlation analysis was performed. LDH (r = 0.94, p < 0.05) and total protein (r = 1, p < 0.01) showed significant positive correlations with phospholipid levels (Fig. 2H and I).

Restoration of pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine levels by PPAR-γ activation

Compared with the vehicle control, IL-1β levels were significantly increased in the positive control, aspirin-, and naproxen-treated groups. In contrast, the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups exhibited IL-1β levels comparable to those of the vehicle control and significantly lower than those of the positive control (Fig. 3A). The levels of CINC-3 were significantly increased only in the positive control compared with the vehicle control. Both NSAID- and PPAR-γ agonist-treated groups showed levels comparable to the vehicle control and significantly lower than the positive control (Fig. 3B). MCP-1 levels were significantly increased in all treatment groups compared with the vehicle control, whereas both NSAID- and PPAR-γ agonist-treated groups showed significantly lower levels than the positive control (Fig. 3C). TNF-α levels were elevated only in the positive control compared with the vehicle control, while a significant reduction was observed exclusively in the pioglitazone-treated group relative to the positive control (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, GM-CSF levels were comparable across all groups, including the vehicle control, and were below the lower limit of quantification (3.9 pg/mL), with estimated values below 2 pg/mL (Fig. S3, see Supporting Information). Correlation analysis between phospholipid and cytokine levels revealed strong positive correlations for both IL-1β (r = 0.89, p < 0.05) and MCP-1 (r = 1, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E and F).

Recovery of ABC transporter and PPAR-γ gene expression in alveolar macrophages

Among the five key genes associated with PAP (ABCA1, ABCB4, ABCG1, ABCG4, and PPAR-γ), the expression of ABCA1 was consistently upregulated in all In2O3 NP–exposed groups without significant differences among treatment groups (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, ABCB4 gene expression showed a significant increase in all In2O3 NP-exposed groups compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 4B). In contrast, ABCG1, ABCG4, and PPAR-γ exhibited differential expression patterns between the NSAID- and PPAR-γ agonist–treated groups (Fig. 4C–E). Although the ABCG1 gene expression in all In2O3 NP-exposed groups did not show a significant change, the positive control, the aspirin, and the naproxen-treated groups were downregulated, whereas the pioglitazone and indomethacin-treated groups were upregulated compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 4C). Notably, ABCG4 expression in the positive control, the aspirin, and the naproxen-treated groups was significantly downregulated compared to the vehicle control, while ABCG4 expression in both the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups showed significant increases compared with the positive control group (Fig. 4D). PPAR-γ expression in only the indomethacin-treated group was upregulated and significantly increased compared to the positive control (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, ABCG4 expression was strongly correlated with the levels of phospholipid (r = -0.89, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4F). ABCA1, ABCB4, and ABCG1 expression were not correlated with phospholipid levels (Fig. S4, see Supporting Information). In contrast, PPAR-γ expression showed a negative correlation with phospholipid levels (r = -0.83), although the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.058) (Fig. S4, see Supporting Information).

Histopathological recovery of PAP lesions following PPAR-γ activation

Representative lung sections showed PAP-like histopathological changes, including the accumulation of foamy macrophages and proteinaceous material, in the positive control, aspirin-treated, and naproxen-treated groups. In contrast, the PPAR-γ agonist–treated groups (pioglitazone and indomethacin) showed less prominent PAP-like lesions, approaching the levels observed in the vehicle control (Fig. 5). Consistent with the H&E findings, PAS staining of lung tissue revealed focal PAS-positive intra-alveolar and macrophage-associated material in the In2O3 NP-exposed groups, whereas this reactivity appeared less prominent in the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups (Fig. S5, see Supporting Information). These representative histological observations further support the attenuation of PAP-like histopathological changes in the lung following treatment with pioglitazone or indomethacin. To assess the potential systemic toxicity of the administered drugs, histopathological evaluation of the liver was performed as an indicator of hepatotoxicity. No notable histopathological alterations were observed in any of the drug-treated groups at the administered doses (Fig. S6, see Supporting Information).

Therapeutic activation of PPAR-γ signaling alleviates established In2O3 NP–induced PAP

To evaluate whether pharmacological activation of PPAR-γ could reverse or alleviate established PAP induced by In2O3 NPs, rats were administered the test drugs by oral gavage for 28 days beginning on day 28 after intratracheal instillation of In2O3 NPs, when PAP was fully developed (Fig. 6A). This dosing regimen was designed to assess the therapeutic potential of PPAR-γ activation during the chronic phase of the disease. At the end of the 28-day treatment, BALF and lung tissues were collected to evaluate the recovery of surfactant homeostasis, the improvement of inflammatory responses, and the restoration of PPAR-γ–related gene expression in alveolar macrophages. Overall, the patterns of response observed in this therapeutic phase were largely consistent with those seen in the preventive study, indicating that PPAR-γ activation exerted similar modulatory effects on lung inflammation and surfactant regulation across both experimental settings.

Attenuation of lung inflammation through PPAR-γ activation in established PAP

Total cell counts in BALF were significantly increased in all In2O3 NP–treated groups compared with the vehicle control, except for the indomethacin-treated group, which showed no significant change. Although the indomethacin group tended to have lower counts than the positive control, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 6B). Macrophage counts showed no significant changes in any of the treatment groups (Fig. 6C). Neutrophil counts and LDH levels exhibited trends similar to the total cell counts (Figs. 6D and E). Total protein levels were significantly increased in all treatment groups compared with the vehicle control, except for the indomethacin group, which showed no significant change relative to the vehicle control but significantly lower levels than the positive control (Fig. 6F). Phospholipid levels were significantly increased in all treatment groups compared with the vehicle control, whereas pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups showed significantly lower levels than the positive control (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, representative BALF cytospin images qualitatively showed less prominent proteinaceous material and foamy macrophage-like changes in the pioglitazone- and indomethacin-treated groups (Fig. S7, see Supporting Information). In addition, dark-field microscopy revealed bright particle-like signals in alveolar macrophages from the In2O3 NP–treated groups, suggesting intracellular localization of retained particles (Fig. S8, see Supporting Information). Moreover, levels of LDH and total protein, as well as the number of total cells, were strongly correlated with phospholipid levels (Fig. 6H–J).

Discussion

PAP has been reported in workers occupationally exposed to indium compounds, particularly In2O3 NPs, which are known to induce persistent lung injury and surfactant accumulation [26]. In addition to indium-based particles, other poorly soluble NPs such as NiO and certain rare-earth oxide NPs have also been shown to elicit PAP-like pathology, suggesting that the incidence of NP-induced PAP may be higher than currently recognized [9, 27]. Considering the mechanistic basis of PAP pathogenesis, characterized by impaired macrophage lipid clearance and dysregulation of PPAR-γ signaling, our previous study proposed the therapeutic potential of PPAR-γ agonists in restoring alveolar macrophage function [2, 11]. Building upon these findings, the present study shows that pharmacological activation of PPAR-γ effectively ameliorates secondary PAP induced by In2O3 NPs, with greater efficacy in the preventive experiment and partial benefit in the therapeutic experiment. Moreover, this mechanistic framework extends beyond PAP itself, offering insight into therapeutic approaches for other diseases involving foamy macrophages, such as atherosclerosis, where defective lipid handling plays a key pathogenic role [28, 29]. Collectively, our findings show that PPAR-γ activation by pioglitazone and indomethacin restored ABC transporter expression and reduced surfactant accumulation, although inflammatory markers in the therapeutic experiment remained incompletely resolved, supporting the potential of PPAR-γ agonists for prevention and partial amelioration of secondary PAP.

The primary cause of PAP induced by In2O3 NPs appears to be the failure of macrophages to regulate surfactant homeostasis, as these poorly soluble particles can persistently affect alveolar macrophages [7, 9]. This persistent burden leads to the accumulation of lipids within macrophages, eventually triggering cell death and suggesting sustained cytotoxicity [30, 31]. Excessive lipid loading not only enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress but also disrupts mitochondrial function, thereby activating apoptotic pathways [30, 31]. In addition to apoptosis, severe lipid-induced organelle swelling and membrane destabilization can culminate in necrotic cell death, further amplifying pulmonary injury [32, 33]. This disruption of lipid homeostasis is further exacerbated by the suppression of PPAR-γ signaling and its downstream ABC transporters (ABCG1 and ABCG4), leading to impaired cholesterol and phospholipid efflux and promoting the transformation of macrophages into foamy macrophages [11, 34]. Moreover, functional impairment of alveolar macrophages reduces surfactant clearance, accelerating surfactant accumulation within the alveolar spaces.

In our study, the severity of PAP was closely associated with elevated LDH levels, indicating a strong correlation between particle-induced cytotoxicity and disease progression. These cellular events are consistent with the histopathological findings of abundant foamy macrophages and proteinaceous material in the alveoli, supporting the pivotal role of macrophage dysfunction and cytotoxic injury in the pathogenesis of PAP. Persistent necrosis of alveolar macrophages leads to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn activate the inflammasome and NF-κB signaling [35]. This process induces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-18, TNF-α, IL-6, and neutrophil-attracting chemokines (e.g., IL-8/CINC family), thereby amplifying pulmonary inflammation [36]. Primary PAP is mainly characterized by defective GM-CSF signaling or surfactant metabolism, leading to impaired surfactant clearance, while prominent neutrophilic inflammation is not a typical feature and may be more relevant in the setting of superimposed infection [37]. In contrast, NP-induced secondary PAP can involve neutrophil recruitment together with surfactant accumulation, as persistent In2O3 NP deposition has been shown to induce PAP-like lesions and sustained neutrophilic pulmonary inflammation for up to 6 months in our previous study [7]. Notably, GM-CSF levels were not altered by drug treatment and remained comparable among all groups. This finding suggests that the beneficial effects of pioglitazone and indomethacin were not associated with measurable changes in BALF GM-CSF, but may instead reflect direct modulation of PPAR-γ–related macrophage lipid-handling pathways.

MCP-1 (CCL2) is a key chemokine that recruits monocytes and macrophages to sites of lung injury [38]. In PAP, where alveolar macrophages fail to adequately clear surfactant, elevated MCP-1 may further enhance macrophage accumulation without restoring their clearance capacity, thereby aggravating surfactant overload. Increased MCP-1 in BALF has been reported in particle-induced lung injury, including indium-related lung disease, in parallel with PAP-like lesions [9, 39]. This suggests that MCP-1 not only amplifies macrophage influx but also perpetuates an inflammatory milieu in which dysfunctional macrophages accumulate, ultimately exacerbating alveolar proteinosis and potentially driving progression toward secondary interstitial changes. IL‑1β has been shown to induce MCP‑1 expression via NF‑κB activation in smooth muscle and endothelial cells, thereby promoting monocyte/macrophage recruitment [40]. Conversely, MCP-1 can enhance the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, through the NF-κB and AP-1 pathways [41]. This interplay likely contributes to a feed-forward loop in which MCP-1-driven macrophage influx sustains inflammasome activation and further IL‑1β release, thereby perpetuating pulmonary inflammation.

Aspirin, naproxen, and indomethacin are NSAIDs that exert their pharmacological effects through inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, thereby suppressing prostaglandin synthesis [42]. As these drugs are administered orally and exert systemic effects, they can reach the pulmonary circulation and modulate inflammatory responses within the lung [43]. NSAIDs can decrease neutrophil recruitment by inhibiting the production of IL-1β and CINC-3 [44, 45]. Aspirin treatment at the early stage of PAP caused by In2O3 NPs in this study resulted in elevated levels of neutrophils, IL-1β, and CINC-3. Notably, in the naproxen-treated group, CINC-3 levels were decreased; however, neutrophil infiltration persisted. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the redundancy of neutrophil chemotactic signals, as other mediators such as IL-1β, CXCL1, and CXCL2 remained elevated [46, 47]. Neutrophil recruitment induced by NPs is primarily driven by persistent activation of the inflammasome and chemokine signaling [48]. This is particularly relevant for poorly soluble particles such as In2O3 NPs, which remain in the alveolar space and continuously stimulate alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells. In contrast, NSAIDs such as aspirin and naproxen attenuate neutrophilia by inhibiting COX-mediated prostaglandin synthesis, a downstream pathway [49]. Therefore, the intense and persistent upstream inflammatory stimulus induced by In2O3 NPs could diminish the inhibitory effects of NSAIDs, resulting in sustained neutrophil infiltration in the lung.

Pioglitazone, as a PPAR-γ agonist widely used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, improves glucose metabolism by reducing insulin resistance and regulating blood glucose levels [50]. A recent case report described radiological and functional improvement in a patient with refractory autoimmune PAP after pioglitazone treatment, including reduced ground-glass opacities and crazy-paving on chest CT and increased PaO2 at 6 and 12 months, suggesting decreased accumulation of intra-alveolar material and improved gas exchange despite stable anti-GM-CSF autoantibody titers [18]. Although the available human evidence remains limited, this observation suggests that modulation of PPAR-γ-dependent macrophage lipid handling may have translational relevance in PAP. Furthermore, indomethacin is an NSAID that has been widely used for the treatment of rheumatism and gout [51]. Although studies on this activity are limited, indomethacin has nevertheless been reported to act as a PPAR agonist [22]. Given that both pioglitazone and indomethacin act on PPAR-γ, these agents are likely to influence lipid regulatory pathways involving ABC transporters that govern cholesterol and phospholipid efflux, as elaborated in the following discussion.

PPAR-γ is a key transcriptional regulator of lipid homeostasis that modulates several members of the ABC transporter family, including ABCA1, ABCG1, and ABCG4. These transporters mediate efflux of cholesterol and phospholipid from macrophages, thereby maintaining cellular lipid balance and preventing foam cell formation [52, 53]. Under physiological conditions, ABCA1 and ABCG1 exhibit compensatory regulation; when one transporter is downregulated, the other tends to remain upregulated to preserve lipid efflux capacity [53, 54]. In the present study, exposure to In2O3 NPs disrupted this homeostatic axis, resulting in persistent ABCA1 upregulation accompanied by marked downregulation of ABCG1 and ABCG4, suggesting that particle-induced PAP is primarily linked to dysfunction of the ABCG transporters. Pharmacological activation of PPAR-γ by pioglitazone and indomethacin restored the expression of PPAR-γ itself and its downstream targets ABCG1 and ABCG4 to near-control levels in the preventive setting. ABCG1 and ABCG4 expression was not normalized, although numerical recovery toward control levels was observed, suggesting only partial restoration of the PPAR-γ–ABC transporter axis after PAP-like pathology had already been established. Although indomethacin showed some improvement in surfactant-related parameters, its overall effects were largely comparable to those of pioglitazone. These findings suggest that the PPAR-γ–ABC signaling axis is more strongly associated with prevention than with full reversal of established particle-induced PAP-like pathology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that exposure to In2O3 NPs induces PAP through impaired surfactant clearance and macrophage lipid dysregulation driven by suppression of the PPAR-γ–ABC transporter axis, particularly ABCG1 and ABCG4. Pharmacological activation of PPAR-γ by pioglitazone and indomethacin ameliorated these pathological alterations, with stronger effects in the preventive setting and partial improvement in the therapeutic setting, by restoring lipid efflux capacity and reducing foam macrophage accumulation, thereby improving surfactant homeostasis. Both agents showed comparable overall efficacy within the limits of the present study. These findings highlight the central role of the PPAR-γ–ABC transporter pathway in the pathogenesis of NP-induced PAP-like pathology and underscore the need for further investigation into PPAR-γ–targeted therapeutics for prevention and partial treatment benefit in particle-induced secondary PAP.
