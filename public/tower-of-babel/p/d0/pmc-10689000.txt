Inequality in uptake of bowel cancer screening by deprivation, ethnicity and smoking status: cross-sectional study in 86 850 citizens
Authors: Alexandra Creavin, Sam Creavin, Charlie Kenward, Jonathan Sterne, Jo Williams
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvival from colorectal cancer depends on stage at detection. In England, bowel cancer mortality has historically been highest in deprived areas. During the initial stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, it was necessary to temporarily halt many screening programmes, which may have led to inequalities in uptake since screening restarted.MethodsCross-sectional data from the Bristol, North Somerset and South Gloucestershire Systemwide Dataset were analyzed. Associations of baseline characteristics with uptake of bowel screening were examined using logistic regression.ResultsAmongst 86 850 eligible adults aged 60–74 years, 5261 had no screening record. There was little evidence of association between no screening and sex (adjusted odds ratio 0.95 (95% confidence interval 0.90, 1.02)). Absence of screening record was associated with deprivation (1.26 (1.14, 1.40) for the most compared with the least deprived groups), smoking (1.11 (1.04, 1.18)) compared with no smoking record and black (1.36 (1.09, 1.70)) and mixed (1.08 (1.01, 1.15)) ethnicity compared with white ethnicity.ConclusionsIn a data set covering a whole NHS Integrated Care Board, there was evidence of lower uptake of bowel cancer screening in adults living in more deprived areas, of minority ethnic groups and who smoked. These findings may help focus community engagement work and inform research aimed at reducing inequalities.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) includes neoplasms of the colon, rectum and rectosigmoid junction. With nearly 50 000 new cases annually, CRC accounts for 11% of cancer registrations and is the second most common cause of cancer mortality in the UK.1,2 Potentially modifiable risk factors for CRC include obesity, heavy alcohol intake and smoking.3–7

In England, inequality of outcomes is seen across most cancers and CRC is no exception with mortality traditionally highest in deprived areas.1 The close association between stage of diagnosis and mortality indicates the important role of bowel screening in reducing premature mortality.1 The national screening programme in England aims to detect early asymptomatic CRC and thus reduce premature mortality.8 Adults aged 60–74 years are sent a bowel cancer screening invitation letter every 2 years, an information leaflet and a faecal immunochemical test (FIT) kit with written and animated instructions.8 The NHS is piloting extension of the programme to adults aged 50–59.8 Approximately 2% of people undergoing screening have an abnormal FIT and are invited for colonoscopy.8

Reductions in cancer screening early in the COVID-19 pandemic were well-documented and profound.9,10 Invitations for FIT testing were almost universally stopped, and colonoscopy services restricted to meet competing service demands.11 In Bristol, North Somerset and South Gloucestershire (BNSSG), services were suspended for 5 months between March and August 2020.

The aim of this study was to estimate associations of CRC screening uptake with demographic and lifestyle characteristics, using a large electronic health record (EHR) data set covering an NHS Integrated Care Board (ICB) in Southwest England. The work was undertaken to inform interventions to increase screening participation and reduce inequalities in outcomes related to bowel cancer.

Methods

Study design

We used data from a large, population-based, prospective clinical data set. EHR data on individuals registered with GPs in BNSSG ICB are collated in monthly cross-sectional extracts in a pseudonymised linked data set that contains data from primary care, secondary care, community services and mental health care.

Population

We included adults aged 60–74 between 1 June 2020 (first data available) and 30 June 2022 registered with a GP in BNSSG ICB. These encompassed adults aged 58–76, who fell in the eligible age bracket during the period under analysis. BNSSG joined the CRC screening extension programme in August 2021 for 56-year-olds and in September 2022 for 58-year-olds and so the extension programme is not included in this study.

Care home status was defined by registered care homes, and IMD for each citizen was provided based on their relevant LSOA (lower super output area of residence: small geographic areas with a mean population of 1500).14,15

Statistical analysis

We wrote, refined and executed analytical code on dummy data, which was then sent to ICB staff and executed on secure ICB computers. No data left the secure ICB servers. Data cleaning and analyses were undertaken using Stata version 16 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

To avoid implausible values for body mass index (BMI) and alcohol units, we replaced values of more than the 99% centile for these variables as missing.

We computed descriptive statistics and used logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) quantifying associations between exposures and both declining CRC screening and having no evidence of CRC screening. We focussed on estimates and 95% CIs rather than hypothesis tests. We included all factors plausibly related to CRC screening in the multivariable model where data were sufficient (i.e. all covariates except BMI and alcohol).

Ethical approval

Yorkshire & The Humber—Leeds East Research Ethics Committee granted NHS HRA Research Database approval for the BNSSG system-wide database in 2020 (REC Reference: 20/YH/0185, 28 July 2020). The data from GP electronic medical records were extracted according to existing data sharing agreements and in line with GDPR and Caldicott principles under pandemic conditions and in accordance with government guidelines and the COPI directive.

Results

The data set contained 86 850 unique eligible adults aged 60–74 between 1 June 2020 and data extraction on 30 June 2022. Figure 1 indicates that 57 586 (66%) had taken up the offer of screening and had a recorded positive or negative result, whereas 23 695 (27%) had ambiguous screening status; 5261 (6%) had no screening record and 308 (0.4%) declined screening. We could not identify within this data set information whether a screening invitation had been sent. However, invitations are sent from a national NHS site and given the period chosen each person should have been invited at least once.

There was a modest graded association between IMD group and having no screening record. The most deprived groups had the highest odds of declining screening; aOR 4.23 (95% CI 2.71, 6.61) and of having no screening record; aOR 1.26 (95% CI 1.14, 1.40) in multivariable analyses.

Percentage of people who declined CRC screening, or have no screening record, stratified by Index of Multiple Deprivation.

Of black people, 89% had a test result, compared with 92% of white people. There was not strong evidence that any ethnic group was more or less likely to decline CRC screening but there was evidence that black people were more likely to have no screening record compared with white people, aOR 1.36 (95% CI 1.09, 1.70). The same was true for mixed-ethnicity people although the association was weaker, aOR 1.08 (95% CI 1.01, 1.15).

There was a small association between unit increase in BMI and both declining screening OR 1.06 (95% CI 1.03, 1.08) per kg/m2 and having no screening record OR 1.01 (95% CI 1.00, 1.02) per kg/m2. There was a lack of evidence of an association between declining screening or having no screening record and alcohol consumption, although numbers with alcohol reported were small.

Of people in care homes, 86% had a test result compared with 91% of people outside a care home. Nursing or residential home residency was particularly associated with declining CRC screening, aOR 6.36 (95% CI 1.52–26.5) in multivariable analysis though CIs were wide because of small numbers. There was a lack of association between care home residency and having no screening record in multivariable analysis, aOR 1.38 (95% CI 0.66, 2.88).

Discussion

Main finding of this study

We demonstrate inequality in uptake of CRC screening for people living in areas of high deprivation, reporting black or mixed ethnicity, with increasing recorded BMI, and smoking in a data set utilizing near real-time data from the live clinical record, in a population of around 1 million people. Improvement in uptake and consequently in health outcomes would benefit from research into barriers to screening and interventions aimed at improving uptake of CRC screening in these groups.

We found that declining CRC screening occurred infrequently and was associated with deprivation (particularly the most deprived category), being a current or ex-smoker, higher BMI and care home residence.

What is already known on this topic?

UK Healthcare policies and services have traditionally tended to take a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach that can lead to lower uptake and poorer satisfaction with care amongst ethnic minority groups.16 Gaps in data mean there is often inadequate evidence on how to meet the needs of underserved groups.16

The pre-COVID-19 pandemic literature indicated associations between low bowel screening uptake and male sex, obesity and smoking.17,18 A study of females found that smokers were 29% less likely to participate in screening.19 Another study from West London reported low screening uptake in those with an ethnicity besides white or Asian Chinese.20

Despite concerted efforts to reintroduce screening after the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic, uptake continued to be impacted in many locations. In one mixed-methods study around a fifth of people reported being less likely to do a CRC screening test during the pandemic than beforehand.21 During the earliest stages of the pandemic, disparities in help-seeking behaviour were reported, with those in lower socio-economic status UK groups least likely to make contact with health services.22 There is scant literature since the first 6 months of the pandemic examining longer-term inequalities in CRC screening uptake and the burden of disease. One study from Wales reported low uptake of bowel cancer screening by males, younger individuals and those living in areas of higher deprivation.23 Uptake also varied according to ethnic group, with uptake lowest in those of Asian and Unknown ethnic background, though these findings were comparable before and after the suspension of services because of the COVID-19 pandemic.23

What this study adds

This pragmatic analysis combines exposures plausibly associated with CRC screening such that decision makers and clinical staff can consider key factors influencing uptake amongst their populations. Contemporaneous data represent the period of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the large population size and rich data allowed several potential exposures to be explored in a fully adjusted model. Importantly, all residents registered with a BNSSG GP were eligible for inclusion, which minimized exclusion of marginalized groups.

We find that BNSSG ICB did not experience the vast reduction in screening rates implied by the findings of Wilson et al.21 This is in keeping with findings of a recent Welsh study and may reflect the fact that the UK CRC screening is free and home-based and so common reasons for avoiding screening during the pandemic such as uneasiness about attending healthcare settings or using public transport did not apply.23

The association found between low screening uptake and deprivation after adjusting for other factors such as ethnicity and smoking is consistent with the findings around deprivation in the pre-COVID-19 pandemic UK literature, and with reports of low screening uptake in those with low educational attainment.18,20,24 Possible explanations include the increased likelihood of concurrent disability or illness amongst deprived groups, low relative health literacy and a cycle of non-participation amongst families.24 As survival is tightly linked with stage at diagnosis, it is likely that low screening uptake in deprived groups contributes, at least in part, to the association between CRC mortality and deprivation.1,18,20

We find an association between low uptake and black or mixed ethnicity compared with white ethnicity. BNSSG, particularly Bristol, has an ethnically and culturally diverse population. NHS ethnic categories have been revised in accordance with the census but the ethnic groupings are highly heterogeneous and generalizations across each group may be misleading. With incomplete data, particularly around first language and a lack of information on migration status in the medical record, it is challenging to disentangle the significance of the role of language barriers, marginalization, cultural barriers and challenges with access.16 We think it is unlikely that observed differences are because of timing of database updates since the database is updated for the entire population on a monthly basis. Though not immune to access barriers, the CRC screening programme is less likely to suffer physical access issues compared with other screening programmes since the kit is posted out, can be done at home and as literature is provided in many languages and formats. The ethnicity report for the UK CRC screening pilot highlighted a lack of trials examining interventions to improve CRC uptake in ethnic minority populations.25 The report supported multi-strategy techniques for improving cancer screening in general including GP receptionist training and follow-up letters in appropriate languages.25 The applicability to CRC and cost-effectiveness of these techniques has not been examined.25 Findings from local focus groups suggest that there may be specific cultural and religious concerns associated with the FOB that do not apply with the FIT test and so the recent change in tests to FIT may improve uptake in some groups.26

Smokers and ex-smokers were less likely to complete CRC screening than presumed never smokers. Hypotheses for this finding include over-optimism about health in smokers and underestimation of cancer risk.19 This finding is of particular concern given the strong evidence for increased CRC incidence and mortality in smokers.6,7,19

We found care home residents were more likely to decline screening. This may be because of chance, because the numbers are small or may reflect a need for assistance in completing screening, differences in values and preferences based on the likelihood of net benefit, contraindications or direct responses by carers rather than participants.27 An estimated 80% of UK care home residents have dementia which in some cases may make involvement in diagnostic tests or treatments for bowel cancer challenging or inappropriate.28 However, people in care homes should not be systematically excluded from screening if they have capacity and would be able to benefit.29,30

UK population-based modelling predicted a 17% increase in CRC deaths up to 5 years after diagnosis because of the COVID-19 pandemic response.31 Our findings suggest that if this occurs, there is likely to be inequality in the burden of such excess deaths related to deprivation and ethnicity. When considering evidence-based interventions for increasing screening uptake, it would be helpful to consider the impact of such interventions on health inequalities and how specific population groups can be supported to alleviate these.

Qualitative research or community engagement work may support understanding of individual and community experiences across low-uptake groups and barriers to CRC screening and inform interventions that increase acceptance and uptake within these populations. Working with communities to co-produce culturally relevant campaigns may result in novel solutions to improve awareness and reduce barriers to uptake.

Limitations of this study

The lack of granularity in categorical variables is a clear limitation but a frequent problem with service data. This impacted outcome data resulting in the ambiguous screening category and exposure data including smoking where no category for non-smoker was provided. The assumption that those not coded as smokers or ex-smokers were non-smokers, where incorrect, may have reduced any association with smoking towards the null. Incomplete ascertainment is also possibly an issue for residential status since 0.2% of the study population was registered as living in a care home compared with 0.6% of the UK population aged 65–74 years in 2011.32 Work is now underway at the ICB to improve the process of mapping codes in SQL.

While a major advantage of this data set is the inclusiveness of the study population, a drawback is that the data are from real clinical data, which does not currently have any validation checks done at the point of entry.

Further limitations of the data include missing data, particularly for BMI and alcohol. There will be biases in the recording of measures such as alcohol, smoking and weight with these variables more likely to be recorded in those with chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension because of regular monitoring in long-term condition clinics. Some exposures of interest were not available or were too incomplete to include such as first language, country of birth and learning disability.

Self-reported exposures, for example smoking and alcohol, may be subject to downward self-report bias.33 The effect of such underreporting will be to have reduced any true associations between these exposures and the outcome towards the null. A further limitation is that we do not know the date that bowel screening invitations were sent to eligible citizens; because the data used are the electronic records for an entire health system, we have made the assumption that the proportion invited from each exposure category would be comparable in the time period of interest.

Finally, despite high coverage there will still be groups who are underrepresented because of reduced likelihood of registration with a GP, for example, the homeless, migrant and Gypsy and Irish traveller populations.

Conclusion

In a data set covering a whole NHS ICB, there was evidence of inequality in uptake of bowel screening with lower uptake in adults living in more deprived areas, in those from minority ethnic groups and in those who smoked.

These findings are already informing uptake-improvement work in the Southwest and may help focus community engagement work and inform research aimed at reducing inequalities in cancer outcomes.
