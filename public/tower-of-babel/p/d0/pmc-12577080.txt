Integrating tactile simulation into thoracic surgery education: a Miller’s pyramid–Based study
Authors: Ganwei Liu, Feng Yang, Zuli Zhou, Guanchao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate intraoperative localization of pulmonary nodules remains a significant challenge in video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), especially for small, deep, or subsolid lesions. This prospective cohort study evaluated the impact of a simulation-based training program using high-fidelity 3D-printed lung models on surgical trainees’ spatial-tactile localization skills. A total of 67 postgraduate year 1–2 trainees were randomized into a lung model group (LMG) or a control group (CG). The curriculum comprised four sequential phases aligned with Miller’s Pyramid: anatomical instruction, CT-to-anatomy mapping, thoracoscopic simulated localization, and intraoperative localization. Participants in the LMG achieved significantly higher scores in radiologic-anatomic mapping (6.94 ± 1.07 vs. 5.82 ± 1.07; p < 0.001) and simulated localization (2.09 ± 0.71 vs. 1.45 ± 0.87; p = 0.002). In clinical procedures, LMG trainees had a higher localization success rate (68.97%) compared to CG (66.67%), though not statistically significant (p = 0.877). Self-reported confidence and satisfaction ratings consistently favored the LMG. These findings suggest that anatomically realistic, simulation-based tactile training enhances thoracic surgery trainees’ ability to mentally integrate radiologic data with intraoperative spatial reasoning. Incorporating such structured modules into thoracic surgical education may improve operative readiness and reduce reliance on adjunctive localization technologies.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. The widespread implementation of low-dose computed tomography (CT) screening has significantly increased the detection of small pulmonary nodules and early-stage lung cancers [2]. Accurate intraoperative localization of these pulmonary nodules remains a fundamental yet challenging skill in thoracic surgery, particularly in the context of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), where tactile feedback is limited and visual access is constrained. This challenge is especially pronounced in cases involving small, deeply situated, or subsolid lesions such as ground-glass opacities (GGOs), which frequently evade both visual detection and manual palpation in the absence of adjunctive localization techniques [3]. Failure to localize nodules precisely may prolong operative time, lead to unnecessary parenchymal resection, or result in incomplete excision, all of which negatively impact oncologic outcomes and postoperative recovery.

Beyond the technical challenges, many surgical trainees face substantial cognitive barriers in mastering thoracoscopic localization. While most are proficient in interpreting CT scans, they often struggle to mentally project two-dimensional (2D) radiologic findings into a three-dimensional (3D) thoracoscopic workspace. Despite advances in 3D reconstruction, conventional imaging remains inherently 2D and limits the development of spatial–tactile reasoning. This cognitive disconnect is further compounded by traditional surgical curricula, which emphasize visual observation while providing limited support for the structured development of tactile–spatial skills essential for accurate localization in minimally invasive procedures. As a result, trainees increasingly depend on adjunctive localization techniques, such as hook-wire placement [4, 5], dye injection [6], ultrasound [7], radiofrequency [8], and electromagnetic navigation [9]. Although effective in specific clinical contexts, these methods may introduce procedural complexity, elevate the risk of complications, and may be unavailable in resource-limited settings [10].

Recently, 3D printing has gained increasing attention in surgical education, not only for anatomical visualization but also for preoperative planning and simulation-based training. It enables the transformation of patient-specific imaging data into tangible models that replicate complex anatomy with high fidelity. Surgeons play a pivotal role in this process, particularly in the segmentation of radiologic data and clinical translation of printed constructs into operative practice [11]. Nevertheless, few educational programs have systematically leveraged 3D-printed models to train spatial-tactile skills essential for thoracoscopic procedures.

To address this educational gap, we developed a structured, simulation-based training program aimed at enhancing residents’ tactile-spatial cognition and improving their ability to localize pulmonary nodules without relying on visual cues or advanced navigation tools. Central to this curriculum is a high-fidelity lung model embedded with artificial nodules of varying sizes and depths, replicating the anatomical complexity commonly encountered in thoracic surgery [12]. The curriculum is based on Miller’s Pyramid, a widely accepted framework for assessing and developing clinical competency, especially in early-stage surgical education [13–15].

Training progresses through four sequential stages: (1) tactile-anatomical recognition, involving direct manual exploration of the model to establish foundational spatial awareness; (2) CT-to-anatomy correlation, achieved through structured spatial reasoning exercises designed to bridge radiologic interpretation and 3D anatomical understanding; (3) simulated operative validation, conducted within a thoracoscopic environment using patient-specific, 3D-printed lung models to assess localization skills under realistic surgical constraints; and (4) clinical application, wherein selected participants perform verbal localization during live VATS procedures. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically evaluated a tactile simulation–based training program for pulmonary nodule localization within the structured framework of Miller’s pyramid. The primary aim of this study was to determine whether a structured, simulation-based training program could improve trainees’ anatomical comprehension and CT-to-structure mapping ability, as measured by a standardized nine-item cognitive assessment (primary outcome). Key secondary outcomes included the number of nodules successfully localized in a simulated thoracoscopic setting, as well as self-perceived confidence and training satisfaction. These outcomes collectively provide insight into the program’s educational value and its potential integration within competency-based surgical curricula.

Materials and methods

Figure 2 provides an overview of the study design. This prospective cohort study was conducted among early postgraduate medical trainees and followed a four-phase structure, with each phase mapped to a specific level of clinical competence based on Miller’s pyramid. Participants were blinded to the specific learning objectives of each phase to minimize potential bias. Performance, self-perceived confidence, and satisfaction were systematically assessed throughout the training process. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant institutional guidelines. All instructional materials, assessment tools, and survey instruments were reviewed and approved by the hospital’s Teaching and Research Expert Committee prior to implementation.

Participants

This prospective controlled educational study was conducted in the Department of Thoracic Surgery at Peking University People’s Hospital between March 2022 and October 2024. Eligible participants included postgraduate year 1 and 2 (PGY1–PGY2) trainees rotating through thoracic surgery who had no prior experience with intraoperative lung nodule palpation or previous exposure to the simulation model.

3D model generation and fabrication

All lung models used in this study were developed in collaboration with a domestic biomedical engineering company (Yingkou Jucheng Teaching Technology Development Co., Ltd., Yingkou, China). Patient-specific models were generated from anonymized, high-resolution chest CT datasets using Mimics software (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) to segment pulmonary lobes, segmental bronchi, and target nodules. The segmented structures were then reconstructed into 3D stereolithography (STL) files, which were printed using a stereolithography-based 3D printer (SPS350C, Hengtong Intelligent Machine Co., Ltd., China). The lung parenchyma, blood vessels, and bronchi were fabricated using multi-material formulations designed to mimic the texture and compliance of native tissues. Material compositions included cellulose, glycerin, polyvinyl alcohol, polyol-based polymers, ferric chloride, and supplementary compounds such as ethylene glycol, silica, potassium hydroxide, and polyvinyl chloride. In the simulation setup, only the internal lung model was customized and replaceable, while the surrounding thoracic box, ports, and lighting remained constant across cases. On average, each model required approximately 12 to 20 h for fabrication, with an estimated unit cost of around $100 USD, depending on anatomical complexity.

Phase 1: baseline anatomy training (Level 1)

This phase was designed to establish foundational anatomical knowledge. All participants completed a standardized, one-week anatomy course covering pulmonary lobes, bronchopulmonary segments, and key anatomical landmarks relevant to thoracic surgery. Upon course completion, participants took a 10-item examination. Those who achieved a score of 6 or higher were deemed eligible to proceed to subsequent phases. Qualified participants were then randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the lung model group (LMG) or the control group (CG). A computer-generated simple randomization sequence was created using the random number function in Microsoft Excel by an independent researcher not involved in participant instruction or assessment. Group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Each envelope was opened only after a participant had been deemed eligible, thereby preventing foreknowledge of assignment. No stratification or block randomization was applied, but baseline characteristics were later examined to confirm between-group balance.

Phase 2: CT-to-structure mapping (Level 2)

This phase focused on translating anatomical knowledge into radiologic interpretation and surgical planning. All participants attended a case-based instructional session that reviewed thoracic imaging principles, including segmental localization, pleural depth estimation, and lesion characterization (pure GGOs, mixed GGOs, and solid nodules). Strategies for surgical decision-making in wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy were also discussed. Participants in the LMG received additional interactive instruction using a high-fidelity, 3D-printed lung model that served as a tactile–spatial scaffold (Fig. 1B). They were guided to mentally project CT-identified nodules onto the model surface, thereby enhancing CT-to-anatomy mapping through structured spatial reasoning. In contrast, CG participants completed similar exercises using only 2D CT images and printed anatomical charts (Fig. 1A).

All participants then completed nine CAQs targeting three core competencies: (1) segmental localization based on CT imaging (Questions 1–3); (2) resection strategy formulation based on lesion features and anatomical context (Questions 4–6); and (3) recognition of intraoperative challenges related to lesion exposure and positioning (Questions 7–9). A standardized dichotomous scoring rubric (0 = incorrect, 1 = correct) was applied to each item, yielding a total score range of 0–9. All questions were developed by two thoracic surgeons (with > 10 years of experience) and independently reviewed by an expert panel (n = 4) to ensure content validity and alignment with clinical decision-making tasks. The overall internal consistency of the 9-item CAQ instrument was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.78), supporting its reliability as a structured knowledge assessment.

Phase 3: simulated localization (Level 3)

This phase assessed participants’ ability to localize pulmonary nodules using tactile feedback in a controlled simulation environment. Each participant was randomly assigned three of six anonymized, patient-specific, 3D-printed lung models generated from high-resolution CT datasets. Each model contained a single artificial nodule (1.0–1.5 cm in diameter), situated within a pulmonary segment and within 2 cm of the pleural surface, thereby mimicking subpleural lesions encountered in clinical practice.

Before attempting localization, participants reviewed the corresponding CT images. The procedure was conducted using a thoracoscopic simulation platform that replicated key features of intraoperative conditions (Fig. 1B). Participants palpated the model through a simulated incision and marked three external points on the pleural surface to form a triangle enclosing the presumed location of the nodule. Each attempt was limited to three minutes, timed from the moment the participant initiated tactile exploration with their finger through the thoracoscopic port to the completion of external surface marking. An independent thoracic surgeon, blinded to group assignment, verified the accuracy of each attempt by palpating the model and determining whether the marked triangle accurately enclosed the embedded nodule.

Phase 4: intraoperative localization (Level 4)

This phase evaluated the real-world transfer of tactile localization skills. Only participants who had successfully identified at least two nodules in Phase 3 were eligible. Qualified trainees assisted in thoracoscopic procedures involving peripheral pulmonary nodules measuring 1.0–1.5 cm in diameter and located within 2 cm of the pleural surface. Each participant independently reviewed the patient’s preoperative CT scans.

Intraoperatively, under the supervision of an attending surgeon, participants performed tactile exploration of the lung surface through the VATS port (Fig. 1C). Using digital palpation to identify subtle pleural landmarks, such as fine surface vessels, fissure contours, or pleural pigmentation, they verbally communicated the presumed location of the target lesion. To ensure patient safety, no physical marking or instrumentation was permitted. An attending thoracic surgeon, blinded to group allocation, subsequently attempted to localize the lesion based solely on the participant’s verbal guidance. Localization was deemed successful if the identified lesion matched preoperative imaging findings in both position and size.

Learner confidence and training satisfaction

At the conclusion of Phases 2 through 4, participants completed Likert-scale questionnaires (1 = disagree, 5 = agree) to assess their self-perceived confidence in applying relevant knowledge and skills. Survey domains included CT interpretation, surgical planning, tactile localization, and intraoperative execution. All confidence responses were submitted before any performance feedback was provided to avoid bias. Following the completion of all four phases, participants in the LMG also completed a satisfaction survey evaluating the perceived realism of the simulation model, clarity of instruction, clinical applicability, and overall educational value in enhancing spatial reasoning. Satisfaction responses were also recorded using a 5-point Likert scale and submitted prior to disclosure of individual or group results.

All questionnaire items were developed by the instructional team based on established simulation-based education evaluation frameworks. Internal consistency was acceptable for both the confidence scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.76) and the satisfaction scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.84), supporting their reliability. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of self-reported measures, including potential response bias and the imperfect correlation between perceived confidence and actual performance.

Statistical analysis

Although a formal a priori power calculation was not performed due to the exploratory nature of the study, the primary outcome was defined as the total score of the CAQs at Level 2, which measured CT-to-structure mapping and surgical planning ability. Secondary outcomes included the number of nodules localized in the simulated thoracoscopic task (Level 3), as well as learner-reported confidence and satisfaction scores. We report between-group effect sizes (Cohen’s d or relative risks) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to aid interpretation. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons due to the structured, sequential nature of the assessments across Miller’s Pyramid. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed data and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected cell counts. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization and figure preparation were conducted using GraphPad Prism, version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Simulated nodule localization (Level 3)

All 67 participants proceeded to Phase 3, which assessed their ability to demonstrate tactile localization skills in a simulated thoracoscopic environment. As presented in Table 2, participants in the LMG localized a significantly greater number of nodules compared with those in the CG (2.09 ± 0.71 vs. 1.45 ± 0.87; p = 0.002), with a moderate effect size (d = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.30–1.30). Notably, 26.5% of participants in the LMG successfully localized all three nodules, compared with only 12.1% in the CG. Conversely, complete localization failure (0 nodules) occurred in only 2.9% of LMG participants, whereas 12.1% of CG participants were unable to localize any nodules.

Intraoperative localization performance and subgroup analyses (Level 4)

A total of 44 trainees advanced to Phase 4, including 29 in the LMG and 15 in the CG. As summarized in Table 2, the overall intraoperative localization success rate was slightly higher in the LMG than in the CG (68.97% vs. 66.67%). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.877). The relative risk was 1.03 (95% CI: 0.68 to 1.72). To further investigate potential determinants of localization success, subgroup analyses were conducted based on nodule type and depth from the pleural surface (Figure 3). When stratified by nodule type, participants in the LMG consistently demonstrated higher success rates across all categories. For solid nodules, the success rate was 88.9% in the LMG versus 80.0% in the CG. For mixed GGOs, LMG participants achieved a success rate of 75.0%, compared with 60.0% in the CG. For pure GGOs, the success rate was 62.5% in the LMG and 20.0% in the CG. Similarly, the LMG outperformed the CG across both depth-based strata. Among nodules located less than 1.0 cm from the pleural surface, the success rate was 76.5% in the LMG and 62.5% in the CG. Self-perceived confidence and training satisfaction

Self-reported confidence ratings across Miller’s Levels 2 to 4 are detailed in Table 2. At Level 2, participants in the LMG reported significantly greater confidence in performing preoperative assessment tasks compared to those in the CG (3.53 ± 0.83 vs. 3.06 ± 0.70; p = 0.015; d = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.12–1.10). A similar pattern was observed at Level 3, where LMG participants expressed higher confidence in performing spatial and tactile localization under simulated thoracoscopic conditions (3.29 ± 0.68 vs. 2.94 ± 0.56; p = 0.022; d = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.07–1.05). At Level 4, although confidence scores remained higher in the LMG (3.48 ± 0.74 vs. 3.07 ± 0.59), the difference approached but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.051; d= 0.58, 95% CI: −0.05–1.22).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a systematically structured tactile simulation program, based on high-fidelity anatomical lung models, can effectively bridge the gap between theoretical instruction and operative thoracoscopic practice. By providing stepwise and contextually relevant exposure to tactile–spatial reasoning tasks, the program significantly enhanced trainees’ ability to localize pulmonary nodules. Improvements were observed across multiple educational domains, including cognitive understanding, procedural performance, and the transfer of skills to real-world clinical settings.

Integration of simulation across Miller’s competency levels

The training program was deliberately aligned with Miller’s hierarchical framework of clinical competence. At the foundational “Knows” level, no significant differences in baseline anatomical knowledge were observed between the groups, suggesting that participants began with comparable theoretical preparation. However, clear performance differences emerged at the “Knows How” level. Participants in the LMG significantly outperformed those in the control group on tasks requiring CT-to-anatomy correlation and surgical planning. These findings underscore the educational value of incorporating high-fidelity anatomical models to enhance radiologic-anatomic integration, which is an essential component of surgical reasoning. The observed improvements are consistent with prior research indicating that tactile and visual scaffolding can help novices to grasp complex visual information and tasks [16].

At the “Shows How” level, participants in the LMG demonstrated significantly superior performance in simulated thoracoscopic nodule localization compared with those in the control group. The markedly lower incidence of localization failure in the LMG suggests that structured tactile simulation training effectively promotes the integration of spatial reasoning with motor execution. This finding underscores the pedagogical value of simulation-based education in facilitating the transition from cognitive understanding to procedural application. It is further supported by existing literature, which identifies spatial–motor coordination as a critical transitional milestone in advancing from foundational declarative knowledge to procedural competence in surgical training [17].

Real-world transfer of skills

Only trainees who demonstrated adequate proficiency at Level 3 were advanced to the Level 4 intraoperative evaluation. While this phase offers preliminary insights into real-world skill transfer, its selective inclusion of higher-performing participants likely introduced a ceiling effect and reduced between-group variability, thereby attenuating the magnitude of observable differences. Although the LMG achieved a higher intraoperative localization success rate than the CG, this difference did not reach statistical significance, potentially due to both selection bias and limited sample size, which may have constrained statistical power to detect moderate effects.

Despite these limitations, exploratory subgroup analyses yielded valuable educational insights. The tactile simulation appeared particularly beneficial for anatomically challenging lesions, such as pure GGOs and deeper subpleural nodules, which are inherently difficult to detect through manual palpation due to their low tactile density and subpleural positioning, as previously reported [3]. These findings suggest that high-fidelity tactile training may enhance surgeons’ intrinsic spatial and haptic reasoning, offering practical value in settings where advanced intraoperative localization technologies are unavailable or infeasible. Nevertheless, the Level 4 outcomes should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Cognitive load and theoretical underpinnings

The observed progressive improvement aligns with cognitive load theory [18, 19]. The simulation’s structured and multimodal design likely minimized extraneous cognitive load while gradually increasing intrinsic task complexity. This approach promotes schema acquisition and deep learning by matching task difficulty with learners’ processing capacity. In addition, the inclusion of anatomically varied scenarios, such as differences in nodule type, size, and depth, required participants to adapt to novel challenges rather than rely on rote procedural repetition. This variability fosters pattern recognition, a cognitive hallmark of expert-level clinical reasoning.

Situated learning theory offers a complementary perspective [20, 21]. This theory posits that procedural learning is optimized in authentic, context-rich environments. In this study, thoracoscopic localization was performed using high-fidelity lung models under realistic spatial constraints. This immersive environment likely enhanced contextual encoding and procedural internalization. The superior performance of the LMG, particularly in detecting deeper or less tactilely distinct lesions, may be attributable to this increased ecological validity. By contrast, virtual simulators often fail to reproduce the sensory–motor integration required for intraoperative palpation, limiting their utility in preparing trainees for the demands of complex thoracoscopic procedures [22].

Self-perception and affective outcomes

Self-reported confidence scores closely paralleled objective performance improvements across Miller’s Levels 2 through 4. Participants in the LMG consistently reported greater confidence in preoperative planning, simulated nodule localization, and intraoperative decision-making. Although the difference at Level 4 did not reach statistical significance, the overall trend suggests that simulation-based tactile training enhances not only technical competence but also psychological readiness for clinical practice. Prior research has shown that learner confidence can serve both as a surrogate indicator of competence and as a facilitator of knowledge transfer, particularly when simulation activities are closely aligned with real-world procedural contexts [23].

Learner satisfaction ratings further supported the training’s educational value. Among the five assessed domains, participants expressed consistently favorable perceptions, with the highest scores assigned to the overall educational value of the tactile simulation. High ratings were also observed for improvements in spatial reasoning and for the clinical relevance of the training content, indicating a strong perceived alignment between the simulation experience and actual thoracoscopic practice. Slightly lower, though still favorable, scores were given for the development of tactile localization skills and the anatomical realism of the lung model. Together, these findings support the program’s pedagogical effectiveness and highlight its potential for broader curricular integration.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, although the 3D-printed lung models provided high anatomical fidelity, they did not replicate variations in tissue consistency or pathological features such as fibrosis, emphysema, or calcification. This lack of tactile heterogeneity may limit the generalizability of the findings to clinical scenarios involving nodules with atypical physical characteristics. Second, although all participants received standardized didactic instruction, the study did not control for individual differences in baseline visuospatial aptitude or haptic perceptual ability. These cognitive and sensorimotor factors are known to influence performance in simulation-based training and may have contributed to interindividual variability in outcomes. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up data limits the ability to draw conclusions about the durability of skill acquisition. It remains unclear whether the observed improvements in tactile localization are retained over time or effectively translate into autonomous intraoperative performance in more complex or unsupervised surgical settings.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that simulation-based tactile training using anatomically accurate, the high-fidelity lung model can significantly enhance technical performance across multiple stages of thoracic surgical education. In addition to improving localization accuracy in simulated settings, the training enabled meaningful transfer of tactile skills to intraoperative practice, particularly in cases involving anatomically complex lesions such as deep or subsolid nodules. Beyond lesion localization, the high-fidelity lung model holds promise for more advanced surgical training purposes, including simulation of wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy. Given its anatomical realism, modularity, and reproducibility, it may serve as a viable alternative to conventional training platforms based on cadaveric or animal models [24, 25].
