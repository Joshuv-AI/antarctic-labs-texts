Migratory Bird and Marine Mammal Surveillance Fails to Find Evidence for an HPAI H5N1 2.3.4.4b Incursion Into Australia in 2025
Authors: Michelle Wille, Tobias A Ross, Robyn Atkinson, David B Boyle, Maureen Christie, Meagan L Dewar, Tegan K Douglas, Rachael Gray, Birgita Hansen, Rosalind Jessop, Lindall R Kidd, Ila Marks, Patrick Mileto, Eric Miller, Matthew J Neave, Sara Ryding, Duncan R Sutherland, Hui Yu, Marcel Klaassen
Published: 2026

High pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b has been devastating for animals globally [1, 2]. It has caused the destruction of hundreds of millions of poultry [3], an enormous economic impact on dairy farmers in the United States [4], has caused population‐level declines in marine mammals and avian species [1, 2] and more than > 70 human cases since 2021 [5]. To date, HPAI H5N1 has spread to all continents, with the exception of Oceania [6]. Prior to 2024, the most likely route of viral introduction into Australia was considered to be with migratory birds along the East Asian‐Australasian Flyway, with millions of shorebirds and seabirds arriving each spring [7] (Figure S1). HPAI H5N1 outbreaks have been ongoing in Southeast Asia for decades [3], which is also an important region for incoming migratory shorebirds to stop on their migrations [8]. With the long‐distance spread of HPAI H5N1 through the Southern Ocean in spring 2024 [9], there is a plausible risk for virus arrival to Oceania with seabirds in the Southern Ocean.

Herein we report the results of our fourth year of migratory bird surveillance, coinciding with the spring migration of wild birds in 2025. We investigated 847 migratory birds of the order Charadriiformes and Procellariiformes and 18 resident Charadriiformes in October–December 2025. Specifically, we captured and sampled Short‐tailed Shearwaters (n = 254) at a breeding colony on Phillip Island, Victoria, upon their arrival from the northern Pacific. We also sampled 13 Asian‐breeding migratory shorebird species at major nonbreeding sites in Exmouth Gulf (n = 147) and Peel Estuary (n = 83) in Western Australia, Western Port Bay (n = 236) and Peterborough (n = 17) in Victoria, Canunda (n = 19) and Nene Valley (n = 109) in South Australia (Table 1, Figure S2). To reflect the potential for HPAI H5N1 to arrive via the ‘southern’ Antarctic route, we also sampled Australian Sea Lions ( Neophoca cinerea ) in Seal Bay, South Australia (n = 38) during spring, and short‐tailed shearwater chicks in April (n = 59) (as adult Short‐tailed Shearwaters are foraging for their chicks in the Antarctic during this time period) (Table 1, Figures S1 and S2). For the sampling, animal ethics approvals were obtained from Deakin University animal ethics committee (AEC; B28‐2023), Phillip Island Nature Parks AEC (9.2024), Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development, Western Australian Wildlife Animal Ethics Committee (WAEC 25‐10‐66 and WAEC 22‐10‐102), University of Sydney Animal Ethics Committee (2025/AE00018) and Federation University Animal Ethics Committee (23‐005).

Of all 924 avian samples collected, one Red‐necked Stint ( Calidris ruficollis ) tested positive (sample ID 21482, Ct = 35.8), and one Red‐necked Stint was indeterminate (sample ID 21518, Ct = 38.7) for influenza A virus by qPCR, following [6]. Both samples were negative for H5 and H7 using qPCR (as per 6), and sample ID 21482 was confirmed as H3N7 through full genome sequencing (as per 6). All segments were interrogated using blastn, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using BEAST using approaches and reference sequences reported in [6]. The H3N7 virus reported here was similar (HA 99.6%, NA 99.4%) to H3N7 viruses detected in shorebirds in Australia in spring 2024 [6]. These viruses are reassortants, with some segments having been in viruses detected in shorebirds > 10 years (PA, NP, M, NS), from Australian waterfowl (HA, NA) and from Asian wild birds (PB2, PB1) (Figure S3). All of the samples from Australian Sea Lions were negative for influenza A virus.

Sixty‐seven serum samples tested positive for anti‐NP antibodies using a commercial ELISA (given an S/N cut‐off of 0.6), with Red‐necked Stint (42/323) and adult Short‐tailed Shearwater (21/254) seroprevalence falling within the previously reported range [6] All positive serum samples with enough remaining volume (n = 61) were negative on a subsequent hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay using a lineage 2.3.4.4b candidate vaccine virus A/Astrakhan/3212/2020(H5N8), a 6:2 recombinant virus on an A/Puerto Rico/8/1934(H1N1)(PR8) backbone with the multi‐basic cleavage site removed. The HI also included a Eurasian lineage LPAI H5N3 now circulating in Australia (A/wild bird/Queensland/P23‐02457‐0051/2023) [10]. In addition to the samples tested here, there is no evidence for HPAI H5N1 in any samples collected for active wild bird surveillance or passive surveillance in Australia, with the exception of Heard Island, an Australian sub‐Antarctic Island [11].

Multiple factors determine the likelihood of HPAI H5N1 reaching Australia, including the movement patterns of migratory wild birds, the capacity of infected birds to migrate or fly long distances and the extent of active virus circulation at staging sites used by long‐distance migrants. What remains poorly understood is how Australia has continued to avoid an HPAI H5N1 incursion given continued detections in neighbouring countries and demonstrated ability for animals to move this virus thousands of kilometres. Prior to 2025, risk assessments were focussed on virus arrival from the north (i.e., Asia) [7], however, the considerable expansion of HPAI H5N1 through the Southern Ocean makes an incursion route through the Southern Ocean plausible. On Heard Island HPAI H5N1 was detected in Southern Elephant Seals ( Mirounga leonina ), Antarctic Fur Seals ( Arctocephalus gazella ) as well as penguin species (Gentoo Penguins Pygoscelis papua ) on Heard Island [11]. Kerguelen Island, only ~450 km away, had an expanded species profile, but importantly, Brown Skuas ( Stercorarius antarcticus ), Southern Giant Petrels ( Macronectes giganteus ), Northern Giant Petrels ( Macronectes halli ), albatrosses, shearwaters and Kelp Gulls ( Larus dominicanus ) were observed foraging on dead carcasses [9]. While the species responsible for virus spread through the Southern Ocean is unknown, there are a diversity of species capable of long‐distance movement, including both seabirds (e.g., albatross and giant petrels) and marine mammals (e.g., Southern Elephant Seals) [12], which may be important in future spread to Australia and New Zealand's sub‐Antarctic Islands.

With the risk of HPAI H5N1, Australia has undertaken considerable preparedness activities, including strengthening biosecurity and developing and bolstering response plans [13]. Surveillance remains a critical tool for HPAI H5N1 response, with early detection and rapid response being critical to mitigate the impacts of this virus on animal, environment and human health.
