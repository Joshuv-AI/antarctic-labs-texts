Prognostic utility of sarcopenia and frailty on postoperative complication and mortality in older intermediate risk cancer surgery
Authors: Jung-Yeon Choi, Sang-Hoon Ahn, Heung-Kwon Oh, Duck Woo Kim, Yoo-Seok Yoon, Sung-Bum Kang, Yun-Suhk Suh, Kwang-il Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConditions such as sarcopenia and frailty, which become more prevalent with age, significantly influence the outcomes of older patients with cancer undergoing surgery. The aim of this study was to identify the combined effects of sarcopenia and frailty in predicting postoperative complications and mortality.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at a university hospital from May 2019 to December 2020 and included patients aged ≥ 65 years undergoing intermediate-risk cancer surgery. Sarcopenia was defined according to the Asia Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 guideline, and frailty was assessed using the Multidimensional Frailty Score (> 5) from comprehensive geriatric assessments. The primary outcome measured was composite postoperative complications, with secondary outcomes including one-year mortality, overall mortality, and total hospital stay.ResultsWe included 553 patients (mean age: 75.6 years). Patients with both conditions (21.1%) or frailty alone (19.3%) experienced significantly higher rates of postoperative complications and mortality than those in the normal group (9.2%) and those with only sarcopenia (14.6%). Multivariate analysis confirmed that frailty alone or both sarcopenia and frailty were independent predictors of adverse postoperative outcomes and increased mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival curves further highlighted the significant impact of the frailty-only and combined sarcopenia-frailty groups on survival.ConclusionFrailty was a strong independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes and mortality in older people undergoing intermediate-risk cancer surgery. Sarcopenia alone did not significantly increase this risk, and its coexistence with frailty did not independently amplify the risk beyond frailty itself. Evaluating frailty through comprehensive geriatric assessment might have helped improve surgical risk stratification and perioperative care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07400-2.Keywords: Comprehensive geriatric assessment, Frailty, Mortality, Postoperative complication, Sarcopenia

Background

As the global population ages, the incidence of cancer diagnoses in the geriatric population is expected to increase because most cancers become more prevalent with age [1]. Sarcopenia and frailty also become increasingly common with age [2]. Therefore, frailty or sarcopenia can significantly influence the outcomes in older patients with cancer undergoing surgery. Sarcopenia (ICD-10-CM, M62.84) is an age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass, loss of muscle strength, and reduced physical performance with grave physiological and clinical consequences [3]. Frailty is a biological syndrome characterized by decreased reserve and resistance to stressors resulting from cumulative declines across multiple physiological systems, leading to vulnerability to adverse outcomes [4].

Previous systematic reviews have shown that frailty in older surgical patients predicts postoperative complications, mortality (1 month to 1 year), and prolonged hospital stays, regardless of the assessment tool [5]. The multidimensional frailty score (MFS) was calculated from the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) and uses the concept of cumulative deficit to assess frailty. The MFS was developed and validated to predict adverse outcomes and short-term mortality more precisely than chronological age, conventional risk stratification tools, or physical performance parameters after low-to-intermediate cancer surgery [6–8].

According to a previous systematic literature search and meta-analysis, surgical patients with gastrointestinal cancer and sarcopenia experienced significantly higher rates of major and total complications, 30-day mortality, and longer hospital stays than patients without sarcopenia [9, 10]. Sarcopenia diagnosis is recommended using three components: muscle strength (hand grip strength), physical performance (gait speed, 5-chair stand test, or short physical performance parameters), and appendicular skeletal muscle mass (measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or bioelectrical impedance analysis) [3]. However, many previous studies used alternative imaging-based methods, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to evaluate muscle mass. This approach is often employed in oncology populations, where routine preoperative imaging is available, and CT/MRI–derived cross-sectional muscle area is considered the gold standard for assessing muscle mass.

Some previous studies evaluated the association between frailty and sarcopenia components and adverse outcomes after geriatric surgery or the effects of sarcopenia and frailty on postoperative recovery in older patients [11, 12]. However, no previous studies considered both frailty and sarcopenia components to examine geriatric composite complications and 1-year mortality. We designed a retrospective cohort study to examine the predictive ability of combined sarcopenia and frailty on composite postoperative complications and mortality in older patients with intermediate-risk cancer surgery. We hypothesized that the combination of sarcopenia and frailty is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications and 1-year mortality.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a university hospital between May 23, 2019, and December 31, 2020. Older patients (aged ≥ 65 years) referred for a preoperative CGA were included, while those with incomplete sarcopenia or frailty assessment were excluded. In this study, intermediate-risk surgery was defined according to the 2014 European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Anaesthesiology (ESC/ESA) Guidelines on non-cardiac surgery, which stratify surgical procedures based on the estimated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [13]. According to these guidelines, intermediate-risk surgeries were those associated with an estimated perioperative cardiac risk of approximately 1–5%. This study included elective non-cardiac surgical procedures classified as intermediate risk, such as gastrointestinal resections, colorectal surgeries, hepatopancreatobiliary procedures, and other major abdominal surgeries requiring general anesthesia. Low-risk procedures and high-risk or emergency surgeries were excluded from the study population. Low-risk surgery was defined as procedures associated with a perioperative cardiac risk of < 1%, including breast and thyroid surgeries, as well as minor orthopedic and minor gynecologic operations. High-risk surgery was defined as procedures with perioperative cardiac risk of > 5%, including major vascular surgery, pneumonectomy, or transplantation.

Definition of sarcopenia

In this study, the definition and cutoff values for sarcopenia were based on the AWGS 2019 guideline, which provides criteria specific to Asian populations [3]. Muscle mass was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody770; Inbody Co., LTD, Seoul, Korea). A Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI) below 7.0 kg/m2 (men) or 5.7 kg/m2 (women) was defined as decreased muscle mass. SMI was calculated as appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by height squared (kg/m²), according to the AWGS 2019 guideline. Muscle strength was assessed based on handgrip strength using a Jamar Plus + Digital Hand Dynamometer (Patterson Medical). Handgrip strength < 28 kg (men) or < 18 kg (women) was defined as low grip strength. Physical performance was evaluated using gait speed, with a speed below 1.0 m/s defined as slow gait. Sarcopenia was described as one of the conditions [1] decreased muscle mass and low grip strength, or [2] reduced muscle mass and slow gait speed, or [3] slow gait speed, low grip strength, and decreased muscle mass.

Definition of frailty

Frailty is an age-related syndrome characterized by a decline in multiple physiological systems and vulnerability to external stress, resulting in adverse health outcomes. The accumulation of deficit model posits that frailty is an age-associated vulnerability that considers symptoms, signs, diseases, and disabilities as deficits, which are combined into a frailty index score system [14]. The MFS was developed and validated as a frailty assessment system for older intermediate-risk general surgical patients [6, 8]. The MFS was calculated using data from the CGA and laboratory tests with established cut-off values [8]. The MFS considers the presence of (1) malignant disease, (2) comorbidities, (3) serum albumin level, (4) activities of daily living (ADL), (5) instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), (6) cognitive function, (7) risk of delirium, (8) nutritional assessment, and (9) midarm circumference. Each item was scored from 0 to 2 according to predefined criteria, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. The MFS ranged from 0 to 15, with scores greater than 5 indicating frailty status.

Comprehensive geriatric assessment and laboratory variables

The CGA was used to evaluate comorbidities, medication-related problems, physical and psychosocial function, nutritional status, and the risk of postoperative delirium. Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index. Physical function was evaluated through ADLs and IADLs. Using the Modified Barthel Index and the Lawton and Brody Index, respectively. ADL dependency was defined using the Modified Barthel Index, with dependency classified as a score < 5 in men and < 8 in women. IADL dependency was assessed using the Lawton and Brody Index and was defined as a total score < 100. Psychosocial function was determined by evaluating cognitive status and mood using the Mini-Mental Status Examination in the Korean Version of the CERAD Assessment Packet (MMSE-KC) and the Korean Geriatric Depression Scale. Nutritional status was evaluated using the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), and the risk of postoperative delirium was determined using the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC). The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification score, retrieved from medical records, was used to assess patients’ preoperative physical status and to estimate surgical risk based on overall health [15]. Laboratory parameters were extracted from electronic medical records, which included white blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, calcium, phosphorus, glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid, total protein, albumin, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, sodium, potassium, and chloride.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was composite postoperative complications, including postoperative infection (urinary tract infection or pneumonia), delirium, unplanned ICU admission, pulmonary thromboembolism, and deep vein thrombosis. Secondary outcomes included 1-year and overall mortality, and length of hospital stay. Mortality data up to December 12, 2022, were obtained from the Ministry of Security and Public Administration.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. Categorical data were reported as frequencies (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Post-hoc analysis was performed using the Bonferroni adjustment in cases of statistically significant differences among multiple groups. Data without information regarding frailty or sarcopenia were excluded. Statistically substantial variables (e.g., age and sex) identified in the univariate analysis were selected as adjustment variables to filter out non-zero characteristic factors. Sex-related comparisons were performed using males as the reference category, and relative risks for females were calculated relative to male patients. Variables used to calculate frailty and those that were clinically closely related were excluded. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to estimate the associations between sarcopenia, frailty, and postoperative composite complications. Model 1 was adjusted for the demographic characteristics (age and sex). Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, number of medications, and ASA. Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, number of medications, ASA, and levels of blood urea nitrate, creatinine, and hemoglobin. Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no significant multicollinearity was detected (all VIFs < 2.0). Hazard ratios (HRs) for 1-year and all-cause mortality, stratified by frailty and sarcopenia status, were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to construct survival curves, and log-rank tests were used to assess statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using PASW Statistics software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc.). Statistical significance levels were two-sided, set at 0.05. Statistical significance for pairwise comparisons was assessed using the Bonferroni correction, with an adjusted threshold of p < 0.008 (0.05/6).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval number: B-1911-577-111). The consent requirement was waived because this study posed minimal potential harm to participants and because obtaining informed consent from all past surgical patients for a retrospective study design is impractical and can lead to selection bias.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From May 23, 2019, to December 31, 2020, 1005 patients aged > 65 years underwent surgery and CGA. A total of 452 patients who underwent low-risk surgeries were excluded, leaving 553 patients included in this analysis. The mean age was 75.6 years (SD = 5.27), and 57.1% were men. The mean follow-up time was 868.3 days (SD = 273.1 days), and the median follow-up time was 907.0 days (interquartile range: 276.5 days). The prevalence of sarcopenia was 25.0% (n = 138), and that of frailty was 36% (n = 199). Patients without sarcopenia or frailty (Group N) constituted 55.3% of the total population (n = 306). Patients with sarcopenia only (Group S) accounted for 8.7% (n = 48), and those with frailty only (Group F) accounted for 19.7% (n = 109). Patients with both sarcopenia and frailty (Group SF) comprised 16.3% of the total population (n = 90).

Clinical characteristics according to frailty and sarcopenia status

Several variables showed statistically significant differences across the diagnostic groups defined by frailty and sarcopenia status (Table 1). These included age (p < 0.001), sex (p = 0.005), BMI (p < 0.001), prevalence of obesity (p < 0.001), and ASA classification (p = 0.003). Markers of nutritional status, including MNA scores (p < 0.001), serum protein levels (p < 0.001), and albumin levels (p < 0.001), differed significantly across the groups. Among laboratory variables, hemoglobin (p < 0.001), platelet count (p = 0.010), and total cholesterol levels (p = 0.001) also differed significantly. Functional status indicators, including grip strength (p < 0.001) and gait speed (p < 0.001), showed significant differences among the groups. Among CGA variables, Charlson’s Comorbidity Index (p < 0.001), number of medications (p < 0.001), MMSE-KC (p < 0.001), SGDS-K (p < 0.001), and multidimensional frailty score (p < 0.001) differed significantly according to frailty and/or sarcopenia status.

Group comparisons according to frailty and sarcopenia status

When comparing Group N and Group SF, patients in Group SF were older (p < 0.001), had a lower BMI (p < 0.001), and had a lower prevalence of obesity (p < 0.001). Hemoglobin (p < 0.001), serum protein (p < 0.001), and albumin levels (p < 0.001) were significantly lower in Group SF. Group SF also demonstrated lower MMSE-KC scores (p < 0.001), higher SGDS-K scores (p < 0.001), lower MNA scores (p < 0.001), and higher multidimensional frailty scores (p < 0.001). In addition, low grip strength (p < 0.001) and slow gait speed (p < 0.001) were more frequently observed in Group SF.

In comparisons between Group F and Group S, patients in Group F had lower albumin levels (p < 0.001), higher Charlson’s Comorbidity Index scores (p = 0.001), lower MNA scores (p < 0.001), and higher multidimensional frailty scores (p < 0.001). Low grip strength (p = 0.001) and slow gait speed (p < 0.001) were also more common in Group F than in Group S.

When comparing Group SF and Group S, patients in Group SF had a lower BMI (p = 0.004), lower albumin levels (p = 0.005), lower MMSE-KC scores (p < 0.001), lower MNA scores (p < 0.001), and higher multidimensional frailty scores (p < 0.001).

Finally, when comparing Group SF and Group F, patients in Group SF were older (p = 0.004), had a lower BMI (p = 0.002), and had a lower prevalence of obesity (p = 0.002). Group SF also had lower MNA scores (p = 0.005) and a higher prevalence of low grip strength (p < 0.001) and slow gait speed (p < 0.001).

Association of sarcopenia and frailty status with postoperative outcomes

Compared with Group N, Group F had higher rates of postoperative complications (19.3% vs. 9.2%, p = 0.005), 1-year mortality (11.9% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.004), and overall mortality (30.3% vs. 11.8%, p < 0.001). Postoperative complications (21.1% vs. 9.2%, p = 0.002) and overall mortality (28.9% vs. 11.8%, p < 0.001) were also more frequent in Group SF than in Group N (Table 1).

Chi-square analyses demonstrated significant differences in the prevalence of composite postoperative complications and 1-year all-cause mortality between the normal group and the frailty-only group, as well as between the normal group and the group with both sarcopenia and frailty (Fig. 1).

Multivariable logistic regression for postoperative complication

In the multivariate logistic analysis Model 1, sarcopenia and frailty [odds ratio (OR), 2.392; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.212–4.717], frailty only (OR, 2.330; 95% CI, 1.243–4.368), age (OR, 1.053; 95% CI, 1.003–1.106), and sex (OR, 0.588; 95% CI, 0.348–0.994) were independently correlated with postoperative complications. In multivariable analysis Model 2, sarcopenia and frailty [OR, 2.284; 95% CI, 1.151–4.531], frailty only (OR, 2.127; 95% CI, 1.121–4.034), and the number of medications (OR, 1.075; 95% CI, 1.011–1.143) were independently associated with composite postoperative complications. With full adjustment, in Model 3, sarcopenia and frailty (OR, 2.176; 95% CI, 1.069–4.428), age (OR, 1.054; 95% CI, 1.002–1.108), sex (OR, 0.564; 95% CI, 0.327–0.975), and the number of medications (OR, 1.077; 95% CI, 1.012–1.146) remained significant predictors of postoperative complications (Table 2).

Multivariable cox regression for 1-year mortality

In the multivariate Cox regression analysis for 1-year all-cause mortality, Model 1 showed that sarcopenia and frailty (HR, 3.760; 95% CI, 1.559–9.067), frailty only (HR, 3.644; 95% CI, 1.644–8.077), and sex (HR, 0.495; 95% CI, 0.249–0.983) remained significant. In Model 2, sarcopenia and frailty (HR, 3.519; 95% CI, 1.453–8.526) and frailty only (HR, 3.364; 95% CI, 1.503–7.532) were independently associated with increased risk of 1-year all-cause mortality. With full adjustment for model 3, sarcopenia and frailty (HR, 2.656; 95% CI, 1.064–6.628), sex (HR, 0.448; 95% CI, 0.215–0.871), and hemoglobin level (HR, 0.808; 95% CI, 0.678–0.953) were statistically associated with 1-year all-cause mortality (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we found that older patients with frailty or both sarcopenia and frailty had poor postoperative health-related outcomes, indicated by a higher incidence of postoperative complications, 1-year mortality, and overall mortality. Age, sex, and number of medications were key factors in predicting postoperative complications. Additionally, sex and hemoglobin levels were crucial in predicting mortality following intermediate-risk cancer surgery. In the survival analysis, which examined 1-year and all-cause mortality over an mean follow-up period of 868.3 days (SD 273.1 days), the survival curves of the normal population were similar to those of the group with sarcopenia only. Conversely, the group with only frailty and the group with both frailty and sarcopenia demonstrated significantly worse survival outcomes than the normal group and the sarcopenia-only group. Furthermore, the survival outcomes in the frailty-only group were comparable to those observed in the group with both frailty and sarcopenia.

Our study confirmed frailty to be a powerful independent risk factor for postoperative complications, showing a similar trend with previous research findings. This alignment was supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis of 71 studies investigating the association between frailty and postoperative outcomes in patients with cancer. This analysis demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of postoperative complications (adjusted OR of 2.39) and increased long-term mortality (adjusted OR of 4.32) among patients with frailty. These results demonstrated a trend that was generally aligned with our study findings [16]. Another previous study highlighted that frailty could identify an elevated postoperative risk of complications and 30-day outcomes preoperatively, emphasizing consistency across various investigations into this issue [17].

Frailty is a reliable predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes. While our initial models suggested higher point estimates when frailty was combined with sarcopenia, our fully adjusted models showed that the coexistence of sarcopenia did not, beyond frailty itself, independently amplify risk to a statistically significant degree. This suggests that while sarcopenia is a physical manifestation of the frail phenotype, it may not necessarily denote an independently more severe state of risk than frailty alone in this specific surgical population. Regarding overall all-cause mortality, both the frailty-only group and the group with combined frailty and sarcopenia demonstrated a significantly higher risk compared with the normal group, and these associations remained significant after adjustment for ASA classification. This observation is generally aligned with the results of two previous studies that demonstrated a dose-response relationship between increasing frailty scores and the incidence of adverse outcomes. These studies provided a robust foundation for understanding how physiological weaknesses and increasing frailty severity lead to negative surgical outcomes [8, 18]. In our study, the presence of sarcopenia alone showed prognostic outcomes similar to those in the general patient population without sarcopenia or frailty. This result contrasts with those of previous studies, where sarcopenia effectively predicted postoperative complications, hospital readmission within 30 days, and mortality [19, 20]. The pathophysiology and etiology of frailty and sarcopenia are not fully understood, but they involve common processes such as chronic inflammation, a sedentary lifestyle, and malnutrition [21]. This discrepancy could be explained in two ways. First, sarcopenia may have been successfully predictive in other studies as part of the physical frailty phenotype. In a previous study identifying sarcopenia as a significant predictor of outcomes, the frailty associated with sarcopenia might have been the key factor, suggesting sarcopenia could serve as a surrogate marker of physical frailty. However, our study revealed that sarcopenia alone did not predict outcomes; instead, it only strongly predicts outcomes when combined with frailty. Another study demonstrated that sarcopenia was associated with all frailty domains [11]. Secondly, sarcopenia is defined as low muscle mass, weak muscle strength, or decreased physical function in the general population. In patients with sarcopenia alone, other intrinsic capacities may be well preserved, resulting in no significant statistical difference in outcomes compared to the normal population. Therefore, when identifying sarcopenia to determine the risk of adverse postoperative outcomes, it is crucial to conduct a multifaceted evaluation of frailty in patients with sarcopenia. Identifying the factors associated with sarcopenia and poor prognosis, such as anemia, poor nutrition, decreased cognitive function, polypharmacy, and frailty, and providing interventions for these components is essential.

This study demonstrated the impact of frailty and sarcopenia on postoperative morbidity and mortality among older adults undergoing intermediate-risk surgery. Although these operations were categorized as intermediate risk, postoperative outcomes may vary depending on the urgency of surgery and the specific organ system involved. In this study, only elective surgeries were included, as CGA could not be performed in emergency settings due to time restrictions. To account for potential heterogeneity among surgical procedures, an additional analysis was conducted, adjusting for surgical category (upper gastrointestinal, lower gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary/pancreatic, and others). The adjustment for surgery type did not materially alter the main findings regarding postoperative complications and one-year mortality (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), suggesting that the associations between frailty, sarcopenia, and adverse postoperative outcomes remained robust after adjusting for surgical types.

The strength of this study was comparing the predictive power of sarcopenia and frailty for adverse postoperative outcomes across four distinct groups: a normal population, a population with only sarcopenia, a population with only frailty, and a group with both frailty and sarcopenia. By analyzing these groups, we determined that the sarcopenia group was not homogeneous and included subgroups with prognoses not significantly different from those of the normal group, as well as those with very poor prognoses. To further compare the frailty-only group with patients who had both frailty and sarcopenia, additional analysis was conducted with a full adjustment model. Patients with coexisting frailty and sarcopenia did not show a statistically significant increase in the risk of postoperative complications, 1-year mortality, or overall mortality compared with patients with frailty alone. These findings suggested that, within this cohort, the coexistence of sarcopenia among frail patients was not independently associated with a statistically significant excess risk beyond frailty itself after comprehensive adjustment for confounders. However, the wide confidence intervals indicated potential limited statistical power, and the possibility of a modest additive effect could not be exclude. Further studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are warranted to clarify the incremental prognostic impact of sarcopenia in frail surgical patients.

This study had several limitations. First, the analysis revealed that the group with only sarcopenia was relatively small, comprising only 8.7% of the total population. This small sample size might have limited the statistical power to detect differences or effects specifically attributable to sarcopenia alone, potentially affecting the generalizability and robustness of the conclusions drawn for this subgroup. Second, as a retrospective cohort study conducted at a university hospital, there were inherent limitations related to data completeness, potential biases in the collection process, difficulty in controlling for all potential confounding variables, and limited generalizability. Importantly, our cohort consisted of patients who were referred to the geriatric center for preoperative geriatric evaluation. Accordingly, selection bias was possible, as very fit patients for whom surgeons considered CGA unnecessary might have been less likely to be referred, while severely disabled or bedridden patients who could not attend an outpatient CGA visit might also have been underrepresented. In addition, some patients could not undergo complete sarcopenia or frailty assessments and were excluded. Although the specific reasons for incomplete evaluations could not be verified retrospectively, these were likely related to outpatient-test feasibility or clinical constraints, such as inability to ambulate independently, contraindications to conduct BIA, or other clinical circumstances that precluded full testing. Furthermore, certain postoperative outcomes, such as minor surgical-site infections or transient complications, might have been underreported or identified. Third, because this study used retrospectively collected data without prior sample-size estimation, the statistical power may have been limited for certain subgroup comparisons. The relatively small number of participants with sarcopenia alone (8.7%) might have contributed to wide confidence intervals and non-significant findings despite potential clinical relevance. Even though the post-hoc analysis for the primary outcome and sarcopenia-frailty variable showed adequate power (0.86), the possibility of a Type II error could not be ruled out, and larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm these associations.

The risk of postoperative complications was twice as high in patients with both sarcopenia and frailty than in those with neither condition. Frailty and sarcopenia were each associated with worse postoperative outcomes, and the coexistence of both conditions showed a tendency toward greater risk. This study identified several patient-related risk factors for adverse outcomes after intermediate-risk cancer surgery, including age, sex, number of medications, anemia, and the differential impact of frailty alone, sarcopenia alone, and their coexistence.

Conclusions

In older people undergoing intermediate-risk cancer surgery, frailty was a strong independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes and mortality. Sarcopenia alone, however, did not significantly increase this risk, and its coexistence with frailty did not independently amplified risk beyond frailty itself in fully adjusted models. These findings suggested that evaluating frailty through CGA might help improve surgical risk stratification. Furthermore, early recognition and multidisciplinary management could potentially contribute to better perioperative care for this vulnerable population.
