Atomic-scale regulation of ion motion and phonon scattering: ALD-driven interface engineering for stabilizing β-Zn4Sb3
Authors: Shiyang He, Jun Li, Dominique Mattlat, Falk Röder, Siyuan Zhang, Xiang Zhang, Christina Scheu, Pingjun Ying, Ran He, Yongchen Rao, Dongdong Li, Amin Bahrami, Kornelius Nielsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal ion migration under operational gradients triggers irreversible decomposition and performance collapse in thermoelectric (TE) materials. β-Zn4Sb3 has high TE performance but suffers from severe zinc (Zn) ion migration under an external field. This work uses powder atomic layer deposition (pALD) to engineer atomic-scale zinc oxide (ZnO) interfaces that simultaneously suppress Zn ion migration and enhance phonon scattering. Through precise ZnO coatings (50 to 200 cycles), we create continuous barriers that immobilize interstitial Zn ions, eliminating Zn motion and inhibiting phase decomposition. Optimized 100 ALD cycle coatings reduce lattice thermal conductivity by >20% through intensified boundary-phonon scattering, yielding a stabilized, nondegrading figure of merit compared to uncoated performance. Crucially, the thermal stability of 100-ALD-cycle-coated sample persists through 39,260 thermal cycles under gradients of 220 kelvin, and Seebeck coefficient mapping exhibits a uniform distribution along temperature difference. Our approach establishes pALD as a promising atomic-level interface design in migration-prone TE materials, bridging high performance with long-term operational reliability.

INTRODUCTION

Mobile ions play an essential role in manipulating physical transports in some fields (1). The variation in the entropy within the crystal lattice leads to the migration of ions, which has been extensively investigated (2). Ion migration usually occurs in materials with lower ion mobility and originates from the electric field-driven degradation in lithium ion batteries and perovskite solar cells (3). In addition, ion migration could be used to form electrical conduction paths, such as oxygen vacancies, in memristors, which allows for the realization of filamentary connections between electrodes (4).

In addition, the motion of electrons and phonons, enabling direct heat-to-electricity conversion, has garnered substantial attention in clean energy research (5). Thermoelectric (TE) performance, governed by the interplay of electrical and thermal transport, is quantified by the dimensionless figure of merit zT (zT = S2T/ρκtot, where ρ, S, T, and κtot denote the electrical resistivity, Seebeck coefficient, absolute temperature, and total thermal conductivity, respectively) (5).

Inspired by the “phonon-liquid electron-crystal” (PLEC) concept, promising PLEC materials, such as Cu2Se and Ag2Se, feature a rigid sublattice framework alongside a liquid-like sublattice of highly mobile ions (5). This unique structure facilitates phonon transport–like liquid behavior while maintaining crystalline electronic properties. This dual nature enables remarkable phenomena, including anomalously reduced specific heat, temperature-independent ultralow lattice thermal conductivity (κlat), and high zT values in optimized systems.

Despite their outstanding performance, PLEC materials face critical stability challenges for practical long-term applications (6). The dynamic ionic sublattice, although beneficial for phonon scattering, facilitates the migration of mobile metal ions under thermal and electric field gradients and forms metallic impurities (6). This ion diffusion leads to surface metal segregation, which is extensively documented in Cu- and Zn-based PLEC systems (6). This ion diffusion alters the material’s stoichiometry, potentially triggering phase decomposition and degrading TE properties. Furthermore, surface metal deposition can compromise electrode contacts by inducing cracks, increasing interfacial resistance, and conversion efficiency (7). Thus, suppressing ion migration is pivotal to advancing reliable PLEC-based TE devices.

Among PLEC materials, β-Zn4Sb3 exemplifies mixed ionic-electronic conduction (8), serving as an ideal model system to investigate stability issues. Its weakly bonded interstitial Zn2+ ions enable ultralow thermal conductivity and exceptional TE performance in the range of 400 to 700 K (9). Iversen’s and Tang’s group systematically demonstrated the main decomposition reaction mechanism for Zn4Sb3 at temperatures from 473 K (9, 10). This decomposition is further accelerated by a thermal gradient or electric current, where the highly mobile Zn2+ ions migrate down the resulting electric field gradient, leading to decomposition into ZnSb in the Zn-poor region (positive electric potential) and growth of Zn whiskers in the Zn-rich region (negative electric potential) (11). Compared with other PLEC compounds, the mobility of Zn ions in Zn4Sb3 is exceptionally high with an activation energy of only 11 kJ mol−1 (12), which is lower than Cu+ in Cu2Se at 13.5 kJ mol−1 (13) and even approaches the value of 9.6 kJ mol−1 for the fast ion conductor of α-AgI (14). Thus, the Zn motion in Zn4Sb3 is more severe than the classic Cu-based TE systems (such as Cu2S or Cu2Se).

Recent advances have proposed that ion-blocking barriers can be introduced to keep the localized potential below the Zn metal reduction potential (Zn ion to Zn metal), as shown in Fig. 1. In general, three methods have been applied in Zn4Sb3 to explore the blocking effect: (i) doping-induced barrier potential: Elemental substitution at Zn sites creates barrier potentials, yet most dopants yield marginal thermal stability improvements (15); (ii) segmentation to lower critical potential: Segmentation (such as stainless steel foil) reduces Zn ion potential limitations but compromises TE performance and mechanical integrity due to interfacial thermal stresses (16); and (iii) incorporating secondary phases: Discontinuous interfaces from secondary phases introduce submicrometer barrier boundaries and ion scattering centers (11, 16–18). However, nonuniform phase distribution and insufficient interface continuity limit ion migration suppression. The above approaches collectively highlight the fundamental challenge: achieving continuous interface boundaries with nanometer scale that simultaneously restricts ionic diffusion while maintaining optimal TE performance.

Precise control of secondary phase composition and thickness is critical for enhancing TE stability but remains challenging (1). Atomic layer deposition (ALD) enables subnanometer precision in depositing thin films or nanoparticles, allowing atomic-scale tailoring of coating properties. Unlike chemical vapor deposition (CVD), ALD uses sequential, self-limiting surface reactions via alternating precursor pulses, yielding highly uniform and conformal coatings. This precision makes ALD ideal for uniform depositing engineered secondary phases on TE powders to improve stability, termed powder ALD (pALD) (19, 20).

The phenomenon of Zn migration is investigated and can be divided into two parts in common electrical characterization: (i) Zn ions migrate from inside to the surface of the samples only under high temperature due to the decomposition of Zn4Sb3, and (ii) Zn whiskers are distributed inhomogeneously along the external field direction (current/temperature difference applied) as shown Fig. 1A. In this study, first, the ALD coating has been deposited on the surface of Zn4Sb3 legs with a 300-nm ZnO layer. However, Zn ion movement cannot be inhibited by macroscale surface coatings alone, indicating that Zn ion migration originates within the Zn4Sb3 grain interiors (Fig. 1B). To impede Zn ion movement at atomic scale, the precise controlling of the ZnO layer with various cycle numbers has been deposited on the surface of Zn4Sb3 powders (Fig. 1C). We demonstrate that ZnO coatings via pALD achieve dual functionality: Nanometer-scale interfaces form physical impeding barriers that suppress decomposition of Zn4Sb3 and Zn ion migration under external fields while boundary scattering to reduce κlat by 21.4% at 673 K. Systematic optimization reveals 100-cycle ZnO coatings effectively suppress Zn migration across 127-day thermal cycling, as validated by spatial Seebeck mapping. This strategy outperforms conventional stabilization approaches by enabling continuous, conformal interfaces at atomic precision through bulk processing techniques.

RESULTS

Synthesis optimization and phase identification of pure Zn4Sb3

Because of the high vapor pressure of Zn, a high temperature of the pressing process may alter the initial composition of Zn4Sb3, which could degrade TE performance. In addition, as previously reported, the grain size must be carefully tuned, which requires a sufficiently small particle size (1). The smaller particle size not only benefits the number of impeding barriers induced by the ALD but also results in a substantial net improvement of electrical/thermal transport. To obtain the high TE performance in pure Zn4Sb3, both its composition and the powder size must be simultaneously reduced through a detailed investigation of the synthesis process (fig. S1). In addition, the strategy to prevent Zn inhomogeneity during pressing is detailed in Supplementary Text and fig. S2. It should be noted that the thickness of the sample is too thin to examine the elemental composition on both sides of the sintered wafers.

The composition optimization process is illustrated in fig. S3 (A and B). As shown in the powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in fig. S3A, the as-ball-milled compositions of powders for both Zn4.0Sb3 and Zn4.2Sb3 correspond well to the Zn4Sb3 crystal structure, with no detectable impurity. It is reasonable that the metastable powders are obtained and the peaks of impurity are hidden in broadening XRD peaks. However, after the pressing process, ZnSb impurities appear in the Zn4.0Sb3 sample. In contrast, the XRD pattern of the Zn4.2Sb3 sample remains consistent with a single-phase Zn4Sb3 structure. This difference in the sintered samples can be attributed to Zn evaporation during the high-temperature sintering process, indicating that excess Zn is essential for obtaining the single-phase Zn4Sb3.

The minimum ball-milling duration required to achieve the smallest powder size was investigated, as shown in fig. S3 (C and D). Notably, the Zn4Sb3 phase does not form when the ball-milling time is less than 2 hours (see XRD patterns in fig. S3C), indicating that sufficient nucleation energy for the crystalline phase is only provided after this threshold. However, bulk XRD patterns of the sintered samples reveal that 2 hours of ball milling is still insufficient for complete reaction of the raw materials, with the Zn3Sb2 impurity phase being detected. Therefore, according to these optimization results, a ball-milling time of 4 hours and the Zn4.2Sb3 composition were selected as the optimal condition to synthesize pure-phase Zn4Sb3 for subsequent investigations.

Figure 2A presents optical images of pure Zn4Sb3 after one-time electrical characterization in the temperature range of 300 to 673 K, where one-time electrical characterization refers to the one common electrical characterization of Linseis Seebeck and electric resistivity (LSR) as demonstrated in Materials and Methods. A prominent observation is the formation of Zn whiskers on the surface of the test bar exhibiting a clear gradient distribution with pronounced enrichment on the cold side. This distribution pattern provides strong evidence for the migration of Zn ions from the hot side to the cold side, driven by the applied temperature difference of 40 K, the maximum achievable in the electrical measurement setup. This behavior is attributed to the combined effects of elevated measurement temperatures, which provide the activation energy for Zn ion migration from the interior of bulk to the surface, and the imposed thermal gradient, which provides the directional driving force for migration.

(A) Optical image of pristine Zn4Sb3 after a one-time electrical measurement. (B) SEM image of Zn4Sb3 after testing. (C) XRD patterns of Zn4Sb3 before and after one-time electrical measurement. a.u., arbitrary units.

Supporting this interpretation, fig. S4 presents a contrasting scenario. In a sample exposed only to heating and cooling cycles—without an applied temperature difference and current basis—Zn whiskers also formed but were uniformly distributed across the surface. This demonstrates that thermal treatment alone (without a gradient) can trigger partial decomposition of Zn4Sb3 and promote outward migration of Zn. However, the absence of a spatial gradient in uniform whisker distribution highlights the crucial role of the temperature difference in directing Zn transport. The distinct influence of this temperature difference on whisker formation and distribution will be examined in greater detail in the following sections.

Figure 2B presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images detailing the morphology of Zn whiskers and fracture surfaces of pure Zn4Sb3 after one-time electrical measurement. The Zn whiskers, originating from the interior of the sample, exhibit a wide range of diameters, from 10 nm up to 15 μm. A native ZnO layer is distinctly visible on the cross sections (Fig. 2B, bottom), likely formed during electrical measurement conducted in ambient air. This oxide layer appears chemically stable against Zn4Sb3, offering a potentially beneficial interface for subsequent coating strategies. Complementary XRD analysis of samples postmeasurement (Fig. 2C) are susceptible to Zn4Sb3 decomposition, as evidenced by the emergence of pronounced Zn diffraction peaks after just a one-time electrical measurement. These findings unequivocally confirm that Zn migration causes the degradation of pristine Zn4Sb3 under operational conditions.

It is important to note that Zn ion migration occurs along grain boundaries with an external activation energy. Only coating on the surface of the leg cannot suppress the Zn growth from inside to outside. As shown in fig. S5A, we deposited 300-nm-thick ZnO coatings on Zn4Sb3 bulk legs. SEM analysis reveals that these surface ZnO layers fail to suppress Zn whisker formation, with emergent Zn pillars penetrating the protective coating (fig. S5, B to D). However, the less whiskers could be observed compared with pristine Zn4Sb3, which may be due to ZnO coating layer suppressed Zn loss on the surface. This demonstrates that Zn ion migration originates within the Zn4Sb3 grain interiors and cannot be inhibited by macroscale surface coatings alone.

Microstructure evolution in pALD ZnO-coated Zn4Sb3

The ZnO layer was deposited on Zn4Sb3 powders by pALD for 50 to 200 cycles [one cycle including diethylzinc (DEZ) pulse-purge-H2O pulse-purge] to systematically examine how an oxide coating affects the microstructural evolution of the composite. The growth per cycle (GPC) has been determined by the cycle-dependent thickness and is ~2.0 Å/cycle as shown in fig. S6A. The native crystalline nature of as-deposited ZnO has been confirmed by the grazing incidence XRD (fig. S6B), which indicates that the as-deposited ZnO has high thermal stability and will not recrystallize under further spark plasma sintering (SPS) process. Elemental maps obtained by energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX) show a uniform oxygen signal enveloping every particle (fig. S7, A and B), and the pronounced O peak in the spectrum of coated powders (fig. S7, C and D), absent in pristine Zn4Sb3, confirms a conformal ZnO film.

Fractography by SEM (Fig. 3, A to E) demonstrates a systematic change in fracture morphology with the introduction of the ZnO layer. The uncoated single-phase bulk Zn4Sb3 (Fig. 3A) shows a coarse, granular structure punctuated by nanopores, most likely generated by elemental Zn volatilization during the pressing process, as similarly reported by previous works (21, 22). In contrast, ALD-coated samples show clear ZnO/Zn4Sb3 phase boundaries (Fig. 3, B to E), confirming the integrity of the coatings. With increasing ALD cycles, the ZnO layer thickness and the interfaces become more defined. Crystalline domains visible at 50 ALD cycles (Fig. 3B) confirm minimal interdiffusion between the coating and the Zn4Sb3 matrix. At 200 cycles, the ZnO coating forms continuous interfaces exceeding 4.8 μm in length and spanning across three particles (Fig. 3, D and E), validating the high conformality of the pALD process. The measured thickness, ~74 nm, aligns well with the GPC for ZnO ALD (fig. S6A). In addition, the boundaries between two ZnO layers in 100-cycle-coated samples can be observed in fig. S8, indicating that the ZnO layers have been uniformly coated on each particle and pressed it together after SPS.

(F) EBSD images of pristine Zn4Sb3 and Zn4Sb3 coated with 100 ALD cycles of ZnO. (G) STEM-EDX mapping of Zn4Sb3 coated with 100 ALD cycles of ZnO.

Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) reveals significant grain refinement in coated samples (Fig. 3F): The 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample exhibits a reduction in average grain size from 1.0 μm (pristine) to 0.4 μm. According to our previous pALD studies (20, 23), the mismatch in the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) between the Zn4Sb3 matrix and the ZnO layer likely acts as a mechanical “cage,” constraining crystal growth. The inhomogeneous grain distribution in pure Zn4Sb3 primarily arises from the coalescence of crystalline regions into larger grains. The ALD treatment partially suppressed this growth, resulting in the retention of smaller grain sizes. This suppression of grain growth accounts for the observed reduction in grain size. In β-Zn4Sb3, more grain boundaries in Zn4Sb3 can also serve as pathways for Zn redistribution under external driving forces (24). However, the key effect of pALD is considered to be the conversion of particle boundaries into ZnO-modified interfaces, which act as barriers to directional Zn migration, whereas the reduced grain size is likely a secondary microstructural consequence of the coating process even with more interface.

In addition, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. 3G) confirms the presence of well-defined triple-junction interfaces in the 100-cycle sample. The enhanced contrast in Zn and O EDX maps at the interface compared to the matrix further supports the presence of a uniform ZnO coating on the particle surface, highlighting the effectiveness of the pALD process in tailoring microstructural evolution.

ZnO pALD coating effect on the TE performance

The influence of ALD ZnO coatings with varying cycle numbers on the TE performance of Zn4Sb3 was systematically investigated, as presented in Fig. 4. Given that ZnO has a lower electrical conductivity than Zn4Sb3, the electrical properties were examined first. Figure 4A demonstrates that depositing ZnO coatings significantly affects ρ. Increasing the number of ALD cycles causes a monotonic rise in ρ from 30 to 42 μΩ·m at 623 K. In contrast, S changes only slightly (Fig. 4B). The Zn4Sb3/ZnO interfaces are expected to introduce additional interfacial scattering and transport barriers, which can readily account for the observed increase in resistivity. In contrast, because the ZnO layer is confined to the interfaces and does not significantly alter the bulk electronic structure of Zn4Sb3, its impact on the Seebeck coefficient remains comparatively limited. This suggests that the ZnO layers predominantly suppress carrier mobility (μ) while exerting only a minor impact on the carrier concentration (n). This observation is in good agreement with previous studies on oxide coatings, where thin layers are known to impede electron transport without significantly reducing n (19, 20, 23). To further evaluate the ZnO-coated samples, the weighted mobility (μW) was calculated from S and ρ (25), as shown in fig. S9A. μW decreases with increasing temperature for all samples. As the number of ALD cycles increase, μW continues to decline, with the 200-cycle sample exhibiting the lowest room-temperature μW value (~44.9 cm2 V−1 s−1), confirming that the ZnO layer effectively reduces mobility. Figure S9B depicts the temperature-dependent power factor (PF = S2/ρ). Because of the decrease in electrical conductivity, the overall PF of the ZnO-coated samples decreases from 1.17 (pure Zn4Sb3) to 0.78 mW/m·K (200 ALD cycles of ZnO) at 623 K.

(F) Schematic images and comparison of zT values with other stabilization strategies (10, 11, 16). (G) Optical images of pristine and ZnO-coated Zn4Sb3 after electrical measurements.

In contrast to electrical properties, ZnO layers deposited along phase boundaries act as effective phonon scattering “barriers,” thereby deteriorating thermal transport. As evidenced in Fig. 4 (C and D), the κtot-κele (κele is the electronic thermal conductivity) of the sample coated with 100 ALD cycles of ZnO is 21.4% lower than that of pristine Zn4Sb3 and remains the lowest across the measured temperature range. It should be noted that the decrement on κtot-κele involved with bipolar effect in the high-temperature region (Fig. 4D), whereas the suppressing on κlat can be confirmed below 500 K. This stronger phonon scattering induced by the ZnO layers also lowers the κtot from 1.0 to 0.8 W m−1 K−1. When the number of ALD cycles increased to 200, however, κtot increases relative to the 100-cycle sample, primarily because κlat increases. This phenomenon likely arises from the excessive ZnO layer thickness: Because ZnO has a higher intrinsic thermal conductivity than Zn4Sb3, a thicker layer can establish thermally conductive pathways along phase boundaries, and the enhanced phonon scattering no longer counteracts the increased thermal conduction through the thick ZnO layer. Thicker ALD oxide layers result in an increment in κlat, which has been reported in our previous work (19). It should be noted that the increased density of grain boundaries enhances phonon boundary scattering, which predominantly suppresses the transport of long-wavelength, low-frequency acoustic phonons and mainly affects κlat in the low-temperature regime. In contrast, the introduction of ZnO coatings generates additional phonon scattering channels, including interfacial scattering at heterogeneous ZnO/matrix boundaries. These mechanisms effectively scatter a broad spectrum of phonons, extending from low-frequency to high-frequency modes and remain effective in the intermediate-temperature and high-temperature regions. Consequently, ZnO-induced interfacial and defect scattering plays a dominant role in the overall reduction of lattice thermal conductivity. For the 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample, the significant κlat reduction compensates for the lower PF, so that the overall zT is unchanged (Fig. 4E). Therefore, a 100-cycle ZnO coating preserves the TE performance of Zn4Sb3 and demonstrates that the TE properties of Zn4Sb3 can be precisely tailored by controlling the ZnO layer thickness.

Figure S10 compares the zT values of pristine Zn4Sb3 synthesized in this study (300 to 673 K) with values reported in the literature. Differences in synthesis methods (e.g., ball milling versus melting) lead to variations in grain size, composition, and impurity level, all of which significantly influence the final TE performance (26). We attribute the higher ρ observed in our samples, relative to previously reported values, to the smaller grain size produced by the ball-milling process, as compared to samples prepared by melting (9, 15, 22, 27–31). As a result, the reduced peak zT in the ALD ZnO-coated samples is a consequence of the matrix Zn4Sb3 powder properties, indicating that further compositional optimization could enhance performance.

Figure 4F benchmarks our pALD-stabilized samples against other stabilization strategies. Compared with these alternatives, the pALD approach maintains comparable TE performance, whereas many other methods deteriorate the zT value (10, 11, 16). Segmenting Zn4Sb3 legs with stainless steel foil, for instance, introduces thermal stresses at the segmented boundaries, ultimately lowering zT (10, 11, 16). Incorporating nanoinclusions such as ZnO and TiO2 requires high loadings (>0.5 wt %) to establish sufficient blocking interfaces but inevitably and rapidly increases resistivity (for example, 1 wt % ZnO addition results in an increment from 15 to 80 μΩ·m at 300 K) (10). In addition, imprecise control over the size and distribution of inclusion can lead to interconnection, elevating κlat and degrading zT through simultaneous stabilized methods.

The blocking effect of ALD coatings on Zn ion migration was clearly observable in postmeasurement optical images (Fig. 4G). As the number of ALD cycles increased, the density of Zn whiskers decreased. Notably, no whiskers were observed on 100-cycle ZnO-coated samples. XRD analysis (fig. S11) further supported these observations, confirming the suppression of Zn4Sb3 decomposition. Whereas pristine Zn4Sb3 showed significant degradation (Fig. 2), a 50-cycle ZnO coating substantially reduced the formation of decomposition products, with only minor ZnSb peaks remaining after a single thermal cycle. The sample with 100 cycles showed few ZnSb peaks even after 10 thermal cycles, and 200-cycle coatings maintained complete phase purity, with no detectable secondary phases, demonstrating excellent thermal stability.

However, most published Zn4Sb3 studies lack performance repeatability data, whereas rigorous repeat measurements and long-term stability testing are conducted to comprehensively validate the stability of ALD-coated Zn4Sb3.

Repeatable TE measurements and long-term stability tests

To systematically evaluate thermal stability and data reproducibility, we measured the electrical properties of pristine Zn4Sb3 and 100-cycle ZnO-coated Zn4Sb3 samples through 10 consecutive runs (fig. S12). As summarized in Fig. 5A, the resistivity of pristine Zn4Sb3 at 623 K changed nonmonotonically, increasing rapidly from 30.17 to 32.79 μΩ·m in the first three cycles and then gradually declining to 27.17 μΩ·m. The samples were taken out and examined after the first cycle before continuously resuming the subsequent nine thermal cycles, which may introduce minor variations in sample positioning and could account for the slight difference (<2.5%) observed in ρ and S between the first and the following cycles for 100-cycle-coated Zn4Sb3. This behavior reflects two competing degradation mechanisms: initial decomposition of Zn4Sb3 into the more resistive ZnSb phase (confirmed by XRD in Fig. 2) and subsequent Zn migration along thermal gradients that creates conductive pathways. In parallel, the S of the 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample decreased from 208.1 to 174.1 μV/K over the same cycles. By contrast, both ρ and S fluctuations were significantly smaller in the ALD-coated specimens, confirming the enhanced TE stability.

As shown in Fig. 5B, HAADF-STEM images taken from the cold side of the pristine and 100-cycle ZnO-coated samples after thermal cycling reveal the pronounced stabilization effect. For the pristine sample, Zn nanoprecipitates are clearly observed by EDX elemental mapping after the thermal tests. In addition, regions with excess Zn and deficient Sb are detected by EDX line scanning at the grain boundaries, indicating that Zn had migrated to these areas with thermal force. In contrast, the 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample shows well-defined phase boundaries with no detectable Zn precipitation. Moreover, the Zn4Sb3 region confined between two ZnO layers (Area 2 in Fig. 5B) remained intact after thermal cycling, implying that the ZnO layers effectively impeded the decomposition of Zn4Sb3 by suppressing Zn migration.

Above results indicate that the ALD ZnO layers effectively inhibit Zn migration from the grains and suppress the decomposition of Zn4Sb3 into ZnSb at elevated temperatures. As is well known, electrical measurement involves not only an applied electrical current but also an applied temperature difference (ΔT) because the ΔT has been applied to the sample for such long times to ensure measuring S accurately. Zn whisker growth observed after LSR measurement is not assigned solely to the temperature difference but is more likely caused by the coupled effects of elevated temperature, thermal bias, and electrical bias during measurement. To better understand the blocking effect of the ALD layer on Zn ion movement under external stimuli, further investigations should isolate individual variables, such as applying either electrical current or thermal gradients independently.

To isolate Zn ion migration driven solely by a ΔT, we designed a rigorous thermal cycling protocol that replicates real operating conditions in TE devices, where Zn4Sb3 is basically used to generate electricity under ΔT. Bulk Zn4Sb3 specimens, either pristine or modified with the 100-cycle ALD ZnO layer, were held with the cold side fixed at 300 K whereas the hot side was repeatedly cycled between 473 and 523 K, generating a maximum ΔT of 223 K (Fig. 5C). After 39,260 thermal cycles (~127 days), pronounced Zn whiskers appeared on the cold side of the pristine sample (Fig. 5C), confirming substantial Zn migration and poor stability, consistent with what is shown in Fig. 2B. Conversely, the 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample remained completely whisker-free, confirming effective Zn migration suppression even under extreme ΔT and prolonged cycling. It should be noted that this long-term thermal cycling test was conducted in glovebox at a maximum temperature of 523 K, which was selected to ensure stable long-duration operation of the custom-built testing platform under a sustained thermal bias. Although this temperature is lower than the highest temperature used in the TE measurements, because of the limitation of maintaining reliable operation of the custom-built test platform, the effectiveness of the ZnO interfacial layers at higher temperatures is additionally supported by the postmeasurement optical images and XRD results after TE testing up to 673 K, where the 100-cycle ZnO-coated sample still showed strongly suppressed Zn whisker formation and decomposition.

Owing to the high sensitivity of S to the local composition, the potential Seebeck microscopy (PSM) was used to investigate the blocking effect of the ALD layer, with scans taken from the hot to the cold side of the samples (Fig. 5D). Uncoated samples exhibited nonuniform S distributions—elevated but irregular values on the hot side (attributed to ZnSb formation) and reduced S on the Zn-depleted cold regions. In contrast, 100-cycle ZnO-coated samples demonstrated an exceptionally uniform S distribution across the entire specimen. This clear contrast confirms that the ALD-ZnO layer effectively suppresses Zn migration under sustained thermal gradients.

Capitalizing on the optimized TE performance of the sample, a performance simulation was conducted for a single-leg TE device to evaluate its energy conversion efficiency. With the cold-end temperature fixed at 300 K and the ΔT progressively increasing from 50 to 350 K, the output voltage, depending on the current shown in fig. S13A, exhibits a well-defined linear relationship. The maximum power output (Pmax) was obtained when the external load resistance matched the internal resistance, resulting in a power density of 20 mW under a ΔT of 350 K. As demonstrated in fig. S13B, finite element simulations verified that this single-leg device achieves a theoretical efficiency of ~7% under a ΔT of 350 K. These findings highlight the material’s potential for practical TE energy conversion applications.

Collectively, our multimodal analysis spanning electrical measurements, microscopic imaging, XRD phase identification, and spatially resolved Seebeck mapping demonstrates that precisely engineered ZnO coatings via ALD significantly enhance thermal stability. Among the configurations tested, the 100-cycle coating stands out as the most effective in suppressing Zn migration while preserving TE performance.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that β-Zn4Sb3 interfaces engineered with pALD ZnO coatings overcome the material’s intrinsic instability by enabling atomic-scale control of both ionic and thermal transport. ZnO layers exceeding 100 cycles establish robust impeding barriers that strongly suppress Zn migration, eradicating Zn whisker growth and phase decomposition even after 39,260 thermal cycles under a gradient of 223 K, as verified by XRD, SEM, and spatially resolved Seebeck mapping. Concurrently, the coating’s thermal mismatch pinning refines the grain size by ~60% and decreased κlat to 0.62 W m−1 K−1 at 300 K. The 100-cycle ZnO coating delivers the best trade-off: It preserves the native zT while granting long-term durability, surpassing earlier approaches that sacrificed performance for stability. These findings position pALD as a versatile, general platform for interface control in mixed ion-electron conductors. Future efforts will focus on multifunctional ALD stacks that synergistically combine stronger ion blocking with enhanced carrier mobility, accelerating the rollout of long-lived TE generators.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials synthesis

Zinc granules (Zn, 99.8%; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and antimony powder (Sb, 99.5%; Sigma-Aldrich) were weighed according to the composition of Zn4.2Sb3. The weighed elements were loaded into a hardened steel ball-milling jar in a glove box under an argon atmosphere with an oxygen and water level below 0.1 ppm (parts per million). The materials were ball milled for 4 hours using an SPEX 8000D machine. A total of 8 g of powder was loaded into the ball-milling jar, and two stainless steel balls with 20 mm in diameter were used.

pALD process of coating the Zn4Sb3 powders

Zn4Sb3 was coated in a commercial ALD reactor with a powder rotation option (Veeco Savannah S200) under a base pressure of 0.2 torr. For each experiment, 4 g of Zn4Sb3 powder was transferred into the coating tube in the glove box. During the pALD coating process, the tumbler was rotating at a speed of 2 rpm. ZnO layers were deposited using water as an oxidant and DEZ (Zn ≥ 52.0%; Sigma-Aldrich) as Zn precursors. The deposition temperature was 423 K. All of the precursors were kept at room temperature. The pulse/purge times were as follows: 0.015/30 s for DEZ and H2O. The precursor was supplied to the chamber with a flow of 5 SCCM (standard cubic centimeter per minute) of N2 gas.

Phase identification and microstructure characterization

Powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) experiments were conducted with a STOE STADI P diffractometer (Mo radiation) in the transmission mode as flat samples were glued between two acetate foil sheets. XRD measurements for bulk samples and thin films were performed using Bruker D8 (Co x-ray source) to examine the phase purity and crystal structure of the samples. The thin-film thicknesses, microstructures, and fractured surfaces of the samples were analyzed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS, Sigma 300 FE-SEM). The EBSD data were acquired using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (ZEISS, Sigma 500 SEM) with an operation voltage of 15 kV. The data were analyzed with the EDAX OIM Analysis 8 Software. A dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB)–SEM system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Scios2) was used to fabricate the specimens for STEM. HAADF-STEM and STEM-EDX spectrum imaging was conducted using a Titan Themis microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 300-kV operation voltage equipped with a SuperX EDX detector (Fig. 3G) and using a Talos F200i (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at a 200-kV acceleration voltage equipped with a Dual-X EDX detector (Fig. 5B). The STEM images at Talos F200i were acquired using a probe semiconvergence angle of 10.5 mrad and a collection angle range from 32 to 192 mrad. The corresponding elemental maps were determined by the background-subtracted intensities of O-K, Zn-K, and Sb-L lines, obtained from integration of drift-corrected frames scanned with step sizes of 1 to 3 nm, at probe currents of 0.2 to 1.0 nA and with dwell times between 8 and 10 μs. Data analysis was conducted with the TF Velox 3.17.0.967 software.

TE measurement

The powders with different coated layers were loaded into a graphite die with an inner diameter of 20.0 mm in a glove box. The details of pretreatment on the graphite die can be found in Supplementary Text. The graphite die with the loaded powder was immediately sintered by field-assisted sintering (FAST, FCT System GmbH) at 683 K for 3 min with a uniaxial pressure of 50 MPa. After polishing both sides, the obtained pellets were cut and used to measure TE properties.

The temperature-dependent S and ρ were measured by the standard four-probe method (LSR-3, Linseis). “One-time electrical measurement” refers to a single complete LSR measurement cycle on one specimen, including the standard heating and cooling process over the relevant temperature range. “Repeated electrical measurements” refers to consecutive full LSR runs performed on the same specimen.

The temperature-dependent thermal diffusivity (λ) was measured by the laser flash method under a helium atmosphere (LFA 1000, Linseis). The density (d) of the samples was measured by the Archimedes method, and the heat capacity (Cp) was obtained from the literature (22). The thermal conductivity (κtot) was calculated according to the relation κtot = λ · d · Cp

Long-term stability test of the legs was conducted within an argon-filled glove box using a custom-made setup. One side of the legs underwent heating from 373 to 523 K, whereas the other side was cooled by a heat sink of 300 K. Spatially resolved room-temperature Seebeck coefficients were scanned along the temperature difference by a potential Seebeck microprobe (PSM II, Panco Ltd., Germany) with a grid point spacing of 50 μm.
