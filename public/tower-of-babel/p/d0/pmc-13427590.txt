Implementation of a clinical trial communication skills training workshop for oncology fellows: A mixed methods evaluation☆
Authors: Naomi D Parker, Susan Eggly, Daphne R Friedman, Leah Szumita, Tithi B Amin, Lauren K Lavender, Martina C Murphy, Maria Sae-Hau, Andrea Sitlinger, Stephanie AS Staras, Jonathan D Thomas, Daniel C Trotier, Elisa S Weiss, Tuo Lin, Tara Hashemian, Carma L Bylund

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer clinical trials (CCTs) are essential to advancing treatment, yet enrollment remains low. An oncologist's recommendation influences participation, but many eligible patients are never offered the option, and discussions often lack clarity or equity. The three-hour, synchronous web-based workshop included a one-hour didactic session followed by two hours of small-group role play with cancer survivors acting as patients. Background

Cancer clinical trials are essential for the development and approval of new treatments, and participation in these trials is widely regarded as high-quality care. [1], [2] Yet clinical trials remain vastly underutilized as a treatment option for cancer as recent data from the Commission on Cancer's accreditation indicate that only 7.1% of patients with cancer ever enroll in treatment trials. [3] A 2019 meta-analysis similarly found that just 8.1% of patients ever enrolled in a trial despite an additional 14.8% of patients being deemed eligible to participate. [4] This persistent under-enrollment reduces research quality, slows scientific progress, and exacerbates disparities in treatment access and outcomes, particularly by limiting our understanding of treatment response in underrepresented populations. [5], [6], [7], [8], [9] These participation disparities have been documented across multiple groups, including adolescents and young adults, geographically disadvantaged people including those living in rural areas, people with fewer socioeconomic resources, and older adults [10], [11], [12], [13].

An oncologist's recommendation is one of the strongest drivers of trial participation. [14] When trials are offered through clear, equitable, patient-centered communication, most patients agree to participate, regardless of race, ethnicity, or background. [15], [16], [17] Yet many eligible patients are never given the opportunity; up to 74% of cancer survivors who did not enroll in a trial reported that they were never offered the option. [14] These missed communication opportunities represent a major barrier to enrollment. Even when trials are discussed, communication quality often falls short, lacking clarity, patient-centeredness, or equity. Oncologists may inconsistently offer trials, use overly complex language, or make assumptions about which patients are likely to accept or be a “good candidate” for participation. [[18], [19], [20], [21]] Some clinicians avoid trial discussions entirely due to their own limited knowledge, uncertainty or misconceptions about trials, or discomfort navigating perceived barriers. [18], [20] Given the well-established influence of a physician's recommendation, improving oncologists' knowledge, attitudes, and communication practices is essential to increasing equitable trial enrollment. [15], [16], [17], [18]

Thus, there is a need to better implement patient-centered communication in conversations with patients about cancer clinical trials. Communication training is often incorporated as an implementation strategy to improve clinical practice. Grounded in decades of evidence on effective communication skills training, such clinician training works best when it includes an experiential component in which clinicians practice skills and receive feedback Communication skills training has been shown to improve oncologists' confidence and communication behaviors in the context of clinical trial discussions. [22], [23] Graduate medical education training is an ideal period in which to equip oncologists with these critical skills. However, our mixed-methods needs assessment of oncology training programs found that most Hematology-Oncology (Hem-Onc) fellowship programs lack formal instruction on cancer clinical trials communication. [24] While Hem-Onc fellows typically gain exposure to trials during their training, few receive a standardized curriculum dedicated to teaching them how to discuss trials with patients. [24], [25] Implementing patient-centered communication about cancer clinical trials in oncology practice begins by training new oncologists in this practice.

To address this gap, we implemented the Cancer Clinical Trials Communication Skills Education Program for Medical Oncology Fellows (hereafter referred to as COMM-CCT). The COMM-CCT workshop is a structured communication skills training workshop designed to equip fellows with the tools needed to engage in effective, patient-centered discussions about cancer clinical trials. Guided by implementation science principles, we conducted a mixed methods evaluation using Proctor et al.'s implementation outcomes framework [26], [27] to assess the reach (also referred to as “penetration” [27]), feasibility, acceptability, and fidelity of implementing COMM-CCT in Hem-Onc fellowship programs across the U.S. The longer-term goal is to improve trial enrollment and reduce disparities in clinical trial participation.

Methods

Sites and participants

To recruit Hem-Onc fellowship programs in the U.S. for the 2024 implementation of the COMM-CCT workshop, we contacted Program Directors from seven sites, including six academic and one community, that had expressed interest during our prior needs assessment study. [20] Sites were selected based on feasibility, prior program interest, and willingness to integrate the workshop into existing academic schedules and training structures, rather than based on a formal assessment of program demographics, geography, or clinical populations served. At each participating site, one to two fellow champions, recommended by Program Directors for their leadership and interest, played a key role in promoting the workshop and encouraging attendance. We collaborated with programs and fellow champions to schedule the 3-h workshops during protected education time for fellows who were in their first, second, or third year of training. Programs could choose to schedule the workshop as single 3-h session or divide it into multiple parts, typically holding the 1-h didactic session on one day followed by one or two 1-h small group role play sessions on separate days within the same week. All fellows at participating sites were invited to attend the workshop as part of their fellowship education program.

COMM-CCT curriculum

COMM-CCT is a comprehensive curriculum designed to equip fellows with the skills needed to effectively engage in patient-centered discussions about cancer clinical trials. Developed and previously piloted by our multidisciplinary team of communication scientists and oncology clinicians, the curriculum was previously shown to improve fellows' perceptions about trial discussions and comfort level in communication about trials. [24] The COMM-CCT curriculum is grounded in adult learning theory, which emphasizes that trainees benefit most when educational content is clinically relevant, interactive, and immediately applicable. [[25], [28]] The curriculum first introduces key concepts and then reinforces communication skills through practical application. The workshop was built around the COMM-CCT framework (Check-In, Outline Options, Make a Shared Decision, and Map Out Next Steps), which we developed during a pilot study and further refined for this training. [29] This framework serves as a structured guide for clinician-patient trial discussions (Fig. 1). The three-hour COMM-CCT workshop was designed to be delivered synchronously via Zoom and as noted above, consists of two primary components: a one-hour didactic session and a two-hour small-group role-play session.

The COMM-CCT Framework.

Content of the didactic session provides rationale for the importance of clinician involvement in trial discussions, addresses common barriers to trial participation, and introduces patient-centered communication strategies to help mitigate these barriers. A key emphasis of the session is encouraging clinicians to discuss trials with all patients early and throughout the care process, including at each treatment decision point. This includes raising the topic even when a trial is not clearly available, to help normalize trials as a part of routine cancer care. Interactive learning techniques, such as case-based discussions and audience polling, engage participants and reinforce key concepts while introducing fellows to the COMM-CCT framework. The content also includes a resource called Smart Phrases for Discussing Clinical Trials, which we developed in response to feedback from early workshop attendees and subsequently distributed at later workshops. This resource provides practical, evidence-based language for communicating about trials with patients and caregivers, including ways to address common concerns and misconceptions. The didactic session additionally highlights clinical trial resources, including an overview of Blood Cancer United's Clinical Trial Support Center, a demonstration of clinicaltrials.gov, and information about nonprofit organizations that offer trial-related support services. By combining communication strategies with real-world resources, the didactic session was designed to equip fellows with both the skills and tools necessary to effectively discuss trials with patients and caregivers, including those from populations that are underrepresented in trials.

The didactic session is followed by small-group role play sessions that give fellows an opportunity to practice applying the COMM-CCT framework in simulated patient interactions. These sessions were designed to take place in Zoom breakout rooms, have 2–5 fellows per group, and be co-led by a facilitator with expertise in communication skills training and a facilitator with clinical experience. [30] Standardized patients (SPs), who are cancer survivors trained to portray patient experiences, give fellows the opportunity to practice trial discussions. We designed the role-play scenarios to reflect diverse patient experiences and common barriers to trial participation, including mistrust in the healthcare system, logistical challenges, and caregiving responsibilities. For example, one scenario featured an older African American man who expressed concerns about past experiences that led him to be mistrustful of medical institutions, while another depicted a patient concerned about balancing the logistics of trial participation with caregiving responsibilities for a parent with dementia (Appendix 1). Facilitators and SPs provide structured feedback during the role play sessions, helping fellows refine their communication strategies. At the conclusion of each session, SPs share their personal experiences with cancer treatment and clinical trials.

Implementation evaluation

To evaluate the implementation of the COMM-CCT workshop across sites, we assessed four key outcomes based on Proctor et al.'s outcomes for implementation research: reach (the extent to which the intended population engages with or is exposed to the intervention), acceptability (the perceived agreeableness or satisfaction with the intervention), feasibility (the ease or difficulty of integrating the intervention in a specific setting), and fidelity (how closely the intervention was delivered as intended). [26], [27] We used a mixed-methods approach to assess these outcomes, collecting survey and interview data.

Quantitative data collection

We assessed reach by tracking fellow attendance rates at each site and across all participating programs. To assess acceptability and feasibility, we invited fellows who attended the workshop, as well as Program Directors at each site, to complete a brief, anonymous online survey via Qualtrics (Provo, UT) immediately following the workshop. The fellows' survey included 8-items measured on a 1–5 Likert-type scale (1 = “Not at all,” 5 = “Extremely”). It assessed overall satisfaction, faculty/facilitator credibility, workshop organization, confidence in applying the communication skills taught, and the feasibility of integrating these skills into clinical practice. The Program Directors' survey included 4-items, also measured on a 1–5 scale (1= “Not at all”, 5 = “Extremely”), and assessed their satisfaction with the workshop, its implementation, and relevance to the fellows at their respective sites. Items on the fellows' and Program Directors' surveys were adapted from Cho's Acceptability, Feasibility, and Appropriateness Scale and aligned with Proctor's implementation outcomes. [26], [31] To assess the fidelity of intervention delivery, one facilitator from each role-play group completed a 10-item online survey via Qualtrics immediately following each workshop. Based on a previously developed facilitator competence coding system, [32] the survey used a 3-point Likert-type scale (1 = Never, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Always) to evaluate how closely facilitators adhered to the workshop protocol. Each item assessed whether a key instructional task (e.g., introduction of essential materials, structured role-play facilitation, guided feedback) was completed during each session as intended.

Qualitative data collection

To gain deeper insight into acceptability and feasibility, we conducted semi-structured interviews with a subset of fellows within three months following the workshop. Participants were recruited through a follow-up survey where fellows could indicate their willingness to be interviewed. We aimed to interview one fellow per training year from each participating program.

Between March 2024 and February 2025, two authors [N.P., L.L.] conducted the interviews via Zoom. Prior to each interview, fellows verbally confirmed their willingness to participate. The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on Cho's AFAS scale, [31] focusing on fellows' impressions of the workshop, its relevance to their training, and their ability to integrate what they learned into practice. The interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed before analysis. Fellows received a $50 gift card for participating in the interview.

Analysis

We analyzed quantitative survey data (fellows post-workshop surveys, facilitator fidelity surveys, and program director surveys) using descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations). We examined fidelity data using mean (M) and standard deviations (SD) to assess adherence to key instructional components across sessions. Reach was summarized using the proportion of fellows who attended the workshop out of the total number of fellows at each site. R Version 4.4.0 [33] was used for all data analysis. Given the limited number of programs and fellows, we did not conduct formal comparisons by delivery format.

For qualitative data, we conducted a thematic analysis guided by the constant comparative method. [34], [35] We employed both deductive and inductive coding strategies, drawing from Proctor's implementation outcomes [26] as a guiding framework while allowing for emergent themes. Two authors [N.P., L.L.] independently coded transcripts and met regularly to refine the codebook, resolve discrepancies, and consolidate similar codes into overarching themes. [34], [35] Axial coding was used to further characterize thematic properties and relationships between identified themes. [36] The interview data and analysis were managed using ATLAS.ti (Version 25.0.1) software. [37]

Following separate quantitative and qualitative analyses, we integrated findings to enhance interpretation, with qualitative data providing contextual depth to the quantitative results. Joint displays are used to illustrate this data integration and to organize results. [38], [39] This study followed the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guideline for assessing quality and reporting results. [40]

Results

Participants and reach

We implemented the workshop in Spring (n = 4 sites) and Fall 2024 (n = 3 sites). Each site participated in a didactic session followed by small-group role play sessions tailored to the number of participating fellows. Four programs received the workshop as a single 3-h session (n = 38 fellows), two received the workshop as a 1-h didactic session followed by a separate 2-h role play session (n = 13 fellows), and one received the workshop as three separate 1-h sessions (n = 11 fellows). In total, we conducted a total of 17 small-group role play sessions.

We assessed reach by tracking attendance across participating programs. Of 86 fellows who were eligible to attend the workshop (Fig. 2), 72% (n = 62) attended, with program-level attendance ranging from 44% to 100%. Among attendees, 87% (n = 54) completed the anonymous course evaluation survey and 23% (n = 14) participated in a follow-up semi-structured interview. To maintain the anonymous nature of the surveys within the small programs, demographic information was not collected.

Participant Flowchart.

The 14 fellows who participated in interviews did provide demographic data and included six (43%) who were in PGY1, three (21%) in PGY2, and five (36%) in PGY3. Nine (64%) self-identified as male, five as female (36%), and the average age was 32 years (range: 28–37). Interviews were conducted an average of 72 days after fellows attended the workshop.

Acceptability

Fellows. Survey responses indicated high levels of acceptability across multiple domains, with fellows rating the workshop faculty and facilitators as highly credible (M = 4.56, SD = 0.63) and describing the workshop as well-organized and well-executed (M = 4.50, SD = 0.61). Overall satisfaction with the workshop was high (M = 4.30, SD = 0.79), as was satisfaction with the content (M = 4.28, SD = 0.79). Fellows also reported feeling comfortable with the communication skills taught in the workshop (M = 4.15, SD = 0.71).

Interview data supported these findings, highlighting fellows' strong favorable perceptions of the workshop's overall acceptability. Three overarching themes were identified, corresponding to the survey constructs: design and implementation, content and resources presented, and practice relevance and skill reinforcement. Fellows consistently described the workshop as well-structured, highly relevant, and valuable in building their knowledge and communication skills. Acceptability of the COMM-CCT Workshop (Fellows).

Program Directors. We received survey responses from 100% of the Program Directors who participated in the COMM-CCT workshop (N = 7). Consistent with fellows' responses, the Program Directors reported high acceptability and feasibility of the workshop. On average, they were highly satisfied with the content and practices covered (M = 4.5, SD = 0.5) and rated the information and practices as highly relevant to the fellows in their program (M = 4.5, SD = 0.5). They also agreed that the workshop was implemented well at their site (M = 4.3, SD = 0.8).

Feasibility

Fellows. In terms of feasibility (Table 2), fellows reported that the concepts and communication skills from the workshop were compatible with their clinical practice (M = 4.35, SD = 0.70). They also indicated that the workshop content would be useful in their everyday clinical practice (M = 4.33, SD = 0.73) and expected they would be able to incorporate the skills into routine patient care (M = 4.22, SD = 0.72).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study demonstrated positive outcomes related to implementing the COMM-CCT workshop across seven U.S. Hem-Onc fellowship programs, including strong reach, acceptability, feasibility and fidelity. [41], [42], [43], [44] Our mixed methods approach provides a nuanced understanding of fellows' experiences and generates actionable insights to inform future implementation and scaling. By evaluating multiple implementation outcomes, this study contributes to the growing literature on implementation-focused communication training. [26]

Acceptability findings underscore the COMM-CCT workshop's value as a training intervention well-aligned with Hem-Onc fellows' educational needs. The integration of didactic and experiential components, especially role plays with cancer survivors as standardized patients, was described as a key strength that fostered engagement and skill-building. Fellows reported that the workshop content was both relevant and actionable, meeting them at an appropriate stage of training. They appreciated the introduction of practical communication strategies and tools, including the COMM-CCT framework, clinicaltrials.gov, Smart Phrases, and Blood Cancer United's Clinical Trial Support Center. Importantly, the workshop addressed a longstanding gap in formal instruction around clinical trial communication, equipping fellows with greater confidence and language to navigate these conversations effectively. Peer observation and real-time feedback were seen not only as structural components of the training but as integral to building skill and fostering a sense of comfort. [45], [46], [47] Overall, these findings affirm the acceptability of the COMM-CCT workshop, reflecting participants' perceptions that it was timely, educationally impactful, and well-suited to the communication challenges encountered in everyday clinical practice.

Feasibility findings suggest that fellows perceived the COMM-CCT workshop content as practical and implementable within their clinical environments. They described strong alignment between the skills taught and the realities of their current practice, highlighting compatibility with institutional structures, clinical roles, and patient populations. Fellows emphasized the value of tools and strategies that felt adaptable to their everyday encounters, noting that the workshop content was not only relevant but also feasible to integrate into their everyday clinical practice. They emphasized that the workshop reinforced the idea that they could initiate trial conversations without being content experts. It clarified the role of institutional and team-based supports, boosting their confidence to engage in clinical trial communication. These findings indicate that the COMM-CCT workshop not only aligns with fellows' clinical context but equips them with feasible, real-world strategies to engage patients in discussions about trials.

Our fidelity data demonstrate that the workshop could be delivered with high fidelity across multiple sites and small group sessions. A few components showed lower adherence, including goal setting before role plays and doing each role play twice. Fidelity gaps likely reflect time constraints, different group sizes, and minor variations in facilitation style. [32] For broader dissemination, these findings highlight the need to develop contingency plans and provide additional facilitator guidance to ensure consistent delivery.

In our future work we plan to examine the impact of the COMM-CCT workshop on fellows' knowledge, attitudes and communication behavior. Thus far, findings indicate that this is a promising workshop that can be disseminated broadly to fellowship programs as well as radiation oncology and surgical oncology training programs. Some limitations should be noted, however. Participating fellowship programs were recruited based on feasibility and program interest rather than a formal assessment of the patient populations served. We also did not collect site-level data on patient demographics, socioeconomic, geographic, or clinical characteristics. Therefore, although engagement varied across sites, we could not determine whether engagement was associated with characteristics of the populations served by each program or factors like geographic location. In addition, although cancer survivors served as simulated patients and contributed to the experiential role-play component, we did not collect formal evaluation data from survivors about their experiences participating in the workshop. Future evaluations should examine survivor simulated patients' experiences, including any perceived burden, value, and potential benefits of participation. Future implementation studies could benefit by examining how site-level contextual factors including institutional setting, faculty support, and fellows' clinical schedules influence engagement with clinical trials communication training.

Preparing a generation of oncologists to feel equipped to talk with all patients about trials, regardless of sociodemographic background or clinical context, is essential to improving the quality of cancer care and moving science forward. This is particularly important as cancer clinical trials are being conducted not just at academic centers, but also more frequently at community cancer centers. [48] The intervention could also be adapted for practicing oncology clinicians who want to improve how they discuss trials with patients, especially as trials expand beyond academic cancer centers. One limitation of implementing COMM-CCT is the number of faculty required to teach it. To broadly implement training on communication about trials across the hundreds of ACGME-accredited oncology programs in the U.S., we need to consider other educational approaches such as train-the-trainer models, [47] flipped classrooms, [49] and develop asynchronous online programs that incorporate experiential learning through innovative technologies (e.g. virtual patient simulations). [50]

Innovation

This study introduces several innovations in its approach to teaching cancer clinical trials communication skills. First, although communication skills training programs commonly combine didactic instruction with experiential practice, no published studies have evaluated communication skills training programs for trainees specifically about discussing cancer clinical trials with patients [25]. This study demonstrates how a nationwide, communication-focused educational intervention can be effectively embedded into existing fellowship training structures across multiple institutions. Second, the workshop integrates a cancer clinical trials-specific communication framework (COMM-CCT) with practice-ready CCT Smart Phrases designed to support real-world CCT discussions. Rather than functioning as generic clinical communication prompts, these tools provide structured language for common clinical trial communication challenges, including explaining trial purpose, distinguishing between standard treatment and clinical trial participation, responding to common patient concerns about trials, and mapping out next steps after a trial discussion. In this way, the tools build on prior communication-support strategies by translating communication principles into language tailored to the practical and relational challenges of cancer clinical trial conversations. [51] Third, the use of cancer survivors as simulated patients provides a unique and authentic experiential learning component. While simulated patient role play is common in clinical communication skills training, simulated patients are often trained actors or standardized patients without lived experience of the clinical context being portrayed. [52], [53] Our use of cancer survivors allows fellows to practice trial-related discussions with individuals who bring lived experience to the encounters. Finally, the program's evaluation applies a structured implementation science approach to examine feasibility, acceptability, and fidelity across diverse training sites, providing insight into how this type of training can be delivered and evaluated in real-world settings.

Conclusion

Findings suggest that the COMM-CCT workshop can be successfully implemented within Hem-Onc fellowship programs in the U.S. This workshop is not only feasible but also highly acceptable to fellows, who described it as timely, well-constructed, and instructionally meaningful. Acceptability and feasibility are critical early indicators of implementation success, and these results provide a strong foundation for broader dissemination and integration into oncology training programs.
