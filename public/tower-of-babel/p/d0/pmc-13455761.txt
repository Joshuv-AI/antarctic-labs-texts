Dysregulated NF-κB dynamics impair proteostasis and drive premature differentiation in aging B cell development
Authors: Yu-sheng Lin, Valentina Alonso, Apeksha Singh, Yi Liu, Ying Tang, Arnav Mehta, Jennifer K King, May Paing, Nathan Salomonis, H Leighton Grimes, Jennifer J Chia, Dinesh S Rao, Alexander Hoffmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAging and inflammation reduce bone marrow B cell output. While myeloid bias among multi-potent progenitors is one cause, how the developmental cascade of B-committed progenitors is affected remains less well characterized. Here, we identify dynamic modulation of the inflammation response factor NF-κB as a hallmark of healthy lymphopoiesis, but it is diminished in aged B-lymphopenic mice. Indeed, genetic dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics results in severe B-lymphopenia. Model-aided analysis of in vivo progenitor populations and ex vivo experimental time courses reveals that accelerated differentiation of pro-B cells results in skipping critical proliferative phases. Single-cell transcriptomics confirmed premature cell-state transitions, characterized by inappropriate activation of NF-κB target genes that cause wholesale increases in protein synthesis rates. This imbalanced proteostasis leaves little spare energetic capacity to support population expansion, leading to premature differentiation. Our findings indicate that developmental NF-κB dynamics safeguard B lymphopoiesis, thereby identifying a mechanistic cause of age-associated humoral immune deficiency.

INTRODUCTION

Aging and inflammation are associated with diminished adaptive immune responses, including reduced vaccine efficacy.1–3 Indeed, these conditions are associated with reduced B cell production in the bone marrow, thereby limiting the supply of B cells that can participate in the humoral immune response.3–5 Extensive literature documents that a myeloid bias of multi-potent hematopoietic progenitors is one reason for the diminished supply of B cells3,6–9 and describes multiple underlying molecular mechanisms. However, a reduction in the supply of B cells is often more pronounced than that of T cells, raising the possibility that inflammation causes developmental abnormalities in the B lymphopoiesis pathway downstream of B lineage-committed progenitors.

B lymphopoiesis is a tightly regulated process that consists of a series of developmental transitions, proliferation phases, and checkpoints to ensure that mature B cells with functional immunoglobulin are produced in sufficient numbers.10–14 Six developmental stages in the bone marrow have been characterized by Hardy,12 as they are readily distinguished by cell surface marker flow cytometry: pre-pro-B cells (fraction A), pro-B cells (fractions B and C), pre-B cells (fractions C′ and D), and immature B cells (fraction E). The differentiation transitions along this developmental pathway must be coordinated with proliferative phases to ensure that the adult bone marrow produces 1–2 × 107 immature B cells daily that are functional and able to populate secondary lymphoid tissues.15 In addition, a critical quality control step in this transition is the pre-B cell receptor (BCR) checkpoint in fraction C, in which the newly recombined immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) is confirmed to be an appropriate fit to the surrogate light chain, before allowing entry into the proliferative phase of pre-B cells (fraction C′) and further differentiation and recombination of the immunoglobulin κ light chain (Igκ) in fraction D.16–19

The transcription factor NF-κB, a family of dimers whose most potent transcriptional activators are RelA and cRel, is required for the progression of B-lymphocyte precursors through the above-described stages of development, as documented in a number of NF-κB and IκB kinase (IKK) knockout or dominant-negative mutant strains.20–24 However, it remains unclear how NF-κB activity is controlled during lymphopoiesis and how different levels of NF-κB activity or dysregulation affect the developmental process or the underlying cell fate decisions of progenitors. This is important because NF-κB activity is responsive to a broad array of stimuli, including developmental and inflammatory cytokines and damage- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs or PAMPs).25–27 Interestingly, while NF-κB is required for B lymphopoiesis, the presence of more NF-κB induced by inflammatory conditions resulting from persistent infections, tissue damage, or aging does not increase B cell output. Instead, such conditions are associated with reduced B cell output and repertoire1,3–5,8,28–32 and hence diminished responsiveness to vaccination, greater vulnerability to infection, and higher mortality.2,3,33,34 This suggests a complex, non-monotonic relationship between NF-κB and B lymphopoiesis that warrants further study to understand how aging and chronic inflammatory conditions affect B cell development.

NF-κB activity and activation dynamics are controlled by three “canonical” IκB proteins, IκBα, -β, and -ε, that bind NF-κB dimers and block their DNA-binding activity; their abundance is diminished by IKK, whose activity is responsive to PAMPs, tissue DAMPs, or inflammatory cytokines. Hence, genetic deficiencies in IκB proteins mimic the presence of chronic inflammatory conditions specifically in the NF-κB signaling pathway. Interestingly, drastically increased NF-κB activity aborts B lymphopoiesis. Deletion of IκBε and IκBα (which causes embryonic lethality) prevents fetal liver cells from differentiating into the B or T lineages in adoptive transplant chimeras or in vitro,35 and elevated non-canonical NF-κB RelB:p52 activity in an Nfkb2 mutant represses Pax5 expression, causing a block in the transition from multi-potent pre-pro-B cells (fraction A) to early pro-B cells1–3 (fraction B), the entry point of the B cell lineage.36 Yet, how NF-κB activity is actually regulated over the course of the B cell differentiation pathway, and how its dysregulation, which may result from inflammatory conditions, might affect the B cell differentiation transitions, has not been reported.

Here, using fluorescent NF-κB cRel and RelA reporter mouse lines, we reveal dynamic regulation of NF-κB during early B lymphopoiesis in healthy mice. Interestingly, among aged individuals, we found that those with diminished NF-κB dynamic control show more severe B lymphopenia than those who maintain NF-κB dynamics. A newly developed compound IκB mutant that is viable into adulthood demonstrates that proper dynamic control of NF-κB is required for B lymphopoiesis. Our analysis reveals that NF-κB dynamics ensure proper proteostasis control, enabling the phasing of proliferation and differentiation decisions. When NF-κB dynamics are dysregulated, mismatched proteostasis energy demands and mitochondrial energy supplies result in premature cell-state transitions and rushed differentiation that skip proliferative phases and the pre-BCR checkpoint. We further show that reducing protein production with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin partially rescues B cell developmental defects in mice with dysregulated NF-κB dynamics. Taken together, these results identified dynamic control of NF-κB as critical for orderly developmental pacing in lymphopoiesis to ensure a healthy quantity and quality of bone marrow B cell output.

RESULTS

NF-κB reporters reveal expression dynamics along B lymphopoiesis

To characterize the expression of the primary NF-κB transcriptional activator proteins RelA and cRel during early B lymphopoiesis, we leveraged the recently generated knockin mVenus-RelA and mTFP1-cRel mouse strains37 and the Hardy fractionation of specific B cell developmental progenitors38 (Figure S1). In young, healthy mice, we found high RelA expression at the fraction A stage (pre-pro-B cell) but then marked downregulation to fractions B and C (pro-B cell); an increased expression in fraction Cʹ (large pre-B cell) cells before being downregulated again in fraction D (small pre-B cell); and a second, milder increase in fraction E (immature B cell) (Figure 1A). The dynamic expression control also pertained to cRel, showing a major downregulation from fraction A to B, with upregulation beginning in fraction C before peaking in fraction Cʹ. We then asked whether NF-κB expression was altered in aged mice, in which the bone marrow milieu contains higher levels of inflammatory cytokines.6 We compared RelA protein dynamics in young (12 weeks) and aged (112 weeks) mice using the statistic D values from the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. As the long aging process results in heterogeneous outcomes, we sought to distinguish individuals in whom RelA dynamics were particularly dysregulated and individuals that maintained dynamics observed in young mice. To this end, we grouped aged individuals to minimize the p value from the two-way ANOVA results on the D-value series (Figure 1B). This data-driven, unbiased classification identified some aged mice with RelA dynamics similar to young individuals, while the other group showed abnormal RelA dynamics (Figure 1C). In fractions B and C, for example, RelA expression was significantly higher in the aged group with dysregulated RelA dynamics than in the aged group with normal RelA dynamics (D = 0.18 and 0.32).

We then examined the early B lymphopoietic output of the two groups of aged mice. Aged mice with normal RelA dynamics showed lower percentages of fractions D (1.98% ± 0.48%) and E (immature B cell, 1.21% ± 0.32%) cells than young mice (8.62% ± 0.66% and 4.26% ± 0.31%, respectively). However, in aged mice with dysregulated RelA, these cell populations were more severely diminished (fraction D: 0.26% ± 0.18%; fraction E: 0.07% ± 0.04%) (Figure 1D). These results suggest that dysregulated RelA expression dynamics are associated with diminished early B lymphopoiesis during aging.

Genetic dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics leads to severe B lymphopenia

To determine whether dysregulated RelA expression dynamics are causally responsible for diminished B lymphopoiesis, we developed a mouse model in which NF-κB dynamics are perturbed. Three IκB proteins control the dynamics of NF-κB activity and hence the expression of RelA via an autoregulatory loop39,40; while a homozygous IκBα knockout is perinatally lethal, we found that IκBα heterozygosity, even in conjunction with homozygous deficiency in IκBβ and IκBε, results in viable, healthy mice.6,7 To determine whether the dynamics of RelA expression were compromised in this compound IκB mutant, we interbred it with the mVenus-RelA reporter mouse to produce the RelaV/+IκB− strain. Immunophenotyping revealed a loss in RelA dynamic control in the mutant, as we found a failure to downregulate RelA in fraction B (Figure 2A), upregulate it in fraction C′, and downregulate it in fraction D. We sought to restore aspects of the normal regulation by removing one allele of the Rela gene, thus weakening the autoregulatory loop. The resulting RelAV/−IκB− mouse strain indeed restored the downregulation of RelA in fraction B and partially the upregulation of RelA in fraction Cʹ (Figure 2A).

To characterize the physiological consequences of dysregulated NF-κB RelA dynamics in early B lymphopoiesis, we quantified the cell frequencies in each fraction (Figure 2B). We found the proportions of fraction C and C′ populations in RelaV/+IκB− mice were severely diminished (fraction C: 0.014% ± 0.002% vs. 0.36% ± 0.06%; fraction Cʹ: 0.004% ± 0.001% vs. 0.33% ± 0.03%), and the subsequent fraction D pre-B cell frequency was also significantly decreased (0.63% ± 0.11% vs. 10.2% ± 0.7%) along with fraction E (0.19% ± 0.02% vs. 3.8% ± 0.3%), while fraction A and B populations remained largely intact, with no significant difference to the control. The heterozygosity of Rela provided partial restoration of these phenotypes.

Overall, the B lineage population in the bone marrow of mutant RelaV/+IκB− mice comprised 1.87 ± 0.31 million cells vs. 12.5 ± 1.1 million cells in control RelaV/+ mice. In the mutant, the proportion of early B cell progenitors (B220+IgM−IgD−, 91.1% ± 1.5%) increased in comparison to controls (62.0% ± 1.8%), while late B cell precursors decreased markedly (B220+IgM+, 6.82% ± 1.33% compared to 31.5% ± 1.6% in RelaV/+) (Figure 2C). Heterozygosity of Rela partially restored B lineage population numbers (6.05 ± 0.62 million cells) and the percentages of late precursors and early progenitors (45.2% ± 8.0% and 50.2% ± 8.5%, respectively). We also generated an IκB−cRel−/− mutant mouse line and found that these mice recovered their late B cell precursors (B220+IgM+, 28.47% ± 1.76%) compared to IκB− (5.55% ± 3.63%), as well as their fraction C (0.27% ± 0.12% vs. 0.01% ± 0.01%) and C′ (0.45% ± 0.10% vs. 0.003% ± 0.001%) cells (Figures S2A and S2B). These results indicate that the IκB− NF-κB dynamic control mutant has a developmental deficiency after the pro-B cell stage that is similar to but more severe than that observed in the cohort of aged mice that show defects in NF-κB dynamics.

Investigating B cell populations in circulating blood (Figure 2D) and spleen (Figure 2E) revealed remarkably strong defects in the NF-κB dynamics mutant. While the B lineage population (B220+CD19+) constituted 64.4% ± 0.7% of purified peripheral blood leukocytes in RelaV/+ control mice, it only constituted 0.11% ± 0.05% in RelaV/+IκB− mice but 8.04% ± 3.32% in RelaV/−IκB− mice (Figure 2D) and 25.94% ± 8.07% IκB−cRel−/− mice (Figure S2C). Similarly, in the spleen, 44.3% ± 1.3% of total splenocytes in RelaV/+ control mice were of the B lineage (B220+CD19+) but only 0.51% ± 0.24% in RelaV/+IκB− mice and 37.2% ± 5.0% in RelaV/−IκB− mice. Histological sectioning and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of the spleens of RelaV/+ and RelaV/+IκB− mice revealed a disrupted splenic architecture, decreased numbers of follicular structures, and increased red pulp in the mutant (Figure 2F, H&E staining, left). Furthermore, the individual follicles in RelaV/+IκB− spleens showed disruption of the normal distribution of large germinal center cells and small lymphocytes, and the B cell-depleted follicular structures were composed primarily of T cells (Figure 2F, IHC staining, middle and right). We examined the few B cells in the spleen in more detail and found that essentially none were IgD positive (Figure S2D) and that IgM expression was reduced (Figure S2E). Both phenomena have been described as features of B cells in human aging.1–3

Since dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics leads to a significantly greater reduction in splenic and circulating B cells than in the bone marrow output, we also investigated the transitional B cell pool. NF-κB is essential for survival and maturation of transitional B cells in the spleen,4 yet in this NF-κB hypermorph, we found a drastic reduction in the total counts of transitional B cells (Figure S2F), which is evident in both T1 (CD93+CD23−IgM+) and T2 (CD93+CD23+IgM+) populations (Figure S2G). Yet a closer look revealed that the relative percentages of T1 vs. T2 B cells were flipped (Figures S2H and S2I). This is of interest because autoreactive B cells that escape the negative selection in the bone marrow are clonally deleted via splenic tolerance surveillance mechanisms at the T1 stage.5

Together, these data suggest that dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics results in two potentially distinct defects: a 20-fold decrease in bone marrow output of immature B cells (0.17% ± 0.05% in RelaV/+IκB− mice vs. 3.61% ± 0.41% in RelaV/+ mice) and a further decrease outside the bone marrow, evidenced by a >100-fold reduction in splenic and circulating B cells.

NF-κB dynamics intrinsic to hematopoietic precursors control early B lymphopoiesis

Prior studies showed that B cell developmental defects in aged mice are due to microenvironmental changes,6 which could impair NF-κB dynamics in progenitors. Therefore, we asked whether the B lymphopenia phenotype seen in NF-κB dysregulated states is intrinsic to hematopoietic cells. To this end, we transplanted donor bone marrow cells with dysregulated NF-κB (IκB−) into control recipient mice (wild type [WT]), and vice versa, and monitored the major leukocyte lineages in peripheral blood at 6, 8, 12, and 15 weeks post-transplantation. We found that the hematopoietic “cell-intrinsic” experimental group (IκB− into WT) showed diminished B lymphoid lineage percentages across all time points (1.44% ± 0.30%, 0.76% ± 0.17%, 1.40% ± 0.14%, and 0.63% ± 0.09%) when compared to the control group (WT into WT, 65.1% ± 3.7%, 62.1% ± 1.8%, 56.8% ± 9.7%, and 49.4% ± 3.0%) but no significant differences in T lymphoid lineage (Figure S3A). In contrast, peripheral blood lineage percentages in the hematopoietic “cell-extrinsic” experimental group (WT to IκB−) showed only minor changes over controls (Figure S3B). Thus, the dramatic B lymphopenia seen in IκB− mice is driven by cell-intrinsic NF-κB dysregulation.

We next investigated B cell progenitor populations in the bone marrow of these chimeric mice 15 weeks after transplantation (Figure S3C). Relative and absolute quantification of B cell progenitors in the cell-intrinsic group revealed reductions in fractions C (0.021% ± 0.007% vs. 0.064% ± 0.007% in controls), Cʹ (0.012% ± 0.004% vs. 0.13% ± 0.02%), D (0.73% ± 0.28% vs. 3.63% ± 0.03%), and E (0.40% ± 0.18% vs. 1.69% ± 0.08%) but not fractions A and B (Figure S3D). Quantification of absolute cell numbers confirmed defects (Figure S3E). These alterations in progenitor populations were similar to the native bone marrow phenotype of native IκB− mice. On the other hand, bone marrow B cell progenitor proportions (Figure S3F), percentages (Figure S3G), and cell numbers (Figure S3H) in the hematopoietic cell-extrinsic experimental group (WT to IκB−) did not recapitulate the native phenotype of IκB− mice.

Together, these results support the conclusion that dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics within hematopoietic cells, which may be driven by microenvironmental changes in the bone marrow niche that are associated with aging,6 disrupts B lymphopoiesis, resulting in (1) a reduction in hematopoietic B cell output and (2) a further reduction in splenic and circulating B cell numbers.

Model-aided analysis of in vivo progenitor population dynamics

We next asked what dysregulated cell-fate decision underlies the reduced bone marrow B cell output. Three causes for developmental deficiencies are typically considered: a differentiation block, a loss of progenitor survival, or reduced cell division rates.43,44 In the case of IκB− mouse bone marrow, we did not observe an accumulation of cells in fraction B (Figure 2B), which suggested that the reduction in fraction C and C′ populations is not due to a differentiation block. Further, Annexin V staining indicated that apoptotic cell percentages were not increased in fraction C (1.2% ± 0.3% compared to 1.6% ± 0.2% in WT mice), while in fraction C′, the apoptotic cell proportion actually decreased (1.1% ± 0.4% compared to 4.6% ± 0.6% in WT mice; Figures S4A and S4B), arguing against a loss of cell survival mechanisms. To determine whether proliferation rates of the B cell progenitor and precursor populations are affected by NF-κB dysregulation, we employed in vivo labeling with the nucleotide analog BrdU,45 which identifies cells that contain newly replicated DNA. These experiments confirmed the expected high proliferative activity in fractions B and, especially, C′ but showed no decrease in their proliferative activity in the NF-κB dynamics mutant; in fact, fraction C showed an increase in the percentage of BrdU-positive cells compared to controls (Figures S4C and S4D). These results indicated that none of these common cell biological explanations accounts for the B lymphoid developmental phenotype in IκB− bone marrow.

We then turned to an unbiased approach to explain the phenotype by mathematically modeling the B cell developmental pathway and to interpret progenitor population data by quantitative fitting. To this end, we formulated a compartment model that describes the developmental progression of early B lymphoid progenitors via five differentiation transitions (Figure 3A). To fit the model, we measured the absolute numbers of each progenitor cell population in the bone marrow of WT and IκB− mice (Figure 3B). Notably, absolute cell counts revealed that WT and IκB− mice differed by 31-fold in fraction C, 100-fold in fraction C′, and 18-fold in fraction D.

Assuming that the differentiation pathway is in steady state, we fit the differentiation transition rates given quantitative population data and population growth rates based on BrdU cell proliferation and Annexin V cell death measurements (Table S1). These data could be accounted for with the model when differentiation rates from fraction C to C′ and fraction C′ to D were 11.0 ± 3.9 and 11.6 ± 0.8 in WT and 227.5 ± 59.5 and 556.6 ± 64.8 in IκB−, suggesting a dramatically accelerated transition from fraction C to D in IκB− mice. Rela heterozygosity could partially restore normal rates (Figure 3C). Using the derived parameters from WT and IκB− data, we calculated the relative cell output flux from each progenitor stage. We found that IκB− mice were predicted to have substantially decreased output cell flux from fractions D and E (Figure 3D). The same conclusion emerged when the model was fit to the data from aged mice with dysregulated NF-κB dynamics but not when NF-κB dynamics were relatively normal (Figure 3E). Given that BrdU and Annexin V measurements provide only a semi-quantitative assessment of relative population growth rates, we examined whether the modeling results were dependent on these rates. When setting growth rates to less cell-stage-specific or even uniform values, model fitting consistently resulted in higher fraction-C-to-C′-to-D transition rates and reduced output cell flux in the mutant (Figure S5). The model-aided interpretation of quantitative population measurements of the lymphopoiesis pathway suggests that the best explanation for the diminished B cell output in IκB− mice is accelerated differentiation rates that lead to rushing through the key proliferative phases (fraction C/C′) of the developmental pathway.

Ex vivo differentiation confirms the accelerated differentiation phenotype

To test the model’s inference of differentiation rates, we turned to an ex vivo differentiation experimental system,46 which has a defined differentiation starting point with purified common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) (Figure 4A). When 500 CLPs are seeded, >10,000 cells can be measured after 5 days, allowing for flow cytometric analyses; we found that fraction C/C′ cells accounted for 26.2% ± 4.8% of B lineage cells in IκB− and 64.2% ± 4.4% in WT cultures, akin to the reduction in the fraction C/C′ population in native IκB− bone marrow (Figure 4B compared to Figure 2B). However, at this early time point, we found the percentages of fraction D cells to be significantly higher in IκB− than in WT cultures (8.5% ± 1.7% vs. 1.5% ± 0.6%). However, by day 7 of culture, the IκB− cells contained a smaller percentage of fraction D cells than the WT controls (4.8% ± 0.4% vs. 7.7% ± 1.1%; Figures 4C and 4D). These data indicated that the IκB− mutant culture was able to generate fraction D cells more rapidly than controls, as seen on day 5, with controls catching up by day 7. This finding suggests that dysregulation in NF-κB dynamics results in an accelerated differentiation transition from fraction B to D, confirming the mathematical model’s interpretation of the in vivo B cell development data. However, the quantitative extent of the phenotype is substantially lower than what is observed in vivo, reflecting the low efficiency of the in vitro differentiation system.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis reveals premature progression of transcriptomic states

To identify molecular evidence for the accelerated differentiation phenotype, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on B cell progenitors (isolated by flow cytometric sorting of the CD49b−Ter119−CD3−F4/80−Gr-1−B220+IgD−IgM− population) from WT and IκB− bone marrow. After excluding potential doublets by Scrublet and DoubletDetection,47,48 we used Cell Ranger49 pipelines to analyze the data. k-Means clustering of both WT and IκB− transcriptomic data identified five related cell clusters and one outlying cluster, potentially containing residual doublets. We then mapped each of the five clusters to the likely developmental B cell Hardy fractions A–D in WT, based on the relative expression levels of identifier genes (Figure S6A), using reference data from the Gene Expression Commons.50 These same cell populations could be validated using an independent analysis with the software ICGS v.2,51 following doublet exclusion (DoubletDecon). By visualizing cells in terms of the first two principal components of their high-dimensional transcriptomic states, we could trace the expected sequence of developmental stages from fractions A to D (Figure 5A). IκB− single-cell data were similarly localized within this principal component space, but we found that cells in fraction B did not only occupy the locale of cells from WT fraction B cells but also extended to where WT fraction C and C′ cells are located. While in WT, the cell frequencies of B cell progenitors defined by flow cytometry coincided with the sizes of clusters defined by transcriptomic states, we found substantial discrepancies in IκB− samples. Although in IκB−, Hardy fraction B comprises over 50% of progenitors (given the dearth of fraction C, C′, and D cells), the number of cells in transcriptomic cluster B was diminished, in favor of cells in clusters C and Cʹ; the size of cluster D was similar to that of fraction D (Figure 5B). These results were confirmed using a second bioinformatic tool, cellHarmony (ICGS workflow).52 A heatmap of Z scored expression values of differentially regulated genes demonstrates that fraction B in IκB− mice includes transcriptomic signatures associated with WT cluster C/Cʹ (Figure 5C). These results suggest that in the IκB− mouse, progenitors defined as fraction B by cell surface markers have already rushed into transcriptomic states of subsequent developmental stages.

Next, we asked whether similar differentiation abnormalities may be identified in aged WT mice. To this end, we generated scRNA-seq data from the B progenitors of a 100-week-old mouse. Examining the expression of the marker genes, we also found here that fraction C marker genes began to appear in fraction B (Figure S6B). To integrate disparate datasets for comparison of differentiation, we trained a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) random forest machine learning classifier on the k-means-clustered WT dataset to define the transcriptomic states associated with healthy developmental transitions. We found that regardless of whether the SGD model was trained on “marker gene” or “all gene” data, the resulting cell classification frequencies by Cell Ranger were comparable (Figure 5B). We then used these WT-dataset-trained models to investigate whether the differences in transcriptomic states led to misclassification of cells not only in the IκB− dataset but also in the aged WT (100-weeks-old) dataset. We found that both the “all gene” and the “marker gene” model predictions misclassified more cluster C cells in IκB− than WT (Figure 5D), placing them into clusters C′ and D. Remarkably, classifications of the aged WT dataset showed similar, albeit less severe, confusion. For example, cluster C′ cells were frequently misclassified to cluster B in both IκB− and aged WT datasets. As an alternative, we applied trajectory analysis and found that while the differentiation trajectories were indeed not altered (Figure S6C), RNA velocity calculations indicated higher differentiation in old WT and the IκB− mutant than in young WT (Figure S6D). These results support the notion that the IκB− mutant is a severe model system for inflammaged WT mice and that rushed differentiation transitions of B progenitors contribute to age-associated defects in B lymphopoiesis.

Failure to downregulate NF-κB in fraction B disrupts proteostasis

To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms for premature differentiation transitions, we performed differential gene expression analysis between WT and IκB− cells within each cluster. We found that clusters B, C, and C′ showed a large number of genes upregulated in IκB− (Figure 6A), many of which contained NF-κB/RelA and downstream IRF binding motifs in their regulatory regions (Figure 6B). Similarly, we found that a set of NF-κB target genes6 was more likely to be increased than decreased in the old-mouse scRNA-seq data (Figure S6E), as illustrated by B2m, Cd69, and Tap1 (Figure S6F). However, scRNA-seq provides gene expression values relative to library size, as illustrated by an almost equally large number of genes that are downregulated in the IκB− cells. To determine whether failure to downregulate NF-κB in fraction B results in more gene transcription per cell, we sorted approximately 204,000 WT and IκB− fraction A–B cells and performed bulk RNA-seq after adding 10 ng of yeast RNA per 100,000 cells as an internal control. This allowed us to quantify the total transcripts per cell, revealing that IκB− progenitors have 2.3× more RNA content than WT controls (Figure 6C). The increased mRNA content pertained to the vast majority of genes (Figure 6D), but while the top 5% of elevated transcripts encoded genes that are hallmarks of lymphocyte activation and cellular stress response (which is driven by NF-κB), the medium 5% tranche encoded genes in cell metabolic processes (Figure 6E). The dramatic increase in mRNA content prompted us to examine the cell size of fraction B progenitors more carefully. Forward scatter area (FSC-A) showed a whole-scale increase with a higher frequency of large cells (defined as >105 FSC units) in the IκB− mouse (Figure 6F).

Elevated gene expression that fuels an increase in cell size also involves increased rates of protein synthesis, the cell’s most energy-demanding process.53 We quantified translation rates using a puromycin incorporation assay,54 first characterizing how protein synthesis is regulated in WT B cell progenitor development. We found that translation rates are downregulated from fraction A to B to C, before being dramatically upregulated in fraction Cʹ (Figure 6G). This is consistent with fraction C′ cells being proliferative and metabolically highly active.12,14 We then applied the puromycin incorporation assay to measure protein translation in the IκB−, focusing on fractions A and B. We found a slight increase in translation rates in IκB− fraction A and a dramatic increase in fraction B (Figure 6H), which was statistically significant (Figure 6I) and was wholly reversed in the IκB−cRel−/− rescue mutant (Figures S6G–S6I).

Given the increase in energy expenditure associated with protein synthesis, we next tested the metabolic activity of IκB− B progenitor cells. Interestingly, we found that IκB− fraction A–C′-enriched progenitor B cells had increased oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) (Figure 6J), including significant increases in basal and maximal OCRs (Figure 6K). This increase in energy production was not due to a global increase in mitochondrial mass, as revealed by staining WT and IκB− progenitor B cells with MitoTracker Green (MTG) staining (Figure 6L).

To test our interpretation of these results, we reasoned that if we reduced protein production rates, we might restore aspects of the rushed differentiation phenotype. To this end, we used the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin, which can reduce translation rates. Providing rapamycin in the drinking water for 7 days resulted in a statistically significant increase in the number fraction C/C′ cells (Figures 6M and 6N). It also resulted in a smaller increase in splenic B cells (Figures 6O and 6P), suggesting that the second phenotype that is localized to the spleen was not rescued.

Together, these results suggest that the failure to dynamically control and downregulate NF-κB in fraction B progenitors results in inappropriate increases in gene expression and translation rates. In turn, the cells respond to the increased energy demand by ramping up their mitochondrial activity without increasing their mitochondrial mass. Remarkably, the fraction C/C′ defects seen in the bone marrow can be improved by rapamycin administration. We conclude that NF-κB dynamic control is essential to ensure the coordination of proteostasis with its ensuing energy demands and supplies, enabling progenitors to respond to pro-proliferative cues and persist in the proliferative C′ phase for a sufficient duration to produce a healthy bone marrow B cell output.

RelA dysregulation overrides the pre-BCR developmental checkpoint

Next, we addressed the second phenotype: despite IκB− mice still generating about 5% of immature B cells (compared to WT), they show <1% B cell content in the spleen or periphery. It is known that the IgH recombination checkpoint is essential to ensure the generation of a functional BCR by requiring pre-BCR signaling activity for the transition from fraction C′ to D.15,17,20 To assess immunoglobulin recombination and expression, we employed immune repertoire profiling using scRNA-seq analysis. To quantitatively assess differences in immunoglobulin expression in addition to the transcriptomic analysis, we plotted the expression of IgH and immunoglobulin light chain for each cell in the principal-component analysis (PCA) space (Figure 7A). We found that whereas in healthy WT controls the majority of B progenitors after fraction B are IgH positive, in the IκB− mutant, very few are, though many are light-chain positive (Figure 7B).

We repeated the analysis for scRNA-seq data from an aged mouse (100 weeks) and quantified the percentages of cells expressing IgH or immunoglobulin light chain in each cluster. Remarkably, we found fewer cells expressing IgH in any of the clusters (cluster B, 68% vs. 80%; cluster C, 25% vs. 36%; and cluster D: 53% vs. 65%) in aged compared to young WT mice (Figure 7C) but more cells expressing only Igκ, without IgH, particularly in cluster D (4.7% vs. 1.8%). Therefore, our analysis of an aged WT mouse indicates defects similar to those that are severe in the IκB− mutant. We next assessed light-chain expression in splenic B cells by gating CD19+B220+ cells into IgM−, IgMlo, and IgMhi bins, given that light-chain surface expression requires a heavy chain. As expected, IgM− cells did not express λ light chains in either WT or IκB− mice (Figures S7A and S7B). However, among IgMlo and IgMhi populations, the IκB− mutants showed an increase in λ expression compared to the WT (Figures S7A and S7B). We then assessed κ light-chain expression in splenic B cells using the same IgM-based binning of CD19+B220+ cells. In IκB− mutants, κ expression was detectable only in IgM-expressing cells, while in WT mice, it may pair with other class-switched IgHs (Figures S7C and S7D).

To understand how fraction C′ cells could bypass the pre-BCR checkpoint, we examined survival-related gene expression in the scRNA-seq dataset. Cells from fraction C/C′ displayed elevated expression of anti-apoptotic genes, including Birc3 and Mcl1, but minimal differences in Rag1 and Rag2 expression (Figure S7E), suggesting that cells are capable of recombination but are not being selected for it. Similar expression patterns were observed in the old-mouse scRNA-seq dataset (Figure S7F).

Overall, these results suggest that age- and inflammation-associated dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics result in elevated NF-κB/RelA activity in cluster B, thus overriding the pre-BCR checkpoint and allowing progenitors to proceed with Igκ recombination and differentiation without having successfully completed IgH recombination. Thus, dysregulated NF-κB dynamics diminish not only the quantity of bone marrow B cell output but also the quality of the few small pre-B cells that are produced; indeed, most of them do not contain a functional BCR, thereby explaining why so few B cells populate the periphery.

DISCUSSION

Given the link between age-associated inflammation and a decline in bone marrow B cell output, we examined here the role of NF-κB regulation in B cell lymphopoiesis. Using knockin reporter mice, we uncovered the dynamic nature of NF-κB control along the B lymphopoiesis pathway in young mice and observed how this is dysregulated in aged mice. Experimental genetic dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics phenocopied numerous aspects of hematopoietic dysregulation in inflammaged mice (Table S1). In sum, it caused severe lymphopenia that had two underlying causes. First, an inappropriate excess in gene expression in the pro-B cell stage disrupted proteostasis, causing progenitor cells to abort proliferative programs in favor of differentiation to the next developmental stage, thereby diminishing overall pre-B cell production. Second, excess NF-κB activity neutralized the pre-BCR checkpoint, thereby allowing B cells with non-functional BCRs to develop, though such B cells are known to have significantly impaired survival and function in the periphery. Importantly, both phenotypes were observed in aged mice, suggesting that the two mechanisms contribute to the impaired B cell humoral immunity associated with inflammaging.

Our data show that the quantitatively diminished B cell output from the bone marrow is not due to a survival defect, a differentiation block, or defects in actual cell proliferation rates. Instead, fewer cells are in the proliferative stage of fraction C′ because those that enter the stage have a shorter residence time, diminishing their ability to expand the population. Indeed, scRNA-seq analysis showed that the rushed transitions already begin in fraction B, where cells acquire RNA transcriptomic signatures of C, C′, and D, with protein cell surface markers not being able to keep pace. While this may be the first report of a developmental deficiency due to a differentiation hypermorph, the converse has been described for various malignancies: for example, failure to progress in the differentiation pathway from pro-B cells (fraction B) results in pro-B acute lymphoblastic leukemia (pro-B ALL) or increased residence times in the common myeloid progenitor (CMP) or granulocyte monocyte progenitor (GMP) results in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDSs) and can lead to malignant transformation.44,55–59 Indeed, therapeutic strategies are being developed that aim to restore the differentiation transitions to less proliferative developmental stages.60,61

Given the importance of residence times, particularly in proliferative stages, in producing a healthy B cell output, what may determine the transition rates between stages, and why might they be accelerated in the IκB mutant? Our study indicates that proteostasis and the associated energy balance represent a key determinant stage transition within the B lymphopoiesis pathway. Indeed, the transition from fraction Cʹ (large pre-BII cell) to D (small pre-BII cell) involves a change in metabolic state from the active energy production that can sustain cell proliferation to a more quiescent state sensed by AMP-activated kinase (AMPK) signaling. AMPK signaling activation then leads to a reduction in glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation and inhibition of mTORC1 signaling.62,63 In the NF-κB dynamics mutant, we not only observed increased protein translation rates in fraction A and B cells but also found increases in both anaerobic and aerobic metabolic activity. This leaves little spare energetic capacity and may also lead to toxic metabolic byproducts that impede sustaining the characteristic proliferative program in fraction Cʹ. On the flip side, an abundance of metabolic activity, often orchestrated by gain-of-function c-Myc or mTOR mutations, can impede the differentiation transition to subsequent proliferative stages in cancer.64,65 Here, we observed partial rescue of the rushed differentiation process by reducing translation rates with the mTOR inhibitor and anti-aging prophylactic rapamycin to restore proteostasis.

The impaired quality of immature B cells also noted here appears to be due to the uncoordinated recombination of immunoglobulin heavy and light chains. Our scRNA-seq immunoglobulin profile analysis revealed that many cells in the NF-κB dynamics mutant express Igκ before successfully recombining the IgH. In normal B lymphopoiesis, κ light-chain gene recombination is regulated by pre-BCR signaling, which requires proper recombination and expression of the IgH gene, in collaboration with other mediators such as PU.1 and IRFs.17 As PU.1 and IRFs motifs are enriched in upregulated genes in IκB− mouse developmental clusters prior to cluster C′, with PU.1 accessibility markedly increased even in IκB− hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs),9 these transcription factor activities, together with NF-κB, may provide accessibility of κ light-chain gene segments for premature recombinase activity. Interestingly, fetal B-1 cell development similarly does not follow the classic heavy-chain-then-κ-light-chain recombination sequence, and recombination of the κ light chain in the pro-B cell stage can bypass the pre-BCR checkpoint.66 As the BCR on B-1 cells is known for self-reactivity, the uncoordinated recombination we discovered in the NF-κB dynamics mutant may provide a potential explanation for why aging is associated with self-reactive antibody production in autoimmune diseases.67,68 At a minimum, overriding the pre-BCR checkpoint through dysregulated NF-κB dynamics reduces the quality of the bone marrow B cell output and hobbles further B cell maturation in peripheral lymphoid organs, which depends on a functional BCR.

Our study uncovers the dynamic regulation of NF-κB as a hallmark of healthy, properly phased B lymphopoiesis. Unlike the well-appreciated dynamic control of NF-κB RelA by IκBα at the hour scale in macrophages and fibroblasts, which controls stimulus-responsive gene expression69 and enables the healthy distinction of stimuli37 while preserving the integrity of the epigenome,70 the dynamic control of both RelA and cRel in B progenitors occurs at the day scale via the integrated functions of all canonical IκBs and preserves the integrity of proteostasis while cells transition through progenitor stages. While the newly revealed mechanisms underlying age- and inflammation-associated B lymphopenia have tangible clinical implications, the findings also illustrate that snapshot assays of signaling regulators in one cell type may be insufficient to identify individuals at risk but that the comparison of successive progenitor stages is diagnostically informative.

Limitations of the study

The key findings of this study are (1) that the NF-κB activator proteins RelA and cRel are dynamically expressed during healthy B lymphopoiesis; (2) that aged mice that show a loss of dynamic expression, and therefore inappropriately high expression in fraction B, have more severe B lymphopoiesis defects than aged-matched mice with more NF-κB dynamics; and (3) that genetic perturbation of NF-κB dynamic control results in severe B lymphopoiesis defects in young mice. While NF-κB dynamic mutant mice appear to phenocopy aged WT mice in many different cellular and molecular assays, we have not been able to restore NF-κB dynamics in affected aged mice to determine whether it restores the phenotype. In other words, we have shown that dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics is sufficient to cause severe B lymphopoiesis defects in young mice, but we have not shown that dysregulation of NF-κB dynamics is required for severe B lymphopoiesis defects in aged mice. In addition, while we have established that rushed differentiation causes diminished B cell output in quantity and quality and that this is associated with higher protein production, we have not shown that a reduction of protein production suppresses the phenotype. Treatment with an mTOR inhibitor only yields a partial rescue. Finally, whether the findings apply to B lymphopoiesis in older human adults who display signs of chronic inflammation remains to be determined in future studies.

METHOD DETAILS

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Dissected tissues from mice were immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Cat#RT-15710) diluted in DPBS. All subsequent tissue procurement, section, H&E staining, and immunohistochemical staining were done by the UCLA Translational Pathology Core Laboratory. Paraffin-embedded sections were cut at 4 μm thickness, had paraffin removed with xylene, and were rehydrated through graded ethanol. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min. Heat-induced antigen retrieval (HIER) or proteolytic-induced epitope retrieval (PIER) was applied on all samples except antigen retrieval was not done for CD45R/B220 staining samples. The slides were then stained with anti-mouse CD3, CD45R, CD11b, and Ly-6G antibodies (KEY RESOURCE TABLE). Rabbit anti-rat secondary antibodies were used for all except CD3 staining samples. The signal was detected using the Dakocytomation Envision+ System-HRP Labeled Polymer Anti-Rabbit (Agilent). All sections were visualized with the diaminobenzidine reaction and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Surface marker flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry staining strategy is delineated in staining panels 1–10 in KEY RESOURCE TABLE and the gating strategy in Figure S1. Primary cells from mouse bone marrow, spleen, and peripheral blood were purified with previously described methods.32,44,75 Live cell percentages were analyzed by staining part of the cells with a 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution. The total live bone marrow cell number of each sample could be determined by the event count from gated non-debris FSC-SSC 7AAD− populations and dilution factor. The total cell number in a specific population was quantified by multiplying the total with the specific cell population frequency in purified and live bone marrow cells.

Cell surface marker staining was done for 2–3 million cells aliquoted into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, pelleted by centrifugation at 400g for 5 min in 4°C, resuspended with 50 μL of flow cytometry buffer (FACS buffer, 3% FBS in calcium and magnesium-free DPBS, Gibco Cat#14200075) with the addition of 1 μL Purified anti-mouse CD16/32 Antibody, and incubated for 15 min on ice in order to block the unspecific Fc receptor binding. The staining master mixes were prepared in 50 μL of FACS buffer for each sample and cells were incubated for 45 min on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed twice with FACS buffer before proceeding to the different procedures according to the staining panel. For samples stained with panel 2B, 3, and 6, cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer with respective fluorophore-conjugated streptavidin, incubated for 30 min on ice in the dark, and washed twice with FACS buffer before proceeding to the further steps. For samples stained by panel 2B, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, and 11, the cell pellets were then resuspended in 300–500 μL FACS buffer with 0.5 μg/mL of DAPI for viability staining, strained through 40 μm cell strainer (Fisherbrand, Cat#22-363-547), and subjected to flow cytometry sample acquisition. For samples stained with panels 1, 2A, 7, 8, and 10, Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 506 staining was applied prior to the surface antibody staining according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All flow cytometry sample data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 Special Order Product at the UCLA Janis V. Flow Cytometry Core Laboratory with adequate compensation setup by using UltraComp eBeads Compensation Beads and live cells with staining antibodies and isotype controls prior to actual sample acquisition. Bone marrow chimeras

Donor bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs of C57BL/6J, B6.SJL-Ptprca Pepcb/BoyJ, or IκB− mice (8–12 weeks old) and retro-orbitally injected (3–5 million cells intravenously per mouse) into 2- to 3-month-old C57BL/6J, B6.SJL-Ptprca Pepcb/BoyJ, or IκB− mouse F1 recipients. 24–48 h prior to the injection, recipient mice were lethally irradiated (1100 rads). Chimeric mice were analyzed 6, 8, 12, and 15 weeks after bone marrow reconstitution. The composition of bone marrow B lymphoid progenitors and hematopoietic lineages in peripheral blood were identified using staining panels 5 and 6. Origin and composition of hematopoietic lineage cells were determined by CD45.1 and CD45.2 markers.

Identification of apoptotic and replicating cells

Cell surface Annexin V levels were used to determine the apoptotic cell population by following the Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit eFluor 450 manufacturer’s instructions. After the surface marker staining on staining panel 7, the cells were washed twice with Annexin V staining buffer prior to Annexin V staining. After the staining incubation and two washes, the cells were resuspended in 300–500 μL of Annexin V staining buffer with 0.5 μg/mL of DAPI for viability staining, strained through a 40 μm cell strainer, and subjected to flow cytometry sample acquisition. To quantify replication in subpopulations, mice were injected with BrdU intraperitoneally according to a previous study.45 After 24 h, bone marrow cells were extracted and stained for cell surface markers identifying B cell progenitor and precursor populations as previously described in the method section, with the addition of staining on intracellular BrdU by PE-conjugated anti-BrdU antibody (Biolegend, Cat#339812) and following manufacturer’s protocol before subjected to flow cytometry analysis.

Preparation of lymphoid progenitor cells

Enrichment of the Lineage− or B lymphoid population was achieved by negative selection on all the non-B lineage cells following protocols derived from Invitrogen MagniSort Mouse Hematopoietic Lineage Depletion Kit (Invitrogen, Cat#8804-6829-74). Total purified bone marrow cell number was determined by the live bone marrow cell counts from the 7AAD staining on aliquots. The cell pellets were transferred to a sterile 5 mL round bottom tube (Falcon, Cat#14-959A), resuspended in FACS buffer at a concentration of 10 million cells per 100 μL, and incubated with all the biotin-conjugated antibody listed in panel 9 or 11 for 30 min on ice. After one wash with FACS buffer, the cell pellets were resuspended in FACS buffer at a concentration of 10 million cells per 100 μL, followed by the addition of MagniSort Streptavidin Negative Selection Beads by the volume of 20 μL per 10 million cells, then incubated for 20 min on ice. After the incubation period, the cells were placed on a magnetic stand with an N52 neodymium block magnet attached and followed by the equivalent steps in the Invitrogen MagniSort Mouse Hematopoietic Lineage Depletion Kit protocol. Cells were then washed and stained with the antibodies listed in surface marker staining panel 9 or 11. The enriched and surface marker-stained cells were then sorted with BD FACSAriaIII sorter in UCLA Janis V. Flow Cytometry Core Laboratory with adequate compensation setup by using UltraComp eBeads Compensation Beads and live cells with staining antibodies and isotype controls prior to actual sample sorting.

Ex vivo differentiation assay

OP9-M2 cell line was a kind gift from the Mikkola lab at UCLA. Cells were cultured in OP9 media [MEMα with no nucleosides (Gibco Cat#12561056) with 20% of fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% 200 mM L-glutamine solution (Corning Cat# 25-005-CI), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (Corning Cat# 30-002-CI) and 1% MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (Corning Cat# 25-005-CI)] at up to 80% confluency at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide in air-jacketed incubators. To generate feeder cells, OP9-M2 cells at 80% confluence were detached from culture dishes with 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (Corning Cat# 25-052-Cl), incubated at 37°C for 5 min before neutralizing trypsin activity by adding 2-fold volume culture medium. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 400g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended with a culture medium. The cell mixture was then irradiated at 4000 rads, pelleted again, resuspended with 90% FBS and 10% DMSO, and aliquoted for cryopreservation. OP9 feeder cells were thawed, pelleted, resuspended to 0.25 million/mL in OP9 media, and plated at 0.5 million cells/well in the 6-well tissue culture plate (Corning, PN#3516) one day prior to the ex vivo differentiation experiment.

Sorted common lymphoid progenitors from C57BL/6J and IκB− mice were pelleted, resuspended in OP9 media, and seeded at 500 cells per well in a 6-well plate with OP9 feeder cells without removing the existing conditioned OP9 media in the wells. Half the volume of the media per well was removed and replenished with fresh OP9 media at 24 h, 72 h, 120 h, and 168 h after the initial seeding of the progenitor cells. At day 5 and day 7 post initial seeding, the cell mixture was detached by trypsinization and resuspended to single cell suspension by passing through a 40 μm cell strainer. The differentiated cell sample was then analyzed by flow cytometry analysis on surface marker staining panel 10.

Single-cell RNA sequencing with Ig profiling

Bone marrow from two C57BL/6J (12 weeks old), C57BL/6J (100 weeks old), and IκB− (12 weeks old) mice were isolated and pooled according to their genotype and age respectively as independent samples. The pooled bone marrow cells were then magnetically enriched with B lineage and progenitor populations and sorted by flow cytometry with surface marker staining described in panel 11. The frequency of each developmental population was acquired by the BD FACS Aria instrument during the process of flow cytometry sorting. Sorted Dump− B220+ IgM− IgD− B cell progenitor/precursors were first washed once and resuspended in PBS with 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, Cat#A3059) while the concentration was adjusted to 1200 cells/μL. The cells were then submitted to UCLA Technology Center for Genomics & Bioinformatics for 10× Genomics Chromium captures. Each independent sample was loaded into separate GEM wells and Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Reagent Kit v1.0 protocol was applied. The cDNA and libraries were evaluated using the Agilent TapeStation system to ensure correct concentrations and size distributions. Enriched mouse B cell V(D)J profiling libraries across three samples were pooled and sequenced on one lane of NextSeq500 with 150 bp paired-end reads setup, while the gene expression libraries were pooled and sequenced on half lane of Novaseq S2 with 50 bp paired-end reads setup.

Single-cell RNAseq with Ig profiling: Data analysis

Cellranger workflows from 10× Genomics were applied in the data analysis.49 After fastq files were generated, data from Enriched mouse B cell V(D)J profiling libraries were processed by the cellranger vdj program to generate single-cell immunoglobulin sequences, while the count from gene expression libraries were generated, aggregated, and normalized to the same sequencing depth by the cellranger count and aggr programs. Potential doublets were removed by DoubletDetection48 and Scrublet.47 PCA was calculated in Seurat76 v4.3.0 with default parameters (counts were logNormalize, PCA calculated using standard scaling of top 2000 highly variable genes “vst” method). Immunoglobulin genes, which can be detected at better resolution by data from B cell V(D)J profiling libraries, and Xist, whose expression depends on sex, were excluded by cellranger and all subsequent analyses. We then used the lowest number of principal component groups sufficient to separate clusters by the cellranger reanalyze program. K-means gene expression clusters were mapped to their respective Hardy-fraction-defined developmental stages in C57BL/6J (12 weeks old) and C57BL/6J (100 weeks old) samples using established identifier genes Pax5, Il7r, VpreB1, VpreB2, VpreB3, Igll1, Rag1, Ccnd3, and Mki67 based on public datasets in Gene Expression Commons.50 An independent orthogonal analysis was performed using the tools cellHarmony52 and ICGS2.51 The expression of immunoglobulin heavy chain in single cells was determined by Ighm inclusion while the expression of immunoglobulin light chain was determined Igkc and Iglc inclusion in the immunoglobulin profiling data.

Machine learning with single-cell transcriptomic datasets

Scikit-learn77 0.23.1 package was used for the machine learning analysis. Aggregated datasets from cellranger including WT, aged WT, and IκB− datasets were used for the model input. The expression matrix with the information of mapped particular cellID and cellranger cluster profiles was used for model training and prediction. Training with stochastic gradient descent (SDG) used two types of WT dataset: a ‘marker gene’ expression matrix of the genes that are differentially upregulated across all different clusters, and an ‘all gene’ expression matrix of all genes detected in the original data matrix. All empty cells in the expression matrix were filled with zero. K-means clustering results from the cellranger analysis were used as the label. After randomized shuffling with fixed random seeds, 20% of the input dataset was used for testing, while 80% of the input dataset was then further randomly divided into 66.7% for training and 33.3% for validation using a 10-fold cross-validation process. The 10 independently trained models were used as replicate models for further prediction. Optimization of the meta parameter alpha aimed for the best accuracy score in the prediction of both the WT test dataset and IκB− dataset: 0.03 was optimal for the ‘all gene’ model, and 0.01 was optimal for the ‘marker gene’ model. Trained models used WT, aged WT, and IκB− datasets to predict cluster membership for each cell. The frequencies of machine learning classification among all cells in the same dataset were plotted in the bar graph and compared to the cellranger clustering results. The comparison between machine learning classification and cellranger clustering results yielded the matching frequencies (mean and standard error) plotted in the confusion matrix.

Pseudotime and RNA velocity analysis

The pseudotime reconstruction was performed using Slingshot78 (v2.3.1) and generated for each genotype independently. For each scRNAseq dataset, pseudotime values were first obtained for cells labeled Cluster A, B, and C. Only these cells were used as input for Slingshot with parameter “start.clus” set to Cluster A and “end.clus” set to Cluster C. Cells only from Clusters C, Cp, and D were then used as input for Slingshot with “start.clus” set to Cluster C and “end.clus” set to Cluster D. Final pseudotime values for Cluster Cp and D were then obtained by adding the maximum pseudotime from the first reconstruction. The first 20 principal components (calculated in Seurat v4.3.0 with default parameters) were the embeddings used for all Slingshot analyses. To generate the pseudotime trajectories for visualization a cubic smoothing spline with degrees of freedom set equal to 10 (“smooth.spline”, R stats v4.1.1) was fit to the paired data of generated pseudotimes and cell embedding for each desired dimension.

For each scRNAseq dataset, spliced and unspliced transcripts were first quantified using velocyto79 v0.17.17 with the command “run10×” on previously obtained cellranger output. Velocity vectors for each gene, were then obtained with scvelo80 v0.2.4. Preprocessing with scvelo (filtering, normalization, moments calculation) was completed with default parameters. Finally, velocity vectors for each cell were calculated using the stochastic model. The L2 norm of the velocity vector were computed for each cell to give the total velocity magnitude.

Bulk mRNA sequencing with spike-in controls

Bone marrow from a C57BL/6J and an IκB− mouse was isolated and lymphoid progenitors were magnetically enriched as previously described using the biotinylated antibodies at a concentration of 1:50 in staining panel 12. Following enrichment of the lymphoid progenitors, the cells were surface marker-stained using staining panel 9 as previously described. The cells were then sorted with the BD FACSAriaIII sorter in UCLA Janis V. Flow Cytometry Core Laboratory with adequate compensation setup by using UltraComp eBeads Compensation Beads and live cells with staining antibodies prior to actual sample sorting. We sorted approximately 204,000 WT and IκB− Fraction A-B cells and added 10ng of yeast RNA (strain BY7473, generous gift from T.L. Johnson lab) per 100,000 cells as an internal control. The RNA samples were then submitted to UCLA Technology Center for Genomics & Bioinformatics for library preparation and were sequenced using Novaseq X Plus 10B (1250M/lane). Raw data were filtered for adaptor sequences with cutadapt 4.181 with Python 3.10.8. Reads were processed by first concatenating the murine GRCm39 and yeast R64 genomes and annotations, and then aligning with STAR 2.7.1a.82 Default parameters were used except outFilterType BySJout, outFilterMultimapNmax 20, alignSJoverhangMin 8, alignSJDBoverhangMin 1, and seedSearchStartLmax 30. Aligned reads were wiltered based on mapping score (MAPQ≥30) by Samtools 1.983 and transcript counts were quantified using featureCounts v1.6.5.84 Data were then normalized by dividing counts for each mouse gene by the total number of yeast transcripts and scaling by 1e6.

Seahorse assay

Bone marrow from C57BL/6J and IκB− mice were isolated and lymphoid progenitors were magnetically enriched as previously described using the antibodies in staining panel 12 at a concentration of 1:50. Enriched progenitor B cells were seeded in a 96-well Seahorse culture plate (Part # 103799-100) (3 × 105 per well) with media that included 5mM glucose, 2mM glutamine, 1mM pyruvate, 5mM HEPES. OCR was then measured using the XF96 Seahorse Extracellular Flux Analyzer. After baseline measurements were taken, the plate was sequentially injected with treatments: first, 2 μM oligomycin; ~20 min later, 4 μM FCCP; and after another ~20 min, 2 μM AA/Rot.

Mitochondrial mass assay

Mitochondrial mass was assessed using MitoTracker Green (MTG). Cells were prepared in pre-warmed FACs buffer and adjusted to a density of ~1–2 × 10 ˆ6 cells/mL. MTG was added at a dilution of 1:1000 for 30 min at 37°C with 5% CO2. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with FACs for flow cytometry analysis.

Puromycin incorporation assay

Cells were plated in 96 well v bottom plates at a density of 2 × 106 in warm media. Puromycin (Invitrogen, Cat code ant-pr-1) was then added into the media at 10μg/mL and cultured for one hour. Cells were then pelleted, placed on ice, washed with FACS buffer and surface stained using the staining panel 13. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized following the FOXP3 kit (eBioscience, Cat # 00-5523-00) and stained with an antibody against puromycin (Biolegend, Cat# 381507) at a dilution of 1:250 in permeabilization buffer for one hour on ice. Cells were then washed with FACS buffer and analyzed on the BD LSRFortessa X-20.

In vivo rapamycin treatments

Rapamycin was prepared as a 15 mg/mL stock solution in ethanol and freshly diluted in water to a final concentration of 15 μg/mL. Mice received rapamycin continuously through their drinking water for 7 days, with fresh solution provided every 2–3 days. Three mice were assigned to the control group (water only) and three to the rapamycin-treated group. After 7 days, mice were euthanized, and bone marrow and spleens were collected for analysis.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

The total cell number in a specific population was quantified by multiplying the specific cell population frequency in purified and live bone marrow cells, acquired from the respective flow cytometry analysis, by the total live bone marrow or splenocyte cell count, determined by the 7AAD− population count under FSC-SSC gate right after primary cell extraction.

Details of the statistical tests applied to replicates of datasets shown in Figures can be found in the corresponding Figure legends. The value ‘n’ described in the figure legends indicated the number of the animals or biological replicates used in each group. All numerical figures were displayed as mean values of the group ±SEM. Unpaired Student’s t tests and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied using GraphPad Prism 6 software (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001). Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test was applied using Scipy.stat.kstest package on a python platform.
