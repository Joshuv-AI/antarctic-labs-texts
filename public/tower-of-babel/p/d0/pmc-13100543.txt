Authors: Kwong-Yue Eric Chan, Ho-On Alston Conrad Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: papillary muscles, tricuspid valve, valve repair

With increasing numbers of cardiac-implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) in use worldwide, the rate of lead failures and complications, such as CIED-related infections that necessitate complete system removal, has also risen. All-cause inpatient or 30-day mortality following transvenous lead extraction (TLE) varies from 1.4% to 4.1%.1, 2, 3

Tricuspid Regurgitation Complicating TLE and CIED Implantation

Complications following TLE, including cardiac tamponade, vascular injury, sepsis, and multiorgan failure, contribute to significant periprocedural mortality. Among these complications, tricuspid valve (TV) damage and subsequent tricuspid regurgitation (TR), with a reported rate of 11.5%, is one to be wary of.4 Consequent to removal of CIED leads with significant fibrotic adhesions to the leaflet or subvalvular apparatus, TR emerges as result of leaflet tear, chordal injury, or papillary muscle (PM) rupture.4 In addition, CIED-related infection and right-sided endocarditis may further contribute to TV damage. Multiple risk factors have been identified as being associated with traumatic TR following TLE, including longer lead implant duration and presence of multiple leads across the TV.

Meanwhile, focus on CIED-related TR has increased significantly with the advent of TV interventions. Multiple mechanisms have been identified, including leaflet impingement, subvalvular entanglement, leaflet perforation, pacing-induced right ventricular dyssynchrony, and right heart enlargement.5 Unfortunately, TLE may only be associated with improvement in CIED-related TR in 35% of the cases.6 Sorajja et al7 reported a similar rate of TR improvement following TLE (39.3%); however, 7.1% of patients experienced worsened TR afterward owing to PM rupture and tricuspid prolapse. A clinical dilemma therefore exists: Attempts to improve TR or facilitate potential transcatheter TV interventions with TLE may result in structural damage to the TV, rendering transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) or other interventions infeasible.

Evaluations for TLE in the Era of Multimodality Imaging

With expanding indications for TLE, one must be prepared for potential complications. Multimodality imaging enables identification of at-risk patients, facilitating prompt diagnosis of complications and guiding subsequent bailout strategies (Figure 1). Electrocardiography (ECG)-gated multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) has been used to evaluate CIED-lead course and termination, degree of fibrosis, patency of central veins, and presence of perforation through cardiac structures.8,9 Furthermore, MDCT is invaluable when planning TTVR in case of inadvertent TV injury. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) are useful in characterizing vegetations, lead adhesions, lead interactions with TV apparatus, and documenting pericardial effusion perioperatively and intraoperatively.10,11 With contemporary multiplanar reconstruction capabilities, TEE and ICE are indispensable for planning transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER).

Multimodality Imaging in Transvenous Lead Extraction

Different imaging modalities play different roles before (cyan boxes), during (purple boxes), and after TLE (orange boxes). Different imaging modalities enable risk stratification and planning for bailout strategies (eg, for T-TEER and TTVR) prior to TLE. For TLE risk stratification: 1) ECG-gated multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) evaluates CIED-lead integrity and course, degree of fibrosis and venous occlusion, and lead interactions with surrounding structures prior to TLE; 2) Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) evaluate lead interactions with surrounding structures and evaluate evidence of lead adhesions or vegetations. During TLE, TEE and ICE may be used to look for the presence of TR and pericardial effusion. For post-TLE complication assessment: 1) MDCT assesses the presence of injury to the surrounding structures and incomplete TLE; 2) TEE and ICE evaluate if there is presence of tricuspid regurgitation, pericardial effusion, or intracardiac shunts. CIED leads may pose challenges in image acquisition for TEE, and ICE may be used as a complementary imaging tool in evaluating TV in the setting of challenging anatomy. Novel Treatment Strategies for TR Complicating TLE

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Jang et al12 report an emergent case of TV-TEER following PM rupture during TLE, which resulted in acute hemodynamic compromise. Similar cases of TLE-related PM ruptures have been reported, requiring interventions in a semielective setting, including edge-to-edge repair of subpulmonary mitral valve, surgical TV replacement, and epicardial lead placement.13,14 One can anticipate a large flail and coaptation gap in the event of PM rupture. The operators employed a zipping strategy, starting from a midleaflet position along anteroseptal coaptation before moving onto the more centrally located pathology, thereby achieving bicuspidization of the TV. Other maneuvers to mitigate a large coaptation gap and improve effective leaflet grasping lengths include aggressive diuresis, ventilator-assisted Valsalva maneuver, increased positive end-expiratory pressure, and reverse Trendelenburg position.15 It is also important to maintain patients in a euvolemic state periprocedurally to minimize tension on the clip-leaflet complex, prevent single-leaflet device attachment, and facilitate right ventricular reverse remodeling. Finally, in case of massive tricuspid flail and annular dilatation, the usual TEER or radial force–dependent TTVR devices may not be suitable, while a TTVR system with a septal-anchoring system may be used, even in destroyed or heavily damaged leaflets after TLE.16
