Systemic remodeling of the multi-organ virome following Echinococcus infection in mice
Authors: Kemei Shi, Han Zhang, Likai Ji, Wang Li, Qing Zhang, Na Liu, Jia Liu, Shuai Guo, Shiyin Huang, Yue Chen, Xiongying Zhang, Wei Wang, Wen Lei, Shixing Yang, Quan Shen, Xiaochun Wang, Ping Wu, Yuwei Liu, Xiao Ma, Hongfeng Yang, Wen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interaction between parasitic infection and the host virome represents a frontier issue in microbial ecology, yet how Echinococcus infection affects the multi-organ virome and whether these alterations hold diagnostic or interventional potential remains poorly understood. In this study, we performed viral metagenomic sequencing on gut, liver, and lung samples from both infected and uninfected mice, integrating community structure clustering, diversity indices, and differential analyses, including STAMP and LEfSe. Our results reveal that Echinococcus infection induced significant tissue-specific virome remodeling. Compared to healthy controls, gut virome diversity increased, characterized by marked expansion of the class Caudoviricetes, particularly the family Siphoviridae (LDA > 4), alongside Picornaviridae enrichment (LDA > 4). In contrast, virome diversity decreased in both the liver and lung, with significant enrichment of Reoviridae (LDA > 4) in the liver and Retroviridae (LDA > 4) in the lung, respectively. Conversely, Picobirnaviridae (LDA > 4) was significantly reduced in the infected liver and lung. Based on phylogenetic analysis, Echinococcus infection significantly altered the murine gut viral community, with eukaryotic viruses (e.g., norovirus, picobirnavirus, and picornavirus) detected exclusively in infected animals, while bacteriophage populations remained stable across groups. Phage host prediction further revealed that phages enriched in infected samples targeted opportunistic pathogens (Clostridium septicum, Trueperella pyogenes), whereas control phages predominantly targeted commensals (Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron). Together, these findings demonstrate that Echinococcus infection drives both eukaryotic virus enrichment and a shift in phage predation toward pathogens, suggesting that infection-induced immune modulation creates a permissive environment for viral replication and associated bacterial dysbiosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04923-x.

Introduction

The genus Echinococcus comprises several clinically significant species that cause distinct forms of echinococcosis in humans: Echinococcus granulosus is the etiological agent of cystic echinococcosis (CE), characterized by unilocular hydatid cyst formation; Echinococcus multilocularis causes alveolar echinococcosis (AE), which manifests as infiltrative, tumor-like lesions with metastatic potential; while Echinococcus Vogeli and Echinococcus oligarthrus are responsible for polycystic echinococcosis in neotropical regions. Among these, AE carries the most severe clinical prognosis, exhibiting progressive hepatic destruction and extrahepatic dissemination in untreated cases, ultimately resulting in mortality rates exceeding 90% within 10 years of diagnosis [1]. Disease manifestation subsequently occurs through two distinct pathophysiological mechanisms: in CE, via mechanical compression effects caused by enlarging hydatid cysts and subsequent local tissue damage; whereas in AE, it proceeds through progressive infiltrative growth of metacestode lesions, predominantly hepatic [2], which exhibit malignant-like biological behavior characterized by local tissue destruction and potential metastatic spread.

Echinococcus multilocularis is a zoonotic cestode causing AE, one of the most lethal parasitic diseases in humans [3, 4]. Human infection with Echinococcus multilocularis primarily occurs through the accidental ingestion of viable parasite eggs present in contaminated environmental matrices (such as water, soil, or food) or via direct contact with infected definitive hosts, particularly domestic canids and wild foxes [5]. The pathogenesis of human echinococcosis begins with an asymptomatic incubation period where, upon ingestion, eggs release oncospheres that penetrate the intestinal wall and migrate via the portal system to target organs (mainly the liver and lungs). These develop into unilocular cysts (1–15 cm) with characteristic germinal and laminated layers, surrounded by host-derived fibrous capsules. Cyst contents (“hydatid sand”) contain protoscolices that can infect definitive hosts upon ingestion, completing the life cycle [6–9]. This prolonged latency-to-onset transition underscores the insidious nature of these zoonoses and highlights the diagnostic challenges inherent in their clinical management.

The parasite’s life cycle involves foxes and other canids as definitive hosts [10], where adult worms reside in the small intestine, and rodents or accidental human hosts, in which larval metacestodes infiltrate the liver, forming tumor-like lesions [11, 12]. AE manifests with a prolonged asymptomatic phase (5–15 years), which is followed by hepatic failure and metastatic spread if left untreated. The disease’s clinical management relies on radical surgery combined with long-term albendazole therapy, yet recurrence rates remain high [13].

Recent advances in the diagnosis of Echinococcus multilocularis infection have incorporated multimodal approaches, including microscopic examination, coproantigen detection by ELISA, and PCR-based copro-DNA analysis [14–18]. However, the lack of standardized protocols and diagnostic criteria remains a significant challenge for reliable epidemiological surveillance and comparative risk assessment across endemic regions.

Beyond direct host-parasite interactions, accumulating evidence highlights the host microbiota’s role in parasitic infections. However, the viral component remains largely unexplored in parasitology. The virome comprises bacteriophages and eukaryotic viruses that influence host physiology. Recent studies demonstrate that virome alterations serve as sensitive indicators of microbiome disturbances in various diseases, with shifts in viral composition emerging as a consistent signature of dysbiosis [19].

Investigating virome dynamics in Echinococcus multilocularis infection is supported by multiple lines of evidence. This is evidenced, in part, by the direct detection of viral sequences within metacestode cysts, confirming that extra-intestinal parasitic niches can harbor viral communities [20]. Complementing this, research on tripartite interactions has shown that bacteriophages can indirectly influence host-parasite outcomes by modulating bacterial phenotypes [21]. Furthermore, studies on other hepatic disorders have documented significant gut virome alterations, characterized by the differential abundance of multiple viral families, underscoring the role of multi-kingdom microbial interactions in disease pathogenesis [22, 23].

To address these limitations and further elucidate host-parasite interactions, this study employs viral metagenomics to systematically investigate virome dynamics during Echinococcus multilocularis infection. By characterizing alterations in viral communities of key target organs (liver, lung, and gut) in infected and control groups, we aim to identify infection-associated viral taxa with significantly altered abundance and pinpoint viral signatures that are consistently enriched or depleted during Echinococcus multilocularis infection. These differentially abundant viral taxa may represent candidate targets for further investigation, offering a foundation for developing novel diagnostic strategies and potentially enhancing our understanding of disease pathogenesis. This virome-based approach complements conventional diagnostic methods and may provide new insights into the development of comprehensive detection strategies. The integration of viral metagenomic data with existing diagnostic frameworks could significantly advance our understanding of disease progression and treatment options for this zoonotic threat.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted using specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male KM mice (body weight: 15 g), purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (China) (Laboratory Animal Production License No.: SCXK(Su)2020-0009). The experiments were performed in a Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory under SPF conditions at the Qinghai Institute for Endemic Disease Prevention and Control (China), which holds an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) license (No.: SYXK(Qing)2023-000). All procedures strictly adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council) and complied with the Jiangsu Laboratory Animal Quality Certification (Certificate No.: B202312190153) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Establishment of the AE model and sample collection

In this study, a murine model of secondary alveolar echinococcosis (AE) was established by intraperitoneal injection of Echinococcus multilocularis protoscolices into experimental mice. The protoscolices were obtained from gerbils maintained in our laboratory for research purposes (Supplementary Fig. 2). All experimental mice were placed in an induction chamber and anesthetized with 3% isoflurane delivered in a mixture of oxygen and air. Complete anesthesia, as determined by the loss of the righting reflex and the absence of response to a toe pinch, was achieved within 2–3 min. The gerbils were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the hydatid cysts were aseptically dissected. The cyst contents were placed in a Petri dish containing physiological saline (0.9% sterile sodium chloride injection, Kelun Pharmaceutical, China), minced, and filtered through an 80-mesh sieve. The filtrate was collected in sterile centrifuge tubes and repeatedly washed with saline until a distinct milky-white lower layer and a clear upper layer were observed. Protoscolex viability was assessed using 0.1% methylene blue staining solution (Leagene, China), confirming a viability rate exceeding 90%. The protoscolices were further washed and resuspended in physiological saline containing 800 U/mL of penicillin-streptomycin (North China Pharmaceutical, China).

A total of 20 six-week-old male SPF KM mice were purchased and acclimatized for one week under experimental conditions. To mitigate the risk of model failure, the infection group was supplemented with four additional mice, resulting in an initial total of 12 mice for infection. After the acclimatization period, the mice were randomly allocated into an experimental group (n = 12) and a control group (n = 8). Each mouse in the experimental group received an intraperitoneal injection of 0.3 mL of protoscolices suspension (approximately 3000 protoscolices) using a sterile disposable syringe, while control mice were injected with an equal volume of physiological saline containing antibiotics. One mouse in the infection group died approximately 20 days post-inoculation of an undetermined cause. Consequently, 19 mice (infection group = 11, control group = 8) were retained for analysis at 180 days post-infection, when they were euthanized by cervical dislocation after being anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, and subsequently dissected. Tissue samples (liver, lung, and gut) were collected and immediately stored at -80 °C for further experiments. All mice were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water.

Sample processing and viral library construction

Frozen specimens stored at -80 °C were thawed at 4 °C to minimize protein degradation. Each sample was homogenized using sterile quartz sand for mechanical disruption, followed by resuspension in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) to facilitate viral particle release. To ensure efficient dissociation, samples were subjected to two high-intensity vortex cycles (12,000 rpm, 5 min each) with a 15-minute incubation interval between cycles. Cellular debris, bacterial contaminants, and insoluble aggregates were removed via centrifugation (15,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), yielding a clarified supernatant. The supernatant was carefully transferred to fresh sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes for subsequent analyses. This standardized protocol was uniformly applied to all tissue types, including liver, lung, and gut specimens, to ensure methodological consistency across samples [24].

Samples were stratified into 57 individual pools (one pool per specimen), comprising gut, liver, and lung tissues collected from 11 infected and 8 control mice. To enrich for viral particles, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to pellet eukaryotic and bacterial cellular debris. The supernatants were collected and subsequently filtered through a 0.45-µm pore-size membrane (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) to remove bacteria and residual cellular debris larger than 0.45 μm, while allowing intact viral particles to pass through, thereby achieving preliminary viral enrichment [25]. To eliminate extracellular host and microbial nucleic acids that could interfere with the metagenomic signal, the virus-enriched filtrates were treated with a nuclease cocktail containing DNases (Turbo DNase, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Baseline-ZERO DNase, Epicentre, USA; Benzonase Nuclease, Novagen, USA) and RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 60 min. This enzymatic cocktail efficiently digested naked DNA and RNA present in the samples, while nucleic acids protected within intact viral capsids remained sequestered. Following enzymatic digestion, total viral nucleic acids (including both DNA and RNA) were extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [26]. Given the presence of viral RNA, the extracted nucleic acids were subjected to reverse transcription using SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with random hexamer primers to synthesize first-strand cDNA. Subsequently, second-strand DNA synthesis was performed using the Klenow fragment polymerase (New England BioLabs, USA) to generate double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) suitable for downstream library preparation. This step simultaneously converted any single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viral genomes present in the samples into dsDNA, ensuring uniform library construction. The resulting dsDNA products from all 57 pools were used to construct sequencing libraries with the Illumina Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). To enable multiplex sequencing, unique dual-index barcoding was applied. Libraries were amplified via limited-cycle PCR and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) using 150-bp paired-end sequencing [27, 28].

Bioinformatics analysis

For comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, the 150-bp paired-end reads from all 57 sequencing libraries were processed through a customized analytical pipeline implemented on a 32-node Linux high-performance computing cluster. The pipeline incorporated the following key steps: first, PCR duplicates were identified and removed by comparing bases 5–55 of each read pair, retaining a single representative copy. Sequence quality control was performed by trimming low-quality terminal regions (Phred quality score < 30) and removing adapter sequences using NCBI’s VecScreen tool with default parameters (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/vecscreen/). The processed reads were then subjected to de novo assembly within each barcode group using Geneious Prime v2020.4.5 [29]. For taxonomic classification, the assembled contigs were analyzed through DIAMOND v2.1.8 BLASTx searches against both the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database and the NCBI viral reference database sequentially, applying a stringent E-value cutoff of < 10⁻⁵ to ensure high-confidence matches [30, 31].

Viral community analysis

Viral community composition and diversity analyses were conducted using an integrated bioinformatics workflow. Taxonomic profiling was performed in MEGAN v6.19.9 [32], where community composition data were normalized using built-in algorithms to account for variations in sequencing depth across libraries. Statistical comparisons of virome structures were implemented in R v4.4.3 through the psych, vegan, and UpSetR packages [33, 34]. All visualizations were generated using ggplot2 (Supplementary File). Alpha diversity (within-sample) was assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Beta diversity (between-sample) was evaluated via Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, with group separation tested using PERMANOVA. Differential abundance analysis was performed using STAMP v2.1.3, applying Welch’s t-test with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons [35]. Biomarker discovery was conducted using LEfSe [36] (Galaxy implementation, http://galaxy.biobakery.org/) to identify differentially abundant taxa between control and Echinococcus-infected groups. Default parameters were applied (Wilcoxon test, LDA score > 2.0). Additionally, we performed phage-host prediction using the CHERRY module within the PhaBOX framework [37]. All analyses were conducted in RStudio 2024.12.1 to ensure computational reproducibility.

Phylogenetic analysis of viral sequences

Phylogenetic reconstruction was conducted using a multi-step analytical pipeline. Viral nucleotide and amino acid sequences identified in this study were compared with their top BLASTx matches from the NCBI GenBank database and representative sequences from corresponding viral families. Putative open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Geneious Prime v2024.0.5. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT v7.450 [38] under default parameters, followed by Bayesian phylogenetic inference using MrBayes v3.2.7 [39]. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis employed mixed substitution models and was run until convergence (average standard deviation of split frequencies < 0.01). To ensure that the posterior probabilities were calculated from a stable distribution, the initial 25% of the sampled trees and parameters were discarded as burn-in. Final phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) platform [40] and ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online), with branch support values representing posterior probabilities.

Quality control

To minimize potential contamination during sample processing, strict preventive measures were implemented throughout the experimental workflow. All procedures were performed in a BSL-3 biosafety cabinet, where the workspace was decontaminated with 10% bleach and 70% ethanol between processing sessions, followed by UV irradiation for 30 min before use. Furthermore, all steps were conducted using aerosol-barrier filter tips to prevent cross-contamination and nucleic acid carryover. All tubes and consumables used were certified nuclease-free.

Results

AE model evaluation

The successful establishment of the murine alveolar echinococcosis (AE) model was confirmed by gross pathological examination, which revealed characteristic hepatic lesions markedly distinct from those of control mice. The Echinococcus multilocularis lesions appeared as off-white or yellowish-white masses, consisting of densely packed small vesicles aggregated in a cauliflower-like pattern, with irregular and nodular surfaces that were smooth and translucent. The vesicles contained trace amounts of clear fluid. The presence of these macroscopic AE lesions during necropsy successfully validated the disease model (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Overview of the virome

To comprehensively assess viral communities across distinct anatomical niches, we performed metagenomic sequencing of 57 libraries derived from gut, liver, and lung specimens using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Our optimized virome enrichment pipeline yielded a total of 1.3 billion quality-filtered reads, with 684,711,330 reads from gut samples (range: 5,370,252–80,719,964 per library), 299,469,926 reads from liver samples (range: 1,679,480–61,928,288 per library), and 320,212,742 reads from lung samples (range: 1,609,888–56,004,670 per library). De novo assembly of these datasets identified 10,852 viral contigs, which were assessed using absolute counts, providing a robust foundation for phylogenetic analysis. To evaluate virome compositional shifts associated with Echinococcus infection, samples were stratified into six experimental cohorts: Echinococcus-infected gut (n = 11, EchC01–EchC11), healthy control gut (n = 7, ConC02–ConC08; one sample [ConC01] was excluded due to the absence of viral hits against the NR database), Echinococcus-infected liver (n = 11, EchG01–EchG11), and healthy control livers (n = 8, ConG01–ConG08), Echinococcus-infected lung (n = 11, EchF01–EchF11), and healthy control lungs (n = 8, ConF01–ConF08). Detailed information for each library is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Species accumulation curves approached asymptotes for most infection cohorts, indicating sufficient sampling depth, whereas control group curves (notably ConG) rose more gradually, reflecting higher richness. To address these saturation variations and unequal library sizes, taxonomic counts were normalized via MEGAN to ensure unbiased comparative analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Viral community composition

Hierarchical clustering of viral family abundance segregated samples by infection status and tissue type: control liver and lung samples formed a tight cluster distinct from their infected counterparts, whereas both infected and control gut samples clustered together. Viral family abundance heatmap further delineated these patterns, with infection-associated taxa including gut-enriched Caliciviridae, Picornaviridae, and Caudoviricetes members (Microviridae, Siphoviridae, Myoviridae, and Podoviridae), as well as infected liver-enriched Parvoviridae and Reoviridae. In contrast, control liver and lung samples showed higher abundances of Schitoviridae, Herpesviridae, Coronaviridae, Picobirnaviridae, and several bacteriophage families (Chaseviridae, Drexlerviridae, Autographiviridae, Demerecviridae, and Herelleviridae) (Fig. 1a).

Stacked plots were employed to visualize the relative abundances and compositional proportions of different viral families across samples. In healthy control gut samples (ConC02–ConC08), the virome was predominantly composed of Microviridae and Podoviridae, reflecting the typical bacteriophage-mediated regulatory network characteristic of a balanced gut ecosystem. In contrast, Echinococcus-infected specimens (EchC01–EchC11) exhibited marked virome restructuring, characterized by notable alterations: the maintained dominance of Microviridae alongside significantly increased Siphoviridae abundance; and an expansion of Myoviridae, Picobirnaviridae, Astroviridae, and Picornaviridae as significant components of the infected gut virome, which may reflect either opportunistic viral proliferation or altered gut permeability (Fig. 1b).

At the family level, control samples (ConG01–ConG08) maintained a stable viral profile dominated by Microviridae, Picobirnaviridae, Retroviridae, and Myoviridae. In stark contrast, infected liver samples (EchG01–EchG11) exhibited dramatic shifts characterized by: an explosive expansion of Reoviridae to dominance; sustained high Retroviridae abundance; a near-complete collapse of the previously dominant Microviridae and Picobirnaviridae; and a significant reduction in Podoviridae and Siphoviridae representation (Fig. 1c).

The lung virome underwent significant structural reorganization at the family level following infection with Echinococcus. Control samples (ConF01–ConF08) displayed a characteristic viral profile dominated by Retroviridae, Podoviridae, Microviridae, Picobirnaviridae, and Chaseviridae, reflecting the low viral biomass but stable bacteriophage colonization typical of healthy pulmonary environments. Infection induced profound virome alterations, including an expansion of Retroviridae and Autographiviridae, accompanied by near-complete depletion of the previously dominant Picobirnaviridae and Microviridae. The phage community exhibited marked restructuring, with Podoviridae abundance decreasing. Notably, we observed increased detection of eukaryotic viruses (Herpesviridae) alongside the virtual disappearance of Parvoviridae. While phages reflect changes in the bacterial microbiome, the shift in eukaryotic viral composition may indicate compromised pulmonary immunity. This altered virome profile could contribute to the pathophysiology of pulmonary echinococcosis during chronic helminth infection (Fig. 1d).

Virome diversity analysis in Echinococcosis

To comprehensively evaluate the tissue-specific virome alterations induced by Echinococcus infection, we conducted comparative diversity analyses of viral communities across gut, liver, and lung specimens from infected and control groups using alpha- and beta-diversity analysis metrics [41, 42]. In gut specimens, the Shannon index and the inverse Simpson index were significantly higher in the infected group than in the control group (p < 0.05), indicating an increase in overall community richness and evenness. Correspondingly, the Simpson index was significantly lower in the infected group (p < 0.05), reflecting a decrease in the dominance of specific taxa within the infected group (Fig. 2a). Conversely, in both lung and liver specimens from infected mice, the Shannon and inverse Simpson indices were significantly lower, while the Simpson index was significantly higher, compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b and c). Principal coordinate analysis based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity demonstrated significant β-diversity segregation between infected and control groups across all tissues (gut: R² = 0.22, p < 0.05; liver: R² = 0.348, p < 0.05; lung: R² = 0.37, p < 0.05), with multivariate dispersion tests confirming substantial β-dispersion heterogeneity (Fig. 2d, e, f).

Difference of virome

Viral species distribution analysis revealed distinct tissue-specific patterns between control and Echinococcus-infected mice. Among the 70 identified species, control mice harbored 16, 8, and 3 unique species in the gut (ConC), liver (ConG), and lung (ConF), respectively, while infected mice possessed 9, 0, and 2 unique species in corresponding tissues (EchC, EchG, EchF). Intersection analysis identified four core species shared across all groups. Pairwise and multi-group overlaps were also observed, notably including two species shared between EchG and EchF, and among ConC and EchC, and the combination of ConG, ConF, EchG, and EchF (Fig. 3a).

Differential viral abundance across tissues was first analyzed using STAMP (Welch’s t-test, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, adjusted p < 0.05), which revealed significant shifts in the virome of infected mice. In the gut, infection led to a significant enrichment of Aichivirus E (Picornaviridae) and increased abundance of Siphoviridae and Myoviridae bacteriophages (Fig. 3b). In the liver, infection amplified murine leukemia virus (MLV) (Retroviridae) and Chenuda virus (Reoviridae) but reduced Myoviridae, Picobirnavirus, Microvirus, and unclassified Carltongylesvirus (Fig. 3c). The lung virome of infected mice showed marked proliferation of MLV, concurrent with the depletion of Picobirnavirus, Microviridae, and Microvirus (Fig. 3d).

To further validate these observations and identify the most discriminant taxa, we performed LEfSe analysis (Fig. 4). This analysis confirmed that the viral families highlighted by STAMP were indeed the most significantly altered. In the gut, both Picornaviridae and Siphoviridae were significantly discriminant, with LDA scores exceeding 4. However, phages from the class Caudoviricetes, particularly the family Siphoviridae, were the most discriminant feature in the infected group (EchC). This finding aligns with their increased abundance observed in the STAMP analysis (Fig. 4a, b). In the liver, LEfSe confirmed the compositional shift observed in STAMP: the control group was characterized by high phage diversity (e.g., Caudoviricetes class, Microviridae) alongside Picobirnaviridae (LDA > 4) and Coronaviridae (LDA > 3), whereas the infected group (EchG) was distinguished by the Reoviridae family (LDA > 4), which consistently showed increased abundance in the STAMP analysis (Fig. 4c, d). Similarly, in the lung, LEfSe validated the STAMP findings by identifying Picobirnaviridae (LDA > 4) as the most discriminant family in the control group (ConF). In stark contrast, Retroviridae (LDA > 4) emerged as the most discriminant feature in the infected group (EchF) (Fig. 4e, f).

Phylogenetic analysis

Using Geneious, we assembled and annotated viral genomes from the gut, liver, and lung tissues of Echinococcus-infected and healthy mice. Near-complete or complete genomes were prioritized for phylogenetic analysis. The murine virome comprised bacteriophages (class Caudoviricetes, and families Microviridae and Inoviridae) and eukaryotic viruses (Picobirnaviridae, Coronaviridae, and Parvoviridae), which were further characterized based on genome completeness and potential biological relevance.

Members of the family Parvoviridae are small DNA viruses known for their ability to establish long-term, often lifelong, infections in vertebrates and have garnered attention as potential modulators of the host immune landscape [43]. In this study, our viromic analysis yielded three complete parvovirus genomes (4,240–4,315 nt), with GC contents ranging from 45.7% to 46.0%. Phylogenetic inference based on both NS1 and VP1 protein sequences consistently placed these viruses within the genus Chaphamaparvovirus (Fig. 5a–c). Notably, these viruses exhibited exceptionally high amino acid identity (> 98.7%) to strains previously isolated from the kidney of Mus musculus (e.g., QLM06163). This high degree of conservation and genomic completeness strongly suggests that these viruses are not recent acquisitions but rather resident viruses naturally associated with the studied mouse population.

Members of the family Caliciviridae infect a broad range of hosts, including humans and animals, with noroviruses representing a leading cause of non-bacterial gastroenteritis in humans [44, 45]. Norovirus was detected exclusively in intestinal samples from the Echinococcus-infected group, including one complete genome (7,452 nt, 56.9% GC). Phylogenetic analysis of RdRp and VP1 protein sequences clustered these strains within murine norovirus clades (Fig. 5d–f). Specifically, the RdRp sequences (CaliEchC04_814 and CaliEchC05_180087) shared 98.69%–99.35% amino acid identity with reference strains, including an immunocompromised-mouse-derived strain (YP_720001). For VP1, CaliEchC05_180087 and CaliEchC07_4421 exhibited 94.59%–97.04% similarity to murine norovirus references (QOF88698 and AEE10027). The exclusive presence of norovirus in infected animals suggests that Echinococcus infection enhances enteric viral susceptibility, identifying norovirus as a potential indicator of intestinal immune status.

Members of the family Inoviridae are filamentous bacteriophages that establish chronic infections in bacteria without lysis [46]. We recovered eight complete inovirus genomes (5,285–7,797 nt; GC 40.7%–57.4%) from gut samples of both Echinococcus-infected and control mice (Fig. 6a). Phylogenetic analysis using both Zot-based single-gene and ViPTree [47] whole-genome approaches consistently placed these sequences within Inoviridae but distinct from known genera (Fig. 6b, c). Zot amino acid identities to reference strains (66.98%–69.08%) fell below the genus-level threshold (> 75%) [48], and whole-genome analysis confirmed they form a distinct clade. Host group analysis suggests an association with Pseudomonadota bacteria. These findings indicate that these viruses represent a putative novel genus within Inoviridae and are likely resident members of the murine gut phage community rather than infection-associated invaders.

Members of the family Picornaviridae are small RNA viruses that infect diverse vertebrate hosts [49]. From intestinal samples of Echinococcus-infected mice, we recovered six complete picornavirus genomes (8,001–8,376 nt). Phylogenetic analysis based on the conserved RdRp gene identified two distinct viral strains (Fig. 6d). One strain (PicornaEchC08_6028; 8,288 nt) showed 92.94% amino acid identity to an unclassified picornavirus derived from Mus musculus (XDO02759). The other strain (PicornaEchC05_10939; 8,338 nt) exhibited 97.77% amino acid identity to Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus (AEC04618), placing it within the Cardiovirus genus. Both strains were detected exclusively in infected samples, suggesting a potential association with Echinococcus infection.

Members of the family Picobirnaviridae are small, non-enveloped viruses with segmented dsRNA genomes [50, 51]. From the intestinal samples of Echinococcus-infected mice, we identified 74 picobirnavirus contigs, including 33 containing the conserved RdRp region, yielding 9 unique sequences (6 Orthopicobirnavirus and 3 unclassified). Phylogenetic analysis based on RdRp revealed diverse sequence identities (75.30%–97.03%) to reference strains from various hosts (marmot, pig, dog, chicken) (Fig. 6e). Notably, one sequence (PicobirnaEchC07_6966) formed an independent clade. All picobirnaviruses were detected exclusively in infected samples, suggesting a potential association with Echinococcus infection.

Members of the family Coronaviridae are positive-sense RNA viruses infecting diverse hosts [52–54]. From Echinococcus-infected samples, two sequences (CoronaEchF02_127011 and CoronaEchF04_188) formed an independent clade based on N-protein phylogeny, showing 97.03% amino acid identity to sparrow deltacoronavirus HKU17 (YP_005352850) (Fig. 7a). In control samples, RdRp-based analysis showed that sequences from the liver, lung, and intestine clustered together (Fig. 7b).

Members of the family Genomoviridae are small single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viruses encoding replication-associated (Rep) and capsid (CP) proteins [55, 56]. From the intestinal samples of Echinococcus-infected mice, we identified three genomovirus sequences (one unclassified and two members of the genus Gemykrogvirus). Rep-based phylogeny showed that GenomoEchC05_90727 formed a distinct branch with 57.14% amino acid identity to a human respiratory-derived sequence (YP_010798590) (Fig. 7c). CP-based phylogeny placed GenomoEchC10_223090_2 and GenomoEchC10_223090 in separate clades, showing 95.56% and 75.16% identity to a peacock-associated sequence (QTE03636) (Fig. 7d).

Members of the family Microviridae are small ssDNA phages with circular genomes (4.4–6.7 kb). We identified 102 complete microvirus genomes (4.4–6.7 kb) from the mouse samples (Fig. 8a). VP1-based phylogeny revealed two distinct groups of unclassified Microviridae, which were separate from established genera (Fig. 8b). Representative sequences showed diverse identities: EchC10_327346 shared 89.55% identity to a microvirus from lizard feces (QPB07455); ConC03_176249 showed 91.02% identity to a mouse feces-derived microvirus (USL90283); others clustered with sequences from elk, peat soil, and environmental swabs.

Bacteriophages of the class Caudoviricetes are prevalent dsDNA phages that modulate microbial ecology and host immunity [57, 58]. We identified Caudoviricetes-associated contigs from murine intestinal samples, including 81 complete TerL-encoding ORFs (744–3,255 nt; GC 28.5%–61.1%), all classified as unclassified Caudoviricetes. TerL-based phylogeny revealed three distinct clusters separate from known Caudovirales families (Fig. 8c). Their presence in both infected and control groups suggests that they are stable components of the murine gut virome, potentially contributing to gut microbial homeostasis.

Host prediction

To explore the biological implications underlying the virome alterations, we performed bacterial host prediction for the assembled viral genomes. Phages enriched in the control group predominantly targeted gut commensal bacteria, including Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron and Lactobacillus reuteri. In contrast, phages enriched in the infected group were significantly directed against various opportunistic pathogens. Notably, phages associated with Clostridium septicum exhibited a higher detection frequency in the infected group (n = 3), compared to a lower frequency in the control group (n = 2). Remarkably, phages targeting Trueperella pyogenes and Porphyromonas gingivalis were detected exclusively in the infected group (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study reveals tissue-specific virome alterations induced by Echinococcus infection. Compared to healthy controls, the gut virome exhibited increased diversity following infection, primarily characterized by the significant expansion of bacteriophage communities such as Siphoviridae (LDA score > 4.0), accompanied by an increase in eukaryotic viruses, including Picornaviridae (LDA score > 4.0). This shift contrasts sharply with the stable phage community dominated by Microviridae and Podoviridae in healthy guts, potentially reflecting infection-induced gut microbiota remodeling and barrier dysfunction [59].

The liver virome displayed a markedly distinct alteration pattern following infection: the diverse communities present in healthy livers (e.g., Microviridae and Picobirnaviridae) were significantly enriched, whereas Reoviridae exhibited explosive expansion (LDA score > 4.0), alongside an increase in Retroviridae. This transition from “phage-dominated” to “eukaryotic virus-dominated” profiles suggests that parasite-induced local immunosuppression may create conditions conducive to opportunistic virus reactivation [60].

Lung virome remodeling was equally pronounced. The stable virome structure in healthy lungs, comprising Retroviridae, Podoviridae, Microviridae, and Picobirnaviridae (LDA score > 2.0), was disrupted following infection, characterized by significant enrichment of Retroviridae (LDA score > 4.0), the near-complete depletion of Picobirnaviridae and Microviridae, and the increased detection of Herpesviridae. LEfSe analysis further confirmed Picobirnaviridae as the most discriminative family in healthy lungs, while Retroviridae emerged as the core discriminant taxon in infected lungs. These alterations point to a potential impairment of pulmonary antiviral immunity driven by Echinococcus infection.

The concurrent proliferation of MLV in the lungs and liver suggests potential systemic dissemination, possibly mediated by immune modulation, though the underlying mechanisms require further validation. Notably, the increased MLV abundance in infected SPF mice may reflect stress-induced activation of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) rather than exogenous transmission. Given the absence of these retroviral elements in humans, extrapolation to human alveolar echinococcosis warrants caution [61].

Based on phylogenetic analysis, we demonstrate that Echinococcus infection induces distinct shifts in the murine gut virome, with eukaryotic viruses (e.g., Norovirus, Picobirnavirus, and Picornavirus) enriched exclusively in infected animals, while bacteriophage populations remain relatively stable across groups. This pattern suggests that helminth infection creates a permissive environment for enteric virus replication, likely through immune modulation and intestinal barrier disruption.

Phage-host prediction revealed that Echinococcus infection enriches phages targeting opportunistic pathogens (Clostridium septicum and Trueperella pyogenes), while control phages predominantly target commensals (Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron). This suggests that infection-induced immune modulation may weaken pathogen surveillance, creating niches for bacterial expansion [62]. These findings are consistent with the broader literature demonstrating that helminth infections (e.g., Schistosoma and hookworms) can reshape host-associated microbial and viral communities through immune-mediated pathways [63, 64].

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides the systematic characterization of the multi-organ virome in Echinococcus infection through a comprehensive comparison with healthy control groups. In the intestine, Siphoviridae (LDA > 4) is significantly discriminant, with Picornaviridae (LDA > 4) also enriched. Reoviridae (LDA > 4) characterizes the infected liver, and Retroviridae (LDA > 4) characterizes the infected lung. Conversely, Picobirnaviridae (LDA > 4) was significantly reduced in the infected liver and lung. These findings uncover tissue-specific virome signatures associated with Echinococcus infection, offering a valuable reference for future clinical applications and diagnostic development.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The absence of technical negative controls during sample processing may affect the interpretation of viral signals, as the possibility of contaminant-derived sequences cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, the use of specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice, while enabling controlled conditions, does not reflect the chronic co-infections and microbial heterogeneityfound in natural settings, which may influence virome composition. Finally, although the identified viral signatures were robust in the murine model, these findings remain exploratory and require validation in human clinical cohorts to assess their translational relevance. Future studies should incorporate these factors to refine our understanding.
