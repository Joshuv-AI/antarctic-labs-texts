Manifold-based approach for neural network robustness analysis
Authors: Ali Sekmen, Bahadir Bilgin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
It is important to understand the mathematical foundations of neural networks and to include robustness in model evaluation. Here, we introduce algorithms based on manifold curvature estimation to assess neural network robustness. These algorithms rely solely on training data and do not require regular or adversarial test data. Initially, a metric is proposed to measure the curvature of discrete data manifolds by introducing weighted angles concept between subspaces. Following this, a robustness measure is introduced that is independent of network architecture or model parameters. Lastly, two additional methods are introduced, utilizing curvature estimation of special manifolds formed by using gradient vectors between output and input network layers, alongside manifold curvature estimation. A comprehensive evaluation is provided on multiple network models using the CIFAR-10 dataset. Manifold geometry-based robustness analysis may lead to the development of not only accurate but also robust neural network models.

Introduction

In recent years, we have witnessed remarkable advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI) that heavily rely on deep learning, i.e., various forms of neural networks. These networks are widely used in numerous fields, including robotics1, bioinformatics2, finance3, entertainment4, and visual art5. With the exponential growth of data generated by businesses, individuals, and devices, as well as increasing availability of computational resources, AI research is largely focused on the applied side. However, there is a strong need to understand the underlying mathematical foundations to develop explainable AI with not only accurate but also robust neural networks.

Machine learning model accuracy is defined as the proportion of correct predictions for a given input set, whereas robustness measures the ability of a model to maintain its performance in the face of naturally occurring changes to the input set6. Relying solely on accuracy-based comparisons can be misleading, especially in safety-critical applications such as robotics, self-driving vehicles, or flight control systems. It is important to include robustness as part of model evaluation, in addition to accuracy. Adversarial attacks are often used to generate naturally occurring changes, but they are specific to each network. Although neural networks tend to outperform other machine learning techniques in terms of accuracy, they lack explainability due to the highly non-linear mapping of the input space to the output space.

The ability of a network model to make accurate and safe decisions when encountering unexpected input or situations is directly related to its robustness. This term is closely related to other terms, such as explainability, input invariance, and network sensitivity7. While earlier studies suggested a trade-off between accuracy and robustness8, recent studies propose methods to achieve both high accuracy and robustness without sacrificing one for the other9. Numerous efforts are underway to improve the robustness of networks and evaluate their performance10.

Various techniques have been proposed to enhance the robustness of neural network models, including stochastic activation functions11, adversarial training methods12–15, model ensembles16–18, regularization methods12,19–21, and optimization with robustness certificates22–25. Many of these methods involve calculating gradients, with some methods using gradients to compute perturbations and others using perturbations to create attacks through adversarial examples. Then, these models either apply various techniques to bound the gradients/perturbations or measure those bounds or performance against adversarial examples. Despite these differences, the shared starting point for robustness definitions is some form of gradient measures. Many of these attack-dependent or attack-independent methods are white-box attacks (such as in13,26) where the model and all model parameters are known. However, there is less focus on black-box attacks and measures. Although there are a limited number of methods to create black-box robustness measures for models (e.g., as in27–29), they employ different ways to approximate model gradients indirectly. Using gradients in one way or another makes sense because robust models, by definition, are expected to exhibit minimal changes in output when inputs are slightly altered, and hence the definition of robustness is directly linked to gradients, and all research in this area is done from this perspective.

However, the difference between test and training errors can also be considered as a measure of robustness. This difference is naturally expected to be higher for more complex models30 compared to lower-capacity models for a given task. Based on the manifold assumption hypothesis31, it can be argued that a robust model must output a data manifold that is as simple as possible. Greater simplicity in the output manifold leads to higher robustness. In other words, as the output manifold becomes simpler (e.g., lower curvature), the impact of input manifold on the network becomes lower, resulting in a more robust network. Methods

Manifold curvature estimation

There are various definitions for discrete curvature. For instance, four discrete curvature definitions of planar curves, including the osculating circle approach, are presented34. This method was applied for Riemannnian curvature analysis of deep neural networks35. In a later work, a special manifold-aware learning approach is introduced36, which estimates the Hessian of an embedding function for curvature computation. This concept was further developed to quantify how each layer of a deep convolutional neural network transforms the curvature of its input manifold at its output37. In that study, the output manifold of a specific layer is treated as a single manifold, regardless of the number of classes involved. it is expected that deeper layers of the network would result in higher local manifold curvatures at the boundary for different classes. This is attributed to the increased interclass separations with deeper layers. The reader should note that in our study, we propose a different manifold curvature estimation approach based on local subspaces and a modified concept of principal angles (i.e., weighted angles). Additionally, instead of obtaining a single curvature estimate for the entire manifold, we analyze the curvature of each class’s manifold representation and compute an average curvature for each layer.

Estimating the manifold curvature based solely on principal angles would be incomplete, as it neglects the significance of singular values. To address this, we propose a matrix that takes into account both principal angles and singular values for the estimation of manifold curvature.

Weighted angles vs principal angles

To elaborate on the previous illustration, principal angles between two subspaces provide information on how separated the subspaces are, but they do not provide information on the curvature of the manifold itself. In other words, the principal angles between two local subspaces may not necessarily correspond to the curvature of the underlying manifold. This is because the principal angles are only based on the direction of the principal vectors and do not take into account the magnitude of the vectors, which is an important factor in the curvature estimation.

Manifold progression via neural network layers

In many real-world problems, it is assumed that high-dimensional data approximately lies on lower-dimensional manifolds, according to the manifold assumption hypothesis31. For example, the trajectories observed in F video frames of M independently moving rigid bodies come from a union of M 4-dimensional subspaces of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbb{R}}}^{2F}$$\end{document}R2F40. It has been experimentally shown that face images of a person with the same facial expression under different illumination approximately lie in a 9-dimensional subspace41.

In this section, we provide experimental evidence that each layer of a neural network receives a high-dimensional manifold as input and generates an output manifold that is less curved or flatter than the input manifold (as shown in Fig. 6). To this end, we conducted experiments to demonstrate how manifold curvature decreases in successive layers of a Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN). The MNIST42 and Extended YaleB43 datasets were used in our experiments.Fig. 6Illustration of manifold progression.Each layer of a neural network receives a high-dimensional manifold as input and generates an output manifold that is less curved or flatter than the input manifold.

It is interesting to note that the curvature decreases as the network goes deeper. This observation is consistent with the idea that each layer of a neural network can be seen as a non-linear transformation of the input data, and these transformations can gradually simplify the input by flattening the manifold structure. In other words, deeper layers can capture more abstract and invariant features that are more separable in the data space, which can lead to a lower curvature. This observation can also help us understand the success of deep learning in many real-world applications, where high-dimensional data is often assumed to lie on lower dimensional manifolds, and deep neural networks are used to extract and model the underlying manifold structure.

Black-box robustness

Our proposed method aims to compare the relative robustness of multiple networks without utilizing any information about the network topology or model hyper-parameters. In a typical neural network model, training data are used to generate the model, which is then evaluated based on its performance on a set of test data that follows the same distribution. The model’s robustness is often measured by evaluating its performance on adversarial data, which is a set of perturbed test data designed to induce misclassification. To compare two networks, it is essential to evaluate not only their accuracy but also their robustness.

Our proposed method involves measuring the curvature of the data manifold at the output layer of the network. By using this approach, we can determine which network produces a flatter manifold, which in turn implies higher robustness.

Experimental approach

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed metrics, we employed the following experimental methodology. We used Robustbench44 to obtain pre-trained neural network models from literature that were trained on the CIFAR-10 dataset45. For each model, we fed a subset of the input data (representing the input manifold) to the model, which generated output data to construct the output manifold. For every output data point xi, we selected its Ni neighbors using the corresponding neighboring relationship from the input manifold. All further computations were performed on the output manifold, which allowed us to determine the curvature of the output manifold produced by the model.

We also evaluated the robustness of the models by subjecting them to adversarial attacks under the ℓ2 norm threat model with ϵ = 0.5. Based on the empirical evidence, we compared and contrasted our metric with the robustness accuracies of the models.

It is important to note that the only similarity between these models is the dimensionality of their output. Their architecture, training methods, loss functions, regularization techniques, and utilization of additional datasets in training differ.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents a framework for evaluating the robustness of neural networks based on manifold curvature estimation. The proposed metrics overcome limitations of existing methods by utilizing only training data and not relying on regular or adversarial test data. The study introduces a metric for measuring the curvature of a data manifold, addressing the limitations associated with principal angles between subspaces. By considering weighted angles between subspaces, a more accurate estimation of manifold curvature is achieved. It is demonstrated that each layer of a neural network maps an input manifold to a manifold of lower curvature, providing insights into the transformation properties of neural networks and their potential for reducing vulnerability to perturbations. A robustness measure is introduced, independent of network architecture or model parameters. It involves determining local subspaces for data points on output manifolds and computing weighted angles between neighboring subspaces. The average of these angles serves as a measure of average curvature of a manifold, and the average curvature of all output manifolds represents the network model’s robustness. Two additional metrics are proposed, utilizing curvature estimation of special manifolds generated using gradient vectors between output and input network layers. These white-box approaches provide insights into network robustness. The methods are tested on multiple network models using the CIFAR-10 dataset, demonstrating their effectiveness in evaluating the robustness of neural networks. Overall, this study contributes to the understanding of network robustness and provides practical metrics that can be utilized for benchmarking and comparing different network models. The proposed framework offers a reliable and comprehensive approach to assessing network robustness, without the need for adversarial testing or modifications to the input set. Further research can explore the application of these metrics to other datasets and investigate strategies for enhancing network robustness based on the insights gained from this study.
