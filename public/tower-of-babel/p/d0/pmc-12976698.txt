What Are the Differences between Multiple Screw Fixation and Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty for Displaced Femoral Neck Fracture?: A Twenty-Year Study from Tertiary Referral Hospital
Authors: Han Jin Lee, You Jung Kim, Jeong Joon Yoo, Hong Seok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHip fracture surgery is a high-stakes topic due to elevated mortality and high economic costs, making the identification of optimal treatment for displaced femoral neck fracture (FNF) pivotal. Our study aimed to evaluate and compare (1) surgery-associated parameters and (2) mortality rates following multiple screw fixation (MSF) or cementless bipolar hemiarthroplasty (BHA) in patients with displaced FNF using twenty years of data from a single tertiary referral center.Materials and MethodsBetween January 2000 and January 2018, we analyzed 1,153 cases of displaced FNFs treated at our institution with either MSF or cementless BHA. We evaluated (1) surgery-associated parameters, (2) hospitalization duration, and (3) postoperative complications and mortality rates at one and five years following the surgical procedure.ResultsMSF showed a reduced waiting period preceding surgery, a shortened duration of the surgical procedure, but an extended period of hospitalization. There was an increase of estimated blood loss and postoperative transfusion with cementless BHA compared to that with MSF. Both the procedures did not differ in postoperative complications. The one-year and five-year mortality rates were also similar in both groups.ConclusionSince cementless BHA and MSF surgeries did not distinctly differ in mortality rates at one year and at five years, it is crucial that surgeons make personalized surgical decisions based on the individual characteristics of the patient. Surgeons should carefully weigh the advantages of MSF, which include shortened surgery time and low blood loss against the benefits (e.g., reduced hospitalization period) of cementless BHA.

INTRODUCTION

There has been a steady rise in the incidence of hip fractures as the population of older adults continues to grow1,2). Femoral neck fracture (FNF) is a common type of hip fracture. Surgery is considered the standard treatment because of increased mortality and morbidity linked to non-invasive treatment3,4). The overall global count of hip fractures is predicted to increase to 6.3 million by the year 2050 with FNFs making up around half of this total5,6). Traditional options for surgical treatment include multiple screw fixation (MSF) and bipolar hemiarthroplasty (BHA) in older adults7,8). The optimal treatment remains controversial. In two randomized controlled trials on the management of displaced FNF, the outcomes of BHA were superior to those of MSF9,10). However, other randomized controlled studies reported less favorable outcomes for BHA11,12). The use of cementless BHA has become increasingly popular to treat displaced FNF in older adults. This is due to the reduced operation time, diminished blood loss, and low healthcare expenses when compared with those of cemented BHA13-16).

It is unclear if the established binary approach for displaced FNF is applicable to cementless BHA because there is limited research that compares MSF and cementless BHA in older adults. There is a paucity of studies with a comprehensive approach that considers factors associated with surgical aspects and postoperative mortality. Furthermore, the few comprehensive studies typically include limited patient populations.

Therefore, the objective of our study was to compare the therapeutic interventions MSF with cementless BHA for displaced FNF with data spanning twenty years from a single tertiary referral center. We wished to evaluate (1) the surgery-associated parameters, (2) duration of hospitalization, and (3) the postoperative complications and the mortality rate at one-year and five-years following surgery. We analyzed and compared the collected data. Through our study, we aim to provide a reference for the future to help surgeons select the appropriate surgical intervention to treat older adults with displaced FNFs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Cohort

This is a retrospective study, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) granted prior approval for the protocol (IRB No. H-2201-089-1290). Given the retrospective nature of the study, it was exempt from the requirement for informed consent. From January 2000 to January 2018 at SNUH, study enrollment included 1,430 adults, aged 50 years and above, who experienced only displaced FNF (Garden grades III and IV). Our study excluded cases of pathological fractures. Highly experienced hip surgeons performed surgeries for all the fractures at a single tertiary referral hospital known for handling a significant volume of cases. The eligibility criteria for this study included (1) individuals who experienced displaced FNF due to low-energy trauma, (2) previously ambulatory individuals, and (3) those individuals who underwent either MSF or cementless BHA. Exclusion criteria included patients with displaced FNF who were treated with total hip arthroplasty, compression hip screw, or cephalomedullary nail, or those who did not undergo any operation.

Hence, this study incorporated the remaining 1,153 individuals comprising 185 patients treated with MSF and 968 patients treated with cementless BHA (Fig. 1). The study samples were divided into the MSF group and the BHA group. The decision to perform surgery depended on the surgeon’s preference, which adhered to the following criteria: MSF was considered when (1) the displacement was ≤10 mm, (2) angulation was ≤20° in any direction, and (3) the patient did not have severe osteoporosis (T-score ≥–3.0). A total of 770 females and 383 males were part of the study, with a mean age of 75.4±14.3 years at the time of the index operation. The mean duration of follow-up was 3.6±4.5 years (Table 1). We compared the demographic characteristics of the two groups, such as the ability to walk before injury, preinjury general health status, presence of osteoporosis based on bone mineral density measurements (T-score ≤–2.5), surgical parameters, duration of follow-up, and mortality following surgery.

For cementless BHA, the postoperative rehabilitation protocol advised patients to perform partial-weight-bearing mobilization within 3 days following surgery. For patients who underwent MSF, the postoperative rehabilitation protocol encouraged rehabilitation with non–weight-bearing mobilization for a duration of 8 weeks after surgery.

2. Data Collection and Evaluation

Koval’s grade was used to evaluate the preinjury ambulatory level by reviewing the patient’s medical records. Because recent studies17,18) have shown that people who are involved in external activities, even with auxiliary equipment, are functionally better and have lower mortality rates than those who are not involved in external activities, we categorized Koval’s grade into outdoor (Koval’s grade I-III) and indoor (Koval’s grade IV-VI) walkers. The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score was used to evaluate the general health status of patients. Surgical records were used to assess surgery-associated parameters, such as the waiting period for surgery, type of anesthesia, the duration of the procedure, estimated blood loss, and the quantity of postoperative transfusion. Medical records were used to determine the duration of hospitalization and the duration of follow-up. We confirmed the deaths of patients with data from the Ministry of Public Administration and Security.

For evaluation, we divided postoperative complications into local complications and medical complications. Local complications were based on out-patient records and follow-up X-rays of each patient. Local complications included nonunion, superficial wound dehiscence, periprosthetic joint infection, osteonecrosis, periprosthetic fracture, and dislocation. Medical complications were based on events, such as pneumonia, urinary tract infection, acute kidney failure, delirium, stroke, and deep vein thrombosis occurring within 3 years following surgery.

3. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics of continuous variables are expressed as mean and standard deviation. Cross-tabulated data were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test and the odds ratio with 95% confidence intervals was calculated. Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables. A univariate analysis of radiologic indices was calculated for the two cohorts. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (ver. 26.0; IBM Corp.) was used for statistical analyses.

RESULTS

1. Preoperative Characteristics of Each Group

Patients who underwent cementless BHA were older (P<0.001) than those who underwent MSF (Table 1). There was no difference in sex and body mass index, irrespective of the surgical procedure.

Patients who received cementless BHA had inferior walking ability before sustaining the injury (P<0.001). There was no difference between cementless BHA and MSF in the ASA score and the prevalence of osteoporosis (Table 2).

2. Surgery-Associated Parameters

Patients who underwent cementless BHA had a longer waiting period than those treated with MSF (3.2 and 2.3 days, P<0.001) (Table 2). The duration of the surgical procedure for cementless BHA was longer than that for MSF (78.9 and 69.3 minutes, P<0.001). There was higher expected blood loss in cases treated with cementless BHA than in those treated with MSF (256.3 and 54.0 mL, P<0.001). Postoperative transfusion was higher in cases treated with cementless BHA than in those treated with MSF (148.8 and 72.6 mL, P<0.001). The type of anesthesia used did not differ between cementless BHA and MSF (Table 3).

3. Length of Hospitalization and Mortality Rate of Each Group

The length of hospitalization was longer in patients with MSF than in those with cementless BHA (12.3 and 10.9 days, P<0.001). The duration of follow-up was longer in patients with MSF than in those with cementless BHA (4.8 and 3.4 years, P<0.001). There was no difference in the one-year mortality rate between the two types of surgery (P>0.05); the one-year mortality rate (P=0.308) for MSF was 14.6% (27/185 patients) and 17.7% for cementless BHA (171/968 patients). The five-year mortality rate also showed no difference, irrespective of the type of surgery performed (P>0.05); the five-year mortality rate was 38.9% for MSF (72/185 patients) and 43.4% for cementless BHA (420/968 patients, P=0.255) (Table 3).

4. Complications

In the overall population and in both the subgroups, local complications did not differ between the groups that underwent either MSF or cementless BHA (Table 4). Nonunion was the main local complication that was presented in the MSF group, and periprosthetic fracture was the main local complication in the cementless BHA group. MSF and cementless BHA groups did not differ from each other when analyzed for postoperative medical complications (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Surgery is commonly required for displaced FNF. However, determining the most suitable surgical option has been a subject of debate in recent times. In our study, we found that in comparison to MSF, cementless BHA was frequently performed in older adults, typically in those with poor preoperative gait status and with a long waiting period for surgery. Cementless BHA also exhibited a long duration of surgery, high estimated blood loss, and an increased likelihood of postoperative transfusion. However, it was associated with a short hospitalization period. Notably, the two surgical procedures did not display any significant variations in postoperative complications as well as in the one-year and five-year mortality rates.

To choose the type of surgery for displaced FNF, we reviewed fracture characteristics and the severity of osteoporosis to provide potential points of reference. However, these features did not provide an absolute reference point. There are several reports of numerous cases of displaced FNF observed in older adults. Presently, cementless BHA is the common surgical procedure in these cases19,20). In our study, patients who underwent cementless BHA were generally older or had a relatively lower activity level before surgery. However, because our criteria for choosing type of surgery were severity of displacement or angulation of fracture and severity of osteoporosis, which are factors associated with the age and activity of the patient, it cannot be interpreted that younger age or higher preoperative activity level were criteria for choosing MSF.

Cementless BHA had a mean surgery wait time of 3.2 days, while MSF had a mean delay of 2.3 days. In both groups, the duration exceeded the 24 to 48 hours suggested in other studies21). Since our study was performed in a tertiary referral hospital, the process of patients reaching the hospital involved a delay period. The general condition of the patients before surgery tended to be poor. These factors resulted in an inevitable delay of surgery due to preoperative management. According to Cha et al.22), delayed surgery in FNF was significantly related to mortality one year following surgery. The authors demonstrated a significant association between delayed surgery and both 30-day and one-year-mortality rates. Specifically, delayed surgery was attributed to the withholding of drugs. Medical comorbidities were found to be associated with mortality in 30 days after adjusting for confounding factors. However, in our cohort, no significant difference was observed in the one-year and the five-year-mortality rates between the MSF and the BHA groups, despite the difference in the waiting period for surgery. This suggested that other supplemental factors, such as patient characteristics or postoperative care may have mitigated the impact of surgical delay. Nevertheless, future studies should incorporate stratified analyses or propensity score matching to evaluate the independent effect of the waiting period for surgery on the outcome of mortality.

Compared to cemented BHA, cementless BHA exhibits a shortened duration of surgery, reduced estimated blood loss, and a low rate of postoperative transfusions23). We did not select cemented BHA due to concerns of cardiac events caused by the cement24). Hence, this explains the increasing predilection for cementless BHA23). If selected, MSF would yield more favorable outcomes than those of BHA—a shortened duration of surgery, reduced blood loss, and a low likelihood of requiring blood transfusion. However, long hospitalization was reported in the MSF group, which might be due to the slow rehabilitation protocol for internal fixation than that required for arthroplasty. The patients who were treated with cementless BHA were usually allowed to perform partial–weight-bearing mobilization within 3 days following surgery25). However, patients who underwent MSF were encouraged to rehabilitate with non–weight-bearing mobilization over 8 weeks after surgery26). In other words, from the perspective of postoperative rehabilitation alone, patients who undergo cementless BHA can be discharged faster because they can ambulate and adapt to daily life sooner than patients who undergo MSF27). The patient’s short hospitalization period can reduce the overall social costs because the patient's treatment costs and the medical resources consumed are reduced28). Other studies have reported that patients with cementless BHA were highly satisfied due to the short duration of hospitalization and the rapid recovery of ambulation26).

There was no difference in the local complications between the MSF and cementless BHA groups. This finding differs from that of the other study that reported surgical outcomes between these two surgical methods18). In our study, one high-volume hip surgeon performed all the surgeries within the same referral hospital, which resulted in different outcomes. Furthermore, we also observed no difference in the medical complications between the MSF and the cementless BHA groups. This reinforced the observation of another study that compared the two surgical methods29). Factors, such as anesthesia exposure, physiological stress of surgery, and the quality of perioperative care appear to increasingly play a critical role in determining medical outcomes30,31).

The mortality rate of FNF has been widely discussed. Kim et al.18) reported that there was no notable difference in the one-year mortality rate between patients treated with MSF and those undergoing cementless BHA for FNF. Similarly, Cui et al.29) stated there was no significant difference in the one-year mortality rate between patients treated with internal screw fixation (multiple screws or nailing) and those undergoing BHA, whether cemented or cementless, for displaced FNF. In our study, we observed no difference in both the one-year and the five-year mortality rates between the MSF and the cementless BHA groups even though the patients who received MSF were young and with relatively good preoperative activity levels. Our study obtained the data on mortality through the official death records from the Ministry of Public Administration and Security rather than through the medical records within the hospital. Therefore, since the data are free from follow-up losses regarding each patient’s mortality status, they can be considered highly accurate. The one-year mortality is relatively high4) when conservative treatment is selected in FNF patients. Therefore, it is reasonable to choose surgery as much as possible, and the surgery of choice should focus more on postoperative patient satisfaction and functional recovery than mortality.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, the retrospective nature of the study introduces the potential for selection bias. The lack of randomization in selecting the treatment is another limitation of our study. To overcome this limitation, larger, multicenter, and longer-duration randomized studies could be conducted. Second, we developed criteria to select MSF or cementless BHA based on experience, and as stated in our article. However, there is currently no standardized criteria and this could be controversial. Third, we did not assess confounding variables, such as age, pre-fracture mobility, and comorbidities in our study, which could also affect the outcomes of our comparison. Future research using propensity score matching or randomized controlled trials is essential to provide more robust evidence. Finally, our study did not analyze functional outcomes that are crucial to evaluate surgical success. The lack of extensive postoperative follow-up or insufficient medical records made it challenging to fully analyze the data. In the future, studies should explore the advantages and disadvantages of both surgical approaches by thoroughly examining functional outcomes with longer follow-up periods and using standardized evaluation tools to functionally assess all patients.

CONCLUSION

Since cementless BHA and MSF surgery did not differ significantly in postoperative complications and mortality rates at one year and at five years following surgery, it is imperative to make personalized surgical decisions that are aligned with the individual characteristics of each patient. Surgeons should carefully weigh the advantages of MSF, which include shortened surgery time and low blood loss against the benefits (e.g., short hospitalization period) of cementless BHA.
