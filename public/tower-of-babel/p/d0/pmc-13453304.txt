Beyond Negative Electrophoresis and Immunofixation: A Three-Case Series of Non-secretory Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Rihab Bentahar, Nouhayla Bentahar, Oumayma Hari, Imane El Boutahiri, Safae Regragui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-secretory multiple myeloma is an uncommon plasma cell neoplasm in which serum and urine electrophoresis and immunofixation fail to identify a measurable monoclonal component. This phenotype may delay diagnosis and make conventional biochemical monitoring difficult. We report three cases of clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma presenting with anemia and multifocal skeletal involvement despite negative monoclonal protein studies. Case 1 was a 33-year-old man with progressive fatigue, normocytic anemia, normal renal function and calcium levels, negative serum and urine monoclonal studies, and a normal serum free light chain ratio. Bone marrow aspiration showed 19% plasma cells, whereas bone marrow biopsy demonstrated extensive CD138-positive plasma cell infiltration; 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) revealed multifocal medullary lesions. Case 2 was a 63-year-old woman with low back pain and anemia, negative serum and urine immunofixation, and a normal serum free light chain ratio. Bone marrow aspiration showed 94% plasma cells, and 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated hypermetabolic skeletal lesions. She achieved partial remission after eight cycles of bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone. Case 3 was a 68-year-old woman with fatigue, diffuse bone pain, anemia, negative serum and urine immunofixation, a normal serum free light chain ratio, 18% marrow plasma cells, and extensive PET/CT-positive skeletal disease. These cases emphasize that negative electrophoresis and immunofixation should not exclude multiple myeloma when the clinical, marrow, and imaging findings are suggestive. Bone marrow evaluation and whole-body imaging are central to diagnosis and follow-up in this rare entity.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma is a malignant clonal plasma cell disorder that is usually characterized by the production of a monoclonal immunoglobulin detectable in serum and/or urine. In most patients, this monoclonal component contributes to the diagnosis and provides a key biomarker for disease monitoring and response assessment [1]. The diagnosis of active multiple myeloma is based on the presence of clonal bone marrow plasma cell infiltration of at least 10%, or a biopsy-proven osseous or extramedullary plasmacytoma, together with one or more myeloma-defining events. These include hyperCalcemia, Renal impairment, Anemia, and Bone lesions (CRAB) features, namely hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, and bone lesions, which reflect myeloma-related end-organ damage, as well as validated biomarkers of malignancy. The rationale for using CRAB criteria is to identify clinically significant disease requiring treatment and to distinguish active multiple myeloma from asymptomatic plasma cell disorders such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance or smoldering multiple myeloma [1].

Non-secretory multiple myeloma represents a rare variant, accounting for approximately 1%-5% of multiple myeloma cases, in which no measurable monoclonal protein is detected in serum or urine by conventional electrophoresis and immunofixation [2,3]. This entity poses substantial diagnostic and monitoring challenges. The absence of a measurable paraprotein may delay recognition of the disease and limits the use of standard biochemical response criteria. Consequently, diagnosis and follow-up rely heavily on bone marrow examination, immunohistochemistry, serum free light chain testing, and whole-body imaging [2,4]. Through this three-case series, we aim to highlight the diagnostic complexity of clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma, emphasize the value of bone marrow and imaging findings, and underline the need to maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with unexplained anemia, bone pain, or suspicious skeletal lesions despite negative monoclonal protein studies.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 33-year-old man with a history of splenectomy performed in 2O18 of unknown indication presented with progressive fatigue over a three-month period. Initial laboratory investigations showed a normocytic anemia with normal ferritin, vitamin B12, and folate levels. Reticulocyte count was low, and there was no evidence of hemolysis or occult gastrointestinal bleeding. These findings supported anemia related to bone marrow involvement by plasma cell myeloma, while renal function and serum calcium levels were within normal ranges. Serum protein electrophoresis, serum immunofixation, and urine immunofixation did not reveal a detectable monoclonal component. Serum free light chain testing showed free kappa light chains of 88.37 mg/L and free lambda light chains of 54.9 mg/L, with a kappa/lambda ratio of 1.60, within the normal reference range. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated extensive marrow involvement by a neoplastic plasma cell proliferation, with CD138-positive plasma cells representing approximately 80% of marrow cellularity (Figure 1).

The cells are medium-sized and display eccentric nuclei with dense chromatin and a slightly irregular nuclear membrane. Normal trilineage hematopoiesis is absent.

Bone marrow aspiration yielded a hypocellular sample showing medullary infiltration by medium-sized plasma cells accounting for 19% of nucleated cells. These plasma cells had regular nuclear contours, were frequently nucleolated, and showed no overt cytological atypia (Figure 2).

Whole-body 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) revealed multiple hypermetabolic medullary lesions involving both humeri, the proximal portions of both femurs, both tibias, and the spine. The report did not specify whether these lesions had corresponding osteolytic lesions on the CT component (Figure 3).

Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT maximum-intensity projection from Case 1 showing multifocal hypermetabolic medullary lesions involving the axial and appendicular skeleton, including the humeri, proximal femurs, tibias, and spine, consistent with active skeletal involvement by plasma cell neoplasm. 18F-FDG PET/CT: 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography.

According to the second revision of the International Staging System (R2-ISS), the patient was classified as low-intermediate risk based on the following staging parameters: serum albumin of 38 g/L, β2-microglobulin of 3.8 mg/L, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) within the normal range at 190 U/L, absence of high-risk cytogenetic/Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) abnormalities including del(17p), t(4;14), and t(14;16), and no 1q gain/amplification. Based on the absence of a detectable serum or urinary monoclonal protein despite significant plasma cell marrow infiltration and multifocal PET/CT-positive skeletal disease, the diagnosis of clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma was made. The patient was started on a triplet regimen consisting of bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (VRd). Four cycles were initially planned; however, at the time of reporting, the patient had initiated only the first cycle. Transplant eligibility was assessed given his young age and clinical status, and autologous stem cell transplantation was considered as part of the subsequent therapeutic strategy after induction therapy.

Case 2

A 63-year-old woman with no relevant past medical history presented with progressive low back pain evolving over three months. Initial laboratory investigations revealed normocytic normochromic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 9.6 g/dL, while renal function and serum calcium levels were within normal ranges. Although serum protein electrophoresis showed increased alpha fractions suggestive of an inflammatory/reactive pattern, alternative diagnoses were considered. There was no clinical, biological, or radiological evidence supporting active infection, chronic inflammatory disease, autoimmune disease, lymphoma, or metastatic malignancy. Imaging did not reveal lymphadenopathy or solid tumor lesions suggestive of lymphoma or metastatic cancer. Serum immunofixation was negative; the serum free light chain assay showed a normal kappa/lambda ratio. Serum free light chain testing showed a normal kappa/lambda ratio; however, the exact serum free kappa and lambda values were not available, and urine immunofixation did not detect any monoclonal component. Bone marrow aspiration revealed massive plasma cell infiltration, with plasma cells accounting for 94% of nucleated marrow cells on aspirate (Figure 4). Flow cytometry/immunophenotyping was not available.

A bone marrow biopsy was not performed. Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated multiple hypermetabolic skeletal lesions involving the sacrum and several ribs, including the anterior aspects of the right fourth, fifth, and sixth ribs, the middle portions of the right eighth and 11th ribs, and the anterior aspect of the left seventh rib (Figure 5).

The arrows indicate the hypermetabolic areas identified on PET/CT imaging. Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT maximum-intensity projection from Case 2 showing multifocal hypermetabolic skeletal lesions, notably involving the sacrum and multiple ribs, consistent with active osseous involvement by plasma cell neoplasm. 18F-FDG PET/CT: 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography.

The differential diagnosis of multifocal fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid skeletal lesions with marrow plasmacytosis includes metastatic malignancy, lymphoma, infection or inflammatory bone lesions, and reactive plasmacytosis. In this case, metastatic malignancy and lymphoma were considered but were not supported by the available imaging findings, which did not identify a primary solid tumor or significant lymphadenopathy. Infection or inflammatory disease was also considered, but there was no clinical, laboratory, or radiological evidence supporting these diagnoses. Reactive plasmacytosis was considered less likely because of the marked plasma cell infiltration on bone marrow aspirate, with plasma cells accounting for 94% of nucleated marrow cells, together with multifocal skeletal involvement. Nevertheless, in the absence of bone marrow biopsy and clonality testing, definitive exclusion of these differential diagnoses was limited.

Based on the absence of a detectable serum or urinary monoclonal protein despite extensive bone marrow plasma cell infiltration and multifocal PET/CT-positive skeletal lesions, the diagnosis of clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma was made. According to the Revised Second International Staging System (R2-ISS), the patient was classified as low-intermediate risk based on the following parameters: serum albumin was 38 g/L, β2-microglobulin was 3.8 mg/L, and LDH was within the normal range at 190 U/L (reference range: 135-225 U/L). Cytogenetic/FISH analysis did not show high-risk abnormalities, including del(17p), t(4;14), or t(14;16), and 1q gain/amplification was not detected.

The patient received eight cycles of bortezomib (Velcade; Takeda Pharmaceutical Company Limited, Tokyo, Japan), lenalidomide (Revlimid; Bristol Myers Squibb, Princeton, New Jersey, United States), and dexamethasone (VRd), achieving partial remission. In the absence of measurable monoclonal protein, response assessment was based on clinical improvement, hematologic recovery, and available imaging/clinical follow-up rather than M-protein reduction. Autologous stem cell transplantation was proposed; however, the patient declined transplantation. She is currently under surveillance.

Case 3

A 68-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with progressive fatigue lasting three months and diffuse bone pain evolving for two months. Initial laboratory investigations revealed normocytic normochromic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 10 g/dL, while renal function and serum calcium levels were within normal ranges. Serum protein electrophoresis showed, with serum albumin at 42.3 g/L, without evidence of a monoclonal spike (Figure 6).

Serum protein electrophoresis from Case 3 showing hypoalbuminemia without a monoclonal spike.

Serum immunofixation was negative, urine immunofixation did not detect any monoclonal component, and the serum free light chain assay showed a normal kappa/lambda ratio. Bone marrow aspiration demonstrated plasma cell infiltration, with plasma cells accounting for 18% of marrow cellularity. A bone marrow biopsy was not performed. Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT revealed multiple pathological hypermetabolic skeletal lesions involving the spine, sternum, ribs, both scapulae, both clavicles, both humeri, pelvic bones, and the proximal portions of both femurs (Figure 7).

Whole-Body 18F-FDG PET/CT Maximum-Intensity Projection (Case 3)

Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT maximum-intensity projection from Case 3 showing multifocal hypermetabolic skeletal lesions involving the axial and appendicular skeleton, consistent with active osseous involvement by plasma cell neoplasm. 18F-FDG PET/CT: 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography.

According to the R2-ISS classification, the patient was classified as low risk based on a β2-microglobulin level of 2.8 mg/L, a serum albumin level of 4.1 g/dL, and a normal lactate dehydrogenase level of 180 U/L. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) did not identify high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, including del(17p), t(4;14), t(14;16), or 1q gain/amplification.

In view of the absence of a detectable serum monoclonal protein despite bone marrow plasma cell infiltration exceeding 10% and multifocal PET/CT-positive skeletal involvement, the diagnosis of clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma was made. The patient was started on a triplet anti-myeloma regimen consisting of bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (VRd). This regimen was selected as first-line therapy despite the non-secretory nature of the disease, as treatment decisions were based on the diagnosis of symptomatic multiple myeloma rather than on measurable monoclonal protein secretion. Four cycles were initially planned, and the patient remained under active treatment at the time of manuscript preparation. Given his age of 68 years, performance status, and comorbidities, transplant eligibility was assessed as part of the therapeutic strategy. Discussion

Our findings are consistent with the clinical spectrum described in non-secretory multiple myeloma. Bone pain and anemia are frequent presenting features, whereas renal impairment and hypercalcemia may be absent or less prominent than in classic secretory multiple myeloma. In our series, all three patients had anemia and PET/CT-documented skeletal involvement, while renal function and serum calcium levels were normal in all cases. Bone pain was present in two patients, lasting approximately two and four months, respectively, while one patient presented mainly with progressive fatigue evolving over approximately three months.

Risk stratification was reported according to the Revised Second International Staging System (R2-ISS), which incorporates ISS stage, LDH level, high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, and 1q gain/amplification [5]. This pattern supports the concept that, in non-secretory disease, the absence of a measurable monoclonal component should not outweigh suggestive clinical, marrow, and imaging findings. The normal serum free light chain ratio observed in all three patients is also noteworthy, because some cases previously classified as non-secretory may in fact correspond to oligo-secretory or light-chain-restricted myeloma detectable only by serum free light chain assays.

An important consideration in the diagnosis of non-secretory multiple myeloma is the distinction between true non-secretory, oligo-secretory, and non-producing disease. Oligo-secretory myeloma refers to cases with very low-level monoclonal protein secretion, which may be undetectable by conventional serum protein electrophoresis but detectable by more sensitive methods such as serum or urine immunofixation and serum free light chain assays. True non-secretory myeloma is characterized by the absence of detectable monoclonal protein in serum and urine, including negative immunofixation studies and a normal serum free light chain ratio, despite the presence of clonal plasma cell disease. In these cases, malignant plasma cells may synthesize immunoglobulins intracellularly but fail to secrete them. In contrast, non-producing myeloma is a very rare entity in which malignant plasma cells do not synthesize immunoglobulins at all, with no demonstrable intracellular heavy- or light-chain expression. This distinction is clinically relevant because some cases historically classified as non-secretory may actually correspond to oligo-secretory or light-chain-restricted myeloma when evaluated with modern serum free light chain testing. In the present series, the term non-secretory multiple myeloma was used to describe cases without a detectable monoclonal spike on serum protein electrophoresis and with negative serum and urine immunofixation. However, because complete bone marrow immunophenotyping and intracellular immunoglobulin assessment were not available in all cases, definitive distinction between true non-secretory and non-producing forms could not be established in every patient, which represents a limitation of this case series.

18F-FDG PET/CT played a central role in the diagnostic work-up in all three cases by demonstrating multifocal metabolically active skeletal lesions despite the absence of measurable paraprotein [6]. In non-secretory multiple myeloma, response assessment is challenging because conventional serum and urine monoclonal protein markers are absent or non-measurable. Therefore, follow-up should rely on a multimodal approach combining clinical assessment, hematologic recovery, renal function, calcium levels, serum free light chain testing when informative, bone marrow reassessment when feasible, and imaging evaluation. PET/CT plays an important role in this setting by allowing assessment of the metabolic response of FDG-avid lesions and detection of residual, progressive, or extramedullary disease. In the present series, response monitoring was based on available clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings.

Response assessment represents one of the main challenges in non-secretory multiple myeloma, as conventional serum and urine monoclonal protein markers are absent or not measurable. Therefore, treatment response cannot be reliably assessed using serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, or monoclonal protein quantification alone. In this setting, follow-up should rely on a multimodal approach combining clinical evaluation, laboratory parameters, bone marrow assessment, and imaging. Clinical monitoring should include improvement of bone pain, fatigue, performance status, and the absence of new skeletal or neurological symptoms. Laboratory follow-up should include complete blood counts, renal function, serum calcium, albumin, LDH, and serum free light chain testing when informative, although free light chain levels may remain normal in true non-secretory disease. FDG-PET/CT is particularly useful for monitoring metabolic response, detecting residual active lesions, identifying new skeletal or extramedullary disease, and guiding further management. Repeat bone marrow evaluation should be considered when feasible, especially in cases with persistent cytopenias, discordant clinical and imaging findings, or when confirmation of response is required. When available, multiparameter flow cytometry or minimal residual disease assessment may provide additional information regarding the depth of response [7,8].

This case series has several limitations. The number of patients is small, as expected for a rare entity, and follow-up duration was heterogeneous. In addition, detailed clonality testing was not available in all cases, and bone marrow biopsy was not performed in Cases 2 and 3, limiting definitive histological confirmation of plasma cell clonality. Intracellular immunoglobulin assessment was also unavailable, preventing a clear distinction between true non-secretory and non-producing myeloma in every patient. Finally, systematic post-treatment PET/CT, repeat marrow assessment, and minimal residual disease (MRD) evaluation were not available for all patients, which limited standardized response assessment.

Conclusions

Clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma remains a diagnostic challenge because routine serum and urine monoclonal protein studies may be negative despite clinically significant disease. In patients with unexplained anemia, bone pain, or suspicious skeletal lesions, negative electrophoresis and immunofixation should not delay bone marrow evaluation and whole-body imaging. Given the incomplete biopsy and clonality data in some cases, the patients described in this series should be interpreted as clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma supported by the available clinical, laboratory, imaging, and bone marrow findings, rather than definitively proven true non-secretory or non-producing myeloma. An integrated diagnostic and follow-up strategy combining marrow assessment, immunohistochemistry, serum free light chain testing, and imaging remains essential in suspected clinically non-secretory multiple myeloma.
