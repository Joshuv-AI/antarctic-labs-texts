Are all robotic technologies created equal? Comparing one of the latest image-free robotic technologies to all other robotic systems for total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Philip Huang, Michael Cross, Anshu Gupta, Dhara Intwala, Jill Ruppenkamp, Daniel Hoeffel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic-assisted technologies have been developed to increase surgical precision and reduce surgical variability in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Several different robotic systems have been introduced in the last decade for TKA. The DePuy Synthes VELYS™ Robotic-Assisted Solution (VRAS) is an imageless system designed to eliminate the need for preoperative CT scans and is one of the latest entrants in the rapidly evolving field of robotic technology in TKA. This study compared the clinical and economic outcomes associated with VRAS and other robotic-assisted technologies for primary TKA.MethodsA retrospective cohort study using the Premier Healthcare Database included patients who underwent primary TKA with VRAS or other robotic-assisted technologies from January 1, 2022, to April 30, 2023. The primary outcome for the study was hospital follow-up visits (revisits) within 90 days post-TKA. Secondary outcomes included readmission and revision rates within 90 days post-TKA, operating room time, length of stay, discharge status and hospital costs. The 90-day revision rates were similar for VRAS and other robotic-assisted technologies (0.48% vs. 0.45%), as was the operating room time (138 vs. 137 min). These are important findings in ever-evolving healthcare systems that are increasingly cost conscious and cognizant of principles associated with value-based care.Keywords: VELYS, Robotic surgery, VRAS, Total knee arthroplasty, Readmission, Revision, Cost

Background

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a generally successful and cost-effective procedure for the treatment of end-stage knee arthritis [1]. Robotic-assisted technologies have been developed for TKA to increase surgical precision and reduce surgical variability [2–5]. Robotic-assisted TKA technologies provide surgeons with tools to accurately execute bone resection in accordance with presurgical planning and provide intraoperative feedback to facilitate the restoration of knee kinematics, alignment, and soft-tissue balance [2–5].

Compared with manual TKA, robotic TKA has been shown to be associated with shorter hospital length of stay, greater likelihood of being discharged to home, and lower likelihood of 90-day readmission [6]. Several different robotic-assisted technologies have been introduced in the last decade for TKA. However, the decision to adopt new robotic TKA systems should be driven by evidence, as studies have shown variations in robotic TKA learning curves, clinical outcomes, complications, healthcare resource utilization, and costs [7, 8].

The DePuy Synthes VELYS™ Robotic-Assisted Solution (VRAS) is an imageless system designed to eliminate the need for preoperative CT scans and is one of the latest entrants in the rapidly evolving field of robotic technology for TKA. The VRAS utilizes new technology to accurately collect data regarding patients’ bony anatomy and soft tissue envelope of the knee [5]. This information is subsequently used by surgeons to intraoperatively plan the anatomical placement of knee prostheses with improved accuracy to balance the knee throughout the flexion arc of motion [5]. VRAS is compatible with the ATTUNE™ Knee System (DePuy Synthes), a widely used knee implant. The VRAS enables patient-specific techniques for TKA alignment, allowing for reduced soft tissue manipulation and releases [9].

Evidence evaluating the real-world clinical and economic outcomes associated with the use of VRAS is currently emerging [5, 10, 11]. A better understanding of the real-world comparative effectiveness of the VRAS compared to other robotic technologies for primary TKA procedures may assist providers and healthcare payers in identifying additional opportunities for improving TKA patient quality of care while simultaneously reducing healthcare costs. The objective of this study was to evaluate early postoperative outcomes for VRAS TKA compared to those of other robotic-assisted TKA procedures using a large hospital billing database. The other class of robotic technologies included Mako® (Stryker), Navio™ (Smith & Nephew), OMNIbotics® (Corin Group), ROBODOC® (Curexo Technology) and Rosa® (Zimmer Biomet). The following papers provide more detail on these technologies [12–14]. There are several key differences between VRAS and the other robotic technologies, most critically is the use of different implants, different requirements for pre-operative imaging modalities, use of registration pins and intra-operative image acquisition.

Materials and methods

Data source

This retrospective, cross-sectional, real-world data study included patients who underwent TKA in the Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) between January 1, 2022, and April 30, 2023. The PHD contains complete standardized clinical coding information, including diagnosis, procedure, and hospital-prescribed medications, from more than 25% of all hospital admissions throughout the United States (data are derived from more than 1,164 hospitals and hospital systems). The use of the PHD was reviewed by the New England Institutional Review Board (IRB) and was determined to be exempt from broad IRB approval.

Patient population

Patients with a Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code indicative of elective TKA from January 1, 2022, and April 30, 2023, and aged ≥ 18 years were considered for inclusion in the study. The index date was the admission date for the TKA surgery performed in the inpatient setting or the service day for the same day/outpatient procedures. All patients were required to have undergone TKA surgery via robotic-assisted surgery. VRAS cases were identified via a fuzzy logic search within the PHD chargemaster. The other class of robotics technologies included ROBODOC®, Mako®, Navio™, OMNIbotics® and Rosa®. About 20% of other robotic technologies were unable to be unidentified through chargemaster data but are most likely to be represented by one of the of the technologies above. Patients who met any of the following criteria were excluded from the study: those who had undergone a unicompartmental knee procedure at the time of index; had a diagnosis of aseptic loosening, cancer, infection, or osteomyelitis at index admission; had a knee fracture diagnosis at the time of index; had an unknown sex; or had a $0 cost at the index. The following were also excluded from the 90-day analysis: patients who underwent a bilateral knee procedure within 90 days of the index and patients who had less than 90 days of follow-up from the index.

Study measures

Baseline characteristics

The patient demographic and clinical characteristics evaluated included age, sex, race, marital status, payer category (Medicare, Medicaid, or commercial health insurance), and patient comorbidities. Baseline comorbidity was assessed using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Score and the Functional Comorbidity Index (FCI). The Elixhauser Comorbidity score is an aggregate measure of comorbidities created by using 31 dimensions associated with chronic disease (e.g., heart disease, cancer) and overall health conditions. Prior research has shown that increasing Elixhauser Comorbidity Scores are associated with increased healthcare utilization and a greater risk of mortality [15, 16]. The FCI includes 18 medical conditions, and the sum of the individual condition scores yields the FCI score (range from 0 to 18) [17]. The FCI is particularly relevant for orthopedic care because it was developed as a measure of patient functioning capacity [17, 18]. Information regarding tobacco use at the time of TKA and the presence of knee pain was also collected. The procedural characteristics that were evaluated included the setting of care (inpatient or outpatient), fixation type (cemented or uncemented), and discharge year (2022 or 2023). The following participant characteristics were evaluated: whether the procedure was performed in an urban or rural setting, whether the hospital was a teaching hospital, the hospital Census region, hospital bed size, annual provider TKA procedure volume, and annual surgeon TKA procedure volume (proxy for surgeon experience).

Hospital clinical outcomes, resource utilization, and costs

The duration of patient hospitalization and the duration of hospitalization within 90 days post discharge were analyzed. The primary outcome for the study was hospital follow-up visits (revisits) within 90 days post-TKA. Secondary outcomes included readmission and revision rates within 90 days post-TKA, operating room time, length of stay, discharge disposition (home vs. skilled nursing facility), and hospital costs (all-cause and knee-related procedure costs and 90-day cost of care).

Revisits include any visit to a hospital in any setting and are further classified into all-cause and knee-related revisits. All-cause-related outcomes included all care for any reason, both medically and orthopedically related. Knee-related outcomes are more meaningful from a clinical perspective but are possibly underrepresented due to coding errors. The readmission rate are inpatient revisits. Knee-related readmissions are likely the most clinically relevant but were included as a secondary outcome due to limited statistical power.

Statistical analyses

All study variables were analyzed descriptively. Continuous variables are presented as the means and standard deviations, and categorical or binary variables are presented as percentages. Healthcare resource utilization outcomes and costs are presented as either means or percentages with 95% CIs. Cohorts were balanced using propensity score fine stratification and weighing. Propensity scores were based on covariates at baseline. This method was previously shown to produce accurate results, particularly when exposure groups are significantly smaller than nonexposed groups [19]. Standardized differences were evaluated to assess the balance between the groups, with an absolute standardized mean difference (SMD) < 0.20 used as a threshold to indicate balance. Generalized linear models were constructed to evaluate outcomes in matched cohorts to adjust for potential remaining confounders. All costs were inflated to 2023 United States dollars using the Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index [20].

Patient and provider post-covariate balancing characteristics

Patient and provider characteristics were balanced after propensity score fine stratification and weighing, as demonstrated by low standardized difference values (SMD < 0.2; Table 2 and 3).

Primary outcome

The 90-day all-cause and knee-related revisit rates were significantly lower for VRAS than for the other robotic-assisted technologies (all-cause 13.9% vs. 22.8%; mean difference (MD): -8.9% [95% CI: -11.3% to -6.4%] and knee-related 2.8% vs. 5.4%; MD: -2.6% [-3.8% to -1.5%], respectively, p value < 0.01) (Table 4). The knee-related revisits were primarily driven by knee pain (30% vs. 29%), superficial infection (13% vs. 15%), deep infection (4% vs. 6%) and fracture (0% vs. 3%) in the VRAS TKA cohort compared to the other robotic technologies cohort.

Secondary outcomes

Readmission and revision rates

All-cause and knee-related 90-day readmission rates were also lower for VRAS compared to other robotic-assisted technologies, although the difference was not statistically significant (all-cause 1.7% vs. 2.5% and knee-related 0.73% vs. 1.3%). The ninety-day revision rates were also similar for VRAS and other robotic-assisted technologies (0.48% vs. 0.45%).

Operating room time, length of stay, and discharge status

The average operating room time was similar for VRAS and other robotic-assisted technologies (138 vs. 137 min; Table 4). The length of stay was greater for VRAS (1.8 vs. 1.5 days), although the sample size of VRAS inpatients was small (123 patients). A smaller proportion of VRAS patients were discharged to skilled nursing facilities (2.2% vs. 3.2%) compared to other robotic-assisted technologies.

Costs

The average 90-day knee-related cost for the VRAS cohort was $15,048 compared to $16,867 for other robotic-assisted technologies, with a mean difference and 95% CI of $1,819 [$1,444 to $2,194]. Supply and operating room costs contributed to more than 85% of the total cost for both cohorts, with, on average, the VRAS supply cost being lower by $682 and the operating room cost being lower by $453.

The average 90-day all-cause and knee-related costs of care were also lower with VRAS compared to other robotic-assisted technologies ($15,437 vs. $17,267; MD: -$1,830 [-$2,236 to -$1,423] and $15,048 vs. $16,867; MD: -$1,819 [-$2,194 to -$1,445], respectively).

Discussion

Robotic TKA enhances surgeons’ surgical planning capabilities resulting in a reduction of outliers, increased precision in bony resections, and improved soft tissue balancing. These factors are essential for achieving optimal functional outcomes and longevity in TKA [2–4, 7]. Although robotic technologies have become increasingly popular for TKA [21], they present several potential limitations. These include: complexity of use, steep learning curves, substantial training requirements [7, 22], increased operating room time [7, 22–24], constraints associated with cut-block-dependent and burr-based robotic systems [24] and large footprints that can be cumbersome in the operating room. The potential inefficiencies of some robotic systems may prevent the full economic benefits of robotics from being fully realized. There are limited studies that have compared different robotic technologies [25–27]. However, since VRAS is a newer technology, these comparisons only include cursory mentions of VRAS at best.

The current study evaluated meaningful real-world clinical and economic outcomes for VRAS TKA and other robotic technologies for TKA. To our knowledge, this is the first large study comparing clinical outcomes, healthcare resource utilization, and costs associated with VRAS to those associated with other robotic technologies in TKA. The analyses revealed that, compared to other robotic technologies for TKA, VRAS TKA surgery was associated with lower hospital revisit and readmission rates. While designing the study, we anticipated equivalence between VRAS and the other robotic technology in terms of these clinical outcomes. The VRAS utilizes registration pins that are placed within the knee replacement incision, which may reduce the risk of fractures and wound complications/pin site infections. Further, VRAS facilitates patient specific alignment techniques reduce the number of releases needed to balance the knee, thereby reducing the soft tissue trauma and hematoma formation, potentially reducing the risk of infection as well. Our hypothesis is that the lower rate of infection and fracture in the VRAS cohort compared to the other robotic technology cohorts may be contributing factors to the reduced incidence of revisit and readmission.

In an increasingly cost-conscious healthcare environment, this study highlights that TKA performed with VRAS is associated with significantly lower costs than are those performed with other robotic technologies. The higher expense associated with other robotic technologies could be associated with supply costs (including implants, disposables and hospital supplies) and/or operating costs (including surgery time, additional personnel, anesthesia, and recovery room). Finally, pre-operative imaging and capital equipment costs were not included in the analysis, which could further skew the differential cost outcomes in favor of VRAS, which does not require pre-operative imaging and may have lower capital costs than other robotic technologies.

One strength of this study is the ability to capture meaningful clinical and economic real-world data. Observational studies leverage data originating from clinical practice and can provide a more accurate representation of real-world conditions. Real-world evidence assists consumers, clinicians, purchasers, and policymakers in making informed decisions [28, 29]. Additionally, this study included comprehensive variables related to patient, provider, and surgeon experience, all of which were balanced using the fine stratification method [17]. This statistical methodology preserves patient data, allowing for the inclusion of outliers and a more representative outcome (compared to matching, which can result in a loss of sample).

This study has several limitations. First, hospital billing data were used; these data were not collected specifically for research purposes and could be subject to temporal changes in billing codes [30, 31]. In addition, patients treated in non-Premier Inc. hospitals for postoperative care were lost to follow-up in this study. This study is also limited in that the findings from the PHD may not be generalizable to all other hospitalized patients with TKA, particularly those in countries outside of the US. Additionally, the study was designed with a 90-day follow-up due to data availability (VRAS being a new product) at the time of the study; hence, resource use and costs beyond 90 days post-TKA were not evaluated, which limits the ability of the study to provide information on the value of the technology over longer periods of time. Finally, all limitations associated with retrospective observational and database studies also apply. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute to the current body of evidence on robotic-assisted technologies, demonstrating the real-world clinical and economic benefits of VRAS compared to other robotic technologies in TKA.

Conclusions

This retrospective database study revealed that VRAS TKA is associated with lower revisit rates and total cost of care compared to the class of all other robotic-assisted technologies. These findings should be considered relevant in an ever evolving and increasingly cost-conscious health care environment. The study included 90-day follow-up and hence continued research with longer-term follow-up is required to assess overall economic impact, efficiencies, and differences in patient outcomes.
