Comprehensive monitoring of the spatiotemporal variation of water quality and its associated human health risks in Luvuvhu river catchment, Vhembe biosphere reserve, South Africa
Authors: T. H. Shibambu, D. M. Kahler, K. C. Rose, E. Riddell, O. M. Oyebanjo, J. N. Edokpayi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the spatiotemporal variations in water quality and assesses the associated human health risks in the Luvuvhu River Catchment (LRC), South Africa. Water quality parameters such as pH, total dissolved solids (TDS), turbidity, temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), salinity, biological oxygen demand (BOD), chemical oxygen demand (COD), electrical conductivity (EC), nitrate (NO3⁻), chloride (Clˉ), sulphate (SO42ˉ), and phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and potentially toxic elements (PTEs) (arsenic, lead, iron, zinc, aluminium, chromium, manganese, cadmium, barium, cobalt, copper, molybdenum, mercury, nickel) were measured across multiple sites during both wet and dry seasons. Seasonal variations using Mann-Whitney U test showed higher pollution levels during the wet season, attributed to increased runoff carrying fertilizers, pesticides, and waste into the river system. Samples were analysed for trace metals levels using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer” (ICP-MS). The spatial distribution analysis using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) technique and temporal analysis highlights the cumulative impact of anthropogenic activities on water quality in the LRC. The Water Quality Index (WQI) was employed to synthesize these data into a single value representing overall water quality, revealing significant degradation downstream (387.48–1357.86) due to domestic discharge, agricultural runoff, and semi-industrial pollution. A human health risk assessment was conducted focusing on two primary exposure pathways: ingestion and dermal absorption. Results indicated that ingestion posed a greater non-carcinogenic risk than dermal exposure, especially for adults (2.43 × 10−9 – 3.41 × 10−5) who consume more water daily than children (7.26 × 10−9 – 1.02 × 10−5). On the other hand, ingestion exposure for cancer risks ranged from 2.26 × 10⁻¹¹ to 1.12 × 10⁻⁸ for adults and 4.78 × 10⁻¹⁴ to 1.18 × 10⁻¹² for children, with arsenic contributing the highest risk. PTEs like manganese (30.26–169.32 µg/L), molybdenum (0.08–0.43 µg/L), and iron (0.64–2.48 mg/L) exhibited the highest Chronic Daily Intake (CDI) values, raising concerns for long-term exposure. Lead (0.30–1.84 µg/L) and arsenic (0.11–0.43 µg/L), both known neurotoxicants and potential carcinogens, were also detected, highlighting risks particularly in vulnerable populations despite concentrations being below some regulatory thresholds. These findings emphasize the need for improved water management strategies and pollution control measures in the LRC to safeguard both human health and the aquatic environment. Future studies should prioritize the integration of advanced computational approaches, such as machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI), to enhance predictive modeling and real-time monitoring of water quality in the Luvuvhu River Catchment.

Introduction

Surface water pollution is a global issue with significant implications for human health1. Exposure to contaminated river water, especially with potentially toxic elements (PTEs), can lead to severe health issues such as cancer and respiratory problems2. While metals like zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), and copper (Cu) are essential for the development and function of living organisms, they can become toxic at elevated concentrations3. PTEs, including cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and mercury (Hg), are particularly hazardous, posing serious risks to both human and aquatic life even at low concentrations4. In developing countries like South Africa, the availability of clean and safe drinking water is still a major concern5. A study by Edokpayi et al.6 also identified land use activities as a key driver of elevated PTEs concentrations in river water, contributing towards water quality issues.

South Africa is one of the thirty (30) driest countries in the world, with limited freshwater resources exacerbated by overconsumption and pollution7. Although South Africa has a chemical monitoring programme across the country, resource constraints within water management areas (WMAs) and other government institutions have further limited consistent sampling, particularly in rural areas where many communities depend on river systems for water use8. The Luvuvhu River Catchment (LRC) is home to a predominantly rural population that relies heavily on surface water for domestic purposes, such as fishing, irrigation, drinking, and recreational activities9. However, this reliance is challenged by deteriorating water conditions caused by pollution from human activities8. Accumulated pollutants, particularly in river sediments, can lead to toxicological impacts on aquatic ecosystems and ultimately affect humans through the food chain4. In light of these concerns, the study emphasizes the importance of assessing the water quality in the LRC to understand its current state and its potential human health risks.

Globally, surface water pollution has significant implications for environmental and human health9. In South Africa, where water resources are already scarce, poor water quality exacerbates these challenges, threatening food security and public health8. This challenge is also reflected in other regions, such as Nigeria, where elevated nitrate concentrations in drinking water have been linked to public health risks10. The LRC is particularly vulnerable to these risks as people rely on it for everyday activities. Therefore the objective of this study is to assess the water quality across different sites in the LRC, using various physicochemical parameters and PTEs concentrations, with the aim to evaluate their associated human health risks from possible exposures. Although several studies have been conducted in the Luvuvhu River Catchment (LRC), the impacts of PTEs remain poorly understood. This study provides critical baseline data to support future environmental and public health risk assessments in the region.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Luvuvhu River Catchment (LRC), located between latitudes 22°10’01’’S and 23°20’50’’S and longitudes 29°45’45.16’’E and 31°28’10.02’’E, is part of the transboundary Limpopo River Basin, shared by Botswana, Mozambique, South Africa, and Zimbabwe11. It covers approximately 5,941 km² and lies within the Limpopo-Olifants Water Management Area. The Luvuvhu River originates from the Soutpansberg mountains and flows around 200 km, before joining the Limpopo River, at Crook’s Corner in the Kruger National Park12. Major tributaries include rivers like Mutshindudi, Dzindi, and Mutale, with several dams such as Nandoni and Albasini within the catchment (Fig. 1). The region experiences summer rainfall (October to March), peaking in January and February, heavily influenced by local topography13,14.

Rainfall is highly variable within the catchment area. The Northern and Eastern Parts receives an average annual rainfall of approximately 450 mm (this is the low-lying regions) while in the region of the Soutpansberg Mountains (south-western and north-western parts) rainfall can be up to 2,300 mm15,16. The mean annual precipitation across the catchment is estimated at 608 mm. The average air temperatures with the catchment vary significantly with elevation, ranging from 17 °C in the mountainous areas to 24 °C in regions closer to the Kruger National Park17. The altitude within the catchment spans from 200 m in the low-lying areas to 2,000 m in the highland zones18. Anthropogenic activity is limited inside the Kruger National Park; therefore, contamination may stem from upstream loading (Table 1).

The catchment’s geology is diverse, consisting of igneous and metamorphic rocks in the south and sedimentary formations in the north, including coal deposits near Tshikondeni11. Hydrogeological complexity is enhanced by the mineral-rich bushveld in the south, though the sandy aquifers of the Limpopo River valley have limited water capacity19.

Water sampling and in-situ physicochemical analysis

Water samples were collected monthly from 22 different sampling points between November 2020 and October 2021 using 500 mL polyethylene bottles in triplicate. The bottles were pre-cleaned with deionized water, rinsed with 65% concentrated nitric acid (HNO3), and then rinsed again with deionized water and river water at the sampling site. Samples were collected at the river centre, approximately 2 cm below the surface. Each sample was collected at 5-minute intervals to reduce sediment disturbance, acidified with 5% HNO3, and labelled with details such as date, time, and location. Samples were stored in a mobile refrigerator at 4 °C and transported to the University of Venda for further preservation and analysis. Onsite measurements of physicochemical parameters, including temperature, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), dissolved oxygen (DO), total dissolved solids (TDS), salinity, and turbidity, were performed using specialized multiparameter instruments (YSI Professional Plus, ExStik EC 500 II, and Extech TB 400). The South African National Standards of 2015 (SANS), the World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines of 2011 and the Department of Water and Sanitation Guidelines of 1996 formally known as the Department of Water and Forestry (DWAF) guidelines for drinking and protection of aquatic lives in surface water was used for compliance study20–22.

Analysis of water quality parameters

Physicochemical characteristics

Water samples were analysed for biochemical oxygen demand (BOD₅) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) using standard laboratory procedures. For BOD₅, samples were incubated in pre-cleaned BOD bottles at 20 °C for five days. Dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were measured after incubation using a YSI Professional Plus multiparameter instrument, and BOD₅ was calculated as the difference between initial (DOi) and final (DO₅) DO concentrations (Eq. 1). COD analysis was conducted using a Spectroquant Pharo 100 spectrophotometer, where samples were placed into COD vials containing pre-measured reagents, heated in a thermoreactor at 150 °C for two hours, and analysed after cooling. For nutrient analysis, preserved water samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter, and anions such as nitrate (NO₃⁻), sulphate (SO₄²⁻), chloride (Cl⁻), and phosphate (PO₄³⁻) were measured using an ion chromatography system. Potentially toxic elements (PTEs) and major metals

The study employed a modified version of US EPA Method 3005 A23 to analyse metals in water samples. A 45 mL aliquot of the homogenized sample was placed in a digestion vessel, followed by the addition of 5 mL of trace metal-grade 65% concentrated HNO₃. The vessel was carefully wiped with a dry cloth or tissue to minimize contamination before being transferred to a microwave digester, where it was heated to 170 °C for 40 min. After digestion, the solution was allowed to cool for 20 min. To eliminate silicates and other insoluble materials that could affect the nebulizer of the analyser, the cooled samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The resulting filtrate was then transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask and diluted to the mark with de-ionised water. Major metals (Na, Mg, K, and Ca) were analysed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), while PTEs (Zn, Al, Cr, Mn, Cd, Ba, Co, Fe, Cu, As, Mo, Hg, Ni, Pb) were quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). To ensure accuracy and minimize batch-specific errors, each sample was analysed in triplicate and blank samples were included in each batch to monitor for contamination.

Water quality index (WQI)

The Water Quality Index (WQI) of the LRC was evaluated using the weighted arithmetic method originally proposed by Horton24 and later refined by Brown et al.25 and Cude26. This index reflects the combined influence of individual water quality parameters on the overall condition of the water27. In this study, the WQI values were calculated based on the values of selected water quality parameters (pH, turbidity, EC, TDS, nitrate, sulphate, chloride, sodium, magnesium, potassium, calcium, Al, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Cd, Ba, Hg, Pb). This selection aligns with available WHO and SANS drinking water guidelines, ensuring comparability with global standards while addressing region-specific concerns and impacts in the Vhembe Biosphere Reserve. Human health risks assessment

To assess the health risks associated with PTEs in the water samples, various formulas were applied to the collected data, categorizing potential risks as either non-carcinogenic or carcinogenic. The study primarily focused on two exposure pathways: ingestion and dermal absorption as explained by US EPA30. The non-carcinogenic risks were evaluated using the chronic daily intake (CDI) and average daily dose (ADD) of water. Statistical analysis

In this study, statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and XLSTAT version 2022.2.1. Means and standard deviations for the measured parameters in water samples were calculated. A Mann-Whitney U test was employed to assess significant differences in water quality parameters between the dry and wet seasons.

River water temperature significantly influences aquatic ecosystems, affecting heat concentration, organism reproduction, organic matter solubility, and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels25. In this study, temperatures ranged from 18.23 to 27.19 °C and 22.93 °C as the mean value. Elevated temperatures, often due to warmer climates, are known to decrease DO levels, impacting aquatic health26,41. A geospatial analysis (Fig. 2) highlighted notable temperature variation, with downstream sites generally warmer than upstream, particularly at site S20 in the KNP (27.19 °C) versus site S1 in an agricultural zone (18.24 °C), this finding is similar to observations from the Turag River in Bangladesh37. Temperatures in the LRC were higher during the wet season which coincides with the austral summer, aligning with studies from South Africa’s Lower uMfolozi Floodplain River system42. Seasonal differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05), a common pattern in areas with intense solar radiation.

pH levels across the LRC ranged from 7.49 to 9.24, with a mean of 8.40, this complies with the DWAF22 guidelines for the protection of aquatic life. This slightly alkaline pH, particularly at midstream and downstream sites such as S20, is beneficial for aquatic life, including fish28. Seasonal stability suggests limited sensitivity to rainfall, with variations more likely tied to land use, including agricultural runoff and urban waste disposal29. These pH levels are typical of minimally impacted river systems, influenced by bicarbonate presence and waste biodegradation due to rain27. Turbidity in river water, which measures the cloudiness caused by suspended particles, is an indicator of pollution intensity43,44. In this study, turbidity values averaged 44.17 NTU, ranging from 4.67 to 151.04 NTU, with the highest levels recorded at site S22. Downstream sites exhibited notably higher turbidity likely driven by sediment influx and increased animal activity in these areas, while sedimentation effects from Nandoni Dam reduced turbidity at upstream sites such as S1043. High turbidity can reduce light penetration and hinder aquatic plant growth, impacting water clarity and aquatic habitats44. There is currently no guideline value for turbidity on the protection of aquatic lives in surface water. However, more turbid waters can lead to reduction of dissolved oxygen below the water surface22.

Salinity levels ranged from 83.72 to 223.67 mg/L, with an average of 123.62 mg/L, indicating influences from upstream agricultural runoff and sewage discharges, particularly at site S27. The dry season recorded higher salinity levels (mean 142.35 mg/L) due to low river flows and concentrated discharge points45. Elevated salinity may reduce DO, placing stress on aquatic ecosystems, particularly during dry periods with lower water flow. DO values, ranging from 3.06 downstream to 4.22 mg/L midstream, reflect the influence of organic pollution and high temperatures, especially in downstream areas like S21 (Fig. 3). Seasonal analysis showed DO levels were significantly higher in the wet season (4.91 mg/L), compared to 2.73 mg/L in the dry season, where higher temperatures and organic decomposition contributed to oxygen depletion46. While midstream sites with cooler, turbulent flows exhibited higher DO, most values fell below the DWAF22 recommended threshold of 5 mg/L, impacting aquatic organisms’ survival and health.

BOD and COD, representing oxygen demand by organic and inorganic matter, varied between sites but were generally within safe limits (Fig. 3). BOD levels ranged from 1.14 to 1.92 mg/L, with higher values observed during the wet season due to organic runoff. Midstream sites showed slight increases in BOD, possibly from sewage and agricultural inputs47. COD levels varied significantly between 2.99 and 22.55 mg/L, with higher values midstream, likely due to wastewater discharge.

The nitrate and phosphate concentrations varied across the LRC. Site S2 showed the highest levels of nitrate possibly due to agricultural runoff and sewage, a pattern similar to other agriculturally impacted rivers29. The levels of nitrate recorded in some of the sites exceeded the DWAF recommended level for the protection of aquatic ecosystem. Phosphate levels ranged from 0.42 to 0.84 mg/L, with seasonal peaks during the wet season in midstream areas impacted by settlements and agriculture47. Sulphate values ranged from 0.94 to 7.92 mg/L, with seasonal differences remaining minor and safe across the LRC48. Chloride levels averaged 11.12 mg/L, with higher concentrations at site S2 due to sewage and road runoff but were not at levels that would pose health risks49. Seasonal chloride variations were minor, suggesting stable chloride sources and dilution patterns.

The mean mercury (Hg) concentration in the LRC was 0.02 µg/L peaking at site S11 and lowest at sites S16, S17, and S19 (Fig. 4). Although Hg levels met WHO and SANS standards for domestic use, they exceeded DWAF limits for aquatic protection, indicating potential ecological risks. Seasonal analysis showed higher Hg concentrations during the wet season, likely due to runoff and pollution sources such as domestic effluents. These results align with previous studies but are lower than those from the Lower Olifants River50. Arsenic (As) concentrations averaged 0.19 µg/L, with the highest level at site S21 and the lowest at site S15 (Fig. 4). While within SANS, WHO, and DWAF limits, As concentrations were significantly higher in the wet season (0.68 µg/L), likely due to mining-related pollution and natural diffuse sources, in line with previous studies22. These values were below those reported in Mooi River, South Africa51. Lead (Pb) levels ranged from 0.30 to 1.84 µg/L (mean 0.79 µg/L), exceeding DWAF’s aquatic protection Limit of 0.2 µg/L but within domestic use standards. Higher Pb levels downstream in the KNP could pose ecological risks, especially during the wet season, driven by agricultural and sewage runoff. These levels were lower than those reported in the Halda River, Bangladesh52 and similar to findings from the Nzhelele River, South Africa4. Cadmium (Cd) concentrations ranged from undetectable levels to 0.05 µg/L, with the highest level at site S22, likely linked to upstream pollution (Fig. 4). Though Cd complied with WHO, SANS, and DWAF standards for health, concentrations were elevated in the wet season (up to 0.09 µg/L) due to runoff from agricultural and semi-industrial sources, exceeding aquaculture guidelines22. These findings were lower than those reported in Emfuleni and Johannesburg53,54.

This study assessed chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), aluminum (Al), and manganese (Mn) concentrations in the LRC and found that, although levels of these metals varied spatially and seasonally, they generally complied with DWAF, SANS, and WHO standards for irrigation, livestock watering, and some aspects of ecosystem protection (Fig. 5). Cu concentrations (2.89 to 8.44 µg/L) exceeded DWAF’s ecological threshold, suggesting risks to aquatic biodiversity in areas like Kruger National Park56, while higher Cu levels in the wet season suggest unsuitability for aquaculture. Al concentrations (0.13 to 7.49 mg/L) surpassed the DWAF’s ecological limits at most sites, particularly downstream of Nandoni Dam, signaling toxicity risks due to seasonal runoff4,57. Mn levels remained below the aquatic life threshold but exceeded WHO and SANS standards for domestic use, particularly in wastewater-impacted areas like site S7, with elevated wet-season runoff contributions52. Overall, while Cr, Cu, Al, and Mn levels in the LRC suggest potential ecological concerns, compliance with several standards indicates limited immediate contamination risks for broader water uses.

The results also showed that different seasons play a critical role in the levels of these PTEs (Fig. 7). Using the Mann-Whitney statistical test, the results showed a statistically significant variation (P < 0.05) for As between seasons, with higher concentrations (0.68 µg/L) recorded during the wet season than in the dry season. Additionally, the Man-Whitney test also showed a statistically significant difference between seasons for the concentration of Pb. The highest concentration of Pb (2.99 µg/L) was found during the wet season (Fig. 7) which could be due to high runoff containing Pb containing materials from agricultural lands and sewage into the rivers. Nyamukamba et al.5 also found a low concentration of Pb in Emfuleni Local Municipality and Johannesburg Metropolitan Municipality, South Africa. The reported levels were within the target water quality range (0.2 mg/L) for irrigation water use, aquaculture, and livestock watering22. According to the statistical test (Mann-Whitney) results, the seasonal recorded values differ significantly (p < 0.05) for Mn. Comparatively, the highest concentrations of Mn were observed during the wet season than in the dry season (Fig. 7). The presence of high concentration of this metal in the wet season may be related to the heavy influx of runoff derived from the land into the river36. The findings of this study were higher than those reported by Bhuyan and Bakar52 in Halda river, Bangladesh. In addition, higher Fe concentrations were measured in the wet season (3.31 mg/L) than in the dry season (1.67 mg/L), and this variance was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7). The obtained results were within the recommended guideline value for irrigation water use (Target Water Quality Range ≤ 5.0) and livestock watering but exceeded the standard for agricultural use in aquaculture22.

Relationship among water quality parameters

The Pearson’s correlation analysis of key water quality parameters (Table 9) highlights significant interdependencies among these variables in the LRC. The results show a strong positive correlation between Na and Mg (r = 0.93) and between Ca and Mg (r = 0.92) suggest that these ions are likely to share common sources or geochemical behaviours in the river system, possibly linked to geological formations and the weathering of minerals upstream. Additionally, the high correlation between some metals (Ni, Cu, As, Cd, and Pb) and TDS indicates that dissolved solids in the water are significantly influenced by these ions. These results conform with the study of Biswas et al.68. The low or negative correlations observed for elements such as Al with major ions may reflect localized anthropogenic sources such as industrial discharge rather than geochemical processes. Furthermore, EC is significantly correlated with salinity, TDS, nitrate, chloride, and sulphate suggesting a potential strong relationship or dependencies between these variables. EC is negatively correlated with DO, indicating an inverse linear relationship between these parameters. These correlations reinforce the weighted influence of these parameters on the WQI, indicating that variations in ion concentrations directly impact the water quality. The Water Quality Index (WQI) of the LRC further corroborates these findings, with spatial variations in WQI values pointing to both natural and anthropogenic drivers. The significant seasonal variation in water quality values also suggests that rainfall and surface runoff during the wet season led to increased nutrient loading and turbidity, which exacerbates the pollution levels. These results emphasize the need for targeted management strategies in heavily impacted areas to reduce pollutant loads and improve overall water quality.

Water quality conditions based on the WQI

The Water Quality Index (WQI) of the LRC varied significantly, ranging from 389.90 to 5298.73 during the wet season and 118.48 to 651.99 during the dry season, reflecting differing water quality across various seasons (Table 10). Based on the WQI classifications by Bouderbala and Gharbi28, and Kangabam et al.29, water from many sites were deemed unsuitable for drinking in their current state, although residents around it often use this water resource for domestic purposes including drinking without treatment. However, there is potable water dependency in some of the communities around the river catchment but due to water rationing, some depend on rainwater harvesting, groundwater sources while others resort to collecting water from the river for drinking purposes36,69,70. Notably, site S3 and S10 recorded the lowest WQI (indicating better quality) during the wet season and dry season respectively, while site S22 and S18 exhibited the highest during wet and dry season, likely impacted by upstream runoff. Increased pollution levels downstream, particularly at site S22, appear to be associated with chemical runoff, animal activities within KNP consistent with observations by Kangabam et al.29. Seasonally, water quality fluctuated (p < 0.05), with WQI values rising during the wet season due to elevated turbidity and electrical conductivity (EC) from rainfall, leading most sites to exceed the recommended WQI threshold (> 300) for potable water. This seasonal pattern aligns with reports by Ambiga and AnnaDurai71 but contrasts with findings by Ernazarovna et al.72. These results emphasize an urgent need for targeted pollution control measures, particularly in severely affected areas, to safeguard downstream water users, including communities in Mozambique.

Health risks assessment results

Non-carcinogenic risks

Table 10 presents the carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk values for selected PTEs in adults and children, focusing on dermal absorption and ingestion exposure pathways. Ingestion was found to pose a higher risk than dermal absorption for both age groups, with adults showing higher chronic daily intake (CDI) values due to greater water consumption. Manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), iron (Fe), and barium (Ba) were identified as metals with high CDI, posing potential risks to water users, consistent with findings from Farhang73 and Muhammad and Usman74. Hazard quotients (HQ) for metals were below 1, indicating non-carcinogenic risks are tolerable, with Mn showing the highest HQ due to its elevated concentration in water, raising concerns about neurological effects75. Although HQs for children were below 1, caution is advised due to children’s heightened vulnerability to environmental toxins76. The health risk findings from the LRC align with observations from other South African river systems where non-carcinogenic risks remain below critical thresholds, but certain PTEs (e.g., Mn, As, and Pb) consistently pose long-term exposure concerns. For instance, studies conducted in the Vaal River77 and the Crocodile River56 reported similar patterns of elevated Mn and Pb concentrations downstream of urban and industrial areas. On a global scale, comparable risks have been reported in semi-arid river systems such as the Turag River in Bangladesh60 and Kakhk city in Iran78, where cumulative exposure to metals through drinking water has raised public health concerns. These parallels support the broader applicability of our findings and underscore the need for regionally contextualized water safety planning. The hazard index (HI) for all metals was below 1, suggesting no immediate non-carcinogenic risk; however, ingestion posed greater risk than dermal exposure. These findings, though lower than those of Kamunda77 in Gauteng and Madilonga et al.36 in Limpopo, suggest that long-term exposure and land use changes could elevate health risks in the LRC. The low HI is attributed to low metal concentrations and complex geochemical factors stabilizing metals in the water79,80.

Carcinogenic risks

According to USEPA20, carcinogenic risk (CR) reflects the probability that a person may develop cancer over their lifetime due to exposure to carcinogenic substances. In this study, the carcinogenic risk was calculated for arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb), as these PTEs are categorized as carcinogenic to both children and adults30. For metals without known cancer slope factors, risks were not assessed. The calculated cancer risks for arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) in adults and children, shown in Table 11 were within acceptable levels, below the 10⁻⁶ target risk threshold30. For ingestion exposure, cancer risks ranged from 2.26 × 10⁻¹¹ to 1.12 × 10⁻⁸ for adults and 4.78 × 10⁻¹⁴ to 1.18 × 10⁻¹² for children, with arsenic contributing the highest risk. The results indicate that adults are more vulnerable to CR from water consumption compared to children, contrasting with findings by Madilonga et al.36, where children were more vulnerable. While estimated CR values fall within the regulatory threshold, the chronic, low-level exposure to these elements, particularly in communities that lack consistent access to treated drinking water, may result in cumulative health effects over time. Both As and Pb are bioaccumulative and have no known safe exposure thresholds, especially in children81. Therefore, while current CR values suggest minimal immediate risk, the long-term public health implications, including cancer, neurotoxicity, and developmental disorders, warrant ongoing monitoring and preventive intervention82. Despite low concentrations, long-term exposure to As and Pb in drinking water can lead to cancer, diabetes, skin issues, hypertension, and neuropathy79–83. These risks were lower than those reported by Kamunda77 in mining areas of Gauteng, South Africa.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical impact of anthropogenic activities on water quality in the Luvuvhu River Catchment (LRC) and emphasizes the need for improved water resource management. The water quality analysis revealed exceedances of recommended water quality guidelines at multiple sampling sites, with elevated concentrations. Seasonal variations further exacerbated these issues, with higher pollution levels observed during the wet season due to increased surface runoff. These findings point to a pressing need for effective pollution control measures and stricter enforcement of environmental regulations to mitigate the degradation of water resources. The study plays an important role in local water governance, within the Vhembe Biosphere Reserve where multiple communities depend on untreated river water for daily use. The observed high levels of PTEs in water highlight the urgent need for improved water quality monitoring program and enforcement of regulatory standards as well as introducing community education programs for water treatment and quality monitoring at household level. Furthermore, Luvuvhu River Catchment lies within a broader transboundary hydrological system, enhanced cooperation between local municipalities, provincial agencies, and regional bodies (e.g., LIMCOM) is crucial for coordinated water resource management. These insights are especially relevant for stakeholders involved in donor-supported programs, such as those funded by USAID, where evidence-based planning can help mitigate human health risks and guide sustainable development in rural and peri-urban communities. The Water Quality Index (WQI) analysis revealed that water quality significantly deteriorates downstream, particularly in areas affected by agricultural runoff, domestic wastewater discharge, and industrial pollution. Sites such as S22, which showed the highest WQI values, indicate water unsuitable for drinking, underscoring the cumulative effect of human activities on water quality in the region. The human health risk assessment demonstrated that ingestion poses a greater non-carcinogenic risk compared to dermal exposure, with metals such as manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), and iron (Fe) being the most concerning. The study underscores the importance of monitoring and regulating PTEs concentrations in the LRC to protect local communities from potential health hazards such as neurological risks due to cumulative exposure to PTEs particularly Mn and Mo. Overall, these results call for action in managing land use activities effectively to ensure sustainable water quality, safeguard human health, and protect aquatic ecosystems in the LRC. It is recommended that future studies should look into sediment quality within the LRC and the evaluation of potentially ecological risks. Future studies should also prioritize the development of advanced predictive models using machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) to enhance real-time water quality monitoring and risk assessment in the Luvuvhu River Catchment. These models should incorporate emerging contaminants of concern such as microplastics, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and bisphenol A (BPA) to address gaps in current regulatory frameworks. By integrating high-resolution sensor data with ML-driven analytics, researchers can improve early contamination detection, source attribution, and adaptive management strategies for this vulnerable ecosystem.
