Rapid review of the impacts of climate change on the health system workforce and implications for action
Authors: Kiera Tsakonas, Simi Badyal, Tim Takaro, Chris G Buse
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe cascading impacts of climate change have significant implications for public health and healthcare delivery globally. This review explores how climate change impacts the health system workforce (both public health and healthcare service delivery), and what adaptation strategies are being deployed to mitigate against extreme climate events.MethodsThe review draws from English language peer-reviewed articles published between 2003 and 2023, that forefront experiences and adaptations to climate change events as they relate to the health system workforce. Out of 1662 articles, upon completing title and abstract review, two reviewers completed full-text review of 130 articles, removing 92 for not meeting inclusion criteria, resulting in 38 articles. Articles were analyzed in relation to the World Health Organization Climate Resilient Health Systems Framework.ResultsEmergent themes highlight occupational health impacts such as physical hazards, burn out and psychosocial impacts. Adaptive strategies to address these impacts include bolstering transformative leadership praxis, psychosocial support provision, emergency preparedness and planning, and scaling up climate-related emergency preparedness through the development of climate change core competencies and multi-sectoral collaboration strategies.ConclusionsOur review illustrates the limitations and opportunities of current adaptive strategies being utilized to support the healthcare workforce around the world, highlights the need for immediate emissions reductions that will reduce future hazards, and provides recommendations for how these findings can be applied to better prepare the health workforce for a range of climate futures.Keywords: Health system workforce, Climate change, Adaptation, Climate emergency, Emergency management

Introduction

Climate change is already impacting the health of populations around the world [[1], [2], [3]]. Without appropriate adaptation planning and immediate emissions reduction, these impacts are projected to be more acute [3,4], and to worsen existing health inequalities borne by equity-deserving populations, such as those with chronic illnesses, disabilities and elderly populations, among others [5,6]. However, less research attention has been directed towards the impacts of climate-related events on health system workers (HSWs), who are already strained under the response to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic [7,8].

This review highlights climate-related experiences of HSWs (broadly defined to include public health staff, emergency response planners, emergency responders, emergency room physicians and nurses, long-term care workers, family physicians and administrators, among others. With the goal of reviewing empirical literature on impacts of climate change on HSWs, this contribution seeks to a) make recommendations that enhance occupational health and safety for the workforce; b) generate adaptation options that may mitigate future risks based on past experiences and better prepare the health system for an uncertain future.

Methods

A rapid review methodology was deployed following Canada's National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools guidance [9], which highlights the role of rapid reviews in reviewing literature with practice-based implications with the purpose of synthesizing information to support organizational change [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. This included the steps of defining our review question, searching for research evidence, appraising information sources, and synthesizing findings.

Searches were aimed at peer-reviewed articles across four databases: PubMed, CINAHL, SCOPUS, and GEOBASE. The initial search utilized combinations of MeSH and non-MeSH search terms related to climate change, terms related to a variety of climate-related hazards or events, and a combination of MeSH and non-MeSH terms pertaining to HSW roles (see Supplemental Material). Articles were screened based on title and abstract, and included if English-language, empirical in nature, published in the past 20 years, drawn from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development member countries, and focused on climate-related impacts to HSWs and/or associated adaptations. Articles were checked by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved in discussion by three team members.

Two reviewers completed full-text review of 130 articles, excluding 92 for not meeting inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1). Articles were coded using Covidence software by two reviewers, with data extraction templates created to identify core contributions made by each article, study design and associated limitations, HSW roles described, impacts documented and adaptations recommended. Metadata for all articles were analyzed by all authors, and core findings coded according to the six building blocks of the WHO Health Systems Climate Resilience Framework, including leadership and governance, health workforce, health information systems, essential medical products and technologies, service delivery and financing. These building blocks comprise the reporting structure for the results of this review, in their applicability to the health workforce and structures that support the health workforce.

Results

The search strategy identified 38 empirical articles comprised of two mixed-methods studies, nine quantitative studies, and 27 qualitative studies. 16 studies focus on capacity building (training, education or continued professional development), but most center on emergency preparedness and response and sharing insights from specific climate-related events. Most studies are drawn from North America (N = 12), Oceania (but primarily Australia, N = 11) and the United Kingdom (N = 7) (see Fig. 2). For HSW roles, 16 were focused on generic roles present in clinics or hospitals and nine were specific to nurses (see Fig. 3).

Leadership and governance

Leadership and governance impacts

Leadership and governance impact HSWs in how a given organization is supporting and directing healthcare services in the context of climate change. A key barrier to developing adaptive climate change strategies are competing priorities for health services when significant cost pressures and political environments are not suited to address these issues [14]. Competing priorities affect HSWs in their ability to implement disaster preparedness and emergency management plans amidst climate change events. Boyson et al. additionally illustrate conflicting priorities between proposed actions in the national heatwave plan in the United Kingdom and guidelines around infection control at the hospital level, leaving health care workers without clear guidance on how to implement the plan [15]. Pertaining to heatwaves in England, competing priorities led to a lack of ability (time and capacity) to respond to high temperatures, as well as a general lack of awareness of the heatwave plan specifically for clinicians [16]. Additionally, HSWs report not having agency to provide adequate end of life care during disasters as a consequence of poorly implemented and delivered disaster plans, which left HSWs making "care compromises" as maintaining disaster requirements on top of basic care led to moral distress [17].

Moreover, institutionalized roles may often limit the time or capacity of practitioners to engage with climate change [18]. In a study on perceptions of climate change and environmental issues, nurses perceived the healthcare sector as reactive rather than proactive [19]. The reactive nature of health care is additionally illustrated by Brooks et al. who outlined a “need for strategic and long term planning across the health care system for resilience to extreme weather hazards” [16]. The role of governance and leadership is therefore crucial in coordinating the multi-sectoral and multi-stakeholder responses required of the healthcare sector in emergency response to climate-driven disasters. Siloed approaches were found to inhibit partnerships between the healthcare system and other sectors, leading to fragmentation of health services in response to extreme weather [20].

Opportunities for leadership and governance action

Recommendations to address impacts to healthcare workers include increasing multi-sectoral collaboration in long term local health system responses to climate change [21]. This includes having more front-line HSW voices included in health system and community disaster management planning [15,22]. Multi-sectoral collaboration should extend to the development of fire and local emergency response groups [23], as well as established partnerships and collaboration across organizations and health facilities [24], and volunteer organizations [20]. Climate change mitigation and adaptation also need to be routinized into strategic planning processes to promote behavioral change in the healthcare sector [14]. This will support HSWs incorporate climate change into their scope of their work.

Transformational leadership is crucial to implementing cross cutting and synergized strategic policies and supporting the healthcare workforce in the implementation of these policies. Key competencies include creating space for organizational culture change, accountability to real-time decision-making, risk navigation, disaster attunement, planning agility, vertical and interprofessional communication and interprofessional and organizational collaboration [25]. Transformational leaders are associated with better staff retention, organizational commitment, and physical and mental wellness of workers [26]. As an example, Sheffield et al. outlines the role of health care providers in education and advocacy with their vulnerable patient populations and the general public to know how to respond and protect themselves during heat events [27]. Kalogirou et al. also posit that once nurses have the education and become experts on the health impacts of climate change, they become trusted sources for patients and communities to limit their own vulnerability and risk [28]. Such leadership leads to better disaster planning, with higher likelihood of avoiding pitfalls noted above, and is also necessary for enhancing sustainability competency among HSWs [29].

Health workforce

Impacts on HSWs

The absence and inadequacy of professional training to respond to long term climate change health risks and acute impacts of climate-related events was a key theme from the literature. In relation to the long term health risks, physicians in the United States lacked the knowledge to be able to speak on the health effects of climate change in clinical encounters [30]. Inadequate professional training to respond to disaster events was also highlighted [25,[31], [32], [33]]. For example, nurses had low efficacy and knowledge from the disaster training that most of them had completed [25]. Previous disaster events often prepared the workforce better to respond to future events, and nurses without this prior experience experienced high stress reactions and mental health impacts.

Staff shortages become compounded by climate change events. Pendrey et al. illustrate that “practitioners are already moving elsewhere to cooler climates due to heat stress, more extreme acute events (heat events, floods and cyclones, coastal community flood threats with rising sea levels, severe bushfires, water shortages, etc.), and uninhabitable living conditions (heat stress)” [34]. Compounded impacts of multiple events also create heavy workloads [31]. Rosenthal et al. similarly show that wildfires can lead to decreased availability of healthcare workers who themselves may be impacted and unable to work [35]. Staff shortages in local hospitals put the health of everyone at risk. Hines and Reid outline that staffing was an issue during Hurricane Harvey, as most staff were physically unable to get into work [24].

During climate acute events and natural disasters role shifting was highlighted across the literature as an impact to HSWs [20,24]. For example, nurses during bushfires in Australia were called upon to do clinical, command and auxiliary tasks they were not trained to deliver [36]. Communication was also outlined as a challenge for healthcare workers during acute climate events. Breakdowns of communication can occur during climate impacts to communication infrastructure causing confusion and stress [20,22]. Communication challenges often stemmed from misdirected information, overwhelming tasks and debriefing that was not comprehensive [26].

A variety of occupational health risks were also documented in the literature. The lack of work life balance, anxiety and burnout that affect the mental health of healthcare workforce, is pronounced during climate events that compound existing stressors. More support is also needed for staff and their families during extreme weather events due to the demands, as staff experience anxiety and burnout, particularly in contexts where they were unable to leave the hospital and were required to sleep in makeshift quarters [24]. The lack of support for healthcare workers to maintain contact with their families was clearly identified as a stressor during extreme weather events, along with the need to better support workers with appropriate accommodation and nutrition [37].

Compromises in care during disasters additionally stress HSWs [14,17,23]. Even for non-frontline healthcare workers such as public health professionals, who are working to develop strategic health adaptation interventions, staff capacity is already overwhelmed [38]. Specifically, for psychosocial healthcare workers, the introduction of psychosocial emergency services added an additional stress, because “as difficult as it was to have your home and business impacted by the flood, now you felt like you were protecting your job because there were other people coming in to do your work” [39]. Additional occupational health impacts include, discomfort, stress, fatigue, inability to cope and less efficiency for healthcare workers during high temperatures [16].

Opportunities for HSW action

Healthcare workers have an important advocacy role in climate change adaptation and mitigation. Specific education and training, coupled with experiential knowledge in supporting health care workers can empower this role. Education and training recommendations are grouped into two categories, core climate change competencies (health and climate change risk, leadership, and climate mitigation and adaptation) and disaster training (updates to workplace safety practices and staffing contingency recommendations). This review also highlights the need for HCWs to have increased mental health support, staffing contingency plans and updates to workplace safety practices to assume this new role.

Climate change exacerbates vulnerabilities and affects the health care system, adding to the need for more professional development and an emphasis on climate change competencies in post-secondary education which better support students and health system trainees. Such preparation can reduce stress and uncertainty in the healthcare workforce. Nurses with minimal disaster experiences were found to benefit from regular practice exercises on disasters, and resources may need to be tailored for specific risks facing specific health facility locations (e.g. for a floodplain or urban heat island) on top of up-to-date standardized disaster education [23,28,32]. Johnson-Campbell et al. similarly found a positive correlation with duration of employment and participation in disaster drills, where a majority of healthcare workers were aware of the disaster preparedness plan and had a clear understanding of roles, before, during and after a hurricane [37]. This illustrates that role expansion is more successful where perceptions of roles and areas of capability are aligned and well communicated [40].

Across multiple types of healthcare oriented education, the literature calls for improved training in environmental sustainability and climate change core competencies, including more training of public health professionals on the health impacts of climate change [27,38,41], climate competence development for physicians in training [21,30,42,43], and education that expands the leadership of nurses [44,45]. Other health professionals such as pharmacists [46] and firefighters [47] would also benefit from climate-specific education which is essential for multi-sector collaboration during disaster events.

Across many acute climate events, mental and occupational health measures were highlighted as an important recommendation to support HSW and first responder mental health [17,48]. For example, Hayes et al. highlight the need for training in mental health and psychological first aid to support health care workers, specifically disaster management professionals, first responders and volunteers [39].

Health information systems

Impacts to health information systems

Because health information systems are made actionable by the health system workforce, few specific impacts to HSWs were present in the literature, beyond those articulated above around HSW workforce competency development.

Opportunities for health systems information action

Health information systems should support HSWs in their response to acute climate change events and adaptation to long term climate change health impacts. This should include considerations of workforce supply and national or regional workforce strategies that leverage the power of digital health systems information [32]. Risk assessments and adaptation strategies need to consider not only workforce supply but also how facilities themselves, can pose occupational health risks to the workforce. Such strategies include better digital data to quantify and monitor the impacts of climate change on health care infrastructure, new performance metrics for quantifying the impacts of climate change on the health system and accounting for the costs and benefits of adaptive actions required [14,31].

Essential medical products and technologies

Impacts to essential medical products and technologies

Accessibility of essential medical products and technologies during emergency responses to acute and long-term climate events can impact the ability of healthcare workers to provide service delivery [20]. An electronic patient database aided in identifying medication prescriptions of patients at evacuation centers, but flooding impacted the ability to contact/identify available pharmacists and source medications to the evacuation centers. Accessibility to medical supplies have also been a documented issue in the United States during wildfire events [35]. Heatwave events in Germany and the United Kingdom damaged healthcare infrastructure leading to medication shortages and failure of medical equipment (e.g. ultrasound from poor ventilation and taxed air conditioning systems), and overheating server rooms leading to disruptions in communication and digital health technology [16,49].

Opportunities for enhancing access to essential medical supplies and technologies

Recommendations include the need to incorporate sustainable practice and planetary health perspectives into healthcare workplace policies for climate resilience. Group messaging chats were beneficial for communication between HSWs, and social media enabled real-time updates for sharing resources and contacting community members [50]. Telehealth is also becoming more popular especially since the pandemic, including virtual triage of urgent care cases able to reduce ER surge capacity, and better continuity of care with patients [17,50]. Since climate events often adversely impact digital health infrastructure (i.e. power grids and telecommunications), further assessment and evaluation of digital health technology usage and resiliency during emergencies may be required in local contexts [16,50]. To alleviate stress on healthcare workers, infrastructure technologies such as modern climate friendly heating, ventilation and air conditioning interventions for indoor environments should be considered [16], as well as independent medical center battery power supplies and spaces for evacuation centers in rural health services/communities [41]. Social media and messaging apps enabled communication among healthcare staff and community members for emergency response during natural disasters in countries such as Australia, UK and the United States [17,20,50].

Service delivery

Impacts to service delivery

Service delivery is primarily impacted by pathways described in the sections above. For example, floods and wildfires also can create geographic barriers, preventing staff from accessing workplaces, creating staffing shortages that impede service delivery [35,37]. Transportation delays include disruptions to emergency response times due to physical barriers and road closures [24]. Moreover, disaster training competencies may require updating in certain contexts.

Opportunities for service delivery action

Recommendations highlight the need for better disaster training and education that supports service delivery and HSWs. This includes prioritizing community outreach and action towards patients during climate events and increasing emergency preparedness and protocols for HSWs. For example, a vulnerable persons protocol was established for contacting vulnerable populations and community outreach during flood events [20] and home visits and/or phone calls increased efficiency in community outreach to vulnerable patients during heatwaves [51]. To improve health service during climate events, HSWs need adequate awareness of emergency/disaster management preparedness plans that clearly define roles and protocols, and disaster planning and preparedness training drills to increase capacity and efficacy/knowledge for response [14,20,23,25,37,41]. Collaboration with community services and voluntary organizations provides services such as helplines and aid in evacuation centers to help reduce burden/surge capacity of healthcare workers in healthcare facilities [14,20]. Partnerships with local transportation networks and fire/local emergency response groups can aid in transportation resilience for patient outreach and emergency response [23,24].

Financing

Financing plays a major role in supply and resource availability, as well as the ability to implement processes and policies for climate change adaptation and resiliency at the health workforce, organizational and government levels. Hayes et al. highlight negative psychosocial impacts for HSWs and community members in Alberta that faced lack of continuity of care as funds for mental health services and flood-recovery support were diminished or withdrawn [39]. Slow and unresponsive processes for funding requests challenged health service delivery and resource supply stock for outreach during flooding events [31].

Lack of governmental prioritization and allocation of funding and resources is a major barrier to supporting HSWs [35,38]. Financing for disaster-response funding pools for disaster management, contingency plans and climate adaptation at local and provincial/state levels [23,37], as well as directing patients towards local contacts for supplies, medications, shelters and other services are also emphasized [22]. Recommendations for contingency plans include emergency allocation funding for healthcare workers and community members; financing for health infrastructure and transportation resilience [24,41]; funding for disaster response workplace training [14,37]; and increased financing for resources and healthcare infrastructure for rural HSWs [41].

Discussion

This review applied the WHO operational framework for health systems resilience to the empirical literature to conceptualize how climate change is impacting HSWs. In practice, the six components are inextricably linked with significant overlap in practice and cross-cutting recommendations for enhancing transformational leadership, promoting occupational health and safety with a specific focus on mental health, and maintaining a workforce with a strategic focus on an equitable response to the climate emergency. The WHO operational framework for health system resilience provides a framework to illustrate that leadership, planning and preparedness, and workforce occupational health and safety extend from all aspects of the six building blocks of a resilient healthcare system to support HSWs. While this literature review focused on OECD countries, the findings and recommendations surfaced from this literature review have implications globally for HSWs, specifically the opportunities for transformational leadership, emergency planning and preparedness and workforce occupational health and safety.

The role of transformational leadership in health system responses to climate change cannot be understated. Climate change champions who possess the skills required for transformational leadership in the health sector can be nurtured through thoughtful planning of post-secondary program delivery of healthcare related credentials and continuing professional development. Transformational leadership has been associated with increased job satisfaction, better staff retention, commitment to the organization, physical health and mental wellness for healthcare workers, and provides co-benefits for expanding training competencies with climate change specific curriculum [26]. This review indicates the need for sustainability (i.e. mitigation) and climate change competencies for public health professionals [38], physicians [21,30], nurses [[42], [43], [44], [45]] and pharmacists [46].

A significant impact on the health care workforce implicated in leadership and governance is the coordination of multi-sectoral collaboration for long term health sector planning. As climate change is characterized by long term stressors and acute climate events, there is an inherent tension between mitigation and adaptation responses, which mirrors the tension between upstream and downstream interventions in the health system [52]. When planning is done with both mitigation and adaptation together as central priorities, how we respond and build back better can support long term mitigation, specifically in upgrading facilities to address both climate mitigation and occupational health and safety for HSWs [53]. The health sector should be mainstreaming long term strategic planning priorities related to climate change mitigation and adaptation, and disaster risk reduction planning for emergency response. Central to this work is enhancing existing relationships between communities and public health and emergency management professionals to identify local assets [54].

Research opportunities

A significant gap highlighted through this review is specific occupational health risks facing HSWs under extreme climate events, and the implementation and evaluation of occupational health and safety plans in light of the increasing severity and occurrence of those events. Viewing existing protocols through the lens of the climate emergency is an identified priority. Boyson et al. [15] and Price et al. [51] were the only studies to advocate for worker-specific considerations, both related to extreme heat. There is need for future research to understand impacts across all climate-hazards, and the risks associated with cascading and concomitant events on occupational workforce health and safety, including physical health impacts such as violence in the workplace due to stress from climate change events [[54], [55], [56]].

The health care workforce also continues to be impacted by routine stressors, and by compounding stressors such as COVID-19, the opioid epidemic and climate change, that create an overburdened work environment [48,49]. Healthcare workers already experience complex mental health impacts from working tirelessly in a reactive sector, with barriers to pursue upstream interventions, adequately do their jobs, and feel confident in shifting roles to meet disaster response policies [17,19,20,24,27]. More research is needed to evaluate mental health supports for healthcare workers in dealing with extreme events that compound existing stressors and burnout, to surface best practices in workplace mental health promotion and occupational health and safety to mitigate mental health impacts.

The review also highlights a significant gap in equity-informed understandings of the composition of the workforce and the unique structural characteristics and stressors that come from working and living in regions with structurally marginalized populations such as unhoused populations, Indigenous communities, racialized and immigrant communities and rural and remote communities who are disproportionately impacted by the impacts of climate change. Understanding the intersectional risks of HSWs may reveal additional pathways of impact and processes of marginalization that differentially impact certain workers in relation to others. Fostering intersectoral collaboration with various community partners such as Indigenous organizations can support healthcare workers in developing culturally safe partnerships while strengthening community resilience to better serve diverse populations and health inequities exacerbated by climate change [34,41,54]. Further research is needed to inform best approaches for adaptation and community engagement among HSWs to help enhance climate resilience of underserved communities in local contexts.

More research is also needed to better understand the structural working and living conditions in rural and remote settings for healthcare workers in disaster responses, to enhance resiliency of rural health service delivery. Geographic isolation and smaller community populations influence unique challenges faced by rural and remote healthcare workers such as staffing retention, disruptions in medical supply chains, inadequate/outdated health infrastructure, and lack of funding [20,32,34]. These adverse working conditions are exacerbated during climate events, especially for rural health practitioners, where evacuations and changing living conditions that prevail during extreme climate events can prevent people from getting to work [22,34]. The limited representation of rural and remote experiences in our final article sample indicates the need for increasing equitable access/redistribution of resources through increased funding for health infrastructure, supplies and workforce contingencies. There is also the need to acknowledge the lived-experiences of HSWs as community members, and how they are also impacted by the intersections of working and living conditions and climate change in smaller communities.

Study limitations

We acknowledge that the scope of search terms used to define HSWs and climate events in this rapid review may be a limitation in this study. We included a combination of generic and specific HSW search terms to explore how health system workforce broadly defined was impacted during emergency response to climate change events, but because of the rapid nature of the review, additional targeted searches for job-specific HSW roles may be warranted.

We additionally chose to focus on empirical articles of specific impacts and/or adaptations relevant to HSWs under a changing climate. The empirical articles that exist in this area of research are largely qualitative with small sample sizes and reflect the gap in climate change specific HSW impacts. Conceptual articles were not included due to the focus on highlighting applied recommendations and adaptations from climate change events, and empirical studies on education and training for the health care workforce to be equipped to work in a changing climate.

It is also important to note that both the WHO framework and conceptual articles in this research area focus heavily on decarbonizing the health system, which has implications depending on the political environment and organizational structure of the health system (i.e. for profit versus non-for-profit healthcare). Decarbonizing the health system is an important upstream intervention to mitigate climate related health risks but was not explicitly included due to the focus on workforce specific impacts and adaptations resulting from acute climate change events.

Conclusion

Rapidly accelerating climate change is already disrupting population health planning and service delivery, as well as acute healthcare. These impacts are profound for the workforce responsible for the delivery of services that care for, protect, treat and promote the health of both individuals and populations. As the emergency quickens, change will come to job roles, core competencies, and health system priorities. Given that health system workers are typically members of the communities that they serve, more attention and planning is required to enable HSWs to undertake the types of systems-wide transformation that will mitigate the climate impacts of the health system and support adaptation efforts that protect the health of their communities. In light of this review, there are many promising opportunities to achieve these goals if such a transformation is undertaken thoughtfully, and with a person-centered approach that humanizes healthcare workers and the challenges and urgency they face under climate change.
