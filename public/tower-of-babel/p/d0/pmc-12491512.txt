Protocol for screening inhibitors of the fungal sphingolipid-metabolizing enzyme ceramide synthase
Authors: Andy Zhong, Deveney Dasilva, Nivea Pereira de Sa, Jeehyun Karen You, Maurizio Del Poeta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryFungal sphingolipids have emerged as potent antifungal targets based on cumulative evidence demonstrating that fungal sphingolipid metabolism is essential to the virulence of pathogenic fungi. Here, we present a protocol to screen for inhibitors of the sphingolipid-metabolizing enzyme ceramide synthase. We describe steps for harvesting microsomes from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, performing in vitro reactions, and performing lipid extractions. We then detail the use of a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-based fluorometric assay to screen large chemical libraries for ceramide synthase inhibitors.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Dasilva et al.1

Before you begin

Fungal sphingolipids and their metabolic enzymes are essential for the growth and virulence of pathogenic fungi which makes them strategic targets for drug discovery and development.2 In the sphingolipid biosynthesis pathway, the enzyme CoA-dependent ceramide synthase catalyzes the synthesis of ceramide from a fatty acyl-CoA and C18 sphingoid base.3 In turn, ceramide serves as the ultimate precursor for the formation of more complex sphingolipids like inositol phosphorylceramide and glucosylceramide.4 Consequently, deletion of ceramide synthase genes disrupts this pathway leading to an accumulation of sphingoid bases and invariably leading to cell death.4 Furthermore, given that the structure of ceramide synthase (S. cerevisiae) is freely accessible, computational biology can be applied to predict selective inhibitors.5 Compounded with an assay to screen these inhibitors then makes ceramide synthase a highly strategic target for the development of novel antifungals.

Here we will present a detailed protocol for screening inhibitors by measuring ceramide synthase activity with a fluorometric assay. A complete list of required reagents and equipment is provided in the key resources table. This protocol was used in a recent publication to identify two synthetic compounds that preferentially inhibited the fungal ceramide synthase over the mammalian ceramide synthase.1

Innovations

The use of radioactive, fluorometric, and mass-spectrometry based assays to measure ceramide synthase activity have been routinely applied to study the mammalian and fungal ceramide synthases.6 Among these, mass-spectrometry based assays are the most accurate but are time-consuming and require expensive equipment and materials. In comparison, the fluorometric assay described in this protocol is more feasible featuring low equipment costs, commercially available materials, and short assay times making it the most efficient for screening large chemical libraries.

Limitations

The use of microsomes to measure ceramide synthase activity is not free from the presence of other proteins. This may lead to differing enzymatic activity between samples and among different batches of yeast cells.

Potential drug-drug interactions and the possibility that inhibitory effects only occur under co-administration are considered after the preliminary cocktail screening. In the preliminary screening, any compound cocktails that demonstrated inhibitory effects were identified. In the next screening, the ten corresponding compounds from each selected cocktail were tested individually. In this way we distinguish whether compounds demonstrated inhibition independently or only under co-administration. But to identify the exact individual compounds responsible for the synergistic effect, will require the identification of the fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index. That means that for each cocktail, all possible two-compound combinations will need to be performed for the identification of the FIC for a total of 45 FIC indexes for each 10-compound cocktail.

The examiner can then select and label the synergistic compounds accordingly (e.g., compound 1, 80% inhibition when combined with compound 3). Analysis of the compound structure can reveal whether this inhibition is due to similarity or difference among the chemical entities.

In general, this process is not warranted for the screening of large libraries (e.g., thousands of compounds) as it is very time consuming. For large libraries, the examiner is looking for the most potent single compound(s) that can then be used for further studies through a well-defined medicinal chemistry and structure-activity relationship (SAR) effort. However, this process can be useful for screening small libraries because the identification of compounds with established synergistic effect will decrease the development of drug resistance.

Problem 4

Signals other than the NBD-sphinganine and NBD-ceramide peaks appear on the HPLC (after step 43).

Potential solution

•The appearance of other signals is likely due to contamination in the HPLC system or the column. This can be determined in negative control samples with substrates only. The presence of additional peaks did not affect the area values of the NBD-sphinganine or NBD-C18-ceramide peak and can largely be ignored.
