The representation of Australians living with dementia in voluntary assisted dying research: A scoping review
Authors: Adrienne Matthys, Belinda Cash, Bernadette Moorhead
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo critically examine which stakeholders are participating in voluntary assisted dying (VAD) research, to identify the representation of Australians living with dementia.MethodsA scoping review of peer‐reviewed literature to examine which stakeholders are represented in Australian VAD research was conducted. This review was informed by the Arksey and O'Malley Framework for Scoping Reviews, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR). Searches were conducted systematically across key academic databases to gather Australian research articles written in English that were published between 2017 and August 2023.ResultsAfter screening, 21 publications formed the dataset. Of the 21 publications, none of them represented Australians living with dementia. The voice of one person living with a terminal illness was included in a study of care partners, and four studies explored the views of community members. The most dominant voices in the dataset were health‐care practitioners, who were represented in 16 studies.ConclusionsAustralian VAD research is a contested space where all stakeholders with a relevant contribution to policy and practice must be represented in contemporary Australian research. As living experience experts with a wealth of experiential knowledge to contribute, the voices of people living with dementia need to be represented in future Australian VAD research through inclusive research design, to ensure a greater balance in stakeholder representation across the VAD literature.Keywords: active, assisted, Australia, dementia, end‐of‐life care, euthanasia, voluntary

Policy Impact

This review identified the exclusion of people living with dementia from Australian voluntary assisted dying (VAD) research. This finding highlights the need for the representation of people living with dementia in future VAD research, to create new opportunities for potential end users to contribute to research‐informed policy development in this space.

Practice Impact

The representation of people living with dementia in voluntary assisted dying (VAD) research will result in policy and practice guidelines that are informed by living experience experts, to ensure that services provided best meet the needs of those who choose to access them.

INTRODUCTION

The nexus between voluntary assisted dying (VAD) and dementia is a heavily contested space. Dementia is a terminal condition which is currently the second‐leading cause of death in Australia. 1 It is estimated that 401,300 Australians are currently living with dementia, with projections this figure will double to 849,300 by 2058. 1 Greater awareness and early detection of dementia means that people are living well for longer with the condition and are capable of making decisions about their end‐of‐life care. 2 , 3 Voluntary assisted dying is a legal end‐of‐life care option for people living with dementia in international jurisdictions such as Canada, The Netherlands, and Belgium. 2 , 4 It is also legally available in most Australian jurisdictions to terminally ill people who meet stringent eligibility criteria. 3 , 5 However, legislative requirements surrounding capacity to engage in end‐of‐life decision‐making, along with stringent timeframes relating to prognosis, typically preclude Australians living with dementia from being eligible to access VAD. 4 These policy decisions are inconsistent with the recommendations of peak body Dementia Australia, who advocate for VAD to be a legal choice for people living with dementia. 3

Australian VAD legislations have been described in politico‐legal contexts as ‘the safest, most conservative model in the world…with the most rigorous checks and balances’. 6 While the contention of politicians surrounding the safe and conservative nature of the Australian VAD models appears meritorious, they have also been criticised by medical researchers and legal scholars. Critics identify the legislation as being designed with a deliberately narrow scope to secure political support and limit access, a phenomenon described by legal scholars as ‘policy drift by a thousand cuts’ (p. 451). 7 , 8 These scholars speak to the importance of population‐level research and have called for parliamentarians to adopt a research‐informed approach to VAD law. 9 These criticisms stem from Australian VAD legislations being developed through non‐academic consultations by state and territory governments, such as the consultation currently being undertaken by the Northern Territory Government, 10 which are often not conducted with the same level of methodological robustness as academic research. 11 , 12 As such, engaging key stakeholders in academic research that can inform the development of legislation is vital as this will lead to policy that reflects the values of the affected community, promote transparency and trustworthiness of the policy, and improve health‐related outcomes relating to the policy measure. 12

There are a number of key stakeholder groups in VAD: people living with a terminal illness and their care partners, the general public, health‐care practitioners and health organisations. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Despite the importance of equal representation of all stakeholder voices in this space, the realisation of equal research participation is problematic. Historically, VAD research in Western cultures generally reflects death as a medicalised event in a domain managed by health‐care professionals and religious groups. 17 Consequently, these privileged groups play a powerful role in shaping death, dying and end‐of‐life research, which can serve to protect their professional identities, to justify their expertise about dying, and to maintain the exclusion of other stakeholders. 17 , 18

It is important to recognise that participation in end‐of‐life care research is valued by many people living with terminal conditions, who perceive this as an opportunity to contribute to research‐informed care. 19 Other types of end‐of‐life research such as hospice and palliative care has effectively incorporated the voices of potential end users, who have identified this as a valued opportunity to contribute to research‐informed care. 19 As dementia is a terminal condition, participation in research informing end‐of‐life care is in keeping with globally accepted best‐practice dementia care, which is achieved when legislative conditions are based on established human rights principles, in this instance, the right to contribute to research with the potential to shape policy. 20 , 21 This principle speaks to the importance of end‐of‐life policy that is informed by the experiential expertise of people living with dementia, to ensure that policy and service delivery is reflective of their needs and wishes.

Despite the importance of the representation of people living with dementia in VAD research, recent systematic review evidence demonstrates the overrepresentation of health‐care practitioners in international VAD research compared with other stakeholders. 22 There is also literature suggesting that people living with dementia have been precluded from participation in international VAD survey research due to their dementia diagnosis. 23 Moreover, labelling‐related stigma is identifiable in the international literature where people living with dementia are known to be represented in the contemporary VAD research as the ‘incompetent patient’ (p. 1078, p. 1), 23 , 24 language which reifies this stigma. Stigmatising language is often evident in studies exploring instances where people living with dementia no longer have the requisite capacity to make decisions relating to their end‐of‐life care. 24 Describing people living with dementia using language such as ‘incompetent’ 24 is steeped in hierarchical paternalism, where the end‐of‐life care options available to them function as a mechanism of control grounded in the stigmatising perception that all people living with dementia are bereft of their legal status as a person. 25

The legal status of people living with dementia is also challenged in the context of vulnerability. Some medico‐legal stakeholders have raised concerns about the potential for elder abuse if VAD were legally available to people living with dementia; these concerns typically relate to decision‐making capacity and coercion. 14 This contention is supported by Behuniak et al., 25 who consider people living with dementia as vulnerable persons who, in some circumstances, may not have the capacity to protect their own interests. However, it is important to acknowledge that vulnerability is also a concept commonly politicised to justify limits placed on access to end‐of‐life care due to perceived vulnerability relating to coercion and capacity. 25 , 26 These decisions are couched in the biomedical‐legal model, where stringent and absolute safeguards neglect the shades of grey inherent to dementia care, thereby risking the erasure of the person and their living experiences of the condition, and stifling the development of more equitable policy. 25 , 26

The recent publication of two international studies that represented people living with dementia as key stakeholders reflects a shift in awareness of this issue, challenging these problematic assumptions in the VAD research narrative. 2 , 27 These studies challenge the dominant narrative of vulnerability, demonstrating that people living with dementia can and do make necessary contributions to this highly contested area of research. Recent Australian research more broadly has also demonstrated positive shifts toward reframing dementia research narratives, highlighting that many people living with dementia are capable of making valued contributions to research. In particular, these studies speak to the importance of attending to inclusive practices which promote equal representation of people living with dementia in research. 20 , 28

Despite these broader advances in dementia research practices, the extent to which people living with dementia are represented in Australian VAD research remains unclear. This gap is significant in the context of active calls for VAD to be a legal choice for people living with dementia, and for legislation to be informed by academic research with end users. 3 , 9 The need for their representation is also grounded in the knowledge that people living with dementia experience suboptimal end‐of‐life care options, including insufficient pain control, greater hospitalisations, less choice and control over palliative interventions, and have limited access to health information to inform their end‐of‐life choices. 29 , 30 This study therefore aims to critically examine which stakeholders are participating in Australian VAD research, to identify the representation of people living with dementia in the literature.

METHODS

A scoping review was conducted using the Arksey and O'Malley Five‐Stage Methodological Framework for Scoping Reviews. 31 As shown in Table 1, the person, intervention, comparison and outcome (PICO) model was adopted to develop the research questions. 32 Based on this model, two research questions were explored: (1) How are people living with dementia represented in Australian VAD research? and (2) How does the representation of people living with dementia differ from the representation of other Australian VAD stakeholders? In accordance with the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR), the protocol for this study was registered on the Open Science Framework. 33 , 34 Ethics approval was not required for this study.

Search strategy

Systematic searches for citations published in English between 2017 and 2023 were conducted in August 2023 within the CINAHL/SocINDEX, Informit, ProQuest and PubMed bibliographic databases. This date range was selected as it captures research conducted since the first contemporary Australian VAD Act was passed in Victoria in November 2017. To ensure that a breadth of references was returned, the search terms ‘voluntary assisted dying’ AND ‘Australia’ were used. Searches conducted in Informit databases were limited to AGIS Plus Text (AGISPT), Australian Public Affairs Full Text (APAFT), Health Collection, Humanities and Social Sciences, and Indigenous Collection.

Study selection

In keeping with the recommendations of Arksey and O'Malley, 31 an iterative and reflexive approach to eligibility criteria development was adopted, where the sources were selected following screening of the full‐text articles, with reference to their relevance to the research question. When working through the literature it became apparent that to meet the study aims, the inclusion criteria outlined in the protocol required slight modification. Under the protocol, original research articles were included. However, addressing the question of participation and representation required a specific focus on research conducted with key VAD stakeholders. For this reason, papers that used methods to analyse secondary source materials such as systematic/scoping reviews, content analysis of grey literature and clinical data mining were excluded. Additionally, snowball searches of the reference list of articles which met the inclusion criteria were completed. However, these searches returned no additional publications for inclusion in the review. In keeping with the protocol, studies that were published after 2017 but where research was conducted prior to Victoria VAD Act being passed were excluded (Table 2).

Search results were exported to EndNote for screening, and duplicates were removed. The articles were screened by the first author, in regular consultation with the second and third author. This screening was undertaken in two stages. First, the articles were screened by title and abstract, and then, the remaining full‐text articles were screened against the eligibility criteria. Data extraction

Data were extracted by the first author using a custom data chart informed by Arksey and O'Malley 31 under the supervision of the second and third authors. Information extracted included author/s; year of publication; location; participants; and primary stakeholder/s represented in the study.

Data analysis

Extracted data were collated in a tabular form using Microsoft Excel software. Frequency counts of the stakeholder groups in the extracted data were completed, and descriptive statistics were used to determine the representation of each stakeholder group. 31

The most significant finding from this dataset was that people living with dementia were not represented in any Australian studies. There was very limited proxy representation of people living with dementia by other stakeholders evident in three studies. In the study by Rutherford, 35 four doctors raised concerns about people living with dementia being excluded from VAD access on the grounds of legal capacity. This concern was also raised by health‐care practitioners in Fuscaldo et al. 36 However, the number of participants sharing this view was not quantified. Finally, exclusion from access on the grounds of capacity was raised by one neuropsychologist in Digby et al. 14

The majority of included studies (76%, n = 16) represented the views of health‐care practitioners. 19 per cent (n = 4) of the included studies represented community members; and five per cent (n = 1) represented the views of people living with terminal illnesses and their informal caregivers. The studies representing community members did not explicitly state whether people living with dementia were part of the sample. As such, it was not possible to ascertain whether their voices were included in research at the community level. Additionally, in two of the studies representing views of community members, 13 , 37 participants were sub‐groups of larger cohorts also including health‐care practitioners. The total sample size of those represented across these 21 studies was 103 (n = 10) participants. Of these participants, the vast majority of the sample (96%, n = 9726) represented the voices of health‐care practitioners. By comparison, only one person living with a terminal illness was represented in this dataset. It should be noted that the same dataset was reported in Rutherford, 35 Rutherford 38 and Rutherford et al., 39 representing (n = 25) physicians; and in Haining and Keough 40 and Haining et al., 41 representing (n = 17) physicians, thus not reflecting any additional voices. In terms of method, the literature included 14 qualitative studies, six quantitative studies, and one study that was characterised as mixed methods. Varied perspectives demonstrating the diversity of VAD stakeholder representation in Australian research were noted, though how this differs for people living with dementia could not be analysed due to their absence in the included studies.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this review have identified that people living with dementia are not represented in Australian VAD research. This finding correlates with the extensive history of social exclusion experienced by people living with dementia highlighted in the broader literature. 28 , 42 , 43 Moreover, this finding is in keeping with the barriers to human rights that are consistently experienced by people living with dementia. 20 , 21

The exclusion of people living with dementia from Australian VAD research is likely grounded in the interwoven complexities of the representations of VAD and dementia more broadly. The exclusion of people living with dementia from research participation stems from stigmatising labels associated with cognitive changes, and the tendency for a dementia diagnosis to be catastrophised in the research narrative. 44 , 45 , 46 Considering the recent implementation of VAD in Australia, stakeholders are reliant on clinical and professional experiences that do not include VAD and dementia. In this sense, stakeholders are challenged to confront their concerns about legislation, conflict with personal beliefs, concerns for potential end users and discordance with the values of the medical profession. 41 , 43 , 47 The perceived vulnerability of people living with dementia impacts their representation in VAD research, particularly how vulnerabilities relating to capacity and coercion can limit their opportunity to participate in research, and to make decisions about their end‐of‐life care. 14 , 26 , 48 Moreover, their exclusion from Australian VAD research is in keeping with research exploring the intersection of dementia with the biomedical model, 49 and the prevailing belief that people living with dementia are unable to express their preferences and opinions. 43 Importantly, the exclusion of people living with dementia from contributing to research‐informed care based on perceived cognitive changes is at odds with human rights principles, leading to research that fails to address their needs. 20 , 28 These failings are particularly pertinent in terms of poorer mental health outcomes for people living with dementia and the increased risk of self‐harm soon after diagnosis. 50

The challenge for people living with dementia, however, is when we, as researchers, respond to VAD research participation from the position of stereotyping potential participants. From this standpoint, we do not make the effort to explore the richness of experiential expertise that people living with dementia bring to the research community and, consequently, fail to shape policy and professional practice in a direction which promotes greater equity and choice in end‐of‐life care. 20 , 28 Thus, addressing the challenges for people living with dementia in terms of achieving greater equality in representation in VAD research lies with the research community, who are entrusted with the co‐creation of knowledge with people living with dementia, and to reimagine how people living with dementia are represented in this discourse more broadly. 50

A starting point for reorienting the Australian VAD research narrative is the shift from problematising the concept of VAD access for people living with dementia, to a narrative grounded in questions of democracy, equity and human rights. 20 , 47 Contemporary best practice in dementia research champions representation and the right of people living with dementia to meaningfully contribute to research‐informed care, a contention backed by a plethora of examples demonstrating how people living with dementia can and do live well with the condition for many years, and are willing to, and are capable of, contributing to research. 20 , 28 Van Rickstal et al. 2 speak to this contention, stressing the urgent need for people living with dementia to be included in the contemporary VAD policy debate, given that ‘patients…themselves are an indispensable voice to be heard at the table as their experience most likely leads to unique perspectives on the practice of euthanasia’ (p. 6). However, reorienting the Australian VAD research narrative also acknowledges that dementia is ultimately a terminal condition where among other changes, people do experience changes in cognitive capacity; certainly, it is the terminal nature of the condition that necessitates the discussion of equal representation in VAD research. It should be recognised that living well with dementia and dementia being a terminal condition are equally valid and inseparable concepts, and that ensuring equal representation of the voices of people living with dementia should be a key focus of reorienting Australian VAD research towards a rights‐based research narrative.

On this basis, the question of representation now becomes a matter of inclusion. 28 , 47 Inclusion requires attention to inclusive research practices that promote equitable research participation, which are vital in changing and challenging the perspectives held by other stakeholder groups towards VAD access for people living with dementia. 20 , 43 , 49 By prioritising the voices of people living with dementia in Australian VAD research, we co‐construct reality with them. As these communications take shape and their voices are centred in the discourse, they will be empowered to make a necessary and valued contribution to the currently polarising research narrative, a contribution with the potential to reorient our collective reified reality of VAD in Australia. 28 , 49

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the first‐known Australian VAD studies to position people living with dementia as key stakeholders in VAD, and to critically evaluate their representation in this emerging research space. As such, this study presents opportunities for future research in this field. However, as VAD is a rapidly evolving area of research, and the search strategy focused on targeted biographical databases, it is possible that not all relevant studies were identified for inclusion in this review. An additional limitation of this review is the exclusion of papers from languages other than English. While this study focuses on the Australian context, it is worth noting that there could be a small possibility of literature in other languages.

CONCLUSIONS

Voluntary assisted dying is a complex area of contemporary research, policy and practice with immense potential to deliver a potential end user informed, end‐of‐life care option to people living with dementia who are seeking to access VAD. To achieve this in the Australian context, this scoping review demonstrated the need for reorientation of the Australian VAD research narrative to attain a greater balance in the representation of all stakeholders in this research space. As a stakeholder of significance, the representation of people living with dementia presents a priority area of future VAD research to ensure that Australian VAD policy and practice is informed by the experiential knowledge of this group.
