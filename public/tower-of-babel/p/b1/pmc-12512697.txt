Proteasome regulation of petite-negativity in fission yeast
Authors: Katie Lin Amberg, Lyrica Hao, Susanne Cranz-Mileva, Mikel Zaratiegui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitochondria carry out essential functions in eukaryotic cells. The mitochondrial genome encodes factors critical to support oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial protein import necessary for these functions. However, organisms like budding yeast can readily lose their mitochondrial genome, yielding respiration-deficient petite mutants. The fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe is petite-negative, but some nuclear mutations enable the loss of its mitochondrial genome.ResultsHere, we characterize the classical petite-positive mutation ptp1-1 as a loss of function allele of the proteasome 19S regulatory subunit component mts4/rpn1, involved in the ubiquitin-dependent degradation pathway. By comparison with another petite-enabling mutation in the g-subunit of the F1-ATPase, we show that ptp1-1 does not rescue mitochondrial membrane potential. Instead, the mutation results in increased levels of mitochondrial and cytoplasmic chaperones and an altered oxidative stress response.Conclusionsptp1-1 is a partial loss of function mutation of the proteasome that enables growth of cells devoid of mitochondrial DNA through a mechanism that is independent of mitochondrial membrane potential rescue and associated with proteasome-dependent regulation of mitochondrial protein import precursors and the oxidative stress response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02409-2.Keywords: Mitochondria, Petite, MtDNA, Proteasome, Fission yeast, Schizosaccharomyces pombe

Background

Mitochondria contain the remnants of their ancestral alpha-proteobacterial genome. While much reduced, after shedding the majority of its genetic content by outright loss or transfer to the nuclear genome, it still encodes a number of proteins as well as complete translational non coding RNA machinery (rRNA and tRNA) [1]. Shortly after the description of the mitochondrial genome (mtDNA), it was discovered that the respiration deficient small colonies that arise spontaneously in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, known as petites, harbor mutations in or complete loss of mtDNA [2, 3]. The study of petite mutations has led to insights into mitochondrial involvement in metabolism, aging, and disease.

Petite-positivity is the ability for survival in the absence of mtDNA. Testing for petite-positivity involves multiple rounds of growth in media containing ethidium bromide (EtBr) which is highly toxic to mtDNA [4] and will result in the formation of petite colonies devoid of mtDNA (ρ0) when plated onto glucose media [5, 6]. Since multiple essential subunits of the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes and the membrane bound proton pore Fo subunit of the ATP synthase are encoded in the mtDNA [1, 7], ρ0 mitochondria are deficient for oxidative phosphorylation and unable to perform respiration. Petite-positive species are able to survive as ρ0 as long as they are in the presence of glucose, which provides ATP by way of fermentation, but are unable to survive in respiration carbon sources such as glycerol or pyruvate [5, 6]. The dysfunction of the ETC results in a large drop in mitochondrial inner membrane potential ∆Ψ [8]. Since protein import into the mitochondrial matrix depends on ∆Ψ, essential mitochondrial functions, like iron-sulfur cluster biosynthesis, are also compromised [9].

Petite mutants are capable of foregoing oxidative phosphorylation in the presence of glucose for fermentation but must retain some measure of mitochondrial protein import for survival [10]. Petite-positivity in S. cerevisiae depends on the action of several nuclear genome encoded factors. Conversely, some petite-negative yeasts like Kluyveromyces lactis and Schizosaccharomyces pombe can be made petite-positive by nuclear mutations. The study of petite-suppressing and petite-enabling mutations has revealed that mitochondrial function can be supported by the inverted action of the nuclear-encoded F1 subunit of the ATP synthase and the ATP/ADP exchange machinery, which can rescue ΔΨ by hydrolysis of ATP produced by the fermentative pathway [10]. Petite-positivity in S. cerevisiae is lost in mutants in the α and β subunits of F1-ATPase and in the PET9 ATP/ADP translocase [10, 11]. Similarly, mutation of the YME1 iAAA protease results in increased levels of Inh1, which inhibits ATP hydrolysis by the F1-ATPase subunit, resulting in petite-negativity [12, 13]. Other mitochondrial chaperones acting in the intermembrane space also are involved: Tim9/Tim10, which shuttle cargo between the outer (TOM) and inner (TIM) membrane protein translocation complexes and are regulated by Yme1 [14], are necessary for petite-positivity in S. cerevisiae [15]. Several petite-enabling mutations in K. lactis and S. pombe have also been identified, painting a consistent picture. In K. lactis, inactivation of INH1 and mutations of the α, β, and γ subunits of F1-ATPase that increase ATP hydrolysis allow for growth of ρ0 cells [16]. In S. pombe, mutants of the β αnd γ subunits of F1-ATPase also become petite-positive [17, 18], but their effect on ATP hydrolysis activity has not been reported. Notably, overexpression of S. cerevisiae Yme1 in S. pombe renders it petite-positive [19].

A survey of gene essentiality suppressors in S. pombe revealed a network of interesting genetic interactions between factors involved in mitochondrial translation and the proteasome [18]. A mutation in the 19S regulatory subunit component mts4 (RPN1 in S. cerevisiae), as well as overexpression of Mts4 and several other proteasome related factors, enabled growth of ρ0 cells after EtBr treatment. This indicates that the proteasome activity can regulate survival in the absence of mtDNA in fission yeast. However, the mechanism of this phenomenon was not addressed. Mutations of proteasome subunits in S. cerevisiae also affect either the stability of the mtDNA or the survival of ρ0 cells: loss of RPN4, a transcription factor that drives expression of proteasome factors, increases survival to EtBr treatment [20], and deletion of the proteasome activator BLM10 and the proteasome chaperone UMP1 increase the frequency of petites [21]. The 26S proteasome, composed of the 19S regulatory subunit and the 20S core subunit, affects multiple cellular pathways by regulating the levels of cellular factors through the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation pathway [22]. The 20S core proteasome can also degrade misfolded and oxidized proteins independently of the 19S subunit [23]. Since the loss of ∆Ψ in ρ0 cells results in cytoplasmic accumulation of mitochondrial protein precursors and the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) [24], 20S proteasome activity may be important for their survival. However, the wide range of substrates of the proteasome makes it difficult to propose a mechanism for its involvement in petite-positivity.

The ptp1-1 mutation was the first nuclear petite-enabling mutation described in S. pombe [6]. It was isolated as a background enabling a high frequency of ρ0 cells after incubation in EtBr. It has since been used to investigate mitochondrial genetics in multiple studies [17], but has remained unidentified. In order to investigate the mechanism of petite-negativity in fission yeast, and to further inform the phenotypes of ρ0 cells obtained with this mutation in the presence of other mutations of interest, we set out to identify and characterize this mutation.

Results

ptp1-1 is a mutation in mts4

After two rounds of backcrosses of the original PHP14 ptp1-1 isolate, following the petite-positive phenotype of the segregants to clean the background of possible mutations caused by the original EtBr treatment, we isolated two complete tetrads that showed a 2:2 segregation of petite-positivity. We sequenced the genome of all 8 segregants and analyzed the polymorphisms present [25]. Only one polymorphism was present in all 4 petite-positive segregants and absent in all 4 petite-negative segregants. This polymorphism results in a mis-sense mutation in the protein coding gene mts4, changing Leucine 636 to a Proline (Fig. 1A–C). Of note, a different mutation in mts4 has been previously described to confer petite-positivity in fission yeast [18] (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the mts4L636P mutation is a candidate for the identity of the ptp1-1 allele.

To confirm that the mts4L636P mutation is the causative ptp1-1 mutation, we undertook a CRISPR editing approach. We performed editing of the mts4L636P mutation to revert it to wild-type (WT) sequence in one of the ptp1-1/ρ+ segregants obtained in the backcrosses. In parallel, we edited the mts4+ gene in an unrelated strain to introduce the mts4L636P mutation. We then tested the parental strains and the edited strains for petite-positivity (Fig. 1D). The ptp1-1 strain reverted to mts4+ sequence lost petite-positivity, but the WT strain mutated to mts4L636P readily yielded ρ0 cells upon treatment with EtBr. These results conclusively identify mts4L636P as the causative ptp1-1 mutation.

mts4L636P is a partial loss of function mutation affecting the 19S proteasome subunit

The mts4 gene codes for a central component of the 19S regulatory subunit of the proteasome. It is an essential and conserved protein, known as Rpn1 in S. cerevisiae and PSMD2 in humans [26]. L636 is located in an alpha-helical region in the domain of 11 helix-turn-helix repeats that form two concentric toroids (Fig. 1A–C). A change to proline of a residue involved in an alpha helix is predicted to disrupt the secondary structure because the cyclic bond between the side chain and the nitrogen in the backbone can’t form hydrogen bonds with residues in the next turn, which likely causes a significant structural change to the protein. Alphafold [27] modeling of the WT and ptp1-1 mutated Mts4 protein predicts a kink in the alpha-helix where the ptp1-1 mutation is located (Fig. 1C). It is therefore likely that the mts4L636P mutation causes a loss of function for this protein by challenging its structural stability.

The original description of the ptp1-1 mutation left open the question of whether it represented a gain or a loss of function mutation because diploids could not be maintained in EtBr long-term cultures [6]. The mts4L636P mutants grow normally as ρ+ cells. Since proteasome function is essential, this indicates that the mutation is either a mild hypomorph or a gain of function mutant. We set out to investigate the molecular phenotype of the mts4L636P mutation to answer this question.

We directly measured the activity of the proteasome in total cellular extracts from WT and mts4L636P mutants with the fluorescent chymotrypsin-like protease substrate Suc-LLVY-AMC (Fig. 2A–C, Additional files 1, 2, 3). These experiments revealed a consistent and significant (p < 0.01, Student’s t test) decrease in MG132-sensitive peptidase activity of between 30 and 40% in the mts4L636P mutant extracts, both in rich media (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1) and in EtBr media (Fig. 2B, Additional file 2). Proteasome activity was similarly decreased in mts4L636P/ρ0 cells when compared with ρ0 cells obtained from the petite-enabling mutation atp3R282C [18] (Fig. 2C, Additional file 3). These results indicate that the activity of the 26S proteasome is compromised in the mts4L636P mutant. To assess the integrity of the 26S proteasome, we performed an in-gel activity assay after separation of the 26S and 20S subunits by native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Fig. 2D, Additional files 4, 5). This assay showed a decreased signal of the 26S proteasome in the mts4L636P mutant. Together, these results show that the mts4L636P mutation partially compromises the activity of the 19S regulatory subunit of the proteasome.

A temperature-sensitive allele of the mts3 component of the 19S subunit (mts3-1), confirmed to lose function at the restrictive temperature [26], exhibits a dramatic and rapid loss of viability when shifted to media without nitrogen that leads S. pombe to transition to G0 stage [28]. To test the viability of mts4L636P mutants in G0, we shifted it after growth to mid-logarithmic phase in rich media to media without nitrogen and maintained it in this media checking for viability by spreading on rich media at regular intervals (Fig. 2E, Additional file 6: Fig. S1, Additional file 7). Surprisingly, the viability of the mts4L636P mutants did not decrease even after prolonged culture and exceeded the viability of the WT after very long exposure to media without nitrogen.

The temperature sensitive mts3-1 exhibits an increase in mitophagy that degrades damaged mitochondria upon G0 arrest [28]. We tested whether the mitophagy pathway is involved in petite-positivity [29]. The mitochondrial outer membrane factor Atg43 is required for mitophagy by recruitment of Atg8 [30]. We crossed the mts4L636P mutation with the atg43-1 allele, which abrogates mitophagy, and tested ρ0 cell generation by treatment with EtBr. mts4L636P/atg43-1 double mutants generated ρ0 cells to levels similar to those of the mts4L636P single mutant, and the atg43-1 single mutant was petite-negative (Additional file 6: Fig. S2). We conclude that the process of mitophagy observed in a mutant with complete loss of proteasome function is not involved in petite-positivity in the mts4L636P mutant. Together, these results indicate that the partial loss of proteasome function mts4L636P causes a phenotype of improved mitochondrial function that is distinct from that of the mts3-1 mutant and independent of mitophagy.

ptp1-1 does not rescue mitochondrial membrane potential

Petite-negative yeasts can be rendered petite-positive through mutations in the F1-ATPase that facilitate ATP hydrolysis activity, which coupled to the electrogenic ATP/ADP exchange across the inner membrane can rescue ΔΨ and mitochondrial protein import. In fission yeast, one such mutation in the central stalk gamma subunit, atp3R282C, is located close to petite-enabling mutations in K. lactis (Additional file 6: Fig. S3) that cluster in the C-terminal alpha helix inserted deep within the α/β hexameric ring of the ATPase [18, 31]. In order to investigate the mechanism of ptp1-1 petite-positivity, we undertook a comparative characterization of the mts4L636P and atp3R282C mutations.

Both mutants grow normally in glucose-rich media. However, while mts4L636P mutants show normal growth in glycerol respiratory media, the atp3R282C mutant exhibited very slow growth (Fig. 3A). This indicates that the atp3R282C mutation, but not the mts4L636P mutation, directly affect the ATP synthase activity of the F1 subunit. Furthermore, ρ0 strains derived from mts4L636P/atp3R282C double mutants showed faster growth than ρ0 strains derived from either single mutant (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the mechanism that enables petite-positivity in mts4L636P is different from the direct effect of atp3R282C on F1-ATPase activity.

We then tested whether petite-positivity in the mts4L636P and atp3R282C mutants require F1-ATPase activity by knocking out atp2, the nuclear gene encoding the catalytic β subunit or the hexameric ring. Interestingly, both mutants lost petite-positivity after knockout of atp2, indicating that the mts4L636P mutation requires a functional F1-ATPase for survival in the absence of mtDNA (Fig. 3C).

To test the hypothesis that mts4L636P enables growth of ρ0 cells by rescuing ΔΨ, as suggested by its dependence on atp2, we directly measured the relative ∆Ψ in mts4L636P, atp3R282C, and mts4L636P/atp3R282C ρ0 cells with thε ΔΨsensitive fluorescent stain dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DiOC6) (Fig. 3D, Additional file 8). WT ρ+ cells exhibited robust staining of mitochondria. As expected from the previously described effect of analogous mutations in atp3, the ρ0 cells derived from atp3R282C mutant displayed significantly reduced but still detectable mitochondrial staining, with evidence of mitochondrial malformations. In contrast, ρ0 cells derived from an mts4L636P mutant exhibit very faint staining, significantly reduced from the levels observed in atp3R282C ρ0 cells, with barely detectable mitochondria that showed morphological defects similar to those of atp3R282C ρ0 cells. The ρ0 cells derived from mts4L636P/atp3R282C double mutants showed levels of staining similar to those of WT ρ+ cells, but with much higher variability, with mitochondrial staining and morphology similar to that of atp3R282C ρ0 cells.

These results show that the mts4L636P mutation supports survival of ρ0 cells despite a severely collapsed ∆Ψ. Together with the positive epistasis between mts4L636P and atp3R282C, this indicates that mts4L636P enables ρ0 mitochondrial function through a mechanism independent of the ΔΨ rescue observed in atp3R282C. However, the dependence of mts4L636P petite-positivity on atp2 establishes that F1-ATPase is needed in some capacity. To further investigate this dependence, we tested the petite-positivity of mts4L636P and atp3R282C mutants in combination with the atp2-R4-3 hypomorph mutation that retains ~ 25% ATP hydrolysis activity [32] (Fig. 3E). The atp3R282C mutant became petite-negative in combination with atp2-R4-3, consistent with a dependence on F1-ATPase activity to rescue ∆Ψ. But the mts4L636P/atp2-R4-3 double mutant remained petite-positive, albeit yielding lower frequencies of ρ0 colonies upon EtBr treatment. ρ0 cells derived from this double mutant showed DiOC6 staining levels similar to those of mts4L636P ρ0 cells (Fig. 3F, Additional file 9), indicating that the atp2-R4-3 mutation does not further decrease ∆Ψ.

Together, these results indicate that the mts4L636P and the atp3R282C mutations confer petite-positivity through distinct mechanisms. The atp3R282C mutant supports ρ0 survival by rescuing ∆Ψ via reversed F1-ATPase activity, whereas mts4L636P ρ0 show a severely reduced ∆Ψ and rely only partially on F1-ATPase for their survival.

Proteomic survey of the mts4L636P mutant

To assess the consequences of the partial loss of the 26S proteasome in cells challenged for mitochondrial function, we performed whole-proteome characterization of WT and mts4L636P mutant cells grown in media with EtBr, and in ρ0 cells derived from atp3R282C and mts4L636P mutants. The liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) results detected peptides from over 3400 proteins in both comparisons, providing a wide survey of protein levels in these conditions [33] (Fig. 4 and Additional file 10, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Proteins showing concordant and statistically significant changes in both comparisons are labeled and colored in red for chaperones and in blue for detoxification factors. Venn diagrams state numbers of statistically significant upregulated and downregulated factors in both comparisons.

The identity of the altered proteins in the WT vs. mts4L636P in EtBr comparison did not show significant enrichment in any Gene Ontologies (GO) (Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 3). Of note, the abundances of Mts4, together with that of its two other 19S submodule assembly partners Rpt1 and Rpt2, are decreased in the mts4L636P mutant (Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 1), indicating that the effect of the mutation challenges the stability of the protein at the level of proteasome assembly [34, 35]. The comparison between ρ0 cells derived from atp3R282C and mts4L636P revealed stronger differences and significant association of altered factors with multiple Gene Ontology annotations (Additional file 10, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3), supporting the hypothesis that the two types of ρ0 cells engage different mechanisms to survive the loss of mtDNA. The atp3R282C ρ0 cells had increased abundance of mitochondrial factors involved in respiration and ATP metabolism, consistent with role of these processes in ∆Ψ rescue in this mutant (Additional file 10, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, mts4L636P ρ0 cells showεd increased abundance of factors involved in the response to cellular stress (ibid.). Notably, there was a significant enrichment for genes controlled by the transcription factor Pap1, which is in turn directly regulated by the proteasome [36] and by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [37].

To distinguish the consequences of mitochondrial disfunction from the effect of the proteasome defect, we combined the two comparisons to obtain lists of factors concordantly upregulated or downregulated in the mts4L636P mutant in both conditions (Fig. 4, Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 3). This approach yielded a limited list (6 factors downregulated and 11 factors upregulated). The downregulated factors showed no significant association with any GO terms or gene regulation groups (Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 3), but the upregulated factors were significantly associated with GO terms related to the unfolded protein response (UPR), due to the presence of the Hsp104 and Hsp78 cytoplasmic and mitochondrial (respectively) ClpB type disaggregases and the cytoplasmic chaperone Ssa1. In addition, the list of upregulated factors shows significant association with clusters of genes regulated by oxidative stress, represented by the indicated chaperones and factors involved in detoxification. These results support the conclusion that the mts4L636P mutation enables mitochondrial function in ρ0 cells via a mechanism distinct from that of the atp3R282C mutant, through increased expression of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial chaperones and cellular detoxification factors regulated by oxidative stress.

Involvement of oxidative stress regulation in petite-positivity

The disfunction of the ETC upon loss of mtDNA is expected to generate superoxide ions and hydrogen peroxide by electron leakage from the redox centers. Since genes implicated in cellular detoxification were upregulated in the mts4L636P mutant, we wondered if petite-positivity in this mutant was a result of an altered response to oxidative stress. In support of this possibility, the 19S proteasome mutant mts3-1 exhibits increased glutathione levels upon shift to media without nitrogen.

To test this hypothesis, first we performed a metabolomic analysis of the WT and mts4L636P mutant grown in EtBr media to evaluate reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels [38]. The results show that the mts4L636P mutant shows decreased levels of GSH and elevated levels of GSSG (Fig. 5A, B), indicative of an altered response to oxidative stress. We then tested whether interfering with oxidative stress with the ROS scavenger N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) affects petite positivity (Fig. 5C). We observed that including NAC in the EtBr media had dose-dependent but opposite effects on the WT and mts4L636P mutant: while NAC enabled survival of ρ+ cells in the WT strain, likely owing to its protective effects upon mitochondrial dysfunction induced by EtBr, in the mts4L636P mutant it causes a decrease in the frequency and growth of ρ0 colonies. We conclude that the altered oxidative stress response observed in mts4L636P participates in the mechanism of petite-positivity in this mutant.

Discussion

After 75 years since the original description of petite-mutants, the mechanisms governing survival in the absence of mtDNA are still unclear. The current consensus posits that ρ0 cells can support mitochondrial function through maintenance of ∆Ψ without a functional ETC [10, 17]. This outcome can be achieved by some species naturally, and by others through loss of regulatory pathways resulting in the increase of F1-ATPase ATP hydrolyzation activity [16].

In this work, we show that fission yeast can achieve petite-positivity through partial loss of 26S proteasome activity. A previous large-scale genetic study, focusing on the suppression of gene essentiality in fission yeast, uncovered that interventions that affect the proteasome, through mutation of mts4 or overexpression of multiple proteasome-related factors, could bypass the lethality of defects in mitochondrial translation and support the survival of ρ0 cells [18]. Our results confirm and extend this observation.

The Mts4L636P mutation is located in helix 33 of the toroid that binds ubiquitinated substrates (Fig. 1B), and being a L to P mutation likely disrupts the local alpha-helical structure through loss of the hydrogen bond of the amide. Li et al. isolated another mutation in mts4, mts4S412F, located in helix 4 of the toroid (Fig. 1B) [18], that also provides petite-positivity. Neither of these mutations directly affect the two ubiquitin binding sites identified in S. cerevisiae Rpn1 [39], but both are predicted to dramatically affect the structure of the protein. These mutations likely destabilize the protein though misfolding. In this context, it is interesting to note that the mts4L636P mutation resulted in a significant decrease in the abundance of Mts4 together with Rpt1 and Rpt2 upon EtBr treatment. In S. cerevisiae, these three components of the 19S proteasome subunit are assembled together as a module during proteasome maturation by the chaperone Hsm3 [34, 35]. The concurrent decrease in abundance of all components of this module indicates that degradation of Mts4/Rpt1/Rpt2 likely occurs at the step of proteasome assembly.

Previous work in S. cerevisiae and in petite-enabled mutants of K. lactis [11, 31, 40] has shown that the main pathway for petite-positivity is the reversal of F1-ATPase activity coupled to electrogenic ATP/ADP transport across the inner membrane to rescue ∆Ψ. Comparison of the genetic dependencies of petite positivity in mts4L636P and atp3R282C mutants, and the characterization of ρ0 cells derived from these two backgrounds suggest that mts4L636P enables survival of mtDNA loss through a different mechanism, independent of ∆Ψ. mts4L636P ρ0 cells exhibit a severely reduced ΔΨ αnd are less sensitive than the atp3R282C mutant ρ0 to decreased Atp2 ATPase activity. This phenomenon has been previously observed in S. cerevisiae, where mutations in the 19S subunits Rpt6 and Rpn1 (the Mts4 homolog) restored petite-positivity in an ATP2 hypomorph [41]. Nevertheless, complete knockout of atp2 eliminates petite-positivity in both atp3R282C and mts4L636P backgrounds, indicating that the WT F1-ATPase might be necessary to maintain a minimum level of ∆Ψ even in mts4L636P ρ0 cells. While we could not detect a further decrease of ΔΨ in ρ0 mts4L636P/atp2-R4-3 cells, it is possible that the already very decreased DiOC6 fluorescence levels cannot provide sufficient sensitivity to evaluate the effect of the decreased ATPase activity of the atp2-R4-3 mutation on ∆Ψ. Alternatively, the F1-ATPase could provide functions unrelated to ΔΨ maintenance but needed for the survival of mts4L636P ρ0 cells.

If not ΔΨ rescue, what is the mechanism of petite-positivity in the mts4L636P mutant? The main function of the 26S proteasome is the degradation of ubiquitinated substrates [22]. Loss of 26S proteasome through disfunction of the 19S subunit would result in accumulation of multiple factors regulated through this pathway. It is possible that petite-positivity is achieved by accumulation of one or multiple protective factors normally degraded by the proteasome. Alternatively, a general loss of proteasome function could drive a proteotoxic stress response that improves mitochondrial function. Proteomic characterization of the mutant reveals multiple candidates as possible effectors for these models.

A comparison of the proteomic profiles in mts4L636P during growth in EtBr and in ρ0 cells with that of the mts3-1 temperature sensitive allele upon switch to nitrogen-free media [28] indicates that the partial loss of activity in mts4L636P has a much milder effect on the proteome, consistent with the partial loss of function in this mutant. The dramatic decrease of mitochondrial protein abundance observed in mts3-1, owing to increased mitophagy [28], is not observable in mts4L636P. Consistently, we observed that mitophagy is not necessary for petite-positivity. Another difference between the effect of these two alleles is that while mts3-1 rapidly loses viability upon a switch to G0, mts4L636P exhibits increased viability over WT after prolonged growth in nitrogen-free media. This might be due to exhaustion of glucose from the media, which could result in increased respiration and mitochondrial stress, revealing the beneficial effects of decreased but not abrogated proteasome function in these conditions.

One aspect where both mts3-1 and mts4L636P coincide is the altered oxidative stress response, albeit in different ways. mts3-1 and mts4L636P exhibit upregulation of multiple detoxification enzymes, with the nitric oxide dioxygenase Yhb1 present in both cases, although at very different levels [28, 42]. Glutathione accumulates to high levels in mts3-1, whereas mts4L636P exhibits a shift from reduced to oxidized glutathione. The proteasome directly regulates oxidative stress responses by controlling the stability of the transcription factor Pap1 [36], and this protein is significantly elevated in the proteomic profile of the mts4L636P ρ0 cells. Interestingly, Pap1 nuclear localization is itself regulated by ROS levels through the formation of a disulfide bridge [37]. Decreased degradation of Pap1 in the mts4L636P mutant may contribute to the altered oxidative stress response, and the implication of ROS on Pap1 nuclear localization may explain the dependence of petite-positivity on ROS revealed by NAC treatment. Alternatively, oxidative stress can regulate proteasome function by direct glutathionylation of the 20S subunit [43], which becomes more efficient in its function to degrade oxidized and misfolded proteins independently of ATP and the 19S subunit [44]. Since the extreme mitochondrial stress resulting from loss of the mtDNA would decrease cellular ATP levels, increase ROS, and result in the cytoplasmic accumulation of mitochondrial protein precursors, such a scenario could provide a possible mechanism for petite-positivity. Testing this possibility will require measuring the activity of the proteasome with oxidized protein substrates.

Another set of factors that show upregulation in both mts3-1 and mts4L636P, providing possible candidates for the proximal effectors of petite-positivity, are cytoplasmic and mitochondrial chaperones. Of particular interest are Hsp78 and Hsp104, the mitochondrial and cytoplasmic versions, respectively, of the AAA+ ClpB disaggregases conserved in fungi. Their upregulation in mts4L636P mutants both upon EtBr treatment and in ρ0 state could be the result of the general stress response observed in this mutant, or it could be due to an UPR response to the cytoplasmic accumulation of mitochondrial protein precursors caused by the import defect due to ΔΨ collapse [24] and exacerbated by proteasome dysfunction. Hsp104 and Hsp78 share mitochondrial protein clients [45], and it has been shown that Hsp104 and the proteasome collaborate to dissolve and degrade cytoplasmic aggregates of mitochondrial proteins [46, 47]. Recent work has introduced the concept of “Mitostores,” cytoplasmic storage granules of mitochondrial proteins maintained by Hsp104 that prevent their proteotoxic effects in situations where mitochondrial protein import is compromised [46, 47]. Indeed, mutants that lose proteasome activation survive such stress better that WT cells with a mechanism dependent on Hsp104 [47]. The survival of mts4L636P ρ0 cells could be analogous to this phenomenon.

Conclusions

We conclude that proteasome dysfunction enables petite-positivity in fission yeast through a mechanism independent of ∆Ψ. These results generalize observations made in other organisms that suggest that partially decreased proteasome activity can be beneficial to mitochondrial function and contribute insight to the mechanism of this universal phenomenon [48]. The wide involvement of the proteasome in virtually every cellular process makes it difficult to establish whether specific proteasome-degraded factors, or a stress response resulting from general loss of proteasome function, or a combination of both, mediate petite-positivity. This study sets the stage for further investigation of this phenomenon, which could have profound implications in our understanding of mitochondrial biology and its consequences on aging.

Methods

Strains and genetic crosses

All strains used in this study are detailed in Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 4. All oligonucleotides used in this study are detailed in Additional file 10, Supplementary Table 5. To characterize the ptp1-1 mutation, the PHP14 strain [6] was first crossed with a ρ+ strain, and the segregants obtained by random spore analysis were subjected to the EtBr treatment described below to identify ptp1-1 ρ0 strains. The cross was repeated twice more. Asci from the final cross were dissected to obtain full tetrads, and the segregants were phenotyped for petite-positivity. Two full tetrads showing 2:2 segregation of petite-positivity were chosen for full genome resequencing. Gene deletion was performed by generation of KanMX6 transforming fragments with oligonucleotides detailed in Additional file 10 Supplementary Table 5 and plasmid pFA6Kan. CRISPR mutagenesis was performed as previously described [49]. The CRISPR plasmid for editing the ptp1-1 mutation was created via PCR using anti WT primers (oM2631/oM2632) to insert the gRNA targeting sequence site into plasmid pMZ374 (adh1Cas9-pgRNA ura4). Similarly, the CRISPR plasmid for reverting from ptp1-1 back to WT was created via PCR using anti ptp1-1 primers (oM2633/oM2634) to insert into plasmid pMZ377 (adh1Cas9-pgRNA LEU2). Plasmid transformation was carried out with the Lithium Acetate method using 600 ng of plasmid (containing Cas9 and the gRNA expression cassette) and 400 ng of Homologous Recombination donor. The cells were plated onto selection media and grown for a week at 32 °C. Small colonies (representing Cas9-expressing survivors) were restreaked on selective media and genotyped by DCAPS PCR (oligonucleotides oM2643/2644 followed by digestion with ScaI). The target region was amplified (oM2645/2646) from mutant candidates and subjected to Sanger sequencing to confirm the intended mutations. The atp3R282C mutation proved recalcitrant to CRISPR mutagenesis and was reproduced instead with the pop-in-pop-out method [50]. Briefly, two fragments of atp3 containing the C744T mutation were amplified with primers oM2688/oM2689 and oM2690/oM2691 and cloned by the Gibson method into an insertional plasmid containing the ura4 gene. This plasmid was digested with ClaI and transformed into strain FWP16, followed by selection on plates lacking uracil. Transformants were then grown to saturation in rich media and plated on Yeast nitrogen base plates supplemented with 40 mg/L of uracil and 1 g/L of 5-Fluoroorotic acid to select for recombination of the tandem atp3 copies, and then genotyped by DCAPS PCR (amplification with primers oM2686/oM2687 followed by digestion with HpaI). The atp2-R4-3 mutant was obtained by transformation of the Δαtp2::KαnMX6 knockout with a transforming fragment produced by PCR containing the atp2Q214Y mutation [32] followed by plating on YE-glycerol media, and confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Ethidium bromide petite-positivity assay

To assay strains for petite-positivity, 5 ml cultures of YES supplemented with 12.5 μg/ml EtBr and 2% potassium acetate [6] are inoculated with ρ+ cells and left to incubate at 30 °C until saturated (up to 2 days). Further, 150 μl of saturated culture are used to inoculate 5 ml of the same media and incubated for up to 5 more days (total 1 week in EtBr media). At this point in the treatment, the near totality of cells in the culture have lost mtDNA. The cells are centrifuged, resuspended in 200 μl of media, and plated onto YES with 3% glucose and incubated at 32 °C. Fast-growing colonies representing ρ+ cells that have survived EtBr treatment appear within a few days. In petite-positive strains the much more numerous and slower growing petite colonies appear after about 1–2 weeks of incubation, while petite-negative strains show no further growth. To confirm their identity as ρ0, the slow-growing colonies were restreaked in fermentation (YES with 3% glucose) and in respiration (YES with 3% glycerol and 0.1% glucose) media. Petite respiratory negative colonies were confirmed as ρ0 by colony PCR with primers amplifying the atp6 gene (oM2526/oM2527).

DNA purification

Cells were grown to saturation in rich media, and 50OD (approximately 5e8 cells) was used for genomic DNA extraction with the NEB monarch gDNA extraction kit, with the following modifications: The cells were first washed twice in water, and then resuspended in CSE buffer (150 mM Citrate buffer pH5.4, 1.2 M Sorbitol, 10 mM EDTA) with 0.4 mg/ml Zymolyase 100 T, and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min to obtain spheroplasts. Spheroplasts were pelleted and resuspended in 500 μl of 5 × TE, lysed by the addition of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) to a final concentration of 1%, and incubated at 50 °C for 1 h. The lysates were cooled in ice, precipitated by addition of 175 μl of 5 M potassium acetate, and further incubated in ice for 15 min. After centrifugation at 18,000 g, the supernatant was pipetted to fresh tubes through a piece of miracloth, and nucleic acids were precipitated by addition of 500 μl cold isopropanol, incubation at − 20 °C for 30 min and centrifugation for 30 min at 18,000 g. The pellets were washed in 70% ethanol, resuspended in 200 μl 5 × TE with 100 μg/ml RNAseA and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then 6 μl of 10% SDS and 40 μg Proteinase K were added, followed by 50 °C incubation for 60 min. After that, the extract was mixed with 400 μl NEB monarch kit binding buffer and loaded onto a Monarch gDNA purification column. DNA purification from this point on followed manufacturer’s recommendations.

High throughput sequencing

Approximately between 1 and 4 μg of genomic DNA was first sheared to a target size of around 150 bp in a microTUBE AFA fiber pre-slit snap-cap Covaris tube in the Covaris M220 focused ultrasonicator (150 W Peak Incident Power, 10% duty Cycle, 200 cycles per burst, 430 s total time). The DNA was then purified with 1.8 volumes of MagBio HighPrep magnetic beads. Then, 1 μg of sheared DNA was used to prepare barcoded Libraries using the NEB ultra II DNA Library prep kit, pooled and sequenced in a NextSeq 500 2 × 75 flow cell.

Sequence analysis

The fastq files were analyzed for polymorphisms using the CrossBow 1.2.1 pipeline [51] using the S. pombe ASM294v2 assembly as a reference. The SOAPsnp output generated by Crossbow was analyzed for polymorphisms present in the petite-positive segregants and absent in the petite-negative segregants.

G0 survival

To analyze the survival on G0, prototrophic WT and ptp1-1 mutants cells grown in 5 ml of Edinburgh minimal media (EMM) media with 3% glucose to a cell density of 5e6 cells/ml, harvested by centrifugation and washed 3 times in G0 media (EMM media without ammonium chloride and 3% glucose), then resuspended in the original volume of culture in G0 media, and returned to shaking at 32 °C. Samples were taken before and at 48-h intervals after the G0 media switch, seeded at 1000 cells per plate in yeast extract (YE) agar plates, grown for 2 days at 32 °C, and counted for colony forming units (cfu). At certain time points, a 5 μl sample of the cultures was spread as the inoculum in YE plates, and cells were individually dissected from the inoculum and arranged in a 9 × 9 grid using an MSM400 tetrad dissector.

Proteasome activity assay

To directly assay proteasome activity, cultures were grown in YES or YES/2% KAc/EtBr media to 1e7 cells/ml density, and 4e8 cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed once in ice-cold water, and resuspended in 500 μl extraction buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 10 mM creatine phosphate, 70 units/ml Creatine Kinase, 4 mM ATP, 1/100 dilution of protease inhibitor cocktail IV (Millipore)). The cell suspension was vortexed with 500 μl of 0.5 mm Zirconia beads for 10 cycles of 1 min vortexing at 3000 rpm and 1 min cooling on ice. The bead/lysate mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, and 300 μl of clarified lysate was recovered, avoiding the precipitated Lipid layer. The lysate was further clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. Protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay along with a BSA standard, and the concentrations were normalized by addition of an appropriate volume of extraction buffer to the most concentrated samples.

The proteasome assay was carried out by mixing 50 μg of extract at 1 μg/μl concentration with 150 μl of assay buffer (final concentrations 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 50 μM Suc-LLVY-AMC substrate). Control reactions in the presence of 50 μM MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) were carried out in parallel. The reactions were performed in white 96-well plates in a Biotek Synergy HT plate reader at 32 °C. Fluorescence was monitored with excitation at 360 nm and emission at 460 nm, every 2 min for 2 h. Proteasome activity was determined by calculating the rate of MG132 sensitive fluorescence release by Linear regression over time. We performed 3 biological replicates of each strain or condition, repeated over 3 technical replicates, and differences were assessed with Student’s t test.

For native gel proteasome activity assay, 50 μg of extract was subjected to electrophoresis in 3.5% polyacrylamide gels as previously described [52], developed in 50 mM Tris pH7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, and 50 μM Suc-LLVY-AMC with or without 0.02% SDS (to reveal the normally inactive 20S subunit), and imaged in a Kodak GelLogic 1500 system with a 530DF100 filter.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Cells of the indicated genotypes were grown in rich media to mid-log phase (OD 0.5–1), harvested and resuspended to a cell density of 1e7 cs/ml in fresh media with 100 ng/ml 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DiOC6). The cells were incubated shaking at 32 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed once with phosphate-buffered saline, resuspended in the same buffer to a density of 5e8 cells/ml and spotted on EMM-2% agarose pads mounted on glass slides. The cells were imaged on bright field and on epifluorescence using an eGFP filter set, with equal acquisition parameters for all strains. The bright field channel images were analyzed with the Cellpose-SAM segmentation algorithm [53] with parameters –use_gpu –gpu_device mps –exclude_on_edges –save_rois, and the resulting region-of-interest files were used in conjunction with the green fluorescent channel images on the FIJI image analysis software. Artifactual segments and brightly stained dead cells were eliminated from analysis, and mean intensity measurements were obtained after background subtraction with a sliding paraboloid rolling ball algorithm with a 75μm rαdius. A minimum of 400 cells were measured in each strain. The mean flourescence per cell was square-root transformed and analyzed for differences with Mann–Whitney’s U test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Proteomics analysis

Three biological replicates each of WT and ptp1-1 mutants were grown in YES media with 2% potassium acetate and 12.5 μg/ml EtBr to a cell density of 1e7 cells/ml. Then, trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added from a 50% w/v solution in water to 2e8 cells of culture to a final concentration of 20% TCA, harvested by centrifugation and washed once in 20% TCA. Pellets were resuspended in 20% TCA, combined with 0.5 mm Zirconia beads and vortexed for 10 rounds of 1 min vortexing at 3000 rpm followed by 1 min cooling on ice. The lysates were recovered from the beads by piercing the bottom of the tube and spinning at 1500 g in a swing-bucket centrifuge, and the beads were washed with 500 μl of 20% TCA, combining the flowthrough. The precipitates from the lysate were recovered by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min. The pellet was washed twice in 100% acetone, then dried and sent for whole proteome analysis by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) separation, protease digestion of gel slices, and liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) to The Biological Mass Spectrometry Facility (BMSF), of Robert Wood Johnson Medical School and Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey (Piscataway, New Jersey). Statistical analysis of LC/MS peptide abundance data was reported as log2 ratios and significance calls (p values) with estimation of the false discovery rate (Strimmer’s LFDR). Significantly altered factors were analyzed for enrichment of functional properties with the AnGeLi tool [54] using Fisher exact tests with FDR correction with a 0.05 p value threshold.

Metabolomics

For Metabolomics analysis, an aliquot of the same cultures harvested for proteomics were processed as follows: 5e7 cells were harvested by filtration onto 0.45 μm pore, 47 mm diameter Nylon filters. The filters were then overlaid, cells face down, onto 1.2 ml of an ice-cold 40:40:20 mixture of Acetonitrile:Methanol:H2O2 in a 5 cm petri dish sitting on crushed ice. The mixture was neutralized by addition of 83 μl of 15% NH4HCO3, and incubated for 15 min on ice. The cells were washed onto the extraction buffer by pipetting over the surface of the filter, and then recovered from the petri dish into an Eppendorf tube. The extracts were then centrifuged for 15 min at 20,000 g and 4 °C, and 700 μl of the clarified supernatant was recovered and sent for positive and negative polarization LC/MS analysis to the Rutgers Cancer Institute Metabolomics Shared Resource (New Brunswick, New Jersey).
