Apolipoprotein C3 drives adverse cardiac remodeling in ischemic heart failure
Authors: Yufei Han, Yixue Zhao, Zihao Zhou, Changyun Liu, Jinxuan Chen, Guolin Miao, Ling Zhang, Yuhui Wang, Jianping Li, Wei Huang, Xunde Xian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic heart failure (IHF) is one of the leading causes of death in the world. Plasma apolipoprotein C3 (ApoC3) levels are significantly elevated in patients with heart failure and positively associated with the incidence of ischemic heart disease (IHD). However, the causal association between ApoC3 and IHD development is unclear.MethodsApoC3 expression changes were assessed in plasma from IHF patients/healthy donors and cardiac tissue from rodent models. 10-week-old male human ApoC3 transgenic (ApoC3Tg) mice, ApoC3 knockout (ApoC3−/−) mice, ApoC3−/− hamsters, and wild-type (WT) controls underwent left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation to establish IHF models 4 weeks post-MI. Echocardiography, biochemical assays, and histopathology were employed to investigate ApoC3’s role and regulatory mechanisms in MI-induced IHF.ResultsOverexpression of human ApoC3 in ApoC3Tg mice exacerbated IHF after MI surgery, characterized by cardiac hypertrophy with thinned ventricular wall thickness and decreased contractile function. Mechanistically, ApoC3 overexpression markedly upregulated its receptor TLR2 at the myocardial ischemic site, and activated the NF-κB pathway resulting in significant increases in inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis. Unfortunately, ApoC3 deficiency did not show an overt protective effect in mouse MI model. We subsequently introduced ApoC3−/− hamsters and found that unlike mouse model, plasma HDL levels were markedly higher in ApoC3−/−hamsters 4 weeks after surgery compared with WT hamsters cardiac remodeling and contractile function were significantly ameliorated with a reduction in TLR2 gene expression, eventually inhibiting inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in MI heart.ConclusionsApoC3 overexpression could activate cardiac TLR2/NF-κB to trigger the inflammation, oxidation, and apoptosis pathways, finally aggravating IHF in mice. Background

Heart Failure (HF) is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by significant morbidity and mortality and affects approximately 64 million people over the world [1]. Ischemic Heart Failure (IHF) is one of the most common types of HF, accounting for 40% −61% of HF [2], in which the adverse remodeling of the left ventricle (LV) after myocardial infarction (MI) is a key event that involves complex changes in LV structure and function [3, 4]. To our knowledge, the final progression to IHF is determined by the extent of the initial infarction and the adequacy of the repair process after MI [5]. Therefore, identification of new therapeutic targets to promote the healing and repair of damaged myocardium in IHF patients will be appreciated.

Apolipoprotein C3 (ApoC3), largely produced by the liver, is mainly carried on triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) [6, 7]. It has been well established that ApoC3 inhibits lipoprotein lipase (LPL)-mediated lipolysis of TRLs [8–10]. Null mutation or loss-of-function of ApoC3 gene reported in the human studies result in a reduction in plasma ApoC3 levels to generate a favorable plasma lipid profile with decreased triglyceride levels accompanied by increased HDL-C levels [11]. Volanesorsen, an antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) of ApoC3, is used to treat hypertriglyceridemia in adult patients with familial chylomicronemia syndrome [12, 13].

In addition to regulating lipid metabolism, ApoC3 modulates inflammation. As an ligand of Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), ApoC3 activates TLR2 and induces the activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway-related inflammation in renal mesangial cell, finally increases diabetic nephropathy [9].Several studies have showed that NF-κB pathway plays an important role in HF by activation of cardiac inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis [14].

Large-scale clinical studies demonstrate that plasma ApoC3 levels are significantly associated with the incidence of ischemic coronary heart disease, with most patients who develop HF [11, 15, 16]. Moreover, plasma ApoC3 levels in patients with HF are increased by 13.1 folds when compared with healthy populations [17]. These results suggest that ApoC3 is a risk factor for IHF. However, the causal association between ApoC3 and IHF development is unclear. Thus, the effects of ApoC3 on IHF and possible involved NF-κB pathway were explored in this study.

In this study, we found that ApoC3 overexpression aggravated IHF by activating the cardiac TLR2/NF-κB pathway with increased inflammation, oxidation, and apoptosis in mice. Although ApoC3 deficiency did not show an overt protective effect on IHF in mice, ApoC3−/− hamsters whose lipid metabolism is more similar to that of humans had a significant protective effect against IHF via the TLR2/NF-κB pathway [18].

Methods

Clinical study

The clinical investigation protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Peking University First Hospital (Beijing, China) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 189 participants were included in this study. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The population consisted of: 1. Post-acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 40% (n = 94). 2. Age- and sex-matched healthy controls without heart failure (n = 95). All participants were recruited from the Department of Cardiology, Peking University First Hospital (Beijing, China) between January 2010 and September 2023, as part of the longitudinal Follow-up Cohort Study on Chronic Cardiovascular Diseases (Project ID: 2021 Scientific Research 100). Blood samples were collected before coronary angiography and separated by centrifugation immediately and stored at −80 °C for future analysis. The plasma ApoC3 levels were tested using commercially available ELISA kit (Elabscience, China).

Animal experiments

Three-month-old male wild-type (WT), human ApoC3 transgenic (ApoC3Tg) and ApoC3 knockout (ApoC3−/−) mice, and three-month-old male wild-type and ApoC3−/−Syrian golden hamsters were used in this study. All mice and hamsters were maintained on a 12 h(h) light/dark cycle and given ad libitum access to a standard chow diet and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Research Advisory Committee of Peking University Health Science Center and followed the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care (NIH Publication, 8th Edition, 2011).

For the experiments using murine model, all animals were divided into six groups (n = 6–8): WT, ApoC3Tg, and ApoC3−/−with sham operation as control groups or with MI operation. Consistently, hamsters were divided into four groups (n = 6–8): WT and ApoC3−/− hamsters with or without MI operation. All MI groups of mouse and hamster were received left anterior descending coronary ligation for four weeks, whereas the sham group only received a sham operation. Echocardiography was performed before and four weeks after cardiac MI surgery. Animals were sacrificed four weeks after surgery. Heart tissues were dissected into border area with the ischemia and Remote region that was the distal part far from the ischemic site for further analysis (Suppl. 1 A).

Cell culture

Neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated from 3-day-old Sprague–Dawley rats as previously described [19]. Briefly, excised hearts were dissected into small pieces and underwent sequential enzymatic digestion cycles (6 min/cycle) in 5 mL of buffer [0.6 mg/mL Collagenase II (LS004176, Worthington Biochemical Corporation) and 1 mg/mL pancreatin (T8150, Solarbio) dissolved in sterile PBS]. NRCMs were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS (FS301-02, TransGen, China). To induce cardiomyocyte hypoxia, NRCMs were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum for 48–72 h. The medium was then changed to serum-free RPMI 1640 (11,879,020, Gibco, USA), and the cells were placed in a 37 °C airtight chamber saturated with 95% N2/5% CO2 for 9 h (the O2 concentration was < 0.1% (Type 5120, Ohmeda Oxygen Monitor)).

Plasmid construction and transfection

Plasmids were constructed using standard molecular cloning techniques. The coding sequence (excluding the stop codon) of human ApoC3 (NM_000040.3) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and inserted into the expression vector pcDNA3.1(Sangon Biotech, China). Cells cultured in 24-well plates were transfected with 0.5 μg plasmids per well using Lipo3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. 24 h after transfection with plasmid, the NRCMs were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum for 48 h.

siRNA transfection

siRNA against rat ApoC3 and TLR2 as well as scramble siRNA were designed and synthesized (Sangon Biotech, China). 50 nM of siRNA was transfected in NRCMs by using RNAiMax reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 24 h after transfection with siRNA, NRCMs were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum for 48 h. The siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Plasma lipid measurements

The mice were fasted for 4 h and the hamsters were fasted for 12 h, blood samples were taken from the retroorbital plexus, and plasma was separated from the blood by centrifugation for 10 min at 4000 rpm. The total plasma cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) levels were determined enzymatically using commercially available kits (Zhongsheng Beikong, Beijing). Plasma free fatty acid (FFA) levels were measured using a nonesterified fatty acid assay kit (Wako, Japan). After ApoB-containing lipoproteins were precipitated with 20% polyethylene glycol solution, HDL cholesterol (HDL-c) was measured with the aforementioned kit.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography using a Vinno 6VET device (Vinno Technology Co., Ltd, China) as previously described [20]. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (Baxter Healthcare Corp, New Providence, RI, USA) and then were placed on a warm pad in the supine position. Afterward, the animals were anesthetized with 1.0–1.5% isoflurane to stabilize the heart rate at 400–500 beats per minute. Two-dimensional parasternal long-axis views and short-axis views were collected at the level of the papillary muscle of the heart samples from the animals. The M-mode cursor was positioned perpendicular to the maximum LV dimension during end-diastole and systole. The following variables were measured digitally in the M-mode images: LVPWd (mm), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at diastolic phase; LVPWs (mm), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at systolic phase; LVEDV (mL), left ventricular end-diastolic volume; LVESV (mL), left ventricular end-systolic volume; The ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated from these parameters.

Myocardial infarction operation

3-month-old mice and hamsters were anesthetized by sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection and a rodent respirator (ALCV9A; Alcott Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used for ventilation. Following the skin incision, the heart was exposed by a left thoracotomy through the fourth or fifth intercostal space. After the pericardium was removed, the left anterior descending coronary (LAD) was occluded by a 6‐0 silk suture for four weeks. The myocardium blanching, ventricular dyskinesia, and elevation of the ST segment on the ECG confirmed effective coronary artery ligation (Suppl. 1B).

Assessment of infarction area

After sacrificing the animals, the heart was removed and frozen for 30 min at −20 °C, then cut into six slices, which were incubated in 1% triphenylte-trazolium-chloride (TTC, Sigma, St, Louis, MO, USA) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 10 min at 37 °C and then immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The infarct size (IS, white region) was differentiated by TTC staining from the non‐infarct AR (red region). Finally, the slices were ordered according to the base-to-apex direction and photographed digitally. ImageJ software (NIH, USA) was used to analyze the IS and AR regions of the heart. The result is presented as IS/(AR + IS) % for in vivo experiments.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from mouse myocardial tissues using RIPA solution, and the protein content was determined by a BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) as previously described [9]. The denatured proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE). ECL chemi-luminescence was used to detect the bands with an imaging system (molecular imager, ChemiDoc XRS, Bio-Rad, USA), and the densities of the bands were also determined semi-quantitatively using the same system. The examined protein levels were normalized to β-actin as a loading control. The results are represented as the ratios of the values of the experimental groups to those of the control group. Antibodies used in this study are summarized as follows: 32Ser-p-IκB (2859, CST, USA), IκB(4814, CST, USA), 536Ser-p-NF-κB (3033, CST, USA), NF-κB (T55034F, Abmart, China), TLR2 (ab16894, Abcam, USA), ApoC3 (YT5550, Immunoway, USA), β-actin (MABT825, Merck, GER).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), the concentration of RNA was measured using Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and first-strand cDNA was generated using the TransScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, China) [9]. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using Luminaris qPCR SuperMix (Life Technologies) primers and a Roche Light Cycler 480. Each experiment was duplicated and normalized to the housekeeping gene as previously described [20]. Detailed qPCR primers can be found in Supplementary Table 2. The results are represented by the ratio of the value to the control group value.

Myocardial histopathological analysis

The ischemic cardiac tissues were harvested for immunofluorescence staining. After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated with 30% sucrose solution, the tissues were embedded in OCT and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Subsequently, the slices were blocked with 7.5% normal goat serum for 1 h, incubated with the CD68 antibody (1:100 rabbit polyclonal IgG; BA3638, BOSTER, California, USA) at 4 °C overnight, then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies to visualize the staining. DAPI reagent was used to counterstain nuclei. The staining was observed, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope [21].

For Immunohistochemical analysis, cryosections were fixed (10 min) in cold 4% paraformaldehyde solution, rinsed (10 min) with PBS (0.1 M, pH 7.4) supplemented with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After an incubation with the blocking solution (PBS containing 10% goat serum) for 30 min, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1:400 rabbit anti-4HNE antibody (ab46545, Abcam) or 1:150 rabbit anti-VCAM-1 antibody (A01199, Boster) diluted in blocking solution, washed three times with PBS, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 37 °C, rinsed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin if needed and examined by light microscopy with diaminobenzidine (DAB) as chromogen. Dihydroethidium (DHE, Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Eugene, USA) staining determined the ROS levels in the ischemia myocardial area. Briefly, transverse cryosections (6 μm thick) of frozen hearts were fixed with acetone for 15 min and then washed with 0.01%PBS three times before DHE (5 μM) incubation for 30 min at room temperature. Fluorescence microscopy assessed DHE fluorescence [21].

The apoptosis levels of myocardial tissue were detected by the TUNEL kit (Roche Applied Science, 11,684,795,910) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [21].

Statistic analysis

All experiments and data analysis were conducted blinded. All data are presented as the means ± SEM. Statistical comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test or the Mann–Whitney U-test for nonparametric data. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for statistical analyses. A value of P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

NRCMs were exposed to hypoxia for 9 h, and we found that the ApoC3 gene expression levels were significantly increased under hypoxia condition (P < 0.01, Fig. 1B). ApoC3 gene expression levels were significantly upregulated in the border region of ischemia than in the sham group in WT mice (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C); For ApoC3Tg mice, both gene expression levels of endogenous mouse ApoC3 (mApoC3) and exogenous human ApoC3 (hApoC3) was significantly upregulated in the border region than in the sham group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1D). Finally, we observed a significant increase in ApoC3 protein expression in the border region of ischemia in WT mice after MI (P < 0.001, Fig. 1E and F). ApoC3 overexpression aggravated IHF in mice

The plasma lipid levels were consistent with the previous study in WT, ApoC3−/−and ApoC3Tg mice [9]. After four weeks of MI surgery in mice, there were no significant differences in plasma TG and TC levels between pre-MI and post-MI of all the three genotypes (Fig. 1G and H). However, plasma HDL levels were significantly decreased after MI in the WT mice (P < 0.05), but not in ApoC3−/− mice and ApoC3Tg mice (Fig. 1I). In addition, plasma FFA levels were elevated in ApoC3Tg mice compared with WT mice under sham conditions, while no difference was observed between ApoC3−/− and WT mice, and MI surgery did not further alter FFA levels in any genotype (Fig. 1J).

Compared to WT MI mice, ApoC3Tg MI mice displayed worsened cardiac performance with the reductions of EF% (27% vs.18%, P < 0.001), FS% (10% vs. 8%, P < 0.05) and LVPWS (P < 0.05), and with the increases of LVEDV (P < 0.05), LVESV (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A and B) at 4 weeks after MI surgery. These results of echocardiography showed the mice with MI surgery had systolic HF and ApoC3 overexpression aggravated it. The result of the whole heart images showed larger heart in ApoC3Tg MI mice with increased heart weight/tibia length ratio compared to WT MI mice (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C and D). The heart tissue cross-sections showed enlarged left ventricular cavity with thinner ventricular wall and increased fibrosis (Fig. 2E and F) in ApoC3Tg mice than in WT mice after 4 weeks after MI surgery. ApoC3 overexpression aggravated myocardial inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis by activation of TLR2/NF-κB in mice with IHF

The cardiac gene and protein expression levels of TLR2 were significantly upregulated in the ApoC3Tg MI mice than in WT MI mice (Fig. 3C and D). Overexpression of ApoC3 significantly increased the ratios of p-P65/P65 (P < 0.01) and p-IκB/IκB (P < 0.01) in MI groups, but not sham groups (Fig. 3D and E). These results indicated that TLR2/NF-κB pathway was activated by ApoC3 overexpression in the setting of IHF. Primary neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) have been extensively validated and widely used as a reliable in vitro model for investigating the molecular mechanisms of cardiac diseases, including IHF [22, 23]. Given that the fundamental signaling pathways mediating hypertrophic and inflammatory responses are highly conserved between rodent species [24, 25]. To further confirm this finding, we performed in vitro experiments using primary NRCMs. NRCMs were transfected with a plasmid expressing human ApoC3 and then subjected to hypoxic stimulation. Consistent with the in vivo observations, ApoC3 overexpression significantly upregulated TLR2 protein expression (P < 0.01) and increased the p-P65/P65 ratio (P < 0.01) under hypoxic conditions compared with controls (Fig. 3F and G). However, ApoC3 knockdown did not reduce TLR2 expression under either normoxic or hypoxic conditions (Suppl. 2A and B), consistent with the lack of protective effect observed in ApoC3−/− mice. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ApoC3 overexpression activates the TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, the result of immunofluorescence staining showed more macrophage infiltration in the border zone of ApoC3Tg MI mice compared to WT MI mice (Fig. 4A and B). Consistently, compare to WT MI mice, the gene expression levels of the inflammatory factors downstream of the NF-κB pathway, such as IL-1β (P < 0.001) and VCAM-1(P < 0.01), were increased significantly in both border and remote region in ApoC3Tg mice, while TNF-α (P < 0.05) and IL-6 (P < 0.01) were increased only in border region (Fig. 4C). 4-HNE immunohistochemistry staining of the heart showed that oxidative stress in ApoC3Tg MI mice was significantly higher than in the WT MI mice (Fig. 4D and E). The gene expression levels of oxidative stress factor NOX4 was increased and antioxidant factor NRF2 was decreased in ApoC3Tg MI mice compared to WT MI mice (Fig. 4F). TUNEL staining showed a significant increase of apoptosis body in the border region in ApoC3Tg MI mice compared to WT MI mice (P < 0.05, Fig. 4G and H). ApoC3 inactivation ameliorated IHF in hamsters

As ApoC3 deficiency did not show an overt protective effect in mouse MI model, we subsequently introduced ApoC3−/− hamsters whose lipid metabolism is more similar to that of humans.

In agreement with previous observation [26], ApoC3−/− hamsters showed a significant decrease in plasma TG levels without affecting plasma TC and HDL-C levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 5A to C); After four weeks of MI, unlike the ApoC3−/− mice, ApoC3−/− hamsters showed a significant increase in plasma HDL levels compared to WT hamsters (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C). In addition, plasma FFA levels did not differ between ApoC3−/− and WT hamsters under sham conditions, and MI surgery did not significantly alter FFA levels in either genotype (Fig. 5D). Discussion

In this study, we found that overexpression of human ApoC3 promoted IHF after MI in mice; however, inactivation of endogenous ApoC3 had no beneficial effect on the outcomes of IHF in mice, but protected against IHF in hamsters. Mechanistic studies discovered that ApoC3 activated canonical TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway to elicit cardiac inflammation, oxidation, and apoptosis, whereas consistently, ApoC3 gene deficiency inhibited the downstream events of cardiac TLR2/NF-κB signal pathway in hamsters, but not in mice. Taken together, our results herein provide the first evidence that ApoC3 is associated with the progression of IHF caused by MI in animal models.

In our study, we found that plasma ApoC3 levels were significantly elevated in patients with HF and the cardiac mRNA expression levels were also significantly increased in the mice after MI surgery (Fig. 1A and C). Consistently, ApoC3 protein expression in the ischemic myocardium was markedly upregulated in post-MI mice compared with sham-operated controls (Fig. 1E to F). Previous studies have been shown that ApoC3 is largely expressed in the liver [6]. The local effects of ApoC3 in heart under MI condition has not been well investigated. Interestingly, our in vitro data showed that ApoC3 mRNA and protein expression were detected in primary cardiomyocytes from neonatal rats, and both were significantly upregulated after 9 h of hypoxia (Fig. 1B and F). This suggested that cardiac-derived ApoC3 might participate in IHF progression. Future studies using conditional transgenic models are warranted to dissect the cell-autonomous role of cardiac-derived ApoC3 in IHF disease. In addition, the specific upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying the high expression of ApoC3 remain to be further explored.

As one of the main components of TRLs, ApoC3 can inhibit the clearance of circulating TG, leading to HTG. Elevated plasma TG levels have been reported to be positively associated with the risk of HF and heart coronary artery disease [27]. In vitro studies have shown that TRLs can directly stimulate monocytes to increase the expression of VCAM-1 and MCP-1 [28]. Recent studies have shown that lipid free ApoC3 can directly act as a pro-inflammatory factor to enhance the inflammatory response independent of plasma TG levels [9, 29]. In the present study, our results showed no significant change in plasma TG, TC, HDL and FFA levels before and after MI surgery in ApoC3 Tg mice (Fig. 1G to J), whereas ApoC3 was significantly up-regulated in the heart, suggesting that the effect of ApoC3 on IHF may not be directly related to changes in plasma lipid levels in this model. Notably, although plasma FFA levels were elevated in ApoC3 Tg mice under basal conditions, they did not further increase after MI, and ApoC3 deficiency did not alter FFA levels in either mice or hamsters. This suggests that FFAs are unlikely to mediate the cardiac effects of ApoC3 in our IHF model. Changes of plasma lipid levels caused by ApoC3 might not be the main initiator of IHF but rather contributes to IHF under some pathologic conditions because of no significant change in plasma lipid levels before and after MI surgery in ApoC3 Tg mice. Cardiac-specific ApoC3 overexpression mice model would be constructed for the future investigation.

Previous studies have been shown that ApoC3 could activated TLR2/NF-κB pathway in Mesangium cells [9] and in monocytes [30], exacerbate developments of diabetic nephropathy and atherosclerosis respectively. Our study demonstrated that ApoC3 overexpression, both in vivo and in vitro, activated the TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway and its downstream inflammatory response, ultimately aggravating IHF progression in mice (Fig. 3A to G). ApoC3 deficiency inhibited activation of the TLR2/NF-κB pathway in hamsters and had a protect effect on IHF (Fig. 7C). NF-κB is an important regulator of inflammation and plays a crucial role in IHF [31]. TLR2 is an endogenous receptor of ApoC3. It has been shown that TLR2 deficiency ameliorated cardiac remodeling after four weeks of MI and that inflammation prolonged myocardial damage [32]. TLR2 induced the activation of NF-κB signaling resulting in cardiac fibrosis [33]. These results indicated that TLR2/NF-κB activation played important role in the progression of cardiac disorder. Consistent with the previous studies, we found ApoC3 could activate TLR2/NF-κB pathway in the heart, leading to enhanced inflammatory response and eventually aggravating IHF.

Oxidative stress participates in the initiation and progression of heart failure [34]. In our study, ApoC3 overexpression caused the elevated 4-HNE and NOX4 expression levels, and decreased NRF2 expression levels in the injured heart of ApoC3Tg mice compared to WT mice at 4 weeks after MI (Fig. 4D to F). This suggested that the effects of ApoC3 on IHF could be associated with accelerated cardiac oxidative stress. Enhanced cardiac NRF2 signaling has been shown to significantly reduce adverse cardiac remodeling after MI [35]; ApoC3 can inhibit the AMPK-ACC-NRF2 pathway via TLR2 in skeletal muscle cells, thereby reducing NRF2 levels, enhancing cellular oxidative stress [36]. Inhibition of the excessive production of NOX4 in hypoxic cardiomyocytes in vitro can significantly reduce ROS levels and ameliorate cardiomyocyte apoptosis and necrosis [37]. In addition to affecting inflammation, the NF-κB pathway can also enhance oxidative stress in the heart after MI [14]. We speculated that the increase of oxidative stress by ApoC3 in the heart after MI may be due to TLR2/NF-κB activating.

It has been reported that ApoC3 could promote apoptosis of Jurkat T cells and increase the spread of oral squamous cell carcinoma [38]. Inhibition of ApoC3 could significantly inhibit the NF-κB pathway in mouse placental cells, decrease levels of downstream inflammation and apoptosis [39]. Nevertheless, the role of ApoC3 in promoting apoptosis in heart failure has not been reported. Similar to the observations described previously, we found that myocardial apoptosis was increased in ApoC3Tg mice compared to WT mice after MI (Fig. 4G and H), confirming that apoptosis could be one of the risk events of ApoC3-induced IHF.

Unfortunately, we did not find that ApoC3 deficiency had an obvious protective effect against MI-induced IHF in mice. Similar to the results of the other studies, overexpression of hApoC3 in mice significantly accelerated the developments of diabetic nephropathy [9] and restenosis and atherogenesis [40], but depletion of ApoC3 from mice did not found the protective effects. Human and mouse ApoC3 share only 62.9% homology [41], and lipid metabolism significantly differs between mouse and human. All of these reasons might result in different effects on IHF between ApoC3Tg and ApoC3−/− mice. The precise mechanisms need the further investigations.

Syrian golden hamsters exhibit lipid metabolism features that closely mirror those of humans, in contrast to mice and rats [18, 26, 42]. Their lipoprotein profile is remarkably similar to the human pattern: 1) like humans, hamsters have abundant VLDL and LDL in plasma; 2) similar to humans, hamsters express ApoB editing enzyme specifically in the intestine, while the liver expresses full-length ApoB100; 3) hamsters express both CETP and hepatic lipase, features not shared by other commonly used rodent models. We speculated that ApoC3−/− hamsters might be a suitable animal model to investigate the beneficial effects of ApoC3 deficiency on IHF. Consistent with the observations from hApoC3 Tg mice, deletion of ApoC3 in hamsters ameliorated the MI-induced IHF by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis through the inhibition of TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway. It suggested that ApoC3 could be a potential target for further investigation in the context of IHF.

We found in the present study that ApoC3−/− hamsters exhibited a favorable lipid profile with higher plasma HDL levels after MI surgery than WT hamsters (Fig. 5C), and this phenotype had not been discovered in mice. We speculated that, apart from the depletion of ApoC3 in the heart, elevated plasma HDL concentration could execute a protective effect in our case as well because HDL has been reported not only to have the function of regulating lipid metabolism, but also have anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidation properties [43–45]. ApoC3 is a crucial component of HDL and increasing lines of clinical evidence have shown that the percentage of ApoC3 in HDL is significantly associated with CHD risk [46]. It is hypothesized that abnormal distribution of ApoC3 in HDL can attenuate HDL function. In vitro studies have also shown that the anti-apoptotic ability of HDL in patients with coronary heart disease was impaired, mainly due to a significant increase in ApoC3 content [47]. Hamsters may be a suitable animal model for studying cardiovascular diseases.

Several open questions remain to be addressed in future studies. Cardiomyocyte-specific gain- and loss-of-function models are needed to dissect the respective contributions of cardiac-derived versus liver-derived ApoC3 to IHF pathogenesis. Further investigation into HDL function—including cholesterol efflux capacity and anti-inflammatory properties—in ApoC3-deficient hamsters will help determine whether the observed protective phenotype is mediated by enhanced HDL functionality. To elucidate the causal role of the TLR2/NF-κB pathway in ApoC3-mediated IHF, it is imperative to conduct both in vitro and in vivo experiments involving pharmacogenetic blockade of TLR2 or NF-κB. Collectively, these future investigations will be critical to clarifying the precise role of ApoC3 in IHF and evaluating its therapeutic potential.

Conclusions

In summary, we detected for the first time that ApoC3 overexpression aggravated the development of IHF with increased cardiac inflammation, oxidation and apoptosis through activating TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway in mice. Depletion of ApoC3 protected against IHF in hamsters, but not in mice. We suggested that ApoC3 could play a role in IHF and may be a potential target for further investigation.
