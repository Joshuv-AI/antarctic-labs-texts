Improving genomic prediction in pigs by integrating multi-population data and prior knowledge
Authors: Junliang Wang, Yujin Lu, Wenjing Zhang, Xiaodian Cai, Shuihua Xie, Yahui Gao, Jiaqi Li, Changguang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic selection (GS) has become an essential tool for improving economically important traits in pigs. However, its accuracy depends heavily on the size and composition of the reference population. This study explores strategies for optimizing multi-population genomic evaluations by integrating prior biological knowledge and leveraging advanced genomic models. We assessed population similarities based on phenotypic distribution, linkage disequilibrium (LD) consistency, heritability, and genetic variance. Three genomic prediction models—GBLUP, bivariate GBLUP, and GFBLUP—were applied to evaluate the joint reference populations. The results indicated that differences in phenotypic means and genetic variance between populations significantly affected the prediction accuracy of joint evaluations, particularly for fat thickness traits. The GFBLUP model, integrating meta-GWAS priors, improved prediction accuracy when the genetic contributions were similar between target and reference populations. These findings highlight the importance of carefully selecting reference populations and integrating biological priors into genomic evaluations. The study offers valuable insights for optimizing genomic selection strategies in pig breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12011-z.Keywords: Genomic selection, Yorkshire pig, Joint evaluation, Prior biological knowledge, Reference population

Introduction

Genomic selection (GS), or genomic prediction, has garnered significant attention since its introduction in 2001 [1]. Subsequent methodological advancements have led to the development of various models aimed at enhancing genomic prediction accuracy. These models include genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) [2], machine learning approach [3], single-step genomic best linear unbiased prediction (ssGBLUP) [4, 5], and models that incorporate prior information, such as GBLUP with trait-specific marker-derived relationship matrix (TABLUP) [6], GBLUP given genetic architecture (BLUP|GA) [7], and genomic feature best linear unbiased prediction (GFBLUP) [8], among others. The advent of high-throughput technologies, coupled with declining genotyping costs, has driven the widespread adoption of genomic selection in modern animal breeding programs [9].

In recent years, a wealth of studies have focused on GS for key economic traits in pigs, such as growth [10, 11], carcass [12, 13], and reproduction traits [14, 15]. However, the practical application of GS in pig breeding has not yet reached the desired level of effectiveness. Studies have shown that the predictive accuracy of GS is influenced by the size of the reference population. When the reference population is small, prediction performance is suboptimal [16, 17], whereas larger reference populations yield higher prediction accuracy [18]. Therefore, combining reference populations from different breeding companies for joint evaluation presents a cost-effective strategy for enhancing predictive accuracy [19]. Nevertheless, the performance of joint evaluations is influenced by multiple factors, and identifying the primary factors remains a challenge [20]. Furthermore, differences in phenotypic distribution, linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure, heritability, and genetic variance across populations may lead to the predictive accuracy of a combined reference population being lower than that of a single population.

As a widely used GS model, GBLUP assumes that the genetic effects of the same causal mutation are identical across different individuals and does not integrate prior biological knowledge of the trait’s genetic structure. However, the genetic effects of the same causal mutation may vary across populations [21], potentially influencing the accuracy of genomic joint evaluation predictions [20]. The multi-trait GBLUP model treats the same trait in different populations as distinct traits, thereby improving the prediction accuracy of genomic selection across multiple populations [20, 22]. In contrast, the GFBLUP model treats loci that affect traits as independent random effects, allowing for the estimation of genetic effects for different components [8]. By integrating prior biological information, the GFBLUP model provides improved GS prediction accuracy for combined populations compared to single-population evaluations [12, 23, 24]. Moreover, a meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (meta-GWAS) interprets SNP effects estimated in different GWAS as local sample estimates [25], thereby enabling the estimation of the overall true parameters based on the estimates from multiple local samples, which increases the likelihood of identifying loci with consistent effects across different populations. On this basis, joint evaluation using the GFBLUP model is expected to further improve prediction accuracy.

Yorkshire is an excellent maternal breed in pig production, renowned for its rapid growth rate, high lean meat percentage, and superior reproductive performance, making it extensively used in commercial breeding. Days to reach 100 kg body weight (DAYS_100) and average backfat thickness at 100 kg body weight (BFT_100) are two key economic traits in pig production, playing a critical role in evaluating and selecting for growth rate and lean meat percentage, respectively [26, 27]. Investigating breeding strategies for multi-population genomic joint evaluation using DAYS_100 and BFT_100 traits in Yorkshire pigs provides a representative approach.

The primary objective of this study is to enhance the prediction accuracy of genomic joint evaluation in pigs by integrating prior meta-GWAS information. We used nine genetically isolated Yorkshire pig populations, focusing on DAYS_100 and BFT_100 as target traits. We compared the joint evaluation performance of the GBLUP, bivariate GBLUP, and GFBLUP models across two populations and analyzed the impact of inter-population differences from various perspectives. Our goal is to optimize the breeding strategy for multi-population genomic joint evaluation in pigs and provide valuable insights for future research and applications.

Phenotypic records and genotyping information for the nine populations

Genotype data standardization was performed using the Pig Genomics Reference Panel (PGRP), which includes 1,602 pigs from the PigGTEx project [29], as the reference for imputation. Imputation was carried out using Beagle v5.1 [30] across the nine populations, followed by the quality control procedures outlined below: (1) Remove SNPs with imputation quality DR2 < 0.8; (2) Exclude SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 in any population; (3) Remove SNPs with a Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium test P-value < 10− 6 in any single population; (4) Exclude SNPs with MAF < 0.025 after merging populations; (5) Remove SNPs with LD r2 > 0.95 after merging populations. After imputation and quality control, the final number of SNPs across all populations was standardized to 345,354.

Additionally, summary statistics from a large-scale meta-GWAS [31] were used as biological priors for selecting genomic feature markers. The meta-GWAS included the P8 and P9 populations from our study. Population similarity analysis

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using PLINK v1.90 [33], and the proportion of variance explained by the first three principal components among the top 20 was calculated. A three-dimensional PCA plot was then generated using R v4.2.1.

LD consistency analysis

LD coefficients (r) between adjacent SNPs were calculated for each population using PLINK v1.90. The Pearson correlation of LD coefficients (r) between two populations was then computed. Finally, an LD heatmap was generated using the “pheatmap” package [34] in R v4.2.1.

Heritability estimation

Genetic variance components and heritability for each population were estimated using GCTA v1.94.1 [35], based on phenotypes adjusted for fixed effects and quality-controlled genotype data.

Joint evaluation results based on the GBLUP model

The P2 to P9 populations were used as external reference populations and sequentially combined with the P1 population to construct joint reference populations for the two-population joint evaluation using the GBLUP model. In the 50-fold cross-validation of the single-population GBLUP model, the average prediction accuracies for the DAYS_100 and BFT_100 traits in the P1 population were 0.171 and 0.239, respectively.

Prediction accuracy of the GBLUP model

Figure 3 displays the boxplot of prediction accuracy for the GBLUP model with different combined populations. For DAYS_100, the average prediction accuracy across combined populations ranged from 0.123 to 0.178, with the P1_P5 reference population yielding the highest accuracy. For BFT_100, the average prediction accuracy ranged from 0.169 to 0.243, with the P1_P4 reference population providing the best results. Compared to the single-population GBLUP model, joint evaluation using P1_P4 and P1_P5 reference populations resulted in improvements of 0.18% and 4.22% in the prediction accuracy for DAYS_100 in the P1 population, respectively. Additionally, the use of the P1_P4 reference population for joint evaluation increased the prediction accuracy for BFT_100 by 1.29%. However, these improvements did not reach statistical significance for either trait compared to the single-population GBLUP model.

Discussion

Selection criteria for reference populations in joint evaluation

Our findings indicate that reference population selection is a critical determinant of joint evaluation efficacy, while the size of the external reference population has a much smaller impact on evaluation performance. In fact, introducing an inappropriate reference population reduces model prediction accuracy, and the larger the reference population, the greater the decline in accuracy. For example, despite the P9 population being the largest, its prediction performance is generally poorer and, in some models, it is the least accurate compared to other populations. Previous studies have demonstrated that using reference populations with a certain level of genetic exchange can enhance prediction accuracy in joint genetic evaluations [36, 37]. Furthermore, incorporating crossbred populations with genetic relatedness into purebred populations can improve genetic prediction performance [38]. Additionally, it has been shown that when populations exhibit low genetic relatedness, the genomic prediction accuracy for the combined population tends to be similar to or lower than that of the single population [39–41]. In the present study, however, the nine Yorkshire pig populations did not exhibit genetic exchange, which may explain why most combined populations failed to improve prediction accuracy. Additionally, substantial differences in LD structure, phenotypic means, phenotypic variance, heritability, and genetic variance across these populations may have influenced the joint evaluation outcomes.

Gebreyesus et al. [23] applied the GFBLUP model for genomic joint evaluation of three Holstein dairy cattle populations with similar LD structures, resulting in a 13%, 23%, and 38% improvement in prediction accuracy for single-population. Similarly, Zhou et al. [42] found that selecting North European Holstein populations with LD consistency greater than 0.97 with Chinese Holstein populations led to significant increases in prediction accuracy for Chinese Holsteins. In contrast, our study found no significant correlation between the LD consistency of populations and prediction accuracy in joint evaluations. For instance, the LD consistency between P7, P6, and P1 was higher than that between P4, P5, and P1. However, when conducting a GBLUP joint evaluation for the DAYS_100 trait, the prediction accuracy for the P1_P7 and P1_P6 reference populations was lower than that of the P1 single population, while the P1_P4 and P1_P5 combinations exhibited higher prediction accuracy than the P1 single population. Furthermore, although the P4 population was more similar to the P1 population for the DAYS_100 trait in terms of phenotypic distribution, heritability, and genetic variance, the P1_P5 combination exhibited higher prediction accuracy than the P1_P4 combination using the GBLUP model. However, when using the GFBLUP_UCRG model, the P1_P4 reference population significantly enhanced the prediction accuracy for the DAYS_100 trait in the P1 population. These findings suggest that differences in LD structure or heritability may not be the primary factors influencing the success of multi-population genomic joint evaluations.

Applicability of different joint evaluation models

This study demonstrates that, compared to the GBLUP model, the bivariate GBLUP model for joint evaluation helps mitigate the impact of population differences on prediction accuracy, thereby reducing the variation in mean prediction accuracy across populations. However, the factors contributing to the reduction in prediction performance are multifaceted. In some combined reference populations, the bivariate model did not yield higher prediction accuracy compared to the single-population model.

When using the GFBLUP model for joint evaluation, incorporating prior information from both shared-effect and population-specific loci identified through meta-GWAS improves prediction accuracy. Using the GFBLUP_CRGM model with a P-value threshold of 1 × 10− 4, the P1_P4 reference population improved prediction accuracy for the DAYS_100 trait in the P1 population by 11.65%. Ye et al. [43] also demonstrated that the GFBLUP model enhances joint evaluation performance, specifically improving prediction accuracy for the startled response trait in male and female fruit flies by 27.40% and 35.36%, respectively.

Porto-Neto et al. [44] found that using a subset of SNPs with consistent effects across different populations can improve the prediction accuracy of joint evaluations. Similarly, we observed that when the genetic contributions of genomic marker sets are similar between the target population and the combined reference populations, the GFBLUP_UCRG model significantly improves prediction accuracy in joint evaluations. At a P-value threshold of 1 × 10− 4, the genetic enrichment values of feature marker sets for the P1 and P4 populations differed by only 0.6%, and the genetic enrichment value of the P1_P4 population closely matched that of the single population. This suggests that the genomic feature marker set likely shares similar genetic effects across the P1 and P4 populations, resulting in a 46.49% improvement in prediction accuracy for the DAYS_100 trait in the P1 population using the P1_P4 reference population. For the other populations, the genetic enrichment values of their genomic feature marker sets differ significantly from those of the P1 population, indicating that the genetic effects may also vary considerably, which leads to a notable decrease in prediction accuracy when using the GFBLUP_UCRG model.

Integrating biological priors for joint evaluation

Complex traits are influenced by various factors, and different populations may exhibit specific gene expressions due to gene-environment interactions and genetic background differences. Integrating biological priors to differentiate markers with consistent versus variable effects across populations could help mitigate the reduction in prediction accuracy when combining reference populations and may even improve the performance of genomic joint evaluations.

Previous studies have demonstrated that integrating prior information can enhance the prediction accuracy of GS. For example, Ye et al. [43] found in their study on fruit flies that, compared to using all markers in GBLUP, selecting key markers based on eQTL mapping improved the prediction accuracy of the startled response trait by 10.78% in females and 19.89% in males. Liu et al. [12] applied the GFBLUP model using feature markers identified through GWAS to improve the prediction accuracy for loin muscle area in Yorkshire pigs by 4.80%. Other studies have also confirmed that integrating biological priors can enhance the accuracy of GS predictions [11, 13, 45–47].

The results of this study further indicate that integrating meta-GWAS priors into the GFBLUP model can improve the prediction accuracy of genomic joint evaluations. For the DAYS_100 trait, at a threshold of 1 × 10− 4, the GFBLUP_CRGM model improved prediction accuracy for the P1 population when using the P1_P2, P1_P4, and P1_P5 reference populations. Moreover, the GFBLUP_UCRG model further enhanced prediction accuracy for the P1 population when using the P1_P2 and P1_P4 reference populations. However, in most combined populations, when the GFBLUP_CRGM model did not improve prediction accuracy, the GFBLUP_UCRG model performed worse.

Additionally, the genetic enrichment results indicate that the feature markers selected from large meta-GWAS summary statistics have a high genetic contribution in most populations. Moreover, population-specific effect loci may play an important role in influencing joint evaluations. However, for the BFT_100 trait, the genetic enrichment values for feature marker sets at various thresholds in the P1 population were relatively low. This suggests that the genomic feature marker set for the BFT_100 trait has a smaller genetic contribution to the P1 population, which may explain why the GFBLUP model did not improve the prediction accuracy for this trait in P1.

Conclusions

By comparing the genomic joint evaluation performance of three models across two populations, we found that selecting an appropriate reference population enables both the bivariate GBLUP model and the GFBLUP model, with the latter incorporating meta-GWAS priors, to improve the prediction accuracy of joint evaluations to some extent. Notably, selecting an appropriate reference population enables the GFBLUP model to significantly enhance prediction accuracy, and when an appropriate SNP selection threshold is applied, it can lead to even greater improvement. However, due to the lack of genetic exchange among the nine Yorkshire pig populations used in this study, the joint evaluation performance of most combined populations was suboptimal. Future implementation strategies should prioritize: (a) enhanced genetic connectivity between breeding stocks and (b) precise identification of markers with conserved and population-specific effects. This approach is expected to significantly improve prediction accuracy when using the GFBLUP model for joint evaluations.
