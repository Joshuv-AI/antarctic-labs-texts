Is there an association between fear of movement and pain intensity and disability in people with chronic neck pain? a systematic review
Authors: Norollah Javdaneh, Roohollah Mohammadi Mirzaei, Arash Shams, AmirHossein Barati, Sadredin Shojaedin, Tadeusz Ambroży, Mateusz Stefanek, Łukasz Rydzik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFear of movement is considered a strong psychological predictor of chronic pain and disability. The purpose of this study was to systematically review the association between fear of movement, pain intensity, and disability in patients with chronic neck pain.MethodsA systematic review of the literature, including an appraisal of the risk of bias using the adapted Newcastle - Ottawa Scale (NOS) was performed. PubMed, AMED, CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubPsych, and the grey literature were searched from January 1950 to October 2023. Observational studies reporting cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between fear of movement and pain intensity and disability in people with chronic neck pain were selected. This review included studies that used the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK) to measure fear of movement and the Neck Disability Index (NDI) to measure disability. GRADE criteria were used to evaluate the overall quality and strength of evidence in terms of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.ResultsFifteen articles (1 longitudinal study, 14 cross-sectional studies, total sample = 1371) were included. Based on the NOS, of the 15 studies examined, 12 (80%) were found to have a moderate risk of bias, while the remaining 3 (20%) had a low risk. Evidence has shown that there is an association between higher levels of fear of movement with pain intensity and greater levels of disability. 9 studies showed a significant association, and 2 studies showed a non-significant relationship between fear of movement and disability. Eight studies showed a significant association, and three studies showed a non-significant association between fear of movement and pain intensity in patients with chronic neck pain. However, the quality and strength of the evidence was low according to the GRADE criteria. The quality of the evidence was low due to methodological limitations (predominantly cross-sectional design).ConclusionsGreater fear of movement was associated with greater disability and pain intensity. The results of this study can help psychotherapist and psychotherapist in planning and designing therapeutic interventions considering the factor of fear of movement. The number of prospective longitudinal studies is very small, thus, the prognosis could not be determined. Therefore, more longitudinal cohort studies that analyze the predictive value of the fear of movement in the chronic neck pain are needed. Prospero registration number: CRD42023474724.Keywords: Chronic Pain, Neck Pain, Kinesiophobia, Pain Measurement, Fear

Background

Chronic neck pain (CNP) is a debilitating disease that can interfere with daily life and reduce productivity and quality of life [1]. The estimated 1- year incidence of neck pain from available studies ranges between 10.4% and 21.3% with a higher incidence noted in office and computer workers [2]. Approximately 14–16% of the adult population worldwide suffers from neck pain [3], with an average lifetime prevalence of 48.5% [3].

CNP arises from various causes, including psychological factors [4]. Psychological risk factors, such as long-term stress, lack of social support, anxiety, and depression are important risk factors for neck pain [4]. In terms of the biological risks, neck pain might occur as a consequence of certain diseases, such as neuromusculoskeletal disorders or autoimmune diseases [4]. There is also evidence that demographic characteristics, such as age and sex, can influence the prevalence and development of neck pain, although further research is needed [4]. Previous research has also shown that some cognitive factors are associated with higher levels of pain and disability in patients with CNP [5]. Psychological risk factors, such as depression, stress, anxiety, and lack of social support, are important risk factors for neck pain [6]. Chronic pain leads to avoidance behaviors and has been shown to be strongly associated with fear of movement [7]. Fear of movement describes a condition in which a patient has an unwarranted fear of physical motion, which is due to fear of painful injury or reinjure [7]. This condition is characterized by an increased likelihood of the negative aspects of pain, an inability to detach from stressful thoughts, and a feeling of helplessness when dealing with pain [8]. In long-lasting pain, as patients become trapped in a vicious cycle that maintains their chronic pain and disability, the problem paradoxically worsens, worsening their disability and pain perception threshold [9]. Fear of movement is one of the main and important factors in the chronicity of acute pain [10]. According to the fear-avoidance model, physical activity can be limited as a result of chronic pain, which can lead to a cycle of increased pain limitations, decreased participation, and disability [11].

Fear of movement causes avoidance behavior, which can lead to overexcitement of bodily sensations, It leads to a person being trapped in a vicious cycle of fear and inactivity [12]. A study of CNP patients revealed that patients with pain-related fear experienced more pain and disability [13]. Pain-related fear, fear avoidance beliefs, fear of movement and kinesiophobia are fear-related concepts in relation to pain [14]. Fear of movement changes the way people move to avoid pain, which affects the behavioral mechanisms related to pain control and disorders related to it [15]. Some studies have reported a positive relationship between fear of movement and pain and disability in people with chronic neck pain [16, 17]. On the contrary, some studies have reported no relationship between fear of movement and pain intensity and disability in people with chronic neck pain [5, 18].

Although kinesiophobia is a core component of the fear-avoidance model, it is distinct from broader fear-avoidance beliefs (often measured by the Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire), which encompass work and activity related avoidance cognitions, and from pain catastrophizing (assessed via the Pain Catastrophizing Scale), characterized by rumination, magnification, and helplessness. Kinesiophobia specifically reflects an excessive, irrational fear of physical movement and activity due to perceived vulnerability to painful injury or reinjure [19]. This review focuses exclusively on studies using the TSK to ensure conceptual and measurement consistency.

A systematic review examined the relationships between fear of movement and pain, disability and quality of life in people with chronic musculoskeletal pain [7]. The target community included individuals with chronic low back pain, chronic neck pain, knee osteoarthritis, chronic whiplash-associated disorders, ankylosing spondylitis, and chronic knee pain [7]. However, it has been shown that the associations between fear-avoidance beliefs, pain intensity, and disability differ between patients with various problems, such as cervical pain, and those with low back pain [20]. On the other hand, fear-avoidance beliefs differed significantly between subgroups of neck patients [20]. The relationship between chronic neck pain and fear of movement has been investigated. In this context, fear of movement has been reported to be associated with disability and pain. However, the evidence in some studies is contradictory [5, 18] .

Synthesizing studies on the relationship between fear of movement, pain, and disability may contribute to understanding these relationships in patients with chronic neck pain, thereby enabling more useful clinical decision-making [21]. It is essential to establish an effective recovery process for CNP patients by assessing psychological factors before and after rehabilitation and identifying which psychological disorders significantly contribute to neck rehabilitation. Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review was to determine whether fear of movement is associated with pain intensity and disability after a CNP.

Methods

The review followed the guidelines set by the PRISMA statement [22]. The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines were followed in the methodology section. PRISMA is a widely recognized standard for ensuring transparent and comprehensive reporting of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. It consists of a 27-item checklist and a four-phase flow diagram, which guide authors in detailing their review’s rationale, methods, and findings. The checklist covers essential items such as study selection criteria, search strategies, and risk of bias assessment. The systematic review protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42023474724).

Data sources and search strategy

Two reviewers (N.J and R.M) independently searched the following electronic databases from inception to October 2023: PubMed, AMED, CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubPsych, in addition to conducting a manual search, we utilized a comprehensive search strategy that included relevant search terms derived from Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords generated from the subject headings. The words chronic neck pain, disability, fear avoidance beliefs, pain-related fear, fear of pain, kinesiophobia, fear of movement, and cognitive factors were used in the search. Studies were identified by using all possible combinations of the following groups of search terms: (A) fear avoidance beliefs AND chronic neck pain, (B) pain-related fear AND chronic neck pain, (C) fear of pain AND chronic neck pain, (D) fear of movement AND chronic neck pain, (E) kinesiophobia AND chronic neck pain, (F) cognitive factors AND chronic neck pain, (H) fear avoidance beliefs OR pain-related fear OR fear of pain OR fear of movement OR kinesiophobia OR cognitive factors AND disability AND chronic neck pain). Search strategies were varied for each database to account for different search capabilities. The complete pattern was used as follows: “chronic neck pain” OR “neck pain” OR “cervical pain” OR “cervicogenic pain” OR “Neck Pain” AND “kinesiophobia” OR “fear of movement” OR “Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia” OR “TSK” OR “fear avoidance beliefs” OR “pain-related fear” OR “fear of pain” OR “cognitive factors” AND “disability” OR “functional disability” OR “Neck Disability Index” OR “NDI” OR “pain intensity” OR “pain severity” OR “VAS” OR “NRS”. Additional articles were obtained through a list of references from published reviews [23]. The reference lists of retrieved articles and grey literature were searched to detect studies potentially eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All studies were evaluated for eligibility based on the following PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study designs ) model: (P) the patients diagnosed with chronic neck pain were included; (I) not applicable; (C) Two or more groups and single-group was applied to assess the relationship of fear of movement and pain intensity and disability with chronic neck pain; (O) Outcome measures consisted of fear of movement and pain intensity and disability; (S) observational studies that were cross-sectional, case-control and longitudinal.

Studies were included if they were observational (cross-sectional, case-control, or longitudinal) investigations of the relationship between fear of movement) measured using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (and pain intensity or disability in adults with chronic neck pain; only full-text articles published in English were considered, with no restrictions based on participant gender. Studies were excluded if they focused on acute or subacute pain; assessed fear of movement in the context of prior cervical fractures, or pre- and post-surgical conditions; involved experimental or interventional designs; or were case reports, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, or investigations of the therapeutic effects of fear of movement.

Study selection

First, two authors contributed to the search strategy by entering key terms into the original database to search for articles before entering the studies into EndNote 21 to check for duplicates. Initial screening of titles and abstracts was performed by two authors (N.J and R.M). The full texts of the included manuscripts were then reviewed by the same authors. In cases of disagreement, a third author (L.R) was consulted to reach a consensus. At this stage, study designs, studies including chronic neck pain, how to measure fear of movement, the language of the articles, and other inclusion and exclusion criteria were reviewed. The consensus of two out of three referee was considered as the final vote. Also, screening and filtering of studies was done using Rayyan application and also manually.

When disagreements arose between reviewers, a systematic approach was used to resolve them. Initially, areas of disagreement were identified through discussion between reviewers. This included reviewing the inclusion criteria, study selection, and data extraction processes to determine precisely where disagreements occurred. Open communication between reviewers was facilitated to discuss any points of disagreement. This helped to provide a clear understanding of the differing views and the main reasons for disagreements. Each reviewer provided evidence from the literature to support their position. Consensus was reached through collaborative discussions. Where necessary, a third reviewer or independent expert was consulted to provide an objective perspective and facilitate agreement. If disagreements persisted, relevant sections of the review protocol and literature were repeatedly reviewed to ensure that decisions were consistent with the best available evidence.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (N.J and Ah.B) completed the data extraction. Any disputes were explored further. When necessary, a third reviewer (R.M) participated until consensus was reached. The following relevant data were extracted from each study: first author with year of publication, sample size, pain duration, mean age, pain status, study design, fear of movement measures, pain and disability measures and follow-up period. If necessary, the corresponding author of the study was contacted to obtain more information. An Excel file was used to record the extracted data.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (N.J and Ah.B) independently evaluated the potential for bias in the studies included in the review, using the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) as a recommended tool for assessing the risk of bias in nonrandomized studies [24, 25]. Among all the possible tools for complex evaluation of systematic review, the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) is one of the most used worldwide, above all for observational studies [26]. Furthermore, it is validated for case-control and longitudinal studies [27]. This tool assesses four key areas of risk of bias: selection bias (how study participants are chosen), performance bias (how confounding is managed), detection bias (statistical methods used) and information bias (how exposure and outcomes are measured). This study evaluated four domains using seven items, each of which was scored on a scale from 0 (high risk) to 3 (low risk). A total score of 0 to 6 indicated a high risk of bias, while scores of 7 to 13 and 14 to 21 indicated moderate and low risk of bias, respectively [25]. Disputes were resolved through consultation, or discussion with a third author (L.R).

NOS is a widely used tool for assessing the quality of non-randomized studies, particularly in the context of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Developed through a collaboration between the Universities of Newcastle, Australia, and Ottawa, Canada, the NOS evaluates studies based on three main categories:

Selection of the Study Groups: This category assesses how well the study groups were selected. For cohort studies, it includes items such as the representativeness of the exposed cohort and the selection of the non-exposed cohort. For case-control studies, it evaluates the selection of cases and controls [24].

Selection of the Study Groups: This category assesses how well the study groups were selected. For cohort studies, it includes items such as the representativeness of the exposed cohort and the selection of the non-exposed cohort. For case-control studies, it evaluates the selection of cases and controls [24].

Comparability of the Groups: This category examines whether the study groups are comparable in terms of design or analysis. It considers factors such as whether the study controls for important confounding variables [24].

Comparability of the Groups: This category examines whether the study groups are comparable in terms of design or analysis. It considers factors such as whether the study controls for important confounding variables [24].

Ascertainment of Exposure/Outcome: For cohort studies, this category assesses the quality of outcome measurement, including whether follow-up was adequate and whether the outcome of interest was measured accurately. For case-control studies, it evaluates how exposure was ascertained, ensuring that the method used was reliable and consistent across cases and controls [24].

Data synthesis and analysis

Two reviewers (N.J and Ah.B) independently evaluated the quality and strength of the evidence. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria are used to assess the strength of research evidence. The GRADE system initially classifies the evidence into high or low, coming from experimental or observational studies; subsequently and following a series of considerations, the evidence is classified into high, moderate, low or very low. In GRADE, risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias are examined. Investigation and analysis were done according to GRADE guidelines [25, 28–30]. The quality of the evidence can decrease or increase based on the presence, or not, of the following factors: serious (− 1) or very serious (− 2) limitation to study quality (risk of bias); some (− 1) or important (− 2) inconsistency of results; some (− 1) or major (− 2) uncertainty about directness; some (− 1) or important imprecision or sparse data and some (− 1) or high (− 2) probability of reporting bias [31]. Specifically, regarding the risk of bias, when 50% or more of the included studies presented a moderate or high risk, the GRADE system recorded a ‘very serious’ risk of bias. Inconsistency was graded ‘very serious’ when 50% or more of the studies showed heterogeneity in statistical significance and the direction of the relationship. Imprecision was graded ‘very serious’ when the optimal information size criterion was not met and the sample size was small.

Results

Study characteristics

After searching the databases, a total of 1513 articles were found. Of these, 15 observational studies (1 longitudinal study; 14 cross-sectional studies) with a total of 1371 participants met our inclusion criteria and were included in this review (Fig. 1). In the case of longitudinal studies, the duration of follow-up ranged from 3 to 12 months. The outcome measures were as follows: disability [5, 17, 18, 32–39], and pain intensity [5, 17, 18, 32, 34, 37, 38, 40–43].

Risk of bias

An adapted version of the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale was used to assess the potential for bias in cross-sectional studies. Of the 15 studies examined, 12 (80%) were found to have a moderate risk of bias, while the remaining 3 (20%) had a low risk. A majority (66%) of the studies included in the analysis exhibited selection bias, while 75% of them had inadequate sample sizes and insufficient power, and 75% of the analyses were unadjusted for potential covariates. Additionally, 44% of the studies lacked appropriate statistical analysis, leading to detection bias. Most of the included studies did not show detection bias or information bias (Table 1).

The correlation between fear of movement and disability

The correlation between fear of movement and disability was investigated in 11 studies (Table 2). Nine studies showed a significant correlation between fear of movement and disability [17, 32–39]. In 2 studies, there was no significant correlation between fear of movement and disability [5, 18]. The predictive value of the fear of movement for pain in people with chronic neck pain based on longitudinal analysis was evaluated in 1 study [39]. It was found that by using a combination of baseline NDI and TSK scores, it was possible to predict chronic disability with a probability of 54.2% after using the NDI alone and 83.3% after using a combination of the NDI and TSK scores [39]. The application of GRADE criteria attained very low evidence across the included studies (Table 3).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to systematically review whether fear of movement is related with pain and disability in subjects with chronic neck pain. Based on the analysis, one longitudinal study and 14 cross-sectional studies were examined. Based on the NOS, of the 15 studies examined, 12 (80%) were found to have a moderate risk of bias, while the remaining 3 (20%) had a low risk. Evidence has shown that there is an association between higher levels of fear of movement and greater levels of disability. 9 studies showed a significant association and 2 studies showed a non-significant association between fear of movement and disability. Eight studies showed a significant association and three studies showed a non-significant association between fear of movement and pain intensity in patients with chronic neck pain. However, the quality and strength of the evidence was very low according to the GRADE criteria.

In three studies, no significant association was observed between pain intensity and disability with fear of movement [5, 18, 43]. The study of Demirbüken et al. [43] analyzed the relationship between chronic neck pain intensity, kinesiophobia, and physical activity scores in 99 subjects (35 men and 64 women) aged 18 to 65 years. There was no statistically correlation between neck pain intensity and kinesiophobia degree. Dimitriadis et al. [18] investigates the association between psychological states and pain/disability in chronic neck pain patients (99 patients 43.65 ± 12.83 years), utilizing various questionnaires to assess pain intensity, disability, and psychological factors. Findings suggest that while anxiety and depression may have some correlation with pain and disability, kinesiophobia did not show significant associations in patients with idiopathic neck pain. Thompson et al. [5] examined the association between cognitive factors, pain and disability in 94 patients with idiopathic chronic neck pain (51.1 ± 14.60 years). There was no significant relationship between pain intensity and disability with kinesiophobia. In these three studies, pain intensity was measured with a VAS, disability with a NDI, and fear of movement with the TSK-17. Variations in study design, sample size, differences in statistical power, selection bias sampling error and statistical unreliability and data analysis methods can lead to different outcomes.

The predominance of cross-sectional designs in the included studies establishes robust concurrent associations between fear of movement, pain intensity, and disability in chronic neck pain, yet precludes causal or temporal inference. The single prospective cohort study [39] offers preliminary longitudinal evidence, demonstrating that baseline Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia scores independently predict 6-month disability after adjusting for initial pain and functional status, whereas pain intensity did not mediate this trajectory. This finding suggests that kinesiophobia may function as a persistent driver of functional deterioration, independent of pain fluctuations) a mechanism not detectable in cross-sectional analyses (.

According to the biopsychosocial model of chronic pain, psychological elements (e.g., pain-related fear) contribute to the beginning and progression of both pain and disability [45]. In accordance with a cognitive-behavioral approach, the fear-avoidance model of chronic pain suggests that fear of pain can trigger avoidance behaviors, leading to a decrease in physical activity and movement. It is believed that while avoiding activities may be beneficial in the short-term when dealing with acute pain, in the long-term, it can lead to decreased daily functioning and increased physical disability [46]. The fear-avoidance model suggests that when individuals experience pain, they may develop a fear of it, which can be reinforced by avoiding activities that cause pain. This can lead to the maintenance and worsening of disability [46].

Other systematic review studies have evaluated the role of fear of movement in patients with various pain conditions [7, 25, 47–51]. Luque-Suarez et al. [7] showed a relationship between greater fear of movement and greater pain intensity and disability in people suffering from chronic musculoskeletal pain. In this study, patients with different conditions, including chronic neck pain, were investigated. Luque-Suarez et al. [47] investigated the relationship between fear of movement and pain and disability in whiplash-associated disorders. At baseline, fear of movement was not associated with pain intensity over time (three studies). The relationship between fear of movement and disability was unclear because the results were inconsistent (six studies). Zale et al. [48] examined the relationship between fear of movement and disability. The results showed that there is a relationship between fear of pain and disability in people with acute and chronic pain. Wertli et al. [49] showed that fear-avoidance beliefs in patients with low back pain. In another study of low back pain, Wortley et al. [50] reported that fear-avoidance beliefs are implicated in subacute low back pain, and therefore, early treatment, including interventions to reduce fear-avoidance beliefs, may prevent chronicity. In a meta-analysis on fear of movement and pain, Kruska [51] concluded that there is a positive and bidirectional relationship between these two variables. Calderon et al. [25] in a systematic review and meta-analysis, showed that higher levels of fear of pain, pain-related anxiety, and fear-avoidance beliefs were significantly associated with greater pain intensity and disability.

The fear-avoidance model suggests that those who experience acute pain can become ensnared in a vicious cycle of chronic disability and distress due to their cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and functional reactions to the pain [52]. This model suggests that when a person experiences a painful event, they may perceive it as a threat, which can lead to catastrophic thinking that any physical activity or movement will cause further pain or injury [10]. This model includes fear of movement, which is an irrational and disabling fear of physical movement and activity due to a feeling of vulnerability to painful injury or reinjure. This fear can be debilitating and can prevent individuals from engaging in activities they would otherwise enjoy [53]. Fear of movement, which affects between 51% and 72% of patients with chronic pain [54, 55], can lead to hypervigilance and worsening disability, ultimately increasing pain sensation [10]. Fear of movement is strongly linked to pain intensity and disability in individuals with chronic pain [56]. It is important to assess and address fear of movement, as physical exercise is a crucial part of rehabilitation treatment, and high levels of fear of movement may hinder treatment adherence. Therefore, fear of movement should be considered in the initial assessment to design a treatment strategy based on psychosocial factors. This can be achieved in the form of behavioral tests through safe behavioral training programs and slow and gradual exposure to fearful activities [57].

The findings from the review are crucial for clinical practice, as they underscore the need to address kinesiophobia in therapeutic settings for individuals with chronic neck pain. Identifying effective exercise interventions could play a significant role in mitigating fear of movement, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes and quality of life [58]. Future studies are encouraged to further explore these relationships, considering confounding variables that may influence outcomes [59].

As a limitation, even with extensive database searches, not all studies may have been identified. There was heterogeneity between studies in titles, conclusions, pain status, statistical conditions, and research plan, which limits comparisons between studies. In this study, TSK was used to measure the fear of movement. However, all studies conducted on chronic neck pain used the TSK, and no other tools were used. All outcomes relied on self-reported instruments (TSK for fear of movement, NDI for disability, VAS/NRS for pain), which are vulnerable to response bias, social desirability, and construct overlap.

Conclusions

Our results revealed that greater levels of kinesiophobia were cross-sectionally associated with greater pain intensity and disability levels. The number of prospective longitudinal studies is very small; thus, the prognosis could not be determined. Therefore, more longitudinal cohort studies that analyze the predictive value of the fear of movement in the chronic neck pain are needed. The results of this study can help therapists in planning and designing therapeutic interventions considering the factor of fear of movement because this factor affects movement therapy programs. In future studies, it is recommended to examine mediating and moderating variables, especially pain catastrophizing, depression, and central sensitization.
