Antipyretic and Antinociceptive Activities of Tri-Phol-That Extract and Its Flavonoids: In Vivo and In Silico Evidence
Authors: Thitaporn Sae−jia, Adisorn Sirichotejirakul, Pacharathon Simking, Sinsamut Sae−Ngow, Worathat Thitikornpong, Watchara Damjuti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the common use of herbal medicines rich in flavonoids to treat fever and pain, the pharmacological mechanisms involved in these effects are poorly understood. Therefore, this study assessed the antipyretic and analgesic effects of Tri-Phol-That extract, characterized its major flavonoids, and investigated its possible molecular interactions with key inflammatory mediators. The antipyretic activity was evaluated against a turpentine-induced pyrexia model in mice. Analgesic activity was evaluated using the acetic acid-induced writhing, tail immersion, and hot plate tests. The concentration of rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography. Molecular docking was performed using GNINA against COX-2 (mouse: 1CX2, human: 5KIR), COX-1 (ovine: 3N8X), and mPGES-1 (human: 4YL1). Interaction profile studies were also performed in the Discovery Studio 2021. The hydroethanolic extract lowered rectal temperature in a dose-dependent manner, with maximum inhibition occurring at 100–200 mg/kg, although the effect was less potent than that of tolfenamic acid. The acetic acid-induced writhing test also showed strong dose-dependent peripheral antinociceptive activity with 84.64% inhibition at 200 mg/kg of extract, comparable to tolfenamic acid. The central analgesic effects of extracts were observed in hot plate tests. Weak central analgesic effects were also observed in tail immersion tests. HPLC analysis indicated rutin (4.01 μg/g), kaempferol (2.13 μg/g), and quercetin (0.96 μg/g) were the most abundant flavonoid compounds. Based on docking results, quercetin had the strongest binding affinity toward COX-2 (−8.44 kcal/mol). Rutin also exhibited the strongest binding affinity toward COX-1 (−7.55 kcal/mol) among the flavonoids tested. On the other hand, kaempferol exhibited the strongest binding affinity toward mPGES-1 (−9.97 kcal/mol). Results also showed that rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol interacted with catalytic residues of ARG120, TYR355, TYR385, SER530, and SER127. Thus, it can be concluded that the extract of Tri-Phol-That had strong antipyretic and analgesic effects via multi-target inhibition of COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1, possibly due to the combined effects of rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol. These results support the traditional medicinal use of this extract and provide justification for further investigation as a phytotherapeutic agent.

Introduction

Fever and pain are among the most common clinical and community complaints. These are manifestations of the underlying processes of infection, inflammation, and tissue injury. However, in spite of the availability of pharmacotherapy such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which have proven efficacious in managing fever and pain through the inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis via COX inhibition, there are significant disadvantages to their use, such as GI toxicity, nephrotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity, which has prompted a search for alternatives with better safety.

Traditional medicine has long provided Asia with therapeutic alternatives. Tri-Phol-That is a classical Thai herbal formulation consisting of three plant materials in equal proportions, comprising the rhizomes of Zingiber zerumbet (L.) Roscoe ex Sm., the culms of Cymbopogon nardus (L.) Rendle, and the rhizomes of Zingiber cassumunar Roxb. Traditionally, this formulation has been used to alleviate fever, inflammation, and body pain, conditions that are interpreted in Thai traditional medicine as manifestations of body-element imbalance. This ethnomedicinal use represents an important indication of its therapeutic relevance among rural populations; however, despite its long history of use, the pharmacological basis and bioactive compounds responsible for its effects have not been systematically investigated.

Flavonoids are polyphenolic compounds with anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antipyretic pharmacological properties. Quercetin has been reported to have antipyretic and analgesic properties in a number of studies. Separately, quercetin is also thought to act as a direct inhibitor of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), mediating a significant portion of its pharmacological effects. , The glycoside rutin is a strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory that suppresses the activation of NF-κB and iNOS. Kaempferol has also been reported to decrease the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by blocking activation of the transcription factor NF-κB. Together, these flavonoids give mechanistic plausibility to the therapeutic effects observed for flavonoid-rich herbal preparations.

Building on this rationale, the present study was designed to bridge the gap between traditional use and modern pharmacological evidence. Specifically, we sought to evaluate the antipyretic activity of Tri-Phol-That extract in an experimental fever model, to assess its peripheral and central antinociceptive activities using validated animal assays, and to quantitatively profile its flavonoid markers, including rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol. Through this integrated approach, we aim to establish a scientific foundation for the standardization and potential therapeutic application of Tri-Phol-That.

Materials and Methods

General Chemicals, Reagents, and Instruments

Rutin (96.0% purity), quercetin (97.0% purity), and kaempferol (98.2% purity) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and served as reference standards for quantitative HPLC analysis of Tri-Phol-That extract. HPLC-grade methanol, formic acid, and acetic acid were sourced from Merck, while all other reagents and solvents were of analytical grade unless otherwise noted. Chromatographic analysis was conducted on a Shimadzu LC-20A system comprising a quaternary pump, column oven, autosampler, and photodiode array detector. Data acquisition and instrument control were managed through LCsolution software (version 1.25). Separation was achieved using a Kinetex C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The extract was concentrated under reduced pressure using a Rotavapor R-210 rotary evaporator (Buchi, Switzerland) and converted to a dry powder using a Buchi spray dryer. For pharmacological studies, rectal temperature was recorded with a Microlife MT 200 digital thermometer. Antinociceptive activity was evaluated using the tail immersion test, performed in a temperature-controlled water bath adapted from an IKA RV10 unit, and the hot plate test, conducted on an apparatus modified from an IKA C-MAG HP 10 heating plate (IKA-Werke GmbH & Co. KG). Molecular docking was carried out using GNINA, and the resulting ligand–protein interactions were visualized and interpreted in Discovery Studio 2021.

Plant Material

Rhizomes of Zingiber zerumbet (L.) Roscoe ex Sm., culms of Cymbopogon nardus (L.) Rendle, and rhizomes of Zingiber cassumunar Roxb. were collected from local farms in Nong Suea District, Pathum Thani Province, Thailand. The plant materials were authenticated according to the Thai Herbal Pharmacopoeia (2018), and voucher specimens were deposited at the Faculty of Integrative Medicine, Rajamangala University of Technology Thanyaburi (RMUTT).

Preparation of Tri-Phol-That Extract

Each plant material was washed, dried at 60 °C to constant weight, and powdered. For extraction, equal proportions of the dried powders were mixed at a ratio of 1:1:1 (w/w), consisting of 100 g of Z. zerumbet, 100 g of C. nardus, and 100 g of Z. cassumunar, giving a total powdered mixture of 300 g. The mixed powder was extracted with 80% ethanol by maceration until exhaustion. The filtrates were pooled, concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, and spray-dried to yield a fine powder. The final dried Tri-Phol-That extract obtained was 41.4 g, corresponding to an extraction yield of 13.8% w/w based on the total dried plant powder. The prepared extract was used for all subsequent experiments, including phytochemical analysis, antipyretic activity, antinociceptive activity, and molecular docking-related interpretation. The extract was stored in airtight containers at 4 °C until use.

Quantitative Analysis of Marker Compounds

The concentrations of rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol in the Tri-Phol-That extract were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Shimadzu LC-20A series system equipped with a quaternary pump, column oven, autosampler, and photodiode array (PDA) detector. Instrument control and data acquisition were performed using LCsolution software (version 1.25). All standard stock solutions were prepared in methanol at a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. Working solutions were obtained by serial dilution to concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 30, and 50 μg/mL. For sample preparation, the extract (1 g) was dissolved in 10 mL methanol, sonicated, vortexed, and centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 min to recover the supernatant. This procedure was repeated three times, and the combined supernatants were evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Rotavapor R-210, Buchi) to approximately 1 mL and reconstituted to a final volume of 5 mL in methanol. The resulting solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.2% aqueous formic acid (solvent A) and methanol (solvent B), delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min under the following gradient conditions: 85:15 (A:B) from 0 to 10 min, 70:30 from 10 to 45 min, 50:50 from 45 to 50 min, 20:80 from 50 to 55 min, 5:95 from 55 to 60 min, 0:100 from 60 to 65 min, followed by re-equilibration to 85:15 from 70 to 75 min. The detection wavelengths were set at 254 and 330 nm, and the injection volume was 10 μL.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking of the screened flavonoids (kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin) and the drug tolfenamic acid against inflammation biomarkers was carried out to predict binding affinity and binding interaction. The 3D crystal structure of mouse cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) (PDB ID: 1CX2), human COX-2 (PDB ID: 5KIR), ovine cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) (PDB ID: 3N8X), and human (Homo sapiens) microsomal prostaglandin E synthase-1 (mPGES-1) (PDB ID: 4YL1) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. These structures were determined by X-ray crystallography with resolutions of 3.00, 2.70, 2.75, and 1.41 Å, respectively.

Protein preparation was performed using GNINA. For each structure, only the relevant protein chain was retained (chain C for 1CX2, chain A for 5KIR and 4YL1, and chain B for 3N8X), while all water molecules, ions, and cocrystallized ligands were removed. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and Kollman charges were assigned. The prepared protein structures were then saved in PDBQT format. Ligand structures were prepared from SMILES representations, converted into 3D structures, and energy-minimized. Hydrogen atoms were added, Gasteiger charges were assigned, rotatable bonds were defined, and the ligands were converted into PDBQT format prior to docking with GNINA.

Docking simulations were performed using GNINA. The docking protocol was validated by redocking the native cocrystallized ligands into their respective binding sites. The coordinates of the native ligand binding sites were used as the centers for grid boxes of 15, 22.5, 30, and 45 Å. The performance of the docking procedure was evaluated by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the redocked poses and the corresponding crystallographic conformations. Redocking showed that grid box sizes between 15 and 30 Å reproduced the crystallographic binding mode with RMSD values below 2.0 Å for several targets, whereas larger grid sizes gave poorer performance. Therefore, target-specific grid settings were applied for the subsequent docking calculations. An exhaustiveness value of 16 was used for all docking runs to ensure sufficient conformational sampling. Each ligand was allowed to generate multiple binding poses, and the reported pose was selected based on the highest CNN pose score for further analysis. Docking results were evaluated based on predicted binding energies (kcal/mol), ligand conformations, and interactions with amino acid residues in the binding pocket. In addition to GNINA binding affinity values, GNINA-derived scoring metrics, including CNN pose score and CNN affinity, were recorded for the selected pose. The CNN pose score reflects the likelihood of a correct ligand binding orientation, whereas the CNN affinity provides a deep learning-based estimation of binding strength. Key molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic contacts, were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio 2021.

Experimental Animals

Adult male ICR mice (4–6 weeks old, 35–45 g) were purchased from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Mahidol University, Thailand. The mice were kept in the animal facility of the Faculty of Integrative Medicine, RMUTT. They were kept under standard laboratory conditions (24 ± 1 °C, 60–70% relative humidity, and 12-h light/dark cycle) and were given food and water ad libitum. Mice were acclimatized for 7 days before experimentation, and all experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of RMUTT (Approval No. RMUTT.IM.2023.R001) and were performed in accordance with the relevant national and institutional guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Evaluation of Peripheral Antinociceptive Activity

To assess the peripheral component of the antinociceptive activity of the Tri-Phol-That extract, the acetic acid-induced writhing model was selected as a representative model of inflammatory nociception. This model is commonly used to evaluate chemically induced peripheral pain, as acetic acid-induced abdominal constrictions are associated with the release of prostaglandins and other inflammatory mediators. Thus, it provides a suitable approach for screening compounds with potential anti-inflammatory or peripheral analgesic effects. Tolfenamic acid, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), was used as the reference control. Consistent with recent analgesic drug discovery approaches, including studies on Rimonabant analog–opioid peptide hybrid compounds and modified biphalin analogs, complementary pain models are necessary to reflect different nociceptive stimuli and mechanisms. Therefore, this assay was incorporated to support the evaluation of the peripheral antinociceptive profile of the Tri-Phol-That extract.

Acetic Acid-Induced Writhing Test

Five groups of six male ICR mice each (n = 6/group; total n = 30) were used. Animals received a single dose of either saline (10 mL/kg, i.p.), tolfenamic acid (4 mg/kg, i.m.), or extract (50, 100, or 200 mg/kg, p.o.) 30 min prior to acetic acid challenge (0.6% acetic acid, 10 mL/kg, i.p.). This was a single-session acute experiment. The criterion for antinociceptive activity was the number of writhes (abdominal constrictions accompanied by stretching of the hind limbs) recorded over 30 min postacetic acid injection. Percent inhibition for the writhing response was calculated as [(Wrcontrol – Wrtest)/Wrcontrol] × 100, where Wr = mean number of writhes.

Evaluation of Central Antinociceptive Activity

To test whether the Tri-Phol-That extract’s analgesic activity is mediated at spinal and/or supraspinal levels of nociceptive pain processing, the central antinociceptive activity of the extract was evaluated by the tail immersion and hot plate tests, which are classical thermal nociception tests used to examine centrally mediated analgesic activity. Consistent with the testing of centrally acting analgesic drugs, structurally modified opioid peptides, such as biphalin analogs with p-fluoro-l-phenylalanine at the 4,4′ position or with nonhydrazine linkers, have been reportedly synthesized and tested in recently described in vivo screening tests of central analgesic activity. Therefore, these thermal assays were included alongside the acetic acid-induced writhing test to provide a broader evaluation of the peripheral and central antinociceptive activities of the Tri-Phol-That extract, with tolfenamic acid serving as the reference drug.

Tail Immersion Test

The tail immersion test is a classical experimental method used to evaluate the central antinociceptive (analgesic) properties of a test substance and to assess its action on pain perception in the CNS. Following the D’Amour and Smith (1941) method, five groups of six male ICR mice each (n = 6/group; total n = 30) received a single dose of the respective treatment, as described above. The distal 4 cm of each animal’s tail was immersed in 55 °C water, and the latency in seconds to tail withdrawal was recorded. Measurements were performed at baseline and at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-treatment. A cutoff time of 45 s was used to prevent tissue injury. Results were expressed as the percentage of the maximum possible effect (%MPE) using the following formula: %MPE=[(TL−BL)/(Cut‐off−BL)]×100 where BL is the baseline latency and TL is the test latency at each post-treatment time point.

Hot Plate Test

The hot plate test is a classical experimental model for assessing the central antinociceptive (analgesic) effects of drugs in laboratory animals and is considered a reliable method for evaluating pain responses mediated by spinal and supraspinal systems. Following the Woolfe and MacDonald (1944) method, five groups of six male ICR mice each (n = 6/group; total n = 30) received a single dose of the respective treatment, as described above. Mice were placed on a temperature-controlled hot plate set at 55 ± 0.5 °C, and the latency in seconds to paw licking or jumping was recorded. Measurements were performed at baseline and at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-treatment. A cutoff time of 45 s was used to prevent tissue injury. Results were expressed as the percentage of the maximum possible effect (%MPE) as above.

Evaluation of Antipyretic Activity

Turpentine-Induced Pyrexia Test

Pyrexia was induced in mice by administering turpentine oil (2.3 mL/kg, i.m.) into both hind legs. Pyrexia was allowed to develop over a 12-h period before treatment administration. Six groups of six male ICR mice each (n = 6/group; total n = 36) were used, including a normal afebrile group, a negative control group (saline, p.o.), a positive control group (tolfenamic acid 4 mg/kg, i.m.), and three extract-treated groups (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, p.o.). All treatments were administered as a single dose 12 h after pyrexia induction. Rectal temperature was recorded using a digital rectal thermometer at baseline (07:00, immediately before treatment administration; time 0) and every 30 min thereafter until 13:00 (6 h post-treatment). Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Antipyretic activity was evaluated based on the reduction in rectal temperature compared with the negative control group and was expressed as absolute rectal temperature and percent change from baseline (%ΔT) using the following formula: %ΔT=[(Tt−T0)/T0]×100 where Tt is the rectal temperature at each assessment time point and T0 is the baseline temperature at time 0.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data from the HPLC quantitative analysis, antinociceptive tests, and antipyretic test were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For single-time-point outcomes, including marker compound contents, number of writhes, and percent inhibition in the acetic acid-induced writhing test, data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test to compare each treatment group with the negative control group. For time-course outcomes, including tail withdrawal latency and %MPE in the tail immersion test, paw licking or jumping latency and %MPE in the hot plate test, and rectal temperature and percent change in rectal temperature (%ΔT) in the turpentine-induced pyrexia test, data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of treatment, time, and treatment × time interaction, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test where appropriate. Dose-dependent effects of Tri-Phol-That extract were further assessed by comparing the 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg treatment groups within each experimental model. Molecular docking results, including binding affinity, CNN pose score, CNN affinity, RMSD, and binding interactions, were analyzed descriptively and were not subjected to inferential statistical testing. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results

Quantitative Analysis of Marker Compounds

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) determination showed that the major flavonoids within Tri-Phol-That extract were rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, as shown in the chromatogram of the standard solution (Figure ), with retention times of 25.000, 37.955, and 46.673 min, respectively. These retention times were then used to assign the peaks found in the extract, and the slopes of the calibration curves (Figure ) were found to be very linear (R2 > 0.999) across the entire concentration range (1–50 μg/mL). This confirmed the correct quantification. The calibration curve equations, with linear regression, of rutin (y = 12,376x + 1451.2), quercetin (y = 44,230x – 12,708), and kaempferol (y = 34,321x – 13,883) were able to determine the concentration of these compounds from the area of their peaks. Quantified results in Table and Figure S1 show that the extraction method from Tri-Phol-That extract presents rutin as the most concentrated compound (4.01 ± 0.05 μg/g, %RSD 1.23), followed by kaempferol (2.13 ± 0.04 μg/g, %RSD 1.99) and quercetin (0.96 ± 0.01 μg/g, %RSD 1.47). The low %RSD values for all compounds show the repeatability and reproducibility of this method.

Molecular Docking of Active Compounds with COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1

Molecular docking studies were performed to compare the binding affinities and interaction profiles of kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin with those of the reference drug tolfenamic acid across the selected inflammatory targets. The molecular docking protocol was validated by redocking the native ligands into their active sites, and the results showed acceptable RMSD values below 2.0 Å between the crystal structure and docked conformations.

The docking results showed target-dependent binding patterns for the tested compounds in the selected binding pockets of COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1. The docking scores and interaction profiles were target- and compound-dependent and are summarized in Table . Most of the compounds showed good binding affinities toward both mouse (1CX2) and human (5KIR) COX-2 isoforms. The highest binding affinity toward 1CX2 was observed for quercetin (−8.44 kcal/mol) and tolfenamic acid (−8.52 kcal/mol), followed by kaempferol (−7.91 kcal/mol). The binding energy of rutin in 1CX2 was unfavorable (+2.8 kcal/mol). The considered hydrogen bond interactions involved active site residues (ARG120, TYR355, and TYR385) important in substrate binding and catalysis. Hydrophobic interactions, including interactions with active site residues such as VAL349, LEU352, ALA527, and LEU531, further stabilize ligand binding in the binding pocket.

Docking Scores (Binding Affinities, kcal/mol) and Interaction Profiles of Active Compounds against COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1

A similar trend was also observed for human COX-2 (5KIR). The flavonoids showed less favorable docking scores toward human COX-2 than toward the mouse COX-2 structure. A stronger binding affinity (−8.67 kcal/mol) was observed for tolfenamic acid, in line with its COX inhibitory activity. Hydrogen bond interactions were shown with residues HIS90, SER353, TYR355, and TYR385, and hydrophobic interactions were shown with residues VAL349, LEU352, and VAL523. These results support possible binding of some flavonoids within the COX-2 active site and are consistent with their potential contribution to the antipyretic and antinociceptive effects observed in vivo.

The docking scores for COX-1 (3N8X) are −4.05, −4.12, and −7.55 kcal/mol for kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin, respectively. For comparison, the binding affinity of tolfenamic acid was −3.9 kcal/mol. All three of the compounds interacted with the active site of COX-1, but rutin showed a higher binding affinity for the enzyme than tolfenamic acid. Interaction analysis of rutin revealed interactions with the following residues: HIS90, ARG120, TYR355, TYR385, and SER530. These results indicate that rutin showed the most favorable docking score among the tested flavonoids toward COX-1.

However, for mPGES-1 (PDB ID: 4YL1), multiple binding conformations were obtained. The binding free energy of kaempferol (−9.97 kcal/mol) was the most favorable, followed by tolfenamic acid (−8.6 kcal/mol). These findings suggest stronger predicted binding to the target. In addition to the non-covalent interactions, a few of the ligands were observed to form hydrogen bonds with the target protein: kaempferol with SER127, and rutin with ASN74, HIS113, SER127, and THR131. Quercetin and tolfenamic acid did not form hydrogen bonds with the protein in their predicted binding poses, suggesting they are bound mainly by hydrophobic interactions with VAL128 and surrounding residues.

The docking results suggest target-dependent binding patterns for the tested flavonoids across COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1. Quercetin and kaempferol showed more favorable docking scores toward COX-2, whereas rutin showed the most favorable docking score among the tested flavonoids toward COX-1. Among the flavonoids tested, kaempferol showed the most favorable docking score toward mPGES-1. Overall, these docking results are consistent with the possibility that some flavonoids may interact with multiple inflammation-related targets, although experimental confirmation is required.

All compounds were located in the cyclooxygenase binding pocket (Figure A–D), as seen when superimposing the compounds onto the 3D structure of mouse COX-2 (1CX2). The 3D conformations of kaempferol and quercetin were almost identical when viewed in the cyclooxygenase binding pocket, being stabilized by hydrogen bonds with ARG120, TYR355, and TYR385 (Figure A–D). The 2D interaction diagrams (Figure I–L) confirmed compound stabilization by the hydrogen bonding with ARG120, TYR355, and TYR385, and hydrophobic interactions with VAL349, LEU352, ALA527, and LEU531. The same binding mode was observed with tolfenamic acid, as it interacts with ARG120 via a hydrogen bond and forms hydrophobic interactions. Rutin forms more hydrogen bond contacts but shows a less favorable binding mode, consistent with its weaker docking score.

Although rutin formed multiple hydrogen-bond contacts in the target, its overall binding orientation was less favorable than that of the other compounds.

Evaluation of Peripheral Antinociceptive Activity

Acetic Acid-Induced Writhing Test

The number of writhes was significantly decreased in the Tri-Phol-That extract-treated groups by 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg as compared to the negative control (55.33 ± 1.86) with 36.17 ± 3.71, 22.83 ± 4.31, and 8.50 ± 2.07 corresponding to a % inhibition of 34.63%, 58.74%, and 84.64%, respectively. The number of writhings in the standard group treated with tolfenamic acid (4 mg/kg) decreased to a mean of 6.83 ± 2.23 (87.66% inhibition). The results were statistically analyzed, and all of the extract doses produced a significant reduction in the number of writhings compared to the negative control group (p < 0.05) (Tables S1 and S2). The results also indicated that the effect of the 200 mg/kg group was not significantly different from that of the standard tolfenamic acid group (p > 0.05). This result indicates that the extract of Tri-Phol-That has strong peripheral antinociceptive activity, and this supports the traditional use of Tri-Phol-That as an analgesic.

Tail Immersion Test

The latency of tail withdrawal was significantly prolonged in a dose-dependent manner after administration of the Tri-Phol-That extract was compared with the baseline and negative control, and there was no significant difference between pre-drug and post-drug latency in the negative control (2.48 ± 1.26 s vs 3.57 ± 1.85 s). The mean %MPE in the negative control was 2.47 ± 6.05. The positive control (tolfenamic acid 4 mg/kg) significantly increased tail flick latency from 2.32 ± 0.53 s to 4.70 ± 1.96 s, resulting in a mean %MPE of 5.56 ± 5.21. In contrast, the Tri-Phol-That extract increased the withdrawal latency only at 100 mg/kg and produced a withdrawal latency of 4.24 ± 1.97 s (%MPE = 5.42 ± 6.34), which was comparable to the positive control. The 50 mg/kg dose of Tri-Phol-That increased the post-drug latency to 3.55 ± 1.31 s (%MPE = 2.72 ± 2.32), which was not significantly different from the negative control. The 200 mg/kg dose was found to have the maximum effect (5.26 ± 2.27 s and %MPE = 7.42 ± 3.53), which shows the central analgesic effect of the extract at the spinal level (Table S3).

Hot Plate Test

In the present study, the Tri-Phol-That extract dose-dependently and time-dependently inhibited nociceptive response in the hot plate assay, as evidenced by an increase in %MPE when compared with the negative control group. At 15 min, only the positive control (tolfenamic acid, 4 mg/kg) produced a statistically significant increase in %MPE (22.54 ± 2.94%) when compared with the negative control. All the extract doses (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg) increased the latencies at 30 and 60 min, but there were no statistically significant differences between the extract and positive control groups. The extract produced non-significant dose-dependent increases in MPE of 6.40–21.56% at 15 min, 17.93 ± 16.34% to 34.03 ± 41.76% at 30 min, and 22.56 ± 14.88% to 45.58 ± 79.36% at 60 min. The best extract performance occurred at 90 min with 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg, with 44.49 ± 4.18% MPE and 55.48 ± 51.42% MPE, respectively. These results were similar to the positive control group (61.96 ± 9.13% MPE) and significantly different from the negative control. At 2 h, 50 mg/kg produced 23.31 ± 28.47% MPE, like moderate analgesia. The extract at a dose of 200 mg/kg had the highest analgesic activity (61.24 ± 22.06% MPE) at 2 h, which was found to have similar effects to the positive control (71.50 ± 8.76% MPE). The 100 mg/kg dose had a strong effect (45.13 ± 11.62% MPE), and the 50 mg/kg dose had a weak effect (5.98 ± 11.63% MPE). Finally, the data clearly shows that the extract of Tri-Phol-That can have a central antinociceptive effect in the hot plate test that is effectively dose- and time-dependent, with the higher doses (100 and 200 mg/kg) having an effect nearly equivalent to that of the control drug, as shown in Table S4.

Evaluation of Antipyretic Activity

Turpentine-Induced Pyrexia Test

In the turpentine pyrexia model, after the observation period, the negative control group had stable pyrexia, whose rectal temperature ranged from ∼37.4 to 37.9 °C during the observation period. In the normal afebrile group, body temperature was similar to physiological levels (≤34.5 °C), confirming the results of the turpentine pyrexia model. Tolfenamic acid (4 mg/kg) is a standard antipyretic medication that was administered to the subjects. This led to a significant drop in temperature within 30 min (36.77 ± 0.05 °C at 07:30 vs 37.87 ± 0.08 °C in the control, p < 0.001; Figure ). This effect was sustained throughout the experiment periods, with a mean temperature of ∼36.4–36.7 °C. The test extract produced a dose-dependent antipyretic effect. The 50 mg/kg dose produced mild but statistically significant decreases in body temperature, from 37.12 ± 0.50 °C at 07:30 to 36.77 ± 0.33 °C at 13:00 (p < 0.01–0.001 versus controls). At the 100 mg/kg dose level, the effect was more pronounced. The temperature range was between 36.82 ± 0.19 °C and 36.37 ± 0.05 °C throughout the experimental period (p < 0.001). At the dose of 200 mg/kg, similar results were obtained (temperature range 36.90 ± 0.13 °C and 36.63 ± 0.23 °C; p < 0.001), confirming that the effect is plateauing beyond the 100 mg/kg dose level (Table S5).

Effect of the extract on rectal temperature in turpentine-induced pyrexia test. Rectal temperatures (Mean ± SD, n = 6) were recorded every 30 min for 6 h following pyrexia induction. The normal group maintained baseline physiological temperature (∼34.5 °C), while the negative control remained febrile (∼37.4–37.9 °C). Tolfenamic acid (4 mg/kg, i.m.) produced a rapid and sustained antipyretic effect. Extract-treated groups (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, p.o.) demonstrated dose-dependent reductions in temperature, with maximal effect at 100–200 mg/kg.

The %ΔT (temperature change from baseline, as a percentage) was also measured and showed little change in the negative control group (−0.3% to −1.3%), indicating continuing pyrexia. Tolfenamic acid achieved the largest %ΔT reductions (−3.2% to −4.2%), indicating it was the strongest antipyretic of all. The extract at 50 mg/kg reduced %ΔT moderately (−0.5% to −1.7%). For the 100 mg/kg dose, changes were −1.1% to −2.4%. Similar to the 200 mg/kg dose, %ΔT mean values of −1.5 to −2.7% reflect the same plateauing effect. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA of %ΔT showed significant effects of treatment group [F­(4,25) = 5.48, p = 0.0026] and time [F­(11,275) = 6.96, p < 0.001], with no significant group × time interaction [F­(44,275) = 1.13, p = 0.271]. The 100 mg/kg extract showed a consistent antipyretic profile, and the absence of a clear additional effect at 200 mg/kg suggests a possible plateau response, as shown in Table S6. Ultimately, the results of this study show that this extract has a significant antipyretic effect. This effect is dose-dependent and maximum at a dose of 100 mg/kg. Although not as effective as the standard drug, tolfenamic acid, the rectal temperatures of extract-treated animals are much closer to the normal physiological range.

Discussion

The present study indicates that the Tri-Phol-That extract possesses highly significant antipyretic activities in mice with turpentine-induced pyrexia, as indicated by the reduction of rectal temperature in a dose-dependent manner, which had a maximum effect in the doses of 100–200 mg/kg of the extract. Though it was not as effective as tolfenamic acid, it reduced the temperature to the physiological range, which indicates its clinically relevant antipyretic activity. In accordance with the pharmacological results, the HPLC results for the phytochemical profile suggested that rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol were the major marker flavonoids present in the extract. The concentration of rutin (4.01 μg/g dried extract) was more than the other two flavonoids, while kaempferol (2.13 μg/g) was more than quercetin (0.96 μg/g). The RSD % values were minimal (1.23–1.99% %RSD) indicating both reliable and precise characterization of contents.

Although quercetin, rutin, and kaempferol were identified in the Tri-Phol-That extract, their individual in vivo pharmacological activities were not directly tested in this study. Thus, their documented biological activities are discussed as supportive mechanistic evidence rather than direct proof of their independent contributions to the observed activity of the extract. Regarding quercetin, multiple preclinical studies have demonstrated its ability to significantly reduce fever and pain responses in rodent models. The antipyretic effect of quercetin has been attributed mainly to the suppression of prostaglandin E2 synthesis through direct inhibition of COX-2, together with modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. , These mechanisms are consistent with the present molecular docking results, which showed that quercetin exhibited strong binding affinity toward COX-2 (−8.44 kcal/mol), supporting its potential contribution to the antipyretic and antinociceptive activities of thr Tri-Phol-That extract.

Regarding rutin, rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside) has been reported to exert antinociceptive activity in acetic acid-induced writhing and hot plate models, as well as antipyretic activity in yeast-induced pyrexia models in rodents. These pharmacological effects have been associated with several anti-inflammatory mechanisms, including inhibition of COX-2, suppression of NF-κB activation, and reduction of iNOS expression. − In the present study, the molecular docking results showed that rutin exhibited its strongest binding affinity toward COX-1 (−7.55 kcal/mol), suggesting that rutin may contribute to the activity of Tri-Phol-That extract through a complementary and nonredundant mechanism alongside other flavonoids in the extract.

Regarding kaempferol, previous studies have shown that kaempferol can suppress the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibit NF-κB activation in several experimental models. , Recent evidence also supports its potential analgesic activity. In the present study, kaempferol exhibited preferential docking affinity toward mPGES-1 (−9.97 kcal/mol), suggesting that it may contribute to the pharmacological activity of Tri-Phol-That extract by modulating prostaglandin E2 biosynthesis at the synthase level. This mechanism may be distinct from classical COX inhibition and may provide an additional pathway through which the extract exerts antipyretic and antinociceptive effects.

To complement the mechanistic elucidation, molecular docking studies were performed to identify the potential source of anti-inflammatory and antipyretic activities found in the in vivo study. The binding affinities and interaction profiles for all the above-mentioned flavonoids varied on the basis of the compound and protein target (Table , Figures –). For COX-2, quercetin showed the strongest binding affinity (−8.44 kcal/mol) in the mouse isoform 1CX2, comparable to the reference drug tolfenamic acid (−8.52 kcal/mol), followed by kaempferol (−7.91 kcal/mol); however, rutin’s binding energy was suboptimal. Quercetin and kaempferol (Figure ) are predicted to form hydrogen bonds with ARG120, TYR355, and TYR385, which are required for binding the substrate and catalyzing the reaction. These results and the presence of hydrophobic interactions in the cyclooxygenase channel indicate that quercetin could play a role in COX-2 inhibition. These results also suggest a strong binding affinity of quercetin to COX-2. Similar affinity results were obtained from human COX-2 (5KIR); however, the binding affinity of the human isoform for flavonoids is lower than for the mouse isoform. Tolfenamic acid (−8.67 kcal/mol) had the highest binding energy of all compounds, followed by quercetin (−5.82 kcal/mol) and kaempferol (−5.69 kcal/mol). As shown in Figure , these compounds showed hydrogen bond interactions with SER353, TYR355, TYR385, and hydrophobic interactions with VAL349, LEU352, and VAL523. These results provided additional evidence of quercetin and kaempferol’s role in COX-2 inhibition, albeit less powerful than the reference drug. In COX-1 (3N8X), rutin demonstrated a higher binding affinity (−7.55 kcal/mol) compared to kaempferol and quercetin, indicating a different binding orientation and possibly a different mode of action within the COX-1 active site. Figure shows the hydrogen bond network between rutin and the functional residues involved in the catalytic activity of the enzyme (HIS90, ARG120, TYR355, TYR385, SER530). This wide-ranging hydrogen bond network seems to be due to the glycosidic moiety of rutin that allows it to be more involved in the whole binding pocket. Kaempferol and quercetin had a smaller number of hydrogen bonding interactions and a lower binding affinity for COX-1 compared with the other two compounds. The binding affinity for mPGES-1 (PDB ID: 4YL1) was greater for kaempferol (−9.97 kcal/mol) than for tolfenamic acid (−8.60 kcal/mol). In Figure , kaempferol, which formed a hydrogen bond with SER127 and a hydrophobic bond with VAL128, had a stable binding conformation. On the other hand, quercetin and tolfenamic acid could not form hydrogen bonds, and their binding was stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, which might not significantly contribute to their binding affinity. However, the binding energy of rutin was unfavorable (positive) despite forming many hydrogen bonds, indicating its poor orientation in the binding pocket.

The targets of the flavonoids were selective. The most favorable docking score among the tested flavonoids was observed for the quercetin–COX-2 complex, suggesting that quercetin may contribute to the observed pharmacological effects. Rutin had a greater binding to COX-1, likely due to its wide-ranging hydrogen bond networks. Kaempferol showed the most favorable docking score toward mPGES-1 among the tested flavonoids, suggesting a possible contribution to prostaglandin-related pathways. Docking results suggest that the coexisting flavonoids may interact with multiple targets (COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1), potentially contributing to the observed anti-inflammatory and antipyretic activities. However, these findings are based on in silico predictions and require further experimental validation to confirm direct enzyme inhibition. Although a murine animal model was used in the in vivo screening experiments, human and ovine structures were used for docking studies in order to take advantage of the high-resolution crystal structures with cocrystallized ligands available in the PDB. It should be noted that several key residues involved in ligand recognition, including ARG120, TYR355, TYR385, SER530, SER127, and VAL128, are highly conserved across species; therefore, the docking results may provide supportive mechanistic context for the observed in vivo effects.

In the pyrexia model, the extract at 100 mg/kg lowered rectal temperatures toward the physiological range (36.37–36.82 °C). When the dosage was increased to 200 mg/kg, it did not improve this effect. This is consistent with the %ΔT analysis, which found no significant difference in temperatures for 100 mg/kg compared with 200 mg/kg. This finding may indicate a saturation effect, where there is maximal occupancy of the relevant febrile signaling mechanisms, possibly indicative of modulation of prostaglandin-mediated pathways, which is consistent with the docking predictions suggesting potential interactions with COX enzymes. − The extract was a powerful antinociceptive agent. The acetic acid-induced writhing model showed dose-dependent inhibition, with a maximum effect of 84.64% at the highest dosage of 200 mg/kg, which was not statistically different from the standard antinociceptive drug tolfenamic acid (87.66%). This finding suggests a strong peripheral antinociceptive effect consistent with modulation of inflammatory mediator pathways, including those related to prostaglandin signaling. The antinociceptive properties of rutin and quercetin have been reported previously, and the docking results in the present study provide additional supportive in silico evidence. −

The central pathways of analgesia seemed more intricate. In the tail immersion experiment, there were only minor rises in latency at the highest administration rate (200 mg/kg), indicating a minimum degree of modulation at a spinal reflex level. On the other hand, traces of delayed action were observed in the hot plate test, as the %MPE increased in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with 100 and 200 mg/kg initiating a substantial effect immediately after 90 min, and they approached the effectiveness of tolfenamic acid. This difference may suggest that the extract is more likely acting centrally as an analgesic, mainly at the supraspinal level. Although involvement of opioid or monoaminergic mechanisms cannot be ruled out, studies of flavonoid-rich extracts have revealed mechanisms involved that still deserve possible exploration. The long delay of action may be simply from pharmacokinetic factors, such as absorption or metabolic capability of flavonoid constituents that are well-known to have a delayed action that is often sustained. ,,

In conclusion, the phytochemical, pharmacological, and docking findings collectively indicate that Tri-Phol-That extract exhibits significant antipyretic and analgesic activities. The integration of phytochemical profiling and in silico analysis suggests that these effects may be associated with the potential interactions of flavonoids with multiple inflammatory targets, including COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1. However, it should be emphasized that these proposed mechanisms are based on predictive docking results and require further biochemical and mechanistic validation. Among the identified constituents, rutin may contribute to broader, non-selective interactions with COX-related pathways, while quercetin may be associated with preferential interactions with COX-2 and modulation of inflammatory cytokines. Kaempferol, despite its relatively lower abundance, may also play a contributory role through interactions with additional inflammatory targets.

Conclusion

This indicates that the Tri-Phol-That extract has significant antipyretic and analgesic properties and confirms its traditional antipyretic and analgesic uses. In turpentine-induced pyrexia in mice, Tri-Phol-That extract significantly reduced rectal temperature in a dose-dependent manner, with the strongest effect occurring at doses of 100–200 mg/kg, similar to the reference drug tolfenamic acid. The reduction in acetic acid-induced writhing is a strong indicator for the peripheral antinociceptive effect of the extract. The dose- and time-dependent increase in latency of response in the hot plate test indicates that the antinociceptive effect of the extract is via central mechanisms (especially a supraspinal site). Phytochemical screening showed that rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol are the main flavonoids in the extract with low %RSD between the components. Even if rutin is the most abundant compound, the biological activity is likely to be the result of the mixed and possibly synergistic action of the three flavonoids, since other studies have reported their action on COX, cytokines, and NF-κB. Molecular docking analysis suggested that the tested flavonoids may interact with multiple inflammation-related targets, including COX-1, COX-2, and mPGES-1. Among the flavonoids tested, quercetin showed the most favorable docking score toward COX-2, rutin toward COX-1, and kaempferol toward mPGES-1. Interactions with residues such as ARG120, TYR355, TYR385, SER127, VAL128, and SER530 provided supportive in silico evidence for target engagement. However, these proposed mechanisms are based on predictive docking results and should be interpreted cautiously pending further biochemical and mechanistic validation. Altogether, the data show that the Tri-Phol-That extract produces its pharmacological effect via multiple peripheral or central sites of action. These findings provide scientific evidence in support of the traditional use, showing that it has potential for further development as a phytotherapeutic drug for the treatment of fever and pain.
