The role of mesenchymal stem cells in IgA nephropathy: current evidence and future directions
Authors: Yuqi Liu, Yi Zhao, Jiaqian Xie, Jiewei Chen, Kaijin Yao, Wenmin Chen, Lingqian Zheng, Tianbiao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary glomerular disease, particularly IgA Nephropathy (IgAN), is one of the leading causes of kidney disease burden. IgAN is characterized by IgA deposition in the mesangial area; this is a typical pathological feature that leads to nephritis symptoms. Though the cause is unknown, the quadripartite attack theory is generally accepted. There are many reasons, but the biggest is an imbalance in the immune system. Recently, some studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can regulate IgA production and clearance by controlling B cells, T cells, and mucosal epithelial cells. Currently, the standard treatment for IgAN primarily relies on supportive care and immunosuppression, but these approaches are ineffective and have serious side effects. In contrast, stem cell therapy is a promising option and shows great promise in diagnosing and treating various kidney diseases. This review provides an update on recent advances in IgAN using stem cell therapy, including disease mechanisms, treatment pathways, clinical trials, case reports, safety concerns, current challenges, and prospects.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, IgA nephropathy (IgAN), Disease mechanism, Supportive care, Immunosuppressive therapy

Introduction

The pathological characteristics of IgA nephropathy (IgAN) are mainly IgA deposition in the glomerular mesangial region. Clinically, asymptomatic hematuria is frequently encountered. In some cases, the onset may be preceded by prodromal symptoms related to respiratory or gastrointestinal tract infections. There are significant regional differences in the incidence of IgAN. The incidence in East Asia is particularly prominent, accounting for about 40% of the cases of primary glomerulonephritis in China [1, 2]. In addition, there are significant individual differences in the prognosis of IgAN patients: some patients can maintain good renal function for a long time, but during the average 5-6-year follow-up period, about 20-50% of patients progress to renal failure and require dialysis or kidney transplantation to maintain life [3, 4]. However, graft and patient survival are still affected by factors such as donor, treatment plan, and viral infections [5]. These findings highlight the poor prognosis of patients with IgAN. Although the pathogenesis of IgAN has not been fully clarified, the “four hit hypothesis” has been widely accepted (Fig. 1). A Normal IgA1 molecule has galactose and neuraminic acid residues at the end of the O-glycosylation chain in the hinge region. In contrast, galactose IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) has abnormal O-glycosylation in this region. These abnormal glycosylated IgA1 molecules can induce an autoimmune response, leading to the deposition of immune complexes in the mesangial region, which in turn activates the complement system and triggers type III hypersensitivity - a mechanism confirmed by many cohort studies [6, 7]. IgA is mainly produced by plasma cells in the mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue. IgA deposited in the kidney includes secretory IgA and aggregated IgA [8, 9]. Therefore, mucosal immune abnormalities are considered to play a key role in the pathogenesis of IgAN. For example, intestinal dysbiosis or chronic bacterial infection may compromise intestinal mucosal integrity, increase epithelial permeability, and promote excessive IgA secretion. The increased permeability also enhanced exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which activated toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on B cells and induced methylation of the core 1 β3-galactoside-specific molecular chaperone (Cosmc). This epigenetic change impairs galactosyltransferase activity, thereby promoting the production of galactose-deficient IgA1 [6, 10]. Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) activation leads to heterogeneous pathological damage: dendritic cell-mediated lesions primarily manifest as mesangial hyperplasia. At the same time, TLR9 signaling in B cells promotes abnormal glycosylated IgA deposition and drives chronic tissue fibrosis [11, 12]. Since T cells, B cells, and mucosal epithelial cells involved in the pathogenesis of IgAN are all derived from bone marrow precursor cells, some researchers have proposed that IgAN may be, in essence, a stem cell disease [13].

The “quadripartite attack theory” of The Pathogenesis of IgAN. This aberrant immunoglobulin elicits an anti-glycan antibody response, leading to the formation of circulating immune complexes (CICs) containing Gd-IgA1. These CICs are subsequently deposited in the glomerular mesangium via systemic circulation, where they activate the complement system through multiple pathways. This activation results in the release of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic mediators, ultimately promoting glomerular sclerosis and progressive renal damage. This sequential “multi-hit” mechanism constitutes the core pathophysiological process underlying IgAN progression. Created in https://BioRender.com

The current treatment of IgAN includes etiological treatment and supportive treatment. The goal is to reduce IgA production while also effectively controlling hypertension and relieving proteinuria. Conventional treatment includes angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEI/ARBs), glucocorticoids, and cytotoxic drugs [14]. In particular, glucocorticoids combined with cytotoxic drugs are effective in IgAN patients with crescents [15]. However, oral glucocorticoids have significant adverse effects, including infection, hyperglycemia, and osteoporosis [16]. In recent years, the diagnosis and treatment strategy for IgAN have shifted from traditional supportive care to interventions targeting the disease’s core mechanisms. Budesonide enteric-coated capsules can inhibit B-cell proliferation and the secretion of Gd-IgA1 [17]. Atacicept, Telitacicept, and Povetacicept can inhibit the development and maturation of immature B cells by targeting APRIL/BAFF [18–20]. In addition to traditional glucocorticoids and MMF, hydroxychloroquine and complement pathway inhibitors (such as Iptacopan) can inhibit the inflammatory response mediated by IgA immune complex [21, 22]. Renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) [23], sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) [24], and endothelin receptor antagonists (e.g., Sparsentan and Atrasentan) ameliorate the general response to nephron loss [25, 26]. However, clinical evidence indicates that the efficacy of these drugs does not reach optimal levels in certain patient groups. In addition, they are accompanied by potential adverse reactions, including an increased risk of infection, thrombosis, and metabolic disorders [27–29]. Therefore, there is still an urgent need to explore a new treatment for IgAN.

Characteristics and therapeutic variations of different mesenchymal stem cells in renal disease treatment

Stem cells are cells with the ability to differentiate, which can promote the repair and regeneration of damaged tissues. They are distributed in bone marrow, kidney, brain, umbilical cord blood, intestine, adipose tissue, skin, and other human structures and tissues [30–33]. At an early stage of ontogeny, stem cells differentiate into three primordial germ layers, which then give rise to various cell lineages. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) isolated from the mesoderm lineage constitute the basic elements of kidney development. MSCs were first discovered in bone marrow by Friedenstein et al. in the 1970s [34]. The International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) has established minimum identification criteria for MSC, including adherence to plastic surfaces, positive expression of markers such as CD73, CD90, and CD105, lack of expression of CD34 and HLA-DR, and multidirectional differentiation potential [35]. These cells were divided into bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs), adipose tissue mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs), umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs), and placenta mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) according to their sources [36]. There are differences in cell phenotype, proliferation capacity, differentiation tendency, and secretory profile among these MSC subtypes, which affect their applicability and therapeutic effects in the treatment of various diseases.

UCMSCs usually have strong proliferative capacity and significant immunoregulatory activity, characterized by high expression of angiogenic factors (such as VEGF and Ang-1), anti-apoptotic factors (such as HGF), and immunoregulatory cytokines (such as PGE2 and IDO), as well as excellent homing characteristics [37, 38]. These biological advantages make UCMSCs uniquely suited for the treatment of a variety of kidney diseases. In contrast, although BMMSCs have relatively weak proliferative capacity and donor-age-dependent variation, their broader clinical safety profile has made them a reliable cell source in clinical research [39, 40]. BMMSCs have been fully verified in a variety of animal models and clinical trials. It can effectively reduce creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels, reduce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, and show therapeutic effects in chronic kidney disease (CKD), AKI, renal fibrosis, and diabetic nephropathy (DN) [41–43]. ADMSCs offer the advantages of being readily accessible and yielding high cell numbers [44, 45]. Their immunophenotype—characterized by expression of CD36 but not CD106 or podocalyxin—confers reduced immunogenicity, purer stromal stem cell properties, and enhanced suitability for allogeneic cell therapy and tissue engineering applications [46, 47]. Currently, early-stage clinical trials are exploring the therapeutic potential of MSCs in kidney diseases.

Owing to MSCs’ multidirectional differentiation potential, they were initially considered to play a role in tissue repair by inducing differentiation into renal tubular epithelial cells, podocytes, and renal interstitial fibroblasts, thereby exerting therapeutic effects [48]. However, existing studies reveal a more complex mechanism. The current consensus is that differentiation (or transdifferentiation) is not the main pathway of MSC action and is considered a secondary mechanism because it occurs to a limited extent in vivo. In contrast, the therapeutic effects of MSCs are mainly attributed to their potent paracrine effects. By secreting a wealth of bioactive factors, including growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles (such as exosomes), MSCs play a key role in immune regulation, anti-inflammation, anti-apoptosis, and pro-angiogenesis in the local microenvironment, thereby promoting tissue repair and regeneration [32, 49, 50]. This mechanism has been verified in the experiments of J. Gao et al. [51]. The Experiment showed that BMMSCs infused via vein or artery were mainly retained in the lungs in vivo. Although there was homing in the kidney, the proportion was low. This conclusion supports the dominant role of MSC paracrine action in the therapeutic effect. Numerous studies have confirmed that MSCs can secrete PGE2 [52], IDO [53, 54], interleukin-10 (IL-10) [55, 56], and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), among others, which jointly play a therapeutic role [57]. Their primary mechanism involves regulating immune homeostasis: MSC-secreted factors, including PGE2, TGF-β, and IL-10, may rebalance pro-inflammatory Th1/Th17 responses toward anti-inflammatory Th2 and regulatory T cell traits, and may also stimulate macrophages to differentiate into a reparative M2 phenotype [58, 59]. MSCs further attenuate renal fibrosis progression by inhibiting key profibrotic signalling pathways (including the EMT-β1/Smad and NF-κB pathways), thereby downregulating fibrosis markers and suppressing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [60]. Concurrently, they exert direct cytoprotective effects by activating autophagy-related axes and secreting factors such as EGF, HGF, and VEGF, thereby preventing apoptosis [61]. Moreover, MSCs enhance cellular antioxidant defences by activating the Nrf2/ARE pathway—upregulating enzyme activities such as SOD, CAT, and HO-1 while directly scavenging reactive oxygen species [61, 62]. These interrelated mechanisms collectively mitigate renal inflammation (Fig. 2).

These mediators inhibit key profibrotic pathways, including TGF-β1/Smad2/3 phosphorylation and NF-κB activation, thereby blocking Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) and downregulating pro-fibrotic gene expression. Concurrently, MSCs modulate immunity by promoting a shift from pro-inflammatory (Th1, Th17, M1) to anti-inflammatory/regulatory (Treg, M2) phenotypes, and mitigate tissue injury by reducing oxidative stress (via Nrf2/HO-1 activation) and apoptosis (via Bax/Bcl-2/Caspase-3 axis regulation). Previous studies have confirmed that MSCs have therapeutic potential in some diseases. At present, MSC therapy has been systematically investigated in preclinical studies for heart disease [63], neurological disorders [64], inflammatory bowel conditions [65], treatment-resistant graft-versus-host disease [66], bone-related ailments, and autoimmune diseases such as lupus, which provides experimental evidence for its possible clinical application in the future [67]. MSCs have demonstrated considerable promise in the treatment of kidney diseases. Many studies have confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in CKD, AKI, and DN [33, 36, 68]. Although research on MSC-based therapies in IgAN remains less extensive than in other conditions, accumulating evidence strongly supports their potential effectiveness and warrants further investigation of their application in IgAN. This article reviews laboratory results and clinical trial data, analyzes the roles of different MSC therapies in IgAN, and describes the therapeutic challenges associated with MSC use.

Preclinical study of MSCs in IgAN and potential mechanisms

Animal studies extensively employ models to investigate the onset and therapeutic strategies for IgAN. However, owing to the significant differences in the immune system between rodents and humans, as well as the differences in the structure and function of IgA and its receptors, the development of IgA nephropathy animal models typically focuses on specific pathological features of the disease rather than recapitulating the entire disease process [69, 70]. At present, the commonly used animal models of IgAN include spontaneous animal models (ddY mice and HIGA mice), induced animal models (oral antigen induction model, dextran sulfate sodium combined with LPS model, and adenovirus-mediated model, etc.) and genetically engineered models (Gd-IgA1 transgenic mice, BAFF transgenic mice, and α1KI/CD89 transgenic mice, etc.) [70, 71]. The spontaneous IgA model can simulate human mesangial IgA deposition and proliferation well. Still, it cannot fully simulate key pathological features, such as the abnormal glycosylation of human IgA1, and the model’s stability and individual variation limit its applicability [72, 73]. The induced model can simulate immune complex deposition and kidney injury, and is relatively simple to construct. However, most induced models are acute, making it difficult to reproduce the chronic and spontaneous disease process in humans fully. Humanized models (such as the α1KI-CD89 double transgene) more closely mimic human disease by introducing human genes and presenting typical symptoms such as proteinuria and hematuria [74, 75]. However, the construction is complex and expensive, so most preclinical MSC data are limited to HIGA and chemical induction models [69].

Immunomodulatory mechanisms

Imasawa et al. divided HIGA mice into two groups: one group received bone marrow transplantation (BMT) from normal C57BL/6j mice (B6→HIGA), and the other group received BMT from the same-strain HIGA mice (HIGA→HIGA) as controls. After 26 weeks of treatment, B6→HIGA mice showed significantly reduced renal IgA and C3 deposition, glomerulosclerosis, mesangial expansion, urinary protein, and serum macromolecular IgA. In the subsequent mechanism analysis, the researchers pointed out that BMMSCs may replace abnormal hematopoietic stem cells, restore the function of T and B cells, and reduce the production of pathogenic macromolecular IgA. In addition, BMT can replace recipient immune cells with donor BMMSCs and regenerate glomerular cells. Thus, the investigators suggested that BMT not only regenerates the immune system but may also directly repair the glomeruli through cell regeneration [76, 77] (Table 1). In a study of adipose-derived stem cell (ASC) therapy in a mouse model of IgAN, ASCs were isolated from the inguinal fat pad of HIGA mice before and after disease onset, as well as from human adipose tissue. Female HIGA mice were divided into four groups: Control group (Group 1), preonset group (Group 2), postonset group (Group 3), and human ASC group (Group 4). Treatment was started when microalbuminuria was present. The results revealed that all ASC-treated mice (Groups 2, 3, and 4) showed significant reductions in 24-hour proteinuria, mesangial proliferation, and glomerulosclerosis, and substantial reductions in serum creatinine and serum IgA levels in mice treated with human ASCs (Group 4). These findings suggest that ASCs have therapeutic effects in IgAN and are effective across all stages of disease progression. In addition, flow cytometry analysis showed that Th1 cytokine activity and Th1/Th2 ratio decreased in the kidneys of treated mice in the ASC-treated groups (Groups 2, 3, and 4), suggesting that the effect of ASCs on IgAN is achieved by regulating Th1/Th2 cytokine balance through paracrine mechanisms [78] (Table 1). The balance between Th1 and Th2 cytokines is essential for the precise control of cell- and humoral-immune responses. Restoring the imbalance of the Th1/Th2 equilibrium might be an essential immunological mechanism for controlling the onset and development of IgAN. Suzuki et al. [79] confirmed, using bone marrow transplantation experiments, that ddY mice at the early stage of IgAN showed clear Th1 polarization, whereas those in the quiescent phase showed Th2 polarization. Bone marrow cells from diseased mice were transplanted into quiescent recipients, leading to Th1 polarization and increased IgA/IgG deposition in the glomerulus and proteinuria. On the contrary, bone marrow transplantation from quiescent mice can reduce kidney injury in diseased recipients. This study not only demonstrated that bone marrow-derived cells play an essential role in the development of IgAN but also showed the extent to which the Th1-type immune response affects the progression of the illness. These results are very similar to the efficiency of ASC-based treatment, which gets good results by changing the Th1/Th2 balance. Whether through exogenous stem cell infusion or endogenous regulation of bone marrow cell function, restoring the balance between Th1 and Th2 responses may be the core immune strategy for preventing and delaying the onset and progression of IgAN.

Antifibrotic mechanisms

The antigen-antibody complex model is one of the earliest animal experimental models applied in the study of IgAN. This model is constructed through the following methods: oral or intravenous administration of bovine serum albumin (BSA) to promote the formation of immune complexes and the deposition of IgA in the kidneys; subcutaneous injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) to establish a liver injury state and simulate the impact of abnormal liver function on the clearance of immune complexes; and supplemented with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation to simulate the abnormal mucosal immunity triggered by infection. The combined action of these intervention measures can induce an animal model that closely resembles the pathological features and clinical manifestations of human IgAN [69]. To examine the impact of UC-MSCs on stem cell homing and glomerular mesangial cell activity, D.W. Zhang and colleagues administered UCMSCs intravenously via the tail vein of IgAN mice. This study demonstrated that treatment with UCMSCs can mitigate renal fibrosis, alleviate hematuria and proteinuria, and notably relieve the symptoms of IgAN [80] (Table 1). In the above experiments, renal fibrosis was reduced in the treatment group. Chronic fibrosis is the underlying pathological process by which IgAN progresses to end-stage renal disease. Previous studies have detailed the mechanisms of renal fibrosis [81, 82]. Epithelial injury triggers fibroblast proliferation and macrophage infiltration. Then it releases fibrosis signals, such as TGF-β/Smad and NF-κb, which drive EMT and increase the expression of profibrotic molecules, ultimately aggravating renal injury. MSCs modulate proinflammatory factor expression, impede signalling pathways, and prevent EMT progression at this point [83–86].

Clinical study of MSCs and IgAN

IgAN is a common health problem worldwide. At present, the treatment of IgAN mainly depends on drugs such as renin angiotensin system inhibitors, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants [87]. These therapies can effectively inhibit immune-mediated kidney injury and exhibit strong anti-inflammatory effects, but long-term use still carries side effects and related risks. Cell therapy has shown its potential across a variety of diseases, opening a new avenue for IgAN treatment. Although numerous laboratory studies have shown that MSCs can effectively delay the progression of IgAN, clinical research on MSCs for IgAN treatment is mainly limited to case reports, and the available evidence is insufficient. So far, only one phase I clinical trial has been carried out. The trial is an open-label, multicenter, dose-increasing phase I study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of allogeneic ADMSCs (referred to as ASCs or ADR-001) in the treatment of refractory IgAN [88]. In this study, nine patients with refractory IgAN were divided into two groups: one group received a single intravenous injection of ADR-001, and the other group received two injections of an equivalent dose of ADR-001 (with an interval of 2 weeks). Analysis shows that ADR-001 has a good safety profile in patients with refractory IgAN and can significantly reduce proteinuria and markers of renal injury. Still, its long-term efficacy appears limited. In addition, there was no significant change in glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) throughout the study period, so it was impossible to draw a clear conclusion about the impact of ADR-001 on eGFR. It is worth noting that another MSC study on the treatment of diabetic nephropathy showed that intravenous injection of allogeneic bone marrow-derived MSCs could significantly delay the decline in eGFR. The therapy was not only safe and reliable, but also well tolerated by the subjects [89].

Long-term clinical trials in patients with severe IgAN complicated by CKD have shown promising outcomes. Researchers discovered that a therapeutic procedure involving methylprednisolone pulse therapy followed by infusion of autologous adipose tissue-derived stromal vascular fraction (SVF)/MSCs significantly enhanced renal function and tissue repair. Over 8–10 years of follow-up, eGFR remained stable or improved. Later biopsies revealed a marked decrease in mesangial cell proliferation, podocyte process regeneration, and complete removal of IgA deposits. The treatment regimen proved safe, with no significant side effects [90].

In addition to clinical studies, case reports also support the potential use of MSC therapy. A patient suffering from acute renal failure and diagnosed with IgAN was given 120 million units of UCMSCs via IV route, resulting in a reduction of creatinine levels to 1.0 mg/dL that stayed normal for 6 months (baseline 3.0 mg/dL), significant improvement in anaemia and hypertension, and no adverse events were reported during administration or follow up [91]. In conclusion, stem cell infusion is a feasible, well-tolerated treatment with significant promise for patients with refractory or advanced IgAN, improving renal function and relieving symptoms. Although existing clinical trials and case analyses mainly focus on treatment safety and short-term efficacy, further research is needed to explore the long-term benefits of this intervention. Future research should also focus on elucidating the potential molecular mechanisms underlying stem cell therapy in IgAN patients.

Safety and challenges of MSC therapy

Short- and long-term safety issues

Although clinical trials showed that MSC therapy did not cause severe adverse reactions, there were still potential risks in stem cell research at the experimental stage. A systematic meta-analysis of 36 clinical trials found a significant association between MSC infusion and transient fever. However, this study did not find any substantial correlation between treatment and severe infusion reaction, infection, organ-related complications, or cancer. It is worth noting that 13 of these trials used mismatched allogeneic MSC, but no acute adverse reactions were reported. This finding supports the confirmed immunomodulatory properties of MSCs and further verifies the safety of allogeneic stem cell therapy [92]. In animal experiments, the distribution of stem cells in mice was tracked by quantitative PCR. The results showed that 99% of the cells were removed from the blood stream within 5 min, and 83% of the, cells remained in the lung, leading to pulmonary embolism [93] (Fig. 3). In a phase I/II clinical study to evaluate the efficacy of UCMSCs in the treatment of acute exacerbation of chronic liver failure (ACLF), a total of 550 adverse events were reported, of which 14.7% were considered to be related to the treatment of UCMSCs. Serious adverse events included thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and anemia. Events such as fever, electrolyte disorders, and gastrointestinal bleeding were also classified as possibly related to UCMSC treatment [94]. It is necessary to conduct additional large-scale phase III/IV clinical trials to comprehensively evaluate the intervention’s long-term safety and therapeutic effects.

Safety Issues and Challenges of MSCs Therapy. Key challenges include reduced cell survival rates due to mechanical stress, oxidative stress, host microenvironmental conditions, and variations in patient age and underlying pathologies—factors that collectively compromise therapeutic efficacy. To address these limitations, emerging strategies such as hydrogel encapsulation, exosome-based alternatives, hypoxic preconditioning, and standardized cell culture protocols have been proposed. In the past, several studies have reported the role of MSCs in tumorigenesis. It was believed that stem cell properties, such as self-renewal, multi-directional differentiation potential, anti-apoptosis, and strong migratory ability, could promote tumor formation and development [95–97]. Still, recent studies have shown that Human MSCs are not susceptible to malignant transformation in vitro and do not significantly increase the risk of tumors in clinical applications [98]. The meta-analysis by Lalu et al. also showed no direct association between MSC therapy and the development of malignancy [92]. In the meta-analysis by Thompson et al., there was no significant increase in the risk of malignancy or ectopic tissue formation and a substantial reduction in the overall risk of death. This newer, larger body of clinical evidence indicates that MSC therapy does not increase cancer risk and has a favorable safety profile in current research [99]. In addition, the extruded Nanovesicles (eNVs) extracted from UCMSCs by mechanical extrusion showed no evidence of tumorigenicity in vitro and in vivo [100]. These findings jointly confirm the safety of MSC-based therapy in clinical application.

Challenges of MSC therapy

Stem cell therapy has been widely studied in neurological diseases, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and cardiovascular diseases [101]. However, most studies are still in phases Ⅰ and Ⅱ, and phase III data are scarce. In addition, MSC therapy faces multiple challenges, including significant heterogeneity, immunocompatibility affected by the microenvironment, weak homing ability, difficulty in expansion, unclear mechanism of action, and lack of standardization of production [102].

The survival rate of stem cells is one of the main factors affecting treatment efficacy and is affected by many factors, such as the source of stem cells, target tissues, and individual differences among patients (Fig. 3). At the technical level, the preparation and infusion of stem cells also determine the final therapeutic effect. During infusion, cells are often damaged by stretching or flow [103]. Even if they can be injected into the body through the artery, more than 90% of MSCs are mechanically impacted in the precapillary arterioles, leading to ischemia, and 86% of MSCs die within 72 h [104]. Intravenously injected MSCs are mostly retained in pulmonary microvessels and then gradually migrate to the liver, spleen, and other organs, and the number of cells decreases rapidly with time [105]. However, the transient survival and activation of MSCs in the lung can also produce powerful anti-inflammatory effects through paracrine mechanisms [93]. Wagner et al. [106] demonstrated that encapsulating cells within hydrogels effectively protects them against mechanical stress during injection, and with increasing hydrogel mass, cell viability progressively improves. Hypoxic preconditioning is an effective strategy to increase cell survival following infusion. Experimental findings by Chang et al. [61] demonstrated that hypoxic preconditioning upregulated the expression of VEGF and HIF-1α in MSCs, thereby activating multiple signalling pathways and improving their therapeutic efficacy. In addition, further studies confirmed that hypoxia preconditioning significantly increased the survival rate of human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (h-AMSCs) at 1% oxygen concentration by upregulating key factors (including vascular endothelial growth factor, bone marrow cytokines, Oct-4, Bcl-2, and Hsp27) [107]. Therefore, improving the cell preparation process and infusion method can significantly enhance cell survival. Also, allogeneic stem cells pose the risk of immune rejection and disease transmission, which makes it difficult for cell therapy to develop. Lalu et al. [92] reported that, among 36 studies, 75% of MSCs were cultured with fetal bovine serum (FBS). FBS could introduce animal pathogens, such as prions, and increase immunogenicity. One thing worth mentioning is that there’s only one study that addresses the risk. This finding underscores the importance of ensuring that all cells used in MSC treatment are prepared identically.

To conclude, although stem cell therapy shows promise, it faces considerable challenges, including low cell survival rates, high costs, and an unknown mechanism of action post-infusion. Standardized cell preparations and improved delivery methods are needed to advance this area.

Discussion

The preclinical trials part of this article summarizes the existing evidence supporting the immunomodulatory and antifibrotic results of MSC therapy. MSC therapy represents a promising therapeutic strategy due to its multimodal mechanisms of action and potential to synergize with pharmacological agents, thereby enhancing overall efficacy. A previous study showed that MSCs have renal-protective effects via anti-apoptotic and antioxidative stress mechanisms [61, 108, 109]. Dysregulated apoptosis is a key pathological mechanism implicated in the progression of various kidney diseases, including IgAN [110, 111]. It has been shown that hypoxic-preconditioned MSCs can reduce the ratio of pro-apoptotic protein Bax to anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and inhibit caspase-3 activity in renal tissues, thereby reducing programmed cell death of glomerular cells in a glomerulonephritis model [61]. As a type of glomerulonephritis, this mechanism might have considerable therapeutic significance for IgAN. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct more experiments on MSC therapy that can regulate apoptosis. Gd-IgA1 immune complex deposition in the glomerular mesangium, either directly or indirectly, leads to mesangial cells producing ROS through complement activation, causing oxidative stress. It creates a vicious cycle of inflammation that worsens kidney damage even more [112]. The oxidative stress mechanism caused by immune complex deposition is found in many kinds of kidney disease. Research shows that oxidative stress leads to glomerular damage in immune-mediated glomerulonephritis. Metabolic disorders such as diabetic nephropathy, high-sugar, and high-fat environments also produce a lot of ROS. MSCs can significantly increase their own HIF-1α/VEGF expression, activating the upstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. And then it will activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway in the kidney, increasing the expression of antioxidant proteins, which can scavenge ROS [61]. Since oxidative stress and Nrf2 pathway disorder have been confirmed as pathological mechanisms in many kidney diseases, it is reasonable to assume that the mechanism by which MSCs produce antioxidant effects via Nrf2 activation would also apply to IgAN. But this assumption needs to be validated by more rigorous in vivo and in vitro experiments.

Conclusion

In short, MSC-based therapy is a good option for treating IgAN, especially when other ways don’t work well. Its multi-modal mode of action fits well with the complicated pathophysiology of IgAN; it can provide both symptomatic relief and possibly modify the disease. The studies show the significant benefits of MSC therapy, including vigorous paracrine activity that helps regulate immunity, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, and the creation of a kidney-regenerative microenvironment. But there are still some limits to how we use it right now, so we need to conduct more thorough, detailed research. Future work will need to focus on refining cell preparation methods, demonstrating their efficacy through animal studies and human clinical trials, and identifying biomarkers that predict treatment response. As more preclinical studies are conducted and clinical investigations become more widespread, it is expected that MSC therapy will become a feasible approach to prevent the progression of IgAN and protect kidney function.
