Economic factors influencing utilization of world health organization recommended ANC visits among women in Jordan: an analysis of Jordan population and family health survey 2023
Authors: Lilian Nuwabaine, Joseph Kawuki, John Baptist Asiimwe, Benjamin Betunga, Mathius Amperiize, Angella Namulema, Imelda Namatovu, Shamim Nabidda, Annet Patience Nakalega, Quraish Sserwanja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) is crucial for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality by identifying and managing complications early during pregnancy. In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its guidelines, recommending a minimum of eight ANC visits to improve maternal health outcomes. Despite Jordan’s efforts to enhance maternal healthcare, disparities in ANC utilization persist, particularly among vulnerable populations such as refugees. This study aimed to assess the economic factors influencing utilization of the WHO-recommended eight ANC visits among women in Jordan.MethodsThe study used data from the 2023 Jordan Population and Family Health Survey (JPFHS). A two-stage sampling technique was employed, selecting 12,595 women aged 15–49 years. The primary outcome was the number of ANC visits, categorized as less than eight and eight or more visits. Economic variables such as wealth index, bank account ownership, land and house ownership, and decision-making power on household purchases were analyzed. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regressions were conducted to assess the relationship between economic factors and ANC utilization.ResultsOf the women surveyed, 89.2% attended at least eight ANC visits. Wealth index emerged as the only significant economic factor influencing ANC utilization. Women from richer (aOR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.38–0.94), middle (aOR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.28–0.70), poorer (aOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.27–0.70), and poorest (aOR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.15–0.37) wealth indices were less likely to utilize the WHO-recommended eight ANC visits compared to those in the richest wealth index. Additionally, socio-demographic factors such as women from female-headed households, those who initiated ANC after the first trimester, those not visited by a field worker, women who involved their partners in healthcare decision-making, and Syrian refugees living in camps were associated with more likelihood of utilizing eight or more ANC visits. Furthermore, lower odds of utilizing the WHO-recommended ANC visits were found among women from the Southern region, households with five or more members, and those not using the internet.ConclusionEconomic disparities play a critical role in influencing ANC utilization in Jordan. Targeted interventions, such as financial support for low-income women and addressing regional healthcare access inequalities, could enhance ANC coverage. These findings underscore the need for policies that address both direct and indirect costs associated with maternal healthcare.Keywords: Antenatal care, Economic factors, Utilization, Jordan

Background

Antenatal care (ANC) is essential for promoting the health and safety of both mothers and their unborn children during pregnancy. Through regular monitoring, ANC helps identify potential complications early, enabling timely interventions that can lead to improved health outcomes and significantly lower neonatal and infant mortality rates [1, 2]. ANC encompasses a range of services, including fetal health assessments, screening for and managing maternal infections and non-communicable diseases, educating mothers on warning signs and birth planning, and providing necessary micronutrient supplements and vaccinations [1]. In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) increased the recommended number of antenatal care (ANC) visits from four to a minimum of eight contacts, with the first contact to occur within the first 12 weeks of pregnancy [3]. The updated WHO ANC model emphasizes that each ‘contact’ should go beyond a basic ‘visit,’ serving as an opportunity to deliver comprehensive, high-quality care, including medical support, guidance, and relevant information throughout the pregnancy [3]. By using the term ‘contact’ instead of ‘visit,’ the new guidelines aim to foster a more active and meaningful interaction between pregnant women and their healthcare providers [4]. This increased frequency of contact allows for more opportunities to provide health education and prepare women for childbirth.

Although Jordan stands out in the Middle East, for its efforts to improve maternal healthcare services, disparities persist, particularly among vulnerable populations like refugees [5]. Jordan hosts over 760,000 registered refugees, predominantly from Syria, which adds pressure to its healthcare system. Refugee populations often face challenges accessing healthcare services, including ANC, due to financial constraints, limited healthcare access, and other socioeconomic barriers [6]. Despite Jordan’s progress, research on the status of the WHO recommended eight ANC visits and factors influencing ANC utilization, especially the role of economic determinants, remains limited. Studies in Jordan have explored general maternal health but often overlook the influence of economic factors like household income, working status, land ownership, decision-making power on household purchases, wealth index, and bank account ownership. Understanding these factors is essential to identifying gaps in ANC access and enhancing maternal health outcomes. This manuscript aims to assess the utilization of the WHO-recommended eight ANC visits in Jordan, with a focus on the role of economic factors. This study will fill that gap by analyzing the economic factors influencing ANC utilization, contributing to more targeted health interventions to improve maternal care in Jordan, particularly among vulnerable groups, including refugees.

Methods

Data source, sample design, and collection

The study used data from the 2023 Jordan Population and Family Health Survey (JPFHS) and data were accessed from MEASURE DHS database at http://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm [7]. Samples were selected independently within each sampling stratum, using a two-stage selection process aligned with the sample allocation. Prior to the sample selection, the sampling frame was organized by district and subdistrict within each stratum. Stratification was achieved by separating each of the 12 governorates into urban and rural areas. Furthermore, the Syrian refugee camps located in Zarqa and Mafraq each formed a special sampling stratum leading to a total of 26 sampling strata [7]. In the first stage, clusters were selected using probability proportional to size, based on the number of residential households recorded in the 2015 census. This process ensured implicit stratification and proportional allocation at lower administrative levels. Nine hundred and seventy clusters were chosen using this method. A household listing operation was then conducted in each cluster, generating household lists that were used as the sampling frame for the second stage. Citizenship data of household heads were gathered during the listing to oversample Syrian households living outside refugee camps. In the second stage, 20 households per cluster were selected systematically with equal probability from the newly created household listing [7].

Survey interviewers were restricted to interviewing only the households that had been preselected. No substitutions or changes to these households were permitted during the implementation phase to avoid introducing bias. Women aged 15–49 who were either permanent residents of the selected households or who stayed there the night before the interview were considered eligible for participation. 20,054 households were selected for the survey sample, of which 19,809 were occupied. Among these occupied households, interviews were successfully conducted in 19,475, resulting in a 98% response rate. Within these households, 13,020 eligible women aged 15–49 were identified for individual interviews, with 12,595 completing the interviews, achieving a response rate of 97%. A detailed explanation of the sampling and data collection process is available at https://www.dhsprogram.com/publications/publication-FR388-DHS-Final-Reports.cfm.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was the total number of ANC visits, which were classified into two categories: fewer than 8 contacts, considered inadequate as per the WHO recommendations and coded as 0, and 8 or more contacts, considered adequate and coded as 1.

Independent variables

Economic factors

The independent variables were chosen based on prior research and the availability of relevant data within the JPFHS dataset [8, 9, 10]. The economic factors included in the analysis encompass several key indicators of financial status and autonomy. Bank account ownership is represented as a binary variable (yes or no), indicating access to banking services and financial inclusion. Land and house ownership is categorized into three groups: ownership jointly, ownership by the woman alone, and no ownership, which reflects property rights and financial security, impacting women’s economic independence. Decision-making on large household purchases is assessed through three categories: decision-making jointly, making decisions alone, and not being involved at all, serving as a proxy for financial autonomy and highlighting the control women have over household financial matters. The wealth index measures a household’s relative economic status, calculated from household asset ownership using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and categorized into quintiles: poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest, providing a nuanced understanding of economic conditions influencing health service access. Working status, although relevant, was excluded from the analysis due to a high number of missing data points, which could have skewed the results.

Covariates

To adjust the study’s analysis for potential confounding variables, socio-demographic variables such as age groups (15–24, 25–34, 35–49), education (no formal education, primary, secondary, post-secondary), Working (not working, working), marital status (married, not married including widowed/divorced/separated), residence (urban, rural), nationality (Jordanian, other nationalities, Syrian within camps and Syrian outside camps), ANC initiation timing (within first trimester and after first trimester), having visited a health facility in the last 12 months (yes and no), having been visited by a field health worker (yes and no), household size (above 5 and 5 and below), sex of household head (male and female), internet use (yes and no), healthcare seeking decision (woman alone, jointly and not involved), listening to radio (at least once a week, less than once a week and not at all) and reading newspapers/magazines ( at least once a week, less than once a week and not at all).

Statistical analysis

Complex sample analysis using SPSS version 25.0 statistical software was used to account for the multi-stage cluster study design and weighted data was used to account for the unequal probability sampling in different strata [11]. Proportions and frequencies for all the independent variables were tabulated. Bivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association of each independent variable with utilization of at least eight ANC visits and crude odds ratio (COR), 95% confidence interval (CI) and p-values were presented. Independent variables found significant at p-value less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable models. Two adjusted models were used with model one including only economic variables that were significant at bivariable level while second model included economic variables and the other potential confounders. Multi-collinearity among all independent variables in the models was tested and no variance inflation factor (VIF) above 2 was recorded. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR), 95% CI and p-values were calculated with statistical significance level set at p-value < 0.05.

Ethical consideration

Authorization was obtained from the DHS Program to access the dataset, and a letter of approval was issued, granting permission to download the dataset from the DHS Program website.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The mean age of the women was 36.65 years (SD 8.3), with a mean household size of 5.48 (SD 2.06). The majority (59.4%) were aged 35–49 years. Most women did not have a bank account (81.4%), owned a phone (95.2%), did not own land (95.4%) or a house (92.7%), and 66.1% made large household purchase decisions jointly with their partners. Additionally, 80.3% were using the internet. Of the 12,595 women, 79% initiated ANC in the first trimester, and 89.2% had eight or more ANC visits (details in Table 1).

Among the socio-demographic factors, women from female-headed households (aOR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.02–2.95) and being a Syrian refugee living in camps (aOR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.05–2.18) had higher odds of utilizing the recommended eight ANC visits compared to their counterparts from male-headed households and being a Syrian refugee living outside the camps respectively. Among the health seeking behavior factors, women who initiated ANC during the first trimester (aOR = 10.15, 95% CI: 7.80–12.92), those who involved their partners in healthcare decision-making (aOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.11–1.78) and those who were visited by a field worker (aOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.07–1.79) also had higher odds of utilizing the recommended eight ANC compared to those who initiated ANC after the first trimester, those who made healthcare decisions alone and those who were not visited by the field worker. However, lower odds of utilizing the WHO-recommended ANC visits among the socio demographic factors were found among women from the Southern region (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.48–0.78), households with five or more members (aOR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.44–0.64), and those not using the internet (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.43–0.78), compared to women from the Central region, households with fewer than five members, and internet users, respectively.

Discussion

This study examined the economic factors influencing utilization of the recommended eight WHO ANC visits among women in Jordan. This is the first study to examine the utilization of the WHO recommended ANC visits in Jordan using nationally representative data. This study revealed that the utilization of eight or more ANC visits in Jordan was high (89.2%). The findings are higher compared to similar studies from Nigeria (20.3%), Benin (8%), and the pooled prevalence of Asian and SSA countries (13%) [12, 13, 14]. This high utilization of ANC in Jordan could be attributed to the improvement in maternal healthcare through universal health coverage and other vital factors such as the implementation of health insurance in the country and ensuring availability of health facilities and presence of health professionals in those health facilities in a way of ensuring increased access to care [15, 16].

After controlling for the socio-demographic factors in the final model, only wealth index was the economic factor associated with ANC utilization. Women from lower wealth indices, ranging from richer index to the poorest index, were less likely to attend the recommended eight ANC visits compared to women from the richest wealth index. Wealth index has been shown in similar studies to be associated with utilization of ANC and other maternal health services [13, 17, 14]. The wealth index, as a measure of financial status, may suggest that women within the richest index are better equipped to handle both the direct and indirect expenses related to accessing timely and high-quality healthcare services [18, 17]. Additionally, women from wealthier households tend to be more informed and empowered, granting them greater decision-making authority. This autonomy allows them to access healthcare services more frequently and in a timely manner, ensuring better health outcomes [17, 19]. Given that health insurance is highly prevalent in Jordan [17], these results suggest that, apart from the direct costs of health services, indirect costs play a very important role in accessing healthcare services [20]. This aligns with findings from other studies that reported economic factors such as transportation costs and miscellaneous fees paid for healthcare to influence the women’s utilization of maternal healthcare [21, 22]. Therefore, policy makers need to conduct further research on exploring the influence of indirect costs on accessing and utilizing ANC.

Other economic factors like bank account ownership, household purchase decision making were significantly associated with ANC utilization at Bivariate levels. However, these associations did not remain statistically significant in the multivariate models. This shift suggests that these variables may be confounded by other socioeconomic factors like education and area of residence as these may be a proxy for financial stability.

Apart from economic factors, some socio-demographic factors showed an influence on utilization of ANC visits. Women from female-headed households were more likely to attend the recommended eight antenatal care (ANC) visits, as were those who involved their partners in healthcare decision-making, Syrian refugees living in camps, and women who initiated ANC during the first trimester compared to those from male-headed households, those who made healthcare decisions alone, Jordanians and those who initiated ANC after first trimester respectively. Furthermore, women from households with five or more members, those from the Southern region, and those who did not use the internet were less likely to meet the recommended ANC visits compared to those from smaller households, central region and using internet respectively. These findings highlight how household structure, regional location, nationality, internet access, and partner involvement can influence maternal healthcare utilization.

Women from female-headed households were more likely to attend eight ANC visits, which can be partly attributed to increased autonomy in decision-making seen in such households. Autonomy in healthcare decision-making has been shown to positively influence healthcare-seeking behaviors, as women are often more likely to prioritize their own health when they have the freedom to make such decisions without needing to negotiate with a spouse [23]. A higher likelihood of completing eight ANC visits was observed among women who initiated ANC after the first trimester. This could indicate a compensatory behavior where late starters make up for delayed visits by attending more frequently later in the pregnancy [24]. Women who involved their partners in healthcare decision-making were more likely to meet the recommended ANC visits. Studies have shown that male partner involvement in maternal health significantly boosts healthcare access by providing emotional, financial, and logistical support, thereby increasing the likelihood of attending more ANC visits [24]. The higher odds of meeting ANC recommendations among Syrian women living in refugee camps reflect the structured healthcare provisions typically available in humanitarian settings. Refugee camps often prioritize maternal and child healthcare due to the increased vulnerability of these populations, ensuring that women have regular and reliable access to ANC services [25]. Women from the Southern region and those from larger households (five or more members) were less likely to attend the recommended ANC visits. Geographical barriers, such as easier access to healthcare facilities in the central region compared to other regions, and the economic and logistical strain of managing large households likely explain this trend [5]. Studies have consistently shown that larger families face additional challenges in accessing healthcare services, including transportation costs and the opportunity cost of time spent attending healthcare appointments​. Internet use was associated with higher odds of attending eight ANC visits, highlighting the role of health information accessibility. Women with internet access are more likely to be informed about the importance of ANC and the recommended frequency of visits. The internet serves as a valuable source of health education and easier access to health insurance [17, 26].

Strengths and limitations

The use of nationally representative data makes the findings to be generalizable. Data were extracted from DHS surveys that use standardized procedures such as validated questionnaires hence ensuring the validity of the results. However, data used for the analysis were self-reported which risked recall bias hence possibility of underestimation or overestimation of experiences. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits establishing casual relationships from the observed associations. Lastly, only available data were used hence missing some other factors such as availability of ANC supplies, skills and professionalism of the healthcare workers. Therefore, further studies should be carried out to assess the quality of ANC services provided by facilities and health workers. Also, further qualitative studies need to be done to explore why late starts are able to have more ANC visits compare to early starters.

Conclusion

The study’s findings highlight that while wealth index is the only economic factor that plays an important role in influencing women in Jordan to attend the WHO-recommended eight ANC visits, other non-economic factors significantly shape ANC access. These included socio demographic and health seeking factors like coming from female-headed households, being a Syrian refugee living in camps, involvement of partners in healthcare decision-making, and initiation of ANC during the first trimester. Addressing wealth disparities at household level could be key in ensuring better healthcare access and utilization. Policy makers can implement targeted financial support or subsidies for women from lower wealth indices to reduce barriers to accessing ANC, such as transportation vouchers or free healthcare services. Furthermore, there is a need for further research to assess facility-level and provider-related factors that influence the quality of ANC services.
