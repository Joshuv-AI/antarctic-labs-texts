Predicting sugar-sweetened beverage intake from the brain and known risk factors in adolescents
Authors: Muzayyana Akhmadjonova, Grace E. Shearrer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Low socio-economic status, male sex, and body mass index (BMI) are known risk factors for high sugar sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption in adolescents. The present analysis aimed to predict SSB intake based on known risk factors and resting-state functional magnetic resonance (rsfMRI) connectivity from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study.Methods:Using the year-2 follow up visit Block Kids Food Screener data, participants were categorized as low SSB consumers (<8 floz/day) or high SSB consumers (>16 floz/day). The high and low groups were matched on baseline age, BMI percentile (BMI%) and combined household income (CHI; n/SSBgroup = 841). We used a grid search linear support vector classifier (SVC) to select baseline features from BMI%, sex, age, CHI, and resting state functional connectivity associated with SSB intake. With the selected features we used a binary logistic model to predict high SSB consumption at year-2.Results:The SVC identified sex and 58 functional connections as relevant features and predicted SSB intake at year-2 with 57 % accuracy. Logistic regression revealed visual-right caudate connectivity (predicted probability [PP]: 0.71, pFDR = 0.02) and sex (PPmale: 0.56, PPfemale: 0.72; pFDR < 0.0001) as significant predictors. Post hoc analysis revealed head motion quality control outcomes were independently associated with low CHI (OR = 1.102, p < 0.0001), higher BMI% (OR = 0.93, p < 0.0001), and high SSB intake (OR = 0.842, p = 0.008).Conclusion:The average correlation between visual cortex and right caudate is significantly related to high SSB consumption in adolescents. Participants from low CHI families are at higher risk of exclusion due to excessive motion.

Introduction

Sugar sweetened beverages (SSBs) are the leading source of added sugar in American diet [1]. SSBs include soft drinks such as sodas, fruit-flavored drinks that are not 100 % juice, sweet teas and coffees, sports drinks, and energy drinks [2,3]. Consumption of SSB increases over the course of childhood [4]. Adolescents (12–19y) are the highest consumers of SSBs in the US with two-thirds of adolescents consuming one SSB per day [5]. High intake of SSBs is associated with a myriad of health concerns centered around excess adiposity including type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [6]. Understanding factors that predict SSB consumption in adolescents is essential for developing early and effective intervention strategies.

A hallmark of SSBs is their lack of nutritional value and lack of satiety and satiation signaling [7]. SSB consumption is a hedonically motivated behavior in that it does not target the homeostatic needs of the body but instead relies on motivational signaling for rewarding food intake [8]. In both children and adults, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies suggest that some individuals have heightened motivational signaling in response to rewarding foods [9,10]. This is thought to bias eating decisions and reflect a biological predisposition toward over-consumption of rewarding foods like sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) [11].

Most neuroimaging research on SSBs has focused on their effects on blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) response, particularly in brain networks involved in attention, reward, and taste perception [12–14]. For instance, adults exhibited increased connectivity in the dorsal attention network during milkshake consumption [15]. Similarly, habitual adolescent consumers of Coca-Colaࣨ (Coke), compared to non-consumers, exhibited increased BOLD activity in the posterior cingulate, a region within the dorsal attention network implicated in learning and reward processing [13,16]. These findings may reflect increased attentional engagement with SSB-related cues among habitual consumers [13]. Furthermore, frequent consumption of SSBs may reinforce habitual intake through incentive sensitization, whereby repeated pairing of a food cue with the rewarding experience of palatable taste enhances attentional and reward-related responses to previously neutral cue [11]. Adolescent regular Coke consumers showed increased visual cortex response during exposure to Coke advertisements compared to non-regular consumers [13]. Reward and taste related BOLD responses increased in healthy weight adolescents following repeated milkshake exposure [17], also potentially reflecting reward habitation. In one of the few longitudinal neuroimaging studies, habitual intake of a novel SSB decreased ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) BOLD response to the SSB’s logo, a change associated with behavioral disinhibition [18]. Obesity, often positively correlated with high SSB intake, is associated with increased connectivity in the attention, reward and taste regions [11]. These findings support the idea that consumption of SSB is related to impaired functional integration of brain regions that encode hedonic motivation, as well as gustatory and attentional processing, and reinforces further SSB consumption.

While neuroimaging studies show how SSB intake can change the brain response to SSB cues and subsequent SSB intake, demographic factors, such as socio-economic status (SES) [19] and sex differences [20], remain the strongest predictors of SSB intake. Across age groups, SES is a reliable indicator of SSB intake [19]. A recent meta-analysis reported adults with high SES had 48 % lower odds of consuming SSB compared to a low SES group [19]. SES not only influences SSB consumption but also influences brain connectivity in adolescents. Previous studies using the Adolescent, Brain, Cognition, and Development (ABCD) Study® dataset were able to predict SES status from functional connectivity between brain regions [21]. Lower SES was also related to increased representation of the somatomotor network [21], important for reward and food related behaviors [22]. Although income is only a single aspect of SES, household income alone is related to increased body mass index (BMI) and obesity [23], poor childhood nutrition overall [24] and poorer cognition in children and adolescents [23,25], all of which are associated with high SSB intake [26]. Additionally, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that male children have higher intakes of SSB compared females [20]. Furthermore, taste-evoked functional connectivity to a sweet taste has been shown to differ between men and women [27]. Thus, demographic differences in SSB intake may be reflected in neuroimaging.

Despite this overlap between sex, low SES, BMI, neuroimaging findings and SSB, no study has examined both brain connectivity and demographic predictors of SSB intake in adolescents. Thus, we examined if resting state connectivity and known risk factors (biological sex, age, BMI and SES) could predict SSB intake a year later in the ABCD Study® dataset. First, we utilized supervised machine learning to select which features, out of our set of predictors, are most related to SSB intake. We then used the selected features in a binary logistic model to determine what features are predictive of SSB intake at the year-2 visit. Given the exploratory nature of the analysis, we did not have a priori hypotheses of the brain networks involved. However, we a priori chose household income, sex, age and BMI% as possible predictors in our model.

Methods

Study design

The ABCD Study® is a longitudinal consortium study that began in 2016 across 21 research sites in the USA. The ABCD Study® recruitment and retention strategies are extensively detailed [28] and we will briefly outline those related to the present secondary data analysis. The ABCD 4.0 release was used for the present analysis [29]. The participating consortium were responsible for ensuring human subjects protection in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Parents provided written informed consent and children provided verbal assent. Data was accessed through a National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Data Archive (NDA) use certification agreement (DAR ID: 14383).

Participants

A total of 11,880 children (ages 9–10y) were enrolled in the ABCD Study®, with 11,878 participants at the year-2 follow up visit in the ABCD 4.0 release used presently.

The ABCD Study® was broadly inclusive, with exclusion criteria limited to inability to undergo an MRI scan at the baseline, lack of English fluency in child and certain medical conditions (neurological and birth-related) [30,31].

For the purposes of our analysis, we applied the following exclusion criteria: the parent/caregiver reported their child had serious medical or neurological condition (brain hemorrhage, cerebral palsy, brain tumor, stroke, aneurysm, brain hematoma), schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, diabetes, muscular dystrophy, multiple sclerosis, kidney disease, cancer, lead poisoning, or heart problems;unreliable anthropometric data, including biologically implausible height or weight values and inconsistent weight measurements between baseline and the year-2 follow up;underweight status as defined by body mass index percentile adjusted for age and sex (BMI%) below the 5th percentile;excessive head motion during MRI scanning (greater than 25 % of frames with framewise displacement (FD) > 0.2 mm).

Anthropometrics

Height and weight were measured in duplicate by ABCD Study® staff during in-person assessments. Given the documented issues with the anthropometric data in the ABCD dataset [31,32], we implemented quality control procedures to exclude participants with potentially inaccurate measurements. Participants with biologically implausible height values at baseline (<40 inches) were excluded. Additionally, for participants with a baseline height exceeding 60 inches, we evaluated one-year follow-up data and excluded those exhibiting implausible growth trajectories, such as a decrease in height or an increase of >5 inches.

Each participant had either two or three recorded weight measurements. If the first two values differed by ≤0.1 lbs, we considered them consistent and averaged the two values. If the difference exceeded 0.1 lbs, we checked whether a third measurement was taken. We then identified and averaged the two measurements that were within 0.1 bs of each other. Participants for whom no two weight measurements were consistent (i.e., all pairwise differences >0.1 lbs) were excluded from analysis. Finally, we excluded participants with an average weight ≤40 lbs due to implausibly low values or underweight status.

Derived measures

BMI % was calculated using baseline average height (inches), average weight (pounds), sex at birth and age in years. To screen underweight participants, BMI % values were categorized as underweight (<5 %), recommended weight (5–84.9 %), overweight (85–94.9 %) and obese (≥95 %) based on CDC cutoffs [33].

SSB consumption was determined from self-reported Youth Block Kids Food Screen (YBKFS) at the year-2 assessment. YBKFS is a 41-item food frequency questionnaire designed for use in children ages 2–17y. The BKFS asks subjects to report how often they consumed each food item over the past week, using a scale from “none” to “every day” [34]. We defined SSB consumption using the bkfs_soda variable. The bkfs_soda variable records the participants’ responses to the question: “How many servings (8 fl/oz) of “Drinks like Coke or 7-Up, Sunny Delight, Hawaiian Punch, or aguas frescas (DO NOT include diet soda)” you consume in one day?” Based on previous work from our lab, we categorized SSB consumption into low (0 servings/day), medium (0> servings/day ≤2), and high (>2 servings/day) [35]. We chose to only compare the low and high groups as they do not share a boundary, and we can reasonably assume these two groups are consuming different amounts of SSB per day.

Race was condensed into 16 categories with participants who indicated more than one race classified as multi-racial.

In the original ABCD Study® dataset, CHI was categorized into 10 levels with 1 representing the lowest income and 10 representing the highest income (see Table 1). Given the ordinal nature of this categorization, we treated CHI as a single continuous variable rather than 10 dummy variables, thereby reducing the model complexity.

Matching

To ensure our feature selection was specific to SSB consumption and avoid potential overfitting to the larger SSB group during feature selection, we matched our low SSB participants to the high SSB participants on age, BMI %, and CHI. We used the MatchIt package (v. 4.5.5) in R and matched 1-to-1 using the nearest neighbor method and general linear model as the distance metric. Details related to the matching are in STable 1. However, we used the full pre-matched sample (n = 2191) for the logistic regression analysis to maximize statistical power for significance testing and estimating odds ratios.

Neuroimaging

Neuroimaging data was collected using a unified protocol on three 3 tesla (T) scanners (Siemens Prisma, General Electric 750 and Phillips) at 21 research sites. For details on image acquisition protocol, see Casey et al., 2018 [36]. For the present analysis we used the resting state fMRI (rsMRI) Gordon Network Correlations (abcd_betnet02) and the rsMRI network to subcortical MRI correlations (mrirscor02). The Gordon network is a set of 12 canonical resting state networks [37]. Subcortical regions of interest were extracted using FreeSurfer v5.3 19 region subcortical atlas. Functional connectivity was defined as cortical to cortical and cortical to subcortical Fisher transformed correlations for a total of 416 region of interest (ROI) pairs. For detailed description of data processing and calculation of functional correlations see Hagler et al., 2019 [38]. We performed quality control for excessive head motion by excluding participants with >25 % of frames showing framewise displacement (FD) >0.2 mm.

Analysis

After initial matching, the matched sub-dataset was divided into training and test datasets with an 80–20 split (nTraining = 1261, nTest = 421). Based on the size of the testing dataset we aimed to find 50 to 60 features (based on a rough 1:10 feature to subject heuristic [39]) from the baseline 416 ROIs, CHI, age, BMI %, and sex that best predicted SSB intake at the 2-year follow-up visit. CHI, BMI %, and age were modeled as continuous variables. All continuous variables were mean centered.

With the training dataset, we used a grid search to optimize our feature selection and classification pipeline. Features were selected based on the ANOVA F-statistic. We used a support vector classifier (SVC) with linear kernel as our classifier. The grid search optimized the number of features (k) between 50 and 60 and the regularization parameter (C) between 0.05 and 0.15 in increments of 0.01 for the SVC. A 10-fold cross validation was used within the training set to optimize the parameters. Based on the number of features (k) and the regularization parameter (C), we then retrained the final model on the entire training set (all 10 folds) using the selected parameters before applying it to the test set. After model fitting, we tested our model using the test dataset.

We report the accuracy score of the model using SciKit Learn’s metric.accuracy_score function. The accuracy score is a ratio of the number of correct predictions over the total number of predictions. The accuracy score works best with balanced classes such as our matched dataset used for the feature selection. To assess potential overfitting and model stability, we also examined cross-validation accuracy across 10 folds (mean = 0.573, SD = 0.049), which was consistent with training and test set performance (mean = 0.573 and 0.575, respectively).

The selected features were then carried forward for the binary logistic regression analysis using the whole sample of low and high SSB consumer before matching (n = 2191). To prevent variance inflation, we identified pairs of features with Pearson correlation coefficients > 0.8. For each highly correlated pair, one feature was randomly selected, resulting in two distinct sets of features. We then fit separate mixed-effects logistic regression models for each of the two feature sets using the glmer() function (lme4 package, R) with a binomial link and the ’bobyqa’ optimizer with a maximum of 100,000 function evaluations (maxfun = 1e5). To correct for potential false positives, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Significance for the final logistic regression was set as pFDR ≤ 0.05. SVC training, cross-validation, and related analyses were performed in Python (v. 3.8.8) using SciKit Learn (v. 1.2.1) and statsmodels (v. 0.12.2).

Post hoc analysis

Initial analysis using less stringent motion control cleaning resulted in a larger dataset. Using this “less-clean” dataset, CHI emerged as a strong predictor of SSB intake. However, this result was attenuated in the smaller, cleaner dataset presented currently. To better understand this discrepancy in results we post-hoc examined the independent relationships between our head motion threshold and CHI, BMI%, and SSB intake.

For the post-hoc tests, we used a sample of participants who passed all other inclusion criteria and were assigned to either the high or low SSB group (n = 3983, Fig. 1). We then created a head motion binary variable: pass (≤ 25 % of frames with FD > 0.2, n = 2191) or fail (>25 % of frames with FD>0.2 mm, n = 1792).

To test for differences between our pass/fail groups we used binary logistic regressions to determine if CHI [40], BMI %, or SSB intake were associated with odds of being excluded due to excessive head motion.

Results

Participants

Fig. 1 shows the consort diagram outlining our exclusion criteria throughout the analysis. From the initial 11,876 participants, 7127 passed our initial eligibility screening on medical conditions and anthropometrics with full data. Furthermore, 3144 participants who reported consuming greater than 0 and <2 servings of SSB per day (medium SSB group) were excluded. The medium SSB group was excluded to ensure that our two SSB groups were distinct without boundary cases.

Of the 3983 high and low SSB consuming participants, 1792 participants failed our head motion check. This sample of 3983 participants (nPass = 2191 and nFail = 1792) was used in post hoc analyses evaluating the relationships between head motion with CHI, BMI %, and SSB intake.

A total of 2191 participants (nLow SSB= 1350 and nHigh SSB= 841) passed all quality control metrics. Because of uneven group sizes, we matched our low SSB group to our high SSB resulting in a matched subset (n = 1682) used for the feature selection analysis. The larger 2191 sample was used for our binary logistic regression analysis.

Demographics for the total sample and matched subsample are detailed in Table 1. Overall, the sample was predominately white (71.5 %), non-Hispanic (82 %), and female (51 %). The average age at baseline was 9.97 ± 0.62 years. Participants in the low SSB group reported drinking 0 servings (8 fl oz) of SSB per day, whereas the high SSB group reported consuming just over 3 servings of SSB per day. The high SSB group had fewer female participants (42.2 % female) compared to the low SSB (56.0 % female; p < 0.001).

Feature selection

For the feature selection analysis, the low SSB group was matched to the high SSB group on BMI %, age in years, and CHI resulting in two groups with 841 participants in each. Details regarding the matching metrics are in Stable 1.

The grid search indicated that a model with 59 features (C = 0.05) provided the best fit. Our model’s test set accuracy was 57.5 %, indicating that it was able to distinguish a high SSB consumer from a low SSB consumer with 57.5 % accuracy overall. The model tended to classify low SSB consumers better than high SSB (60 % versus 55 %, Fig. 2). The selected features included sex and functional connectivity between 58 brain regions (STable 2). Fig. 3 illustrates selected functional connectivity features.

Binary logistic regression

All selected features, outlined in STable 2, were tested using binary logistic regression. We used the larger pre-match sample of 2191 participants (nLow SSB= 1350; nHigh SSB= 841) in the binary logistic regression (Fig. 1). To address multicollinearity among the selected features, we identified highly correlated feature pairs (R > 0.8) and constructed two non-overlapping predictor sets. For each highly correlated pair, we randomly selected one feature to include in the first set and the other in the second. We then fit separate binary logistic regression models to each set, controlling for age and BMI %. Following correction for multiple comparison, two features remained significant in each model: the average correlation (functional connectivity) between the visual network and the right-caudate (predicted probability [PP]: 0.71, z-value: 3.21, standard error [SE]: 1.29, pFDR= 0.02), and sex (z-score: −6.67, SE: 0.10, PPmale: 0.56, PPfemale: 0.72, pFDR < 0.0001).

Post hoc testing

Before extensive data cleaning, we observed a strong association between combined household income (CHI) and SSB at the year-2 follow-up. However, after increasing the stringency of our motion threshold to remove participants with 25 % of their frames with a framewise displacement FD > 0.2, the effect was attenuated. Given the prominent effect of CHI in the original model (pFDR < 0.001), we sought to explain why this relationship did not persist in our final analysis. Three separate analyses showed that FD exclusions were independently associated with lower combined household income (OR= 1.102, 95 % CI: 1.072–1.132, p < 0.0001), higher BMI % (OR= 0.93, 95 % CI: 0.917–0.948, p < 0.0001), and high SSB intake (OR= 0.842, 95 % CI: 0.741–0.956, p = 0.008).

Discussion

Summary

Using a data-driven modeling approach, we identified a sugar sweetened beverage (SSB) network composed of 31 unique regions of interest (ROIs) and sex. The predictive ability of the network was only marginally better than chance (57 % vs. 50 % chance). Our binary logistic regression further highlights that of the 58 functional connectivity pairs and sex, only the functional connection between the right caudate nucleus and visual network, and sex predicted SSB at the 2-year follow up visit. Post hoc analysis of participants excluded due to head motion revealed a positive association between framewise displacement (FD) and combined household income (CHI), as well as with BMI % and SSB intake. Our analysis suggests that connectivity between the right caudate and visual network at rest is predictive of future SSB intake.

We used the SVC model to identify the network of features that are most predictive of SSB intake (SSB network). This is particularly relevant for modeling brain ROI features, where activity in one region may influence other ROIs. However, SVC does not estimate the independent effect of each feature. In contrast, binary logistic regression allows for interpretability at the level of individual predictors. It also enabled us to model random effects of imaging site and family structure, and to control for known covariates (age, sex, BMI %).

Sugar sweetened beverage network

Using the SVC model to reduce the number of possible ROI to ROI functional connections, we found 31 functionally connected ROIs, comprising 58 ROI pairs, and sex best predicted SSB intake group at the 2-year follow up with 57 % accuracy overall and was able to predict the low group better than the high group (60 % vs 55 %). This difference could be related to feature selection bias, although our use of independent datasets for feature selection training and feature selection testing reduces this risk [41]. The difference in accuracy may indicate that the low SSB group is more homogenous compared to the high SSB [42]. Although our feature selection data was matched for BMI %tile, age, and CHI, the low SSB group had a higher proportion of females (57 % female low SSB vs. 43 % female high SSB). Alternatively, we may be missing an important, but unknown, feature related to high SSB consumption but not low SSB consumption. One potential important feature is parental BMI, which we have previously shown to be related to excess weight gain and BOLD response in adolescents [43].

We consider the 31 functionally connected ROIs represent a SSB network. The SSB network is composed of 10 cortical to cortical network connections connecting the auditory (AUD), somatosensory hand (SMH), visual (VIS), cingulo-parietal (COPA), ventral attention (VAN), somatosensory mouth (SMM), and cingulo-opercular (CO) networks. The majority of the SSB network is cortical to subcortical connections with the previously mentioned cortical networks and the addition of the default mode (DMN), dorsal attention (DAN), salience (SA), retro splenial temporal (RST), and “none” networks connected to the thalamus, caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), hippocampus, pallidum, and ventral diencephalon. Finally, the SSB network also exhibits connections between the cerebellum and SA, SMH, RST, and AUD networks.

Our SSB network aligns with previous work examining SSB intake [13,18], sweet taste [27,44,45], and stimulant consumption [46,47] networks and BOLD response. A recent meta-analysis of sweet taste BOLD response revealed 12 ROIs including the bilateral anterior, middle and inferior insula, the bilateral thalamus, left caudate nucleus, left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and the bilateral precentral gyrus [27]. The insula is of particular interest as three subregions (anterior, middle, and inferior) are associated with sweet taste. The insula has been implicated in a variety of cognitive [48], homeostatic [49], and hedonic processes [50], including taste perception [51], fullness perception [52], reward response [50], and decision making [48]. Glasser and colleagues defined eight insular areas based on cortical folding, myelination, thickness and functional connectivity [53]. These eight regions are largely included in the Gordon atlas (used presently) defined CO network, with additional overlap in the AUD and SA networks. The insula in our SSB network is a common region across cortical connectivity (AU to VIS and within network connectivity in the CO), subcortical connectivity (CO to caudate, amygdala, and other subcortical areas), and cerebellar connectivity (SA to cerebellum) related to SSB intake. Within the SSB network, the CO network exhibited the second highest degree (most connections to other ROIs) after the SMH network. Previous work from our group showed increased insula BOLD response in adolescents at higher risk of excess weight gain (by virtue of parental BMI status) to a milkshake taste compare to a tasteless solution [43]. Additionally, Burger and Stice have shown the insula is sensitive to SSB consumption compared to a tasteless solution [13]. Despite similarities with the existing literature our entire network predicts low SSB consumers better than only slightly better than chance. The binary logistic regression model highlights the important features within the network.

Connectivity between the right caudate and visual network predicts SSB intake

Binary logistic regression with the 58 ROI pairs and sex revealed that of all the 59 selected features, sex and connectivity between the visual network and right caudate were significantly related to SSB intake at the 2-year follow up visit. The functional connection between the right caudate and visual network exhibited strong predictive power, with each increase in unit of connection associated with a 71 % increased probability of being in the high group. The concept of functional connectivity is nebulous at best, and quantifying a unit increase in functional connectivity does not have meaning outside of statistics. However, from this we can say that a stronger positive correlation between the right caudate and visual network increases the likelihood an adolescent is to consume 2 or more servings of SSB per day (where a single serving is defined as 8fl oz).

Although the caudate and the visual cortex do not share direct anatomic pathways, the caudate modulates visual input via the substania nigra and superior colliculus to facilitate coordinated eye movements [54]. Theoretically, this path is important for orienting visual towards rewards and rewarding cues. Interestingly, both the ventral striatum (location of the caudate and putamen) and visual regions show increased activity during non-visual reward feedback [55]. The increased connectivity seen between the visual cortex and caudate presently, during a resting state scan with no feedback, seems to suggest that this pathway is active during rest and may predispose adolescents to consume higher amounts of SSB. Furthermore, research on psychological pathologies, binge eating disorder [56] and internet gaming addition [57], both show increased BOLD response in visual regions (fusiform face complex [56] and lingual gyrus [57]) and the caudate. Additionally, stimulants (cigarettes and betel quid) have also been shown to increase activity in both the caudate and visual cortex [46,47]. Predicting cravings in general, using data driven connectome predictive model, shows that the subcortical-basal ganglia, motor/sensory, and visual networks represent a craving core in the brain [58]. In sum, previous work indicates that the association between the caudate and the visual network is not unique to SSB but rather is associated with a variety of habit-forming stimuli.

The limited research that has directly examined the relationship between SSB intake and BOLD activity further supports our results. Burger in his experimental SSB study saw an increase in caudate BOLD response after daily SSB intake, however this was in the left caudate only [18]. Additionally, Burger and Stice saw bilateral caudate BOLD activity when participants anticipated a Coke taste compared to a tasteless solution [13]. Potentially, the left and right caudate nucleus may serve different functions. As previously mentioned, the sweet taste meta-analytic ROIs included only the left caudate similar to Burger’s results [18,27]. On the other hand, a meta-analysis of gut-brain satiety signaling showed that only the right caudate BOLD activity was negatively associated with satiety regulating signals [59]. Potentially, the left caudate may be more sensitive to active SSB consumption, whereas the right may play a more regulatory role.

Head motion-related exclusions linked to lower CHI, higher BMI, and high SSB intake

Our post-hoc analysis sought to explain the difference between our preliminary and final results. Before applying more stringent quality control, we found a strong association between CHI and high SSB intake (OR: 0.37, pFDR< 0.001). Of the participants allocated to SSB groups 45 % were excluded due to high motion. Thus, we tested if head motion outcomes excluded a specific sample. Three separate analyses showed that head motion exclusions were independently associated with lower CHI, higher BMI % and high SSB intake. These findings are consistent with the well-established correlation between low SES, high BMI and high SSB consumption [6,19,60]. Previous work using the ABCD® cohort has shown an association between body weight and head motion during the MRI protocols [61]. Additionally, in adults higher, BMI and body weight best explained excess head motion during rsfMRI [62]. However, our results also highlight an important socio-economic disparity. Our results indicate that participants from low CHI families were more likely to be excluded from the analysis due to failing the head motion FD quality check. Together, our post-hoc results suggest a sample with specific socioeconomic, anthropometric, and diet intake characteristics may be more likely to be excluded from analyses due to excessive head motion. This has likely contributed to the attenuation of the CHI-SSB association in our final model, highlighting a potential source of bias in neuroimaging studies.

Limitations

This is the first study to develop a predictive model of SSB consumption based on a combination of known risk factors and functional brain correlations. The present analysis benefits from a large sample of subjects (n = 1682 to train and test our network model, and n = 2191 for our binary regression). We used a standard 10-fold cross-validation approach for the SVC model. While this improves evaluation stability compared to fewer folds, the per-fold sample size was relatively small (~126 participants for 59 features), which may increase overfitting risk. However, our cross-validation performance was stable (mean= 0.573, SD= 0.049), and accuracy was comparable on training and test sets (0.573 and 0.575). Generalizability may nonetheless be limited by training subset size and potential fold variability. Overall, the accuracy of the model (57 %) was only slightly better than random chance (50 %). SSB intake is a nuanced behavior that cannot only be predicted through functional connectivity and known risk factors used presently. Ideally, future studies will incorporate additional features such as parental behaviors related to SSB intake [63], SSB availability in the home and school, and parental SSB intake [64]. Additionally, although the overall sample is large, it is important to note that our sample was predominately white. Further, as our post hoc analyses show, participants with high BMI % and from low CHI households were more likely to excluded from our analysis due to high head motion.

The whole-brain approach ensured that all potentially involved brain regions were considered and the regions eliciting strongest effect were selected. Additionally, our post hoc analysis showed that head motion-related exclusions disproportionately removed participants with low combined household income, high BMI and high SSB intake. This in turn may be related to an important, but unknown, feature related to high SSB consumption but not low SSB consumption (e.g., brain connectivity). Furthermore, a notable limitation is the nature of the SSB data. SSB consumption was estimated from a self-reported food frequency question and is subject to recall and social desirability bias [65]. Lastly, from the block food screener we were only able to assess intake of soda and fruit drinks, but not sweetened tea, coffee, sports drinks, nor energy drinks.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using a large sample and data driven methods we found that sex and baseline functional connectivity between the right caudate and the visual network showed strong predictive power to detect SSB consumption at the 2-year follow up visit. Future research needs to explore whether connectivity between the caudate and visual network is a trait or early life influences alter the relationship and predispose children to consuming high amounts of SSBs.
