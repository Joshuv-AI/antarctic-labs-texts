24-h smartphone usage patterns in university students using high-granularity tracking
Authors: Mingyue Chen, Xin Hui Chua, Natarajan Padmapriya, Sarah Yi Xuan Tan, Sarah Edney, Bee Choo Tai, Falk Müller-Riemenschneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive smartphone use among university students can significantly impact academic performance, social interactions, and overall wellbeing, yet most studies rely on self-reported screen time, which lacks accuracy and granularity. A micro-longitudinal study was conducted among 100 undergraduates in Singapore using objective, high-granularity smartphone tracking of smartphone screen time (SST), frequency, and app usage. Using zero-inflated gaussian generalized linear mixed models (ZIG GLMMs) for SST and zero-inflated negative binomial generalized linear mixed models (ZINB GLMMs) for use frequency, 24-h temporal patterns were characterized based on a 7-day analysis, with differences examined by sex and day type (weekday/weekend). Average SST was 6.1 ± 2.8 h/day, with sustained use across waking hours and a peak at night. Sex differences in screen time and use frequency varied across a day. Also, weekdays exhibited a higher smartphone use frequency, likely linked to academic or social demands, while weekends featured prolonged leisure use. Self-reported and objective SST showed weak agreement (Cohen’s Kappa ~ 0). This study demonstrates the feasibility of scalable and objective smartphone tracking to capture granular digital behaviors with minimal participant burden. Findings offer valuable insights into temporal patterns of smartphone use and provide a methodological foundation for future digital health research and interventions development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52696-0.Keywords: Digital health monitoring, Mobile technology, Behavioral patterns, Temporal analysis, Digital determinants, Digital phenotypingSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

With constant network connectivity and a wide range of applications, smartphones have become integral to daily life. Young adults aged 18–24 have the highest smartphone ownership rates globally, reaching 100% in Singapore1,2. University students, in particular, represent a critical population for digital health research, as they experience high academic demands, increasing independence, and rising mental health challenges3. These factors may contribute to unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, with prevalence estimates of smartphone addiction among university students ranging from approximately 28% to 80%2,4. The pervasive use of smartphones has raised concerns about both physical and mental health. Excessive smartphone screen time (SST) has been associated with insufficient sleep5, physical inactivity6,7, altered cortical processes8, and musculoskeletal disorders related to prolonged poor postures9. Furthermore, smartphone use, particularly for social media, has been linked to poorer mental well-being, higher levels of anxiety and depression among young people10,11, and disruptions to essential daily routines like studying and socializing12.

However, much of the existing evidence on SST and its health effects is based on self-reported data, which is subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and incomplete reporting, particularly for habitual behaviors that occur frequently and often go unnoticed by individuals13,14. Studies comparing self-reported and objectively measured SST have revealed significant discrepancies, with self-reports often underestimating actual usage15. While some research has attempted to move toward objective measurement16, many prior studies have relied on limited or indirect data sources, such as static screenshots of device “Screen Time” summaries17,18, single application logs19,20, or computer activity logs21. Those using mobile tracking applications mainly report daily total screen time, lack analysis of time of day or day type (weekday/weekend) patterns, or focus on a single usage indicator without integrating duration, frequency, and app-level behaviors6,22.

Recent advances in passive smartphone monitoring applications allow for the continuous, timestamped collection of screen state and app usage, enabling high-granularity assessment of smartphone use in real-world settings13,15,23. Such data offer several advantages: they minimize reporting bias, enable examination of usage frequency and timing across the 24-h day, and allow differentiation of behaviors by app type and context. Importantly, evidence suggests that these dimensions, including when screens are used, how often they are checked, and for what purposes, may be more relevant to health outcomes than total daily screen time alone6,16,24,25. Despite these advantages, most objective studies remain limited in scope. To our knowledge, no study has comprehensively characterized high-granularity 24-h smartphone usage patterns, combining screen-state duration, use frequency, and app usage to examine temporal and contextual variation among university students1,13,14,22,26.

To address these gaps, this study aims to (1) propose a scalable, objective measure of timestamped SST and app usage, evaluating its feasibility for future implementation in large cohorts; (2) systematically characterize detailed smartphone and app usage patterns among university students, exploring differences by sex and day type (weekday/weekend); (3) compare self-reported SST with objectively measured SST to evaluate the reliability of self-reports. We hypothesize that objective measurement will yield highly complete data, reveal significant temporal and app-based variations in smartphone usage, and identify discrepancies in self-reports. By moving beyond the repetitive development of self-report assessments and simple volume metrics to highly granular, context-aware measures, this study offers a methodological foundation for linking digital behaviors to health and well-being outcomes. These insights can inform future epidemiological studies, digital health interventions, and evidence-based strategies to mitigate the potential harms of excessive smartphone use.

Methods

Study recruitment and participants

The Healthy Campus Initiative (HCI) is a four-year longitudinal cohort study following students at the National University of Singapore (NUS) from their first year to the completion of their undergraduate degree. Data collection occurred at three time points: baseline (September 2021–February 2022), midway follow-up (January–April 2024), and final follow-up (February–April 2025). HCI participants were invited to enroll in the current study (“digital sub-cohort”) at the midway follow-up. Project briefings and enrolment were conducted one-on-one in person. This study was reviewed and approved by the NUS Institutional Review Board (IRB) with approval number NUS-IRB-2023–916, dated December 19, 2023. All methods used in the present study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of NUS-IRB, including the most recent version of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and Singapore’s Human Biomedical Research Act (2015). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in this study.

The study included participants who were students in a four-year degree program at NUS, declared their intention to stay in Singapore for the duration of their studies, and used a smartphone with internet access. Participants who did not provide informed consent for participation and those who provided less than 1 complete day (24 h, midnight to midnight) of app usage data were excluded from the study.

Study design

The study employed a micro-longitudinal design. At onsite enrolment, participants were asked to complete online surveys on demographics delivered via the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a web-based survey application27. Objectively measured screen time and app usage data were obtained via Avicenna (Avicenna Data Services Inc., Waterloo, ON, Canada), an epidemiological smartphone application specifically adapted for this study. Participants completed up to 10 days of assessments, during which the app captured SST data on both Android and iOS (iPhone Operating System) platforms, while app usage data were collected exclusively on Android devices due to limitations of the current tracking technology (Fig. 1). No private or personal information, such as the content of instant messages or web pages visited, was recorded. Once installed, the app operated in the background without interfering with regular smartphone use. Screen time data were automatically uploaded to the secure application server and stored in the database. For analysis, only the last 7 consecutive days of complete valid data were used to ensure comparability across participants.

Experimental design workflow chart.

Assessment of objectively measured smartphone screen time and app usage

Objective and time-stamped smartphone data were passively collected using built-in sensors, capturing screen state (ON/OFF) for screen time calculations and app usage (app name and time). To ensure accurate measurement of screen-state and app usage metrics, we applied a series of rigorous data processing steps. We considered numerous real-life scenarios such as auto-notifications that result in non-interactive screen activations and developed a series of “notification” cut-points for ON-state, including 5, 10, 15, and 20 s. Sensitivity analysis indicated that using 5- or 10-s thresholds produced near identical results, while thresholds of 15 and 20 s introduced noticeable differences. Furthermore, a review of the literature on temporary notification settings for both Android and iOS devices indicated that such notifications typically last 10 s or less26,28. Additional in-house testing during the study analysis phase demonstrated that most auto-notifications resulted in screen activations lasting close to 10 s. Based on these findings and prior evidence, we adopted a 10-s threshold for excluding brief screen activations. This threshold was applied consistently for all analyses including SST and app usage.

Primary analyses were conducted at the hourly level for SST, app usage, and use frequency within a full 24-h day. To enhance interpretability, six-hour intervals were also summarized for app usage: morning (06:00–11:59), afternoon (12:00–17:59), night (18:00–23:59), and midnight (00:00–05:59). Smartphone applications were categorized into 6 groups: Social/Communication, Entertainment/Gaming, Productivity, Utilities/Built-in, News/Information, and Lifestyle. Initial classification was based on Google Play Store categories via automated scraping, followed by consolidation into broader categories informed by prior literature and manual review of individual apps29. This approach provides a systematic, literature-based framework for grouping apps while maintaining consistency across participants.

Assessment of subjectively reported smartphone screen time

The questions were based on a screen time questionnaire adapted from the domain-specific Adult Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire (ASBQ)30. Participants were asked to report the time spent on their smartphone using a four-item questionnaire on typical weekdays and weekends separately and indicate the purpose of use (recreation or study/work) for each. The average daily SST was then calculated based on these responses.

Assessment of demographics

Age, sex, citizenship, ethnicity, and faculty of study were collected using standard questionnaires. Year of study was classified into years 2, 3, or 4. Ethnicity included Chinese or others. Citizenship was classified as Singaporean/Permanent Residents (PRs) and international students. Faculty of study included three categories: humanities and business, science and engineering, and health sciences. Whether students worked part-time was also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized as mean (± standard deviation, SD) for continuous variables and count with percentage for categorical variables. Students’ t-tests were used to compare age and daily app usage across different categories. Fisher’s exact test was applied to assess associations between categorical demographic variables.

To analyze smartphone usage patterns, further exploratory data analysis on screen time and use frequency across different hours of the day was conducted. The proportion of participant-days with active smartphone use across different hours of a day was calculated and visualized using bar charts. Results showed that 37.5% of the screen time observations across different hours of a day were zero, indicating potential zero inflation. Non-normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q (Quantile–Quantile) plot, while overdispersion was identified through variance-to-mean comparison and confirmed using DHARMa residual (Residual Diagnostics for Hierarchical Multi-Level/Mixed Regression Models) diagnostics. A similar pattern of zero inflation and overdispersion was observed for screen use frequency. Based on these distributional characteristics, four models were applied to examine screen time and usage frequency across hours of the day31. Model-based marginal plots were presented to visualize predicted values over time, incorporating interaction effects between time of day and sex, or time of day and day type. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each outcome.

App usage by category was further visualized using stacked bar charts, showing the percentage contribution of each app category to total usage, stratified by sex and day type. Self-reported and objectively measured SST were then categorized into low (< 4.0 h/day), medium (4.0–6.9 h/day), and high (≥ 7.0 h/day) based on thresholds from previous studies14,32. To assess the agreement between these two measures, we conducted a Cohen’s Kappa test for all participants and by sex. Bland–Altman plots were used to visually evaluate agreement between subjective and objective SST, displaying the mean bias with 95% and 1 SD limits of agreement (LoA)33. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess reliability between measurement methods which was based on a mean-rating, absolute-agreement, two-way mixed-effects model34. ICC values were classified as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.74), good (0.75–0.90), and excellent (> 0.90)34.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2, with two-sided tests performed at the 5% significance level.

Results

Participants characteristics

The participant recruitment process is shown in Fig. 2. 120 students provided consent; however, 14 withdrew, 1 did not register on the customized app, 2 were inadvertently recruited twice, and 3 had less than 1 complete day of data. The present analysis was based on 100 students who provided consent and valid surveys and tracked screen time, with 41 Android users and 59 iOS users. Among the 100 participants, 90 (including all 41 Android users) provided 7 complete days of data. The remaining 10 participants (all iOS users), contributed between 2 and 6 days (2 with 2 days, 2 with 4 days, 2 with 5 days, 4 with 6 days). Sensitivity analyses applying stricter inclusion criteria (e.g., requiring at least 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day) excluded 2 participants (n = 98) and did not materially alter the primary findings. In total, 676 participant-days were included in the analysis.

The majority of participants were Chinese (83.0%) and Singaporean or PR (86.0%). A large percentage of students (58.0%) majored in science and engineering, followed by humanities and business (31.0%) and health sciences (11.0%).

Smartphone usage pattern – screen time and use frequency

Overall, the average daily screen time was 6.1 ± 2.8 h/day, with minimal differences observed between weekdays (6.0 ± 2.8 h/day) and weekends (6.1 ± 3.2 h/day), or between females (6.0 ± 2.8 h/day) and males (6.2 ± 2.9 h/day). The average daily screen use frequency was 118.2 ± 79.0 times/day, with slightly higher frequency on weekdays (124.7 ± 80.7 times/day) compared to weekends (102.7 ± 72.5 times/day), and among females (120.6 ± 84.5 times/day) compared to males (114.7 ± 70.0 times/day). Additionally, active usage percentage steadily declined after midnight, reaching its lowest point (< 9.8%) at 05:00, then increased throughout the day, peaking between 10:00 and 23:00 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Analysis of smartphone screen time across the day revealed interaction effects with both sex and day type. In Model 1, the interaction between time of day and sex was statistically significant (p = 0.005), indicating that sex differences in screen time varied across 24 h. Specifically, males exhibited higher screen time than females during 07:00–11:00 and 13:00–14:00 (Fig. 3a), and females higher at 22:00–23:00. Similarly, in Model 2, the interaction between time of day and day type was also significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the effect of day type on screen time differed by hour of the day. Screen time was slightly higher on weekdays between 08:00 and 10:00, whereas it was higher on weekends from 23:00 to 04:00 (Fig. 3b).

Smartphone use frequency follows a similar pattern. In Model 3, a significant interaction between time of day and sex (p < 0.001) revealed that the frequency of phone use varied by sex throughout a day. Females used their phones more frequently in the early morning (07:00–09:00), while males showed more frequent use during 13:00–16:00 and 01:00–05:00 (Fig. 3c). In Model 4, the interaction between time of day and day type was statistically significant (p = 0.002), indicating that the frequency of smartphone use differed between weekdays and weekends across the day. Notably, use frequency was higher on weekdays between 10:00 and 18:00 (Fig. 3d).

Smartphone usage patterns – app usage

A total of 555 different apps were recorded and classified into six mutually exclusive categories (Table 2). Gaming/Entertainment (2.1 ± 1.9 h/day) and Social/Communication (1.9 ± 1.2 h/day) were the most used, while Utilities/Built-in (0.4 ± 0.3 h/day) and Lifestyle (0.4 ± 0.3 h/day) apps were the least used. Females spent more time on News/Information (1.3 ± 1.1 vs. 0.6 ± 0.6 h/day, p < 0.001) and Productivity apps (0.4 ± 0.3 vs. 0.1 ± 0.1 h/day, p < 0.001), whereas males spent more time on Gaming/Entertainment apps (2.6 ± 2.2 vs. 1.6 ± 1.4 h/day, p < 0.001). These sex differences in app usage remained consistent across different time windows throughout the day (p < 0.001, results not shown). Additionally, Gaming/Entertainment app usage was higher on weekends compared to weekdays (2.5 ± 2.4 vs. 1.9 ± 1.7 h/day, p = 0.041).

Difference between self-reported SST and objectively measured SST

Overall, the mean difference between self-reported and objectively measured SST was not statistically significant at the group level (p = 0.314, Supplementary Table 1: 10-s threshold). Among females, the mean difference between the two measures was small, with self-reported SST slightly overestimated by 0.3 h/day (p = 0.484). In contrast, males showed a significant discrepancy, underestimating their SST by an average of 1.5 h/day (p = 0.012).

Agreement analyses further demonstrated substantial discrepancies at the individual level. The Bland–Altman analysis plotted the average of the two measures on the X-axis and their difference on the Y-axis. The mean difference indicated a fixed bias, with self-reported SST underestimating objective SST by 0.4 h/day (Fig. 5). A proportional bias was observed, indicating that participants tended to underestimate their screen time when their actual usage was low and overestimate it when their actual usage was high (slope = 0.420, p = 0.046). The standard 95% LoA ranged from −7.5 to 6.8 h, indicating substantial disagreement between the two methods. To better reflect the observed distribution, we proposed using 1 standard deviation (SD = 3.7 h/day) as a more reasonable limit. Notably, around 23% of participants fell outside the 1 SD limit. Supplementary Table 2 further illustrates poor agreement between self-reported and objective SST across low, medium, and high usage groups, with Cohen’s Kappa values near zero. Similarly, the ICC was 0.110 (p = 0.246), consistent with poor agreement based on predefined criteria in methods. These findings underscore systematic differences between self-reported and objective measures at the individual level.

Discussion

This study successfully addressed three key objectives regarding smartphone use patterns among university students. First, we demonstrated the feasibility of implementing an objective, time-stamped measure of smartphone usage patterns and app usage, achieving high data completeness with minimal participant burden, supporting its scalability for larger cohorts. Second, by leveraging continuous 24-h data, our analysis characterized detailed smartphone usage patterns across time of day, sex, and day types, revealing variations in screen time, use frequency, app preference. Finally, we identified important discrepancies in self-reported versus objectively measured SST, underscoring potential biases in self-reported data. These findings provide valuable insights into smartphone usage behaviors among university students and highlight methodological considerations for future research in digital health research.

This study demonstrates the feasibility and replicability of an approach to objectively measure SST and app usage. 90% of participants provided 7 complete 24-h days of high-granularity screen usage data, yielding almost 700 complete participant-days in total. The custom tracking app operated unobtrusively in the background, automatically uploading usage data to a secure server without interrupting normal smartphone use, and participants reported no complaints or burden from this passive data collection. This approach illustrates how personal smartphones can serve as reliable digital phenotyping tools35 at low cost and effort for both users and investigators. Moreover, for the development of this objective tracking method, this study tested a range of cut-off points and determined that a 10-s threshold was the most reliable for excluding non-interactive screen activation, consistent with prior literature suggesting that short-duration activations are often unrelated to active use1,26. Previous studies have applied varied cut-off points, such as excluding 1-s activations6, often without formal validation26,36. Additionally, some research focused specifically on prolonged screen-on usage26, but given extensive smartphone use among university students, we did not set specific thresholds for daily or continuous screen/app usage1,37. Notably, after excluding brief screen-on under 10 s triggered by notifications, screen-on windows were short (90% lasted under 10 min), with only one instance exceeding seven hours (not shown in results). While SST tracking was feasible across all smartphone brands, app usage data collection was limited to Android devices. This aligns with prior studies, where detailed app usage data was primarily available for Android due to iOS restrictions on third-party tracking13,14,24,26,36,38. For iOS users, previous studies often relied on built-in settings for tracking smartphone usage, requiring participants to manually upload or record the reported data14,39. To date, no study has reported detailed app and smartphone usage patterns across all phone brands. Future research could bridge this gap by leveraging iOS SensorKit and Screen Time APIs (Application Programming Interfaces) to enable passive, privacy-preserving data collection.

This study provides valuable insights into the smartphone usage patterns of university students in Singapore. On average, participants spent 6.1 h daily on their smartphones, lower than prior self-reported studies that reported averages of 8.2 h among Singapore university students1, and 8–10 h among US college students based on self-reported data40. Despite these differences, the findings reinforce growing concerns about smartphone dependency among university students globally. By leveraging timestamped and app-level data, this study fills a key research gap by characterizing smartphone use at high temporal granularity and highlighting not just daily totals, but how usage varies by hour, day type, and sex. Usage duration and frequency declined after midnight, reaching their lowest point at 05:00, and peaking between 18:00 and 23:00. Sex differences in usage patterns varied temporally: males exhibited marginally higher screen time during morning and afternoon, though these differences were minor. Conversely, phone use frequency showed more pronounced variations, with females engaging in more frequent interactions in the morning and males displaying higher frequency in the afternoon and late-night hours. Moreover, phone use frequency was significantly higher on weekdays, suggesting a greater tendency for brief but frequent phone interactions, possibly related to academic or social commitments. On weekends, phone use frequency was lower in the morning but increased in the afternoon and evening, which may reflect a shift toward prolonged, leisure-driven usage15,36. These differences likely reflect distinct usage modes: frequency captures habitual, brief checking behaviors, whereas duration reflects sustained engagement. Supporting this, bout-level analyses indicated that most smartphone interactions were short but frequent, while prolonged usage episodes (e.g., ≥ 30 min) were rare (not shown in results). This distinction highlights that similar total screen time may arise from fundamentally different behavioral patterns, emphasizing the importance of considering both frequency and duration in digital behavior research15,36.

When analyzing app usage patterns, both males and females tended to use the same few apps (Telegram, Instagram, Google Chrome, TikTok, and YouTube), reinforcing the dominant role of smartphones in social and communication activities, a trend observed globally41,42. However, males demonstrated a greater tendency toward gaming and entertainment applications, whereas females showed higher engagement with social media and information-oriented content. These differences are consistent with earlier findings, and may reflect underlying variations in usage motivations, such as entertainment-seeking versus social interaction and information consumption43. Notably, the higher usage of news and information apps among females in our study contrasts with earlier findings43. While the present study was not designed to establish causal mechanisms, this pattern may reflect evolving digital engagement or a broader societal trend in information consumption44. Importantly, these findings highlight that smartphone use is not homogeneous, and that both the purpose and context of app engagement vary across individuals. Further work incorporating richer behavioral and psychosocial measures, as well as session-level analyses (e.g., session length, within-session app sequences, and app co-occurrence), may offer deeper insights into underlying behavioral processes.

Moreover, this study revealed low agreement between self-reported and objectively measured SST. While average self-reported SST appeared close to objective values, only one-third of participants accurately estimated their usage. Consistent with prior research, self-reported data tended to underestimate actual SST, particularly among males15,16,39,45,46. Notably, males in our study underestimated their usage more than females, possibly due to unrecognized time spent on entertainment and gaming during early morning and afternoon hours19. These findings align with a broader body of literature demonstrating systematic discrepancies between subjective and objective behavioral measures47. Future research should minimize reliance on self-reports for movement behavior analysis, especially in research on smartphone-related behaviors and health impact16,26,41,48. Interventions and public health programs should consider integrating objective monitoring tools to provide users with feedback grounded in actual behavior rather than perception26.

Future research should adopt such objective smartphone tracking in large cohorts to explore how usage patterns relate to psychological, cognitive, and physical health outcomes. Incorporating richer participant-level covariates, such as personality traits, problematic smartphone use measures, and academic performance indicators, will further strengthen the interpretation of these associations. While the present study focused on feasibility and descriptive characterization, the high-granularity temporal data generated provide a robust methodological foundation for future research. This framework is currently being extended in a larger longitudinal cohort to enable more advanced analyses, including the identification of high-risk usage phenotypes. In addition, integrating these data with ecological momentary assessments and interventions (EMAs/EMIs) and leveraging data-driven methods such as machine learning, will facilitate real-time feedback and tailored interventions37,49–51. Together, these approaches hold promise for optimizing intervention strategies to improve engagement and effectiveness and mitigating the negative health impacts of excessive smartphone use.

Strengths and limitations

This study leveraged objectively measured smartphone screen time and app usage, reducing the biases inherent in self-reported data. It provided high-granularity insights into 24-h usage patterns, app preferences, and variations by sex and day type. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to examine temporal smartphone usage with screen time and use frequency analysis across a day, offering a more detailed perspective beyond daily averages. By passively tracking usage on personal devices to capture fine-grained data, this approach provides a scalable methodological framework for studying digital behaviors in larger cohorts, supported by high data completeness and minimal participant burden. These insights may inform targeted interventions, such as addressing prolonged evening screen time to mitigate potential negative effects.

However, there are notable limitations. First, the backend logging mechanism recorded cumulative app usage at irregular intervals, potentially misattributing some app activity when analyzing patterns across different hours of a day. Second, app data collection was limited to Android users due to iOS restrictions on passive tracking apps, introducing potential selection bias and limiting comparability. Given known personality differences between Android and iOS users52,53, future studies should explore more advanced methods to track screen time across all smartphone brands to improve generalizability. Third, this study was conducted in a single university with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit generalizability. As a pilot study designed to evaluate the feasibility of objective, high-granularity smartphone tracking, larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to confirm the robustness and external validity of these findings. Fourth, self-reported screen time was assessed retrospectively prior to baseline, whereas objective measurements were collected during the subsequent tracking period. Although the temporal gap was short (within 11 days for each participant) and both measures were obtained within the same academic semester (AY23/24 Semester 2), with previous studies suggesting that smartphone use remains relatively stable over short periods18,54, this misalignment may still introduce bias if smartphone use varied due to academic demands or personal circumstances. Fifth, several analytical choices, including the non-interactive screen activation threshold, time-window aggregation, and app categorization, may influence results. While sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the selected threshold and hourly aggregation, alternative specifications may yield different patterns. In addition, app categorization was not formally validated against established taxonomies or inter-rater agreement, which may introduce classification bias. Finally, sleep–wake timing was not directly measured. As a result, interpretation of clock-time smartphone use may vary across individuals, since the same usage time (e.g., morning hours) may correspond to different points in the daily routine depending on wake time. Future studies integrating wearable-derived sleep measures of circadian timing could enable more precise alignment of smartphone use patterns with individuals’ daily rhythms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using objective, high-granularity smartphone tracking to capture screen use and app usage patterns among university students. Our findings revealed that students averaged approximately six hours of daily smartphone screen time, with distinct temporal patterns showing peak usage during late evening hours. While smartphone screen time remained relatively consistent across weekdays and weekends, use frequency varied significantly, with more frequent, shorter interactions on weekdays. Sex differences were observed in app preferences: males favored Entertainment/Gaming apps, whereas females engaged more with News/Information apps; both sexes used Social/Communication apps extensively. The significant discrepancies between self-reported and objectively measured smartphone screen time underscore the critical importance of objective measurement tools in digital health research. By moving beyond aggregate volume metrics toward fine-grained and temporal indicators of smartphone use, this study provides a scalable methodological foundation for linking digital behaviors to health and well-being outcomes in larger cohorts. These findings may inform future epidemiological research, digital health interventions, and evidence-based strategies aimed at mitigating potential harms associated with excessive smartphone use.
